#5450. New Hilton Pay With Points App Feature: Why You Should (Probably) Skip It

Hilton Honors has announced a new way for members to redeem their points directly toward the cost of on-property spending at check-out, exclusively for elite members through the app. More options are always a good thing, but please don’t use your points this way, despite the data that Hilton is using to suggest that this is appealing.

Hilton Honors has launched Pay with Points. The idea is that elite members — Hilton Honors Silver, Gold, and Diamond members — can apply points toward eligible hotel charges during digital check-out in the Hilton Honors app.

This feature is now available at 150 participating hotels in the United States. This is expected to expand to more than 800 hotels by the end of 2026, with a full rollout to more than 6,000 properties in the US and Canada by early 2027. Availability and eligible redemption options may vary by property.

Hilton claims it’s the first major hotel brand to offer something like this through a fully digital check out experience with no front desk visit required. That’s true, though the point of differentiation here isn’t the ability to redeem points toward on-property spending, but instead, the ability to do this without interacting with someone at the front desk. Accor Live Limitless, Global Hotel Alliance, and World of Hyatt, all let you redeem points toward hotel costs at check-out.

Okay, so let’s actually talk about this for a second. More redemption options are always a good thing, assuming nothing is being taken away, so I don’t want to criticize Hilton for this.

While Hilton isn’t stating how much value you’ll get per point when redeeming this way, I think it’s safe to say the value will be very poor. After all, Hilton will presumably be on the hook for paying cash to properties to compensate them for purchases offset with points.

I value Hilton Honors points at 0.5 cents each, and there’s no denying that outsized redemptions have become harder to come by. I’d expect that you’ll get significantly less value per point with this method. For example, if you want to redeem Hilton points toward Amazon purchases, you get 0.2 cents of value per point. So I’d expect the value to be between the two.

The reason these redemptions are lower value is because you can redeem smaller quantities of points, so that helps consumers avoid breakage. It’s also interesting how this is exclusively available to elite members. What’s the logic for that? Well, the idea is that Hilton doesn’t want non-elite members to be able to easily redeem all their points.

However, I can’t help but laugh a bit at how Hilton is describing the logic for this new feature:

Pay with Points was created in direct response to Hilton Honors member feedback. According to recent Hilton research, 70% of Hilton Honors members surveyed are interested in using Points toward on-property purchases, and approximately half said the capability could make them more likely to stay or book with Hilton. Pay with Points brings flexibility into the digital stay experience, giving Hilton Honors members the ability to put their Points to work even before they leave the hotel.

Was the research really conducted by asking “hey, would you be interested in redeeming points for on-property purchases?” Who would say no to that? Is the 70% number supposed to be impressive? I assume the question wasn’t framed with disclosure of the subpar value this would offer.

Hilton is rolling out a new Pay with Points feature, whereby elite members can redeem points through the Hilton Honors app toward the cost of stays, including on-property spending. The idea is that by checking out through the app, members can choose to partially offset costs.

Hilton isn’t publishing how much value you’ll get per point, but I think it’s safe to say that the redemption value here will be very subpar, given that Hilton will presumably be paying face value for these redemptions.

#5445. The Best Hotels Across Thailand That Deliver Island Luxury, City Elegance, and Jungle Seclusion

When it comes to the world's most luxurious places to stay, Thailand hotels punch well above their weight. In Bangkok, glossy city hotels rival those found in New York and London, and some of the country's landmark addresses are tucked away in beach towns. Chiva-Som is regularly voted as the best destination spa in the world, while Aman, Banyan Tree, and Six Senses properties are dotted throughout the best islands in Thailand. There are plenty of boutique stays and under-the-radar bolt-holes to be found too, where friendly service and traditional cooking will leave you feeling like you've gained a glimpse into life as a local. For a shortlist of our favorites, we rounded up the best hotels in Thailand below.

The Thai capital has no shortage of swanky beds to book, but it's only once in a blue moon that a hotel with such star power as the Capella Bangkok comes around. Opened in 2020 on a garden-clad estate along the Chao Phraya River, this low-rise pile of blonde wood and creamy marble entered the scene with such Bangkok firsts as travertine hot tubs on suite balconies, and villas pitched up right on the waterfront. Côte, the hotel's Mediterranean-tinged fine-dining spot by Argentine chef Mauro Colagreco, earned a Michelin star within a year after opening, while those in the know flock to the boudoir-like Stella Bar after dark to sip on drinks inspired by some of Bangkok's most storied districts.

Compared with many of its rivals, this Bangkok stalwart doesn’t have a location that’s wildly convenient: The postcard sights need a boat transfer and then a taxi ride. But for the legions of loyal fans of this monochrome masterpiece by hotel guru Bill Bensley, that’s the point. Pitched up on a lush riverside plot in the hushed Dusit district, The Siam feels like a portal to a bygone Bangkok. Even though it opened over a decade ago, it’s easy to imagine it as a plush playground for the city’s erstwhile big guns: the midcentury movers and shakers whose heirlooms—vintage Pan Am posters, tattered travel trunks, and chipped ceramics—adorn the marble-floored hallways. The Thai capital’s five-star hotel scene gets plusher every year, but this timeless beauty remains in a league of its own.

Aman Nai Lert Bangkok is not a place one loves then leaves. It stays with you. For nearly a year, over and over, I’ve wished to be back in its ninth-floor infinity pool, delicate shadows from a massive sampong tree canopy dancing on the salt water as a wispy breeze fluttered in my hair. I can’t count how often I’ve begun salivating when I've thought of the sweet-and-salty moo ping pork skewers and limitless bowls of melt-in-your-mouth mango I devoured at breakfast. And I crave deeply the energy-releasing tap-tap-tap of the hand-carved teak tools used in the hotel spa’s Nud Tok Sen massage.

These are not only happy memories; they are all connected to the rich storytelling of this urban resort built amid a seven-acre bubble of lushness that was the early-20th-century teak retreat of visionary Thai entrepreneur Phraya Bhakdinorasreth, known as Nai Lert. His descendants’ hotel and private members club is intimately tethered to its place down to the littlest detail. Glamorously artful and thoughtful in both aesthetics and hospitality, the bolt-hole is perhaps Bangkok’s most personal, chockablock with intimate references in a setting best described as elysian. Being a guest here feels like being woven into the fabric of its history too. —Kathryn Romeyn

#5442. Frontier Airlines’ New First Class Won’t Include Food, Drinks, Or Recline

In early 2025, Frontier Airlines announced plans to introduce first class, along with several other initiatives intended to become more competitive when it comes to premium demand and loyalty. While first class was initially supposed to be rolled out as of late 2025, that timeline didn’t stick.

There’s now an update, as we have more of a sense of when this product will launch. For that matter, we’ve learned more about the soft product, and this will be even more basic than we were expecting…

View from the Wing reports on a memo to Frontier flight attendants, about the carrier’s rollout of first class. For context, Frontier plans to reconfigure its entire fleet to add eight first class seats at the front of the cabin, with two rows of seats in a 2-2 configuration. Specifically, the airline has selected the Geven Comoda seating platform.

Up until now, we haven’t known the timeline for this being introduced, though now we know. The expectation is that Frontier will start reconfiguring planes with first class as of late October 2026, and then seats will be on sale for travel as of March 5, 2027. So while the seats will already be in service, and some customers will luck out, that’s the formal launch plan.

The expectation is that all planes will have first class by then. That’s obviously a fast timeline, but keep in mind most of the cabin will remain unchanged, so it shouldn’t take much time to reconfigure each aircraft.

This is where it gets interesting. Initially Frontier hadn’t revealed any details about its first class soft product, but now it has. Here’s what Frontier’s first class page says:

First Class seats offer our most spacious seating experience, providing exceptional comfort and additional personal space at the front of the aircraft. These seats are pre-reclined and do not convert into lie-flat beds.

Complimentary food and beverages are not included with the First Class experience. However, a variety of food and beverage options are available for purchase by all passengers.

So Frontier’s first class seats will be “pre-reclined” (meaning they won’t recline), and won’t actually offer complimentary snacks and drinks, so it’ll be just like economy in that regard. Interestingly, when Frontier first shared renderings of its new first class seats in 2025, there were buttons that made it look like there would be a recliner function, but clearly they’ve backtracked on that.

On the plus side, the seats are expected to feature in-seat power, which is a pretty novel amenity for Frontier. The carrier is also going from offering no Wi-Fi, to offering Starlink Wi-Fi as of mid-2027, so that’s pretty exciting.

There’s no single right or wrong way to offer a premium product. However, I feel like Frontier might be taking the wrong strategy here. What Frontier is offering is essentially more along the lines of the initial Spirit “Big Front Seat” — you get more space, and nothing else.

This just strikes me as a missed opportunity, because I feel like psychologically, willingness to pay is going to be substantially higher if you just include free alcoholic drinks and at least some sort of food. It’s not like I was expecting them to install ovens and have hot meals (JetBlue’s new first class will only have cold food).

But I feel like if the airline just invested $10 in drinks & snacks they could charge more, and would also expand the potential customer base. Whether logical or not, there are some number of passengers who would be more likely to even consider a product if it actually had a dedicated soft product, rather than just being a more spacious seat. At least that’s my take.

Frontier Airlines is expected to shortly start reconfiguring planes with first class seats, with the plan being for tickets to be on sale for travel as of March 5, 2027. While Frontier first class will have first class-style seats (minus the recline), it sounds like there won’t be any differentiation in terms of the soft product, as food and drinks will still be available for purchase.

I would’ve liked the airline to actually roll out a differentiated soft product, but it sounds like that’s not happening. I’m curious to see what the soft product will be like. And at least Starlink is also good news.

But the Ukrainian long-range strikes, coupled with years of Western sanctions, have successfully choked off much of Moscow’s energy earnings, the life blood of its war machine. The Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries—the direct cause of Trump’s ire—have been especially effective, with Russian exports of refined products at their second-lowest level of the war last month.

Zelensky met with U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff in Oslo, Norway, on Tuesday for another round of talks, with Jared Kushner joining remotely. The Ukrainian president reiterated his willingness to accept a mutual cease-fire on attacks on energy facilities but only if Moscow reciprocates.

The pressing concern for Ukraine this fall is that the Russian strikes threaten to overwhelm an energy system that has been battered but has still survived four winters of war. Not a day has gone by since mid-July without a Russian attack on Ukrainian energy facilities, Ukrainian Prime Minister Sergii Koretskyi said over the weekend, with more than 2,000 strikes during that time. Ukraine has now lost about two-thirds of its prewar electricity generation capacity.

Both Ukraine’s power sector and Russian attack capabilities have evolved during the war. Ukrainian power plants have been hardened, with protective shielding and more robust air defenses, and the electricity system itself has been made more resilient through decentralization, including a greater use of renewable energy. Russian attacks, though, have kept pace with Ukraine’s efforts; the introduction of jet-powered drones, coupled with long-range ballistic missiles, is making it harder for Ukraine to prevent the increasing number of successful Russian strikes.

That’s why the sudden reversal of U.S. policy this month—including limitations on Ukraine’s use of Starshield, lingering questions over U.S. intelligence sharing (such as warnings of ballistic missile launches), and a U.S. refusal so far to allow European partners to transfer Patriot interceptors to Ukraine—has caused Zelensky so much concern.

It goes without saying that the premium card business model nowadays is largely based around offering credits and benefits that can help offset the annual fee. In almost all cases, the headline value of the credits is way higher than the annual fee, and that’s because there’s intended to be some breakage, plus some additional monetization opportunities:

With premium cards offering hotel credits, it’s incredibly common for those credits to require a minimum stay. The concept there is simple, and it’s that they want you to spend a lot on your hotel stay, so that the commission offsets the credit as much as possible.

Visitors will notice the Dolomites remain deeply rooted in Ladin culture and traditions, with Roman and Milanese weekenders setting a palpably Italian (not touristy) tone despite the area's rising popularity. Ready to stay and see the fairy tale with your own eyes? #5424. Polestar and Oxford wired up drivers to measure how performance feels

Polestar and the University of Oxford put 12 drivers in eye trackers and heart-rate monitors and sent them around a Swedish racetrack in two versions of the Polestar 4. The goal was to put numbers on something no spec sheet captures: how performance feels to the person driving.

The 544 hp Polestar 4 Dual motor with Performance pack delivered 32% higher peak acceleration than the 268 hp Rear motor version on matched laps, and it needed 15% less steering effort to do it. Drivers also rated it far higher on the experience.

The pilot study comes from researchers at the University of Oxford’s SDG Impact Lab, working with Polestar. It took place at the Gotland Ring, a track built inside an old limestone quarry on the Swedish island of Gotland.

Each participant drove both cars in calm and dynamic conditions:

The researchers logged vehicle telemetry alongside heart-rate data, eye tracking and psychological assessments, then interviewed the drivers afterward. The idea is to line up what the car was doing at a given moment with what was happening to the human inside it.

Photos from the test also show drivers wearing electrode caps, though brain activity isn’t among the measures Polestar published results for.

Several of those measures reacted when the driving got more intense. In dynamic driving, participants blinked less, their pupils got bigger, and they held their gaze longer on a given point. Researchers read that as deeper attention. They could also tie moments in the telemetry to changes in the drivers’ physiological activation.

The car-to-car comparison is the most concrete part of the study. On matched fast laps, where both versions were driven at virtually the same pace, the Performance pack car hit 32% higher peak acceleration while requiring 15% less steering effort. It also pulled bigger cornering loads and logged more hard-acceleration events.

That steering figure is the one I’d focus on. You’d expect twice the power to mean more work for the driver at the same lap time. Instead, the dual-motor car asked less of them.

The drivers’ own scores moved in the same direction. On the study’s Performance/Dynamics scale, the Performance version scored 89.2 out of 100, versus 67.7 for the Rear motor. Overall experiential quality came in at 93.0 versus 77.9, with most drivers rating the Performance version higher on both.

The interviews are where it gets more human. Drivers didn’t describe performance as pure adrenaline. They talked about a mix of physical arousal, trust in the car, a sense of control, and intense focus. Several of them, independently, landed on the same image: a “controlled roller coaster.”

One participant said the car “makes you feel like a better driver than you are.” Researchers found drivers linked that combination of thrill, trust and control more consistently to the Performance version.

Max Klapow, a doctoral researcher at the Oxford SDG Impact Lab, framed it this way:

“Performance is not simply about going faster. It is about giving the driver a sense of confidence and control through predictable vehicle responses.”

Christian Samson, Polestar’s head of product attributes, said the company wanted to understand “something that can’t be captured by a specification sheet alone.”

Now, the caveats. The researchers themselves call this a feasibility pilot and say a larger study is needed to establish whether the findings are reliable and valid. It’s 12 people, Polestar’s own cars, and Polestar is attaching a documentary series to it.

There’s also an awkward backdrop for US readers. As we reported in June, Polestar was barred from selling new cars in the US starting with the 2027 model year under the connected vehicle rule, even though the Polestar 4 is built in South Korea. The brand is now centering its plans on Europe, with the Formula 2030 concept previewing its next models.

This is a topic that has fascinated me for years. I’ve driven a lot of EVs, and the spec sheet tells me very little about whether I’ll enjoy one. Two cars with the same 0-60 mph time can feel completely different. One makes you grin. The other pins you to the seat and leaves you oddly numb.

The problem is that it’s genuinely hard to discuss. What feels like confidence to one driver feels like an overassisted steering rack to another. It’s different for everyone, and there’s basically no quantitative data on it, so the conversation ends up being reviewers, me included, trading adjectives.

So kudos to Polestar and Oxford for trying. Matching blink rate, pupil size and heart rate against steering effort and telemetry is a real attempt to connect the engineering to the experience, and I haven’t seen an automaker publish anything like it.

Seems an assessment worth doing on all cars given how high baseline performance is these days. And it matters more now than ever. EVs turned acceleration into a commodity. Family crossovers do 0-60 mph in under 5 seconds. If everyone has the straight-line numbers, the difference comes down to how the car makes you feel, and right now most automakers are tuning that by gut.

What I’d love to see next is the bigger version with cars from different brands. Would a Model 3 Performance, an Ioniq 5 N and a Polestar 4 produce measurably different responses at the same lap times? That’s the study I want to read.

#5417. How electric school buses power 1,000 homes in California – and make money

A fleet of 74 school buses have spent the last two years powering about a thousand homes, taking stress off the grid, thanks to V2G chargers built in America.

If you’ve spent much time around the electric vehicle space, you may have heard about vehicle-to-grid, perhaps by the acronym V2G (or its cousins, V2H/V2L/V2X, etc).

The basic idea is: if we’re looking to add batteries to the grid or to homes to increase stability and smooth out demand, why don’t we use the massive amounts of batteries available inside electric vehicles for that purpose?

The idea has been talked about for years, but for the most part has not reached wide adoption. The California Energy Commission estimates that V2H could save homeowners a few hundred dollars per summer, and cut peak demand by up to 5GW statewide if it was broadly implemented. But to do this with consumer EVs, homes would mostly need some sort of costly equipment added, and most EVs aren’t V2G-enabled (though that may be changing).

But for fleet EVs, installation can be a lot easier. Putting V2G in homes requires a lot of small scale projects, distributed across a region. But putting V2G in a fleet location lets you access a lot of batteries in one big project, and lets you aggregate those batteries more easily.

So that’s exactly what Tellus Power, a charging equipment manufacturer in Irvine, CA, is doing. We took a tour of its facility and met with CEO Mike Calise and CTO Srikanth Kanaparthi.

The company has been building AC and DC chargers (up to 480kW) since 2010, but its newest push is into V2G chargers, particularly for fleets. It assembles them in America, and they’re “Build America Buy America” compliant.

Its first project in this push was an installation of V2G chargers for Oakland, CA’s school district, covering 74 school buses. The chargers were built by Tellus, and the project is operated by Zum. We first wrote about the Oakland project in 2024, but now we have a couple years of data to show how it’s going.

Collectively, the buses have a total of 11.5MWh of energy storage. Buses fill up overnight, use about 20-40% of their charge during the day, then plug in to V2G chargers after the school day and discharge to feed the grid, just in time for the late afternoon rush on electricity, when people get home and turn on air conditioning, start cooking, turn on the lights and so on.

Or on holidays and summertime (when the grid is the most stressed in California), they can be a grid resource all day long.

74 buses may not sound like much, but with the amount of energy available on board and the total power output of the chargers, it can fuel a lot of houses. The fleet can discharge at about 2 Megawatts at once, and each house uses about 2 kilowatts on average in the evening (though that can spike higher due to HVAC/EV use). That means, altogether, this fleet of 74 buses can power around a thousand homes.

Over the course of the last two years, the bus fleet has returned a total of 1.7GWh of energy to the grid, and has had over 99.5% uptime. This basically turns the fleet into a small and reliable peaker plant, which has delivered on the order of hundreds of thousands of dollars worth of electricity to the grid over the last couple years.

Tellus says V2G capacity can get onto the grid faster than any other kind of power plant. A fleet V2G depot can take months to deploy, compared to a timeline of years to build and attach other power plants to the grid. And Tellus says that its chargers differ from others on the market in that they don’t require large transformers on-site, which makes deployment faster and less expensive.

V2G isn’t strictly a “power plant,” since it’s not generating electricity. But it does shift supply to when it’s needed most, which is also when it’s most expensive. This “energy arbitrage” lets Zum buy power when it’s cheap and sell it when it’s expensive, which makes money.

That’s Tellus’ oldest project, but there are others in place and being built – 4 school buses in Burlington, VT; 2 city bus chargers for Lynch Bus Lines in Burnaby, British Columbia, Canada; a pilot program at 3 school district sites for ComEd in Illinois; and a fleet of 104 school buses (scaling to 238) in San Francisco, CA.

It's only one school district. If it is most school districts in CA, like 100 school districts, it could be 200 MW of power, which definitely chips at reducing gas peaker plant usage.

If these busses had solar PV on top? It's ever chipping at reducing emissions. That's the great thing about solar+storage, you can incrementally add it, just a little at a time, and year by year, it becomes cheaper, more effective and will power more and more of our energy needs.

But even though buses are a popular choice for V2G, there are plenty of other municipal vehicles that are ripe for electrification, which have similar duty cycles and could be used for grid services.

Trash trucks are an obvious one, and they’re the next direction Tellus would like to go in. There are a number of electric garbage trucks out there, and they could generate revenue to offset costs in the same way that school buses can.

All of this together offers a large revenue opportunity for fleets, whether those fleets be private or public. If you have a bunch of vehicles, running those vehicles off of batteries can both save you money on fuel (especially with oil prices being at all-time highs) and generate revenue with grid services, if you install V2G chargers.

#5412. FAA Certifies 737 MAX 10

After years of delays, Boeing’s 737 MAX 10 has been certified by the FAA.

The U.S. Department of Transportation and FAA announced the approval Tuesday afternoon.

“After extensive safety reviews – including thousands of hours of test flights – the team at the FAA is ready to approve this critical aircraft for sale,” U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said in a statement. “This is a major win for American manufacturing and our nation’s status as a global aviation powerhouse. The MAX 10 was initially expected to enter commercial service in 2020 or 2021, but two deadly MAX crashes, an overhaul of the FAA’s certification system, and issues with the variant’s engine anti-ice system caused years of delays.

Canada’s WestJet is the launch customer for the MAX 10. Other confirmed buyers include United, Delta, American Airlines, Alaska Airlines, and Ryanair.

The new Seagull EV is now on sale in China, starting at just 62,900 yuan, or about $9,500. That’s with BYD’s “Super Trade-in” promotion.

Regular prices start at 65,900 yuan ($9,900), which is still less than its predecessor. The first-gen Seagull EV normally cost 69,900 yuan ($10,500) without incentives.

BYD’s new Seagull EV is available in five variants, priced from 65,900 yuan to 99,900 yuan ($9,900 to $15,000), without a trade-in.

The second-generation Seagull is 425 mm longer and has a 150 mm longer wheelbase than its predecessor. It now measures 4,205 mm long, 1,810 mm wide, and 1,570 mm high, with a 2,650 mm wheelbase. It also gains another seat thanks to the added space, with room for five passengers.

Unlike the current model, which is front-wheel-drive, the new Seagull switches to rear-wheel-drive. The setup uses a more powerful 85 kW (114 hp) or 95 kW (127 hp) electric motor, up from the 55 kW (75 hp) that its predecessor offered.

To improve the ride quality and handling, BYD upgraded the rear suspension to a multi-link independent system.

Small battery plus extremely fast charge equals a surprisingly useable cheap car.

The new Seagull EV is available with two BYD Blade 2.0 LFP battery packs: 30.1 kWh and 39.2 kWh, providing CLTC ranges of 320 km (199 miles) and 420 km (261 miles), respectively.
