#4796. Anthropic introduces Claude Duet, a proactive AI agent for professionals

Anthropic PBC Wednesday introduced Claude Duet, a proactive artificial intelligence agent for professionals and small business owners in research preview, which completes tasks when asked, keeps working behind the scenes and checks in.

The company said the new assistant will send a short brief each morning of the most important items across connected apps and keep tabs on messages only when something needs attention. It can also handle numerous different administrative tasks, like a digital secretary. These include scheduling meetings and more complex tasks, such as building dashboards with interactive tools, creating financial or sales forecasts and prototyping and building web apps.

Users will message Duet like another teammate, by text or voice, with no prompts or setup. The idea is to use it like a secretary who can access apps, calendar, email and other connected information. It’s part of Claude, so it also has a running history in one thread. It remembers what a user said before, and returns finished work like a doc, sheet or deck for review.

Duet is part of the Claude that users already participate with. It can access memory, skills and apps from Claude – so there is no additional setup.

Anthropic stressed that Duet is built on the company’s framework for trustworthy agents, which also guides the company’s existing agentic systems such as Claude Code and Claude in Chrome. An automatic classifier approves any actions the agent takes by checking messages for sensitive information leaks and double-checking with the user before taking important actions.

The company added that people at Anthropic are using Duet themselves. Claude Code’s own Boris Cherny uses it on walks to talk through to-dos and save time at his desk, and the team behind Duet uses it to build the product. Karan Ahuja, who leads the team, works with it to schedule user interviews and even ship code.

The research preview will help Anthropic better understand Duet’s use cases and failure modes. Right now, it is an early, experimental version. It still might misread a thread or miscalibrate what is urgent: The agent is designed around work, so personal errands, such as booking tickets, won’t be treated as urgently and it can’t show its browser. The company will use what it learns from early users to fine-tune how it works.

#4794. Jackson Pollock’s Former ‘Urban Treehouse’ in Greenwich Village Has Hit the Market for $2 Million

A Greenwich Village loft that once served as the home and studio of a young Jackson Pollock is up for grabs for $1.99 million.

The single-bedroom abode sits on the top floor of a Federal-style townhouse—46 Carmine St.—which was built in 1827. In 1932, a then 20-year-old Pollock and his brother, Charles Pollock, called it home.

According to the listing, the siblings were studying under painter Thomas Hart Benton at the time.

Before the legendary abstract expressionist artist moved in, 46 Carmine St. had already begun its storied past. In the 1850s, a milliner sold hats and bonnets from a lower level, and many more artists lived and worked in the building through the 1930s.

In 2010, the building was landmarked as part of the Greenwich Village Historic District, an initiative that celebrates and preserves a number of iconic buildings and locations, including properties owned by photographer Annie Leibovitz, a series of small businesses, and sites that have played an integral role in LGBTQ+ history.

“When you walk into this door, you’re stepping into 200 years of New York history,” listing agent Shane Boyle of Compass explains in an Instagram tour of the home.

The home has since been updated to support modern, while historic details remain, including the exposed brick and wooden ceiling beams. A newly renovated kitchen boasts sleek appliances and cabinetry, while the contemporary bathroom looks fresh with colorful tile and stylish finishes.

Thoughtful additions include a split-system air conditioning and hidden storage.

There’s also a generously sized walk-in closet, as well as a lofted space that could be used as a home studio or office. Outside, a shared garden space features a grill and some greenery as a city retreat.

The listing likens the home—which features a 14-foot vaulted ceiling and skylights that flood the space with sunshine—to an “urban treehouse with treetop outlooks.”

“When we’re on the top floor and sitting above the surrounding low-rise buildings, there is a real sense of openness up here,” Boyle adds.

Although it’s been nearly a century since Pollock lived in the 687-square-foot loft, the current owner, Fergus Hurley, can confirm the space still fosters creativity.

Having moved from Ireland to New York City in January 2020 as a founder-in-residence at Google Labs, Google’s startup incubator, Hurley first resided in the apartment as a renter before purchasing the abode in an off-market deal in 2021.

Hurley tells Realtor.com® that “the apartment became much more than somewhere to live. It was a place to experiment and create.”

In addition to the startup he built while residing at 46 Carmine St., the AI entrepreneur worked with SoHo street artists in the building’s shared garden space to create a large Pollock-inspired painting. The piece hangs in a custom-built frame in the shape of the roofline.

“There’s something inspiring about creating something new in a 199-year-old building that Jackson Pollock once called home,” says Fergus.

While the painting does not currently convey with the sale of the property, it can be negotiated separately.

A wooden beam salvaged from the renovation was also repurposed into a guitar by Carmine Street Guitars, a small business steps away from the residence that's been crafting instruments from wood salvaged from New York City historic buildings since 1990.

“Selling is about starting my next chapter, not a change in how I feel about the apartment,” Fergus says. After marrying his wife in March 2026, the couple plan to build a new life in Miami.

“It’s been such a special home for me, and I want someone else to have the chance to enjoy it and make it a creative home of their own.”

Interestingly, 46 Carmine St. isn’t the only Manhattan home on the market with ties to Pollock. Less than a half-mile away, 9 MacDougal Alley is a historic townhouse in a gated mews.

The nearly $8 million listing is a two-bedroom, two-bathroom apartment of 1,500 square feet. According to the listing, it once served as a “creative workspace” for Pollock, as well as artist Lee Krasner, who each used the townhouse as a studio in the 1950s.

#4793. Home Sellers Now Outnumber Buyers By 63%, the Biggest Gap Since the Post-Recession Housing Crash

Homebuyers have more negotiating power than they’ve had since the housing market crashed in the aftermath of the Great Recession. There were an estimated 62.6% more home sellers than buyers in the U.S. housing market in September, the biggest gap in records dating back to 2013.

That marks the fourth record-breaking month in a row, and the margin is up sharply from 56.9% in August. The Sun Belt is driving the extreme buyer’s market nationwide; led by huge seller surpluses in Miami, Nashville, TN, and a handful of Texas metros.

When sellers outnumber buyers, buyers typically have more negotiating power because they have options. That’s why a market with a lot more sellers than buyers is considered a buyer’s market. We define a market where there are over 10% more sellers than buyers as a buyer’s market and a market where there are over 10% fewer sellers than buyers as a seller’s market. A market where the gap is plus or minus 10% is considered a balanced market.

We estimated the number of buyers using proprietary Redfin data on the typical time from a buyer’s first tour to close of purchase, and MLS data on active listings and pending sales. The estimated number of sellers in the market is simply the number of active listings in the MLS. These estimates are seasonally adjusted and subject to revision. It’s only a buyer’s market for people who can afford to buy. High housing costs and widespread economic uncertainty have caused many would-be buyers to back off in recent years, creating the imbalance of buyers and sellers we see today.

“This is a buyer’s market born out of caution, not strength,” said Asad Khan, a senior economist at Redfin. “Buyers have tremendous negotiating power in most of the country because so many other would-be buyers have been priced out of the market, or scared off by economic uncertainty. For house hunters who have the budget and job security to purchase a home in today’s market, that creates a unique opportunity: They can be picky, negotiate and even walk away if the deal doesn’t make sense financially or they find something they like better. The irony is that in terms of supply, these are some of the best conditions for buyers in many years–but they have arrived at a time when costs are so high that fewer people can afford to take advantage of them.”

The gap between buyers and sellers widened in September because buyers pulled back, while sellers kept showing up.

There were an estimated 928,892 homebuyers in the market nationwide in September, the lowest level in records dating back through 2013. That’s down 2.4% from a month earlier, the fifth straight monthly decline.

On the flip side, there were an estimated 1,510,784 home sellers in the market, the most since March 2020. That’s up 1.2% month over month.

The number of homes on the market is rising largely because many homes are lingering without finding buyers. That builds up a big pool of listings, and each month, new listings add to that pool.

Homeowners have continued listing despite falling demand because many have simply given up waiting. After years on the sidelines hoping rates would drop or demand would rebound, some sellers see little reason to keep waiting, especially with prices still elevated enough to sell at a gain. Some homeowners are choosing to sell now because they’re concerned prices could drop if they wait longer. Others are listing out of necessity: new jobs, growing families, divorce or retirement, for example. But note that many homeowners who don’t need to move may decide not to list, or pull their homes off the market: Many have substantial equity, and the relatively strong job market means there’s typically not much pressure to sell.

Over 80% of U.S. metro areas—41 of the 50 U.S. metro areas Redfin analyzed—are buyer’s markets. That’s the most in records dating back through 2013.

For the second month in a row, Miami, Nashville and Houston have the biggest surpluses of sellers over buyers. In the top 13 buyer’s markets, there were more than twice as many sellers as buyers in September. All of them are located in the Sun Belt; four are in Texas and four are in Florida.

The Sun Belt—particularly Texas and Florida—is home to some of the nation’s biggest buyer’s markets partly because homebuilding has been so robust. Builders continue to add newly constructed homes even as buyer demand has cooled, contributing to a buildup of inventory and giving buyers more options and negotiating power.

Demand has cooled largely because buyers are increasingly priced out, with mortgage rates rising and home prices holding up. And in Florida, other costs associated with homebuying are rising, too, including HOA and insurance fees.

Four metros were “balanced” in September—neither buyer’s nor seller’s markets. Two New England metros are trending toward becoming buyer’s markets: Boston, where sellers outnumbered buyers by 7.4% after being roughly even the month before, and Providence, RI, where sellers outnumbered buyers by 2.4%, a reversal from the prior month, when there were 7.4% fewer sellers than buyers.

The surplus of home sellers over buyers grew month over month in all but two of the buyer’s markets in the nation in September.

The surplus increased most in Miami, which  also has the biggest surplus of sellers over buyers; there were 158.9% more sellers than buyers in September, up from 134.5% the month before. The next-biggest monthly increase was in Tampa, where there were 103.7% more sellers than buyers in September, up from 82.2% in August. Warren, MI rounds out the top three (47.1% more sellers than buyers, up from 27.3%).

“Sometimes, when buyer demand drops, seller activity drops right along with it—so the overall balance of power barely budges,” Khan said. “But that’s not what we’re seeing this month. In every single metro where the number of buyers fell from the month before, the gap between sellers and buyers increased, causing the market to tilt further in buyers’ favor. That’s because sellers didn’t pull back nearly as fast, or pull back at all.”

House hunters lost negotiating power in just two buyer’s markets. The surplus of sellers over buyers fell from August to September in St. Louis and Detroit, both of which are tilted in buyers’ favor, but not nearly as much as the Sun Belt metros. In Detroit, there were 37.9% more sellers than buyers in September, down from 46.5% the month before. In St. Louis, it was 24.5%, down from 26.9%.

Just five of the major U.S. metro areas Redfin analyzed were seller’s markets in September.

Nassau County, NY was the number-one seller’s market, with 28.6% fewer sellers than buyers. The other seller’s markets were Montgomery County, PA (-22.7%), Newark, NJ (-18.5%), Milwaukee (-16.1%) and San Francisco (-10.6%). That’s the second month in a row those five were the only seller’s markets in the nation.

The seller’s markets are concentrated in places where limited homebuilding has kept housing supply tight. In the greater New York City area, years of constrained construction, combined with strong demand for homes near a major job center, have left buyers competing for relatively few listings. San Francisco is an exception: The housing market has heated up as the AI boom creates wealth, fueling an increase in home sales and competition among buyers.

Home-sale prices rose an average of 6.4% year over year across the five seller’s markets in August. That’s compared with a 1% uptick across all of the buyer’s markets—a sign that in seller’s markets, competition among buyers is pushing up home prices.

#4787. Retro tech meets modern AI: Inside an Amazon developer’s garage turned working computer museum

Geek Life: Fun stories, memes, humor and other random items at the intersection of tech, science, business and culture.  SEE MORE

by Kurt Schlosser on Oct 16, 2026 at 7:06 amOctober 16, 2026 at 7:24 am

Darko Mesaros used to park a car in the garage of his Maple Valley, Wash., home. These days, it’s wall-to-wall computers.

A towering server rack glows with red LEDs. CRT monitors are stacked beside vintage workstations, and a workbench overflows with tools, cables, and half-repaired components.

“I started getting stuff, and then … it just kind of snowballed to a point I actually had to slow down because I legitimately don’t have space for it anymore,” Mesaros told GeekWire during a recent tour of his retro hardware labyrinth.

The lore of tech innovation is full of suburban garages where visionary founders tinkered their way to world-changing companies. Mesaros, a distinguished developer advocate at Amazon Web Services, isn’t launching a startup out of his. Instead, he’s using it as a living museum to rescue, repair, and resurrect the discarded digital ghosts of computing history — and then daring them to run modern artificial intelligence.

The collection has spilled into an upstairs office and now spans more than 100 full computers, from an early-1970s minicomputer to Silicon Graphics workstations from the ’90s and HP workstations from the 2000s. Add roughly 25 vintage video game consoles, rescued software, peripherals, a leather-bound TRS-80 owner’s manual, and nearly a decade of 1980s Byte magazines, and the total tops 1,000 items.

Most of it arrives broken, and that’s the point.

“Buying a mint old system is not fun,” he said. “Buying something that’s broken and then having that excitement of fixing it is just fantastic.”

He finds gear at Goodwill, through local Facebook Marketplace groups, and through Seattle’s large retro computing community. Sometimes it takes a rescue mission: when a man in Washington, D.C., was about to throw away a pallet of 1980s computers, Mesaros had a friend there grab it, then paid to ship the whole load to Seattle.

At one point, he had seven Commodore 64s. He gives extras to other collectors, and he’s never sold a single piece.

Mesaros’ fascination with vintage hardware goes beyond nostalgia; it’s the foundation of a lifelong obsession with how things work under the hood.

Growing up in Serbia, he faced an economic reality where cutting-edge tech was out of reach. From 1991 to 2003, his only computer was a Commodore 64 — a machine released in 1982. While the rest of the computing world sped through the 1990s and early 2000s, Mesaros skipped straight to modern PCs.

“I feel like I missed that whole thing,” he said. “I missed the whole, you know, Sun workstation, Silicon Graphics, HP 9000, all of those things … and I just wanted to explore how that was.”

So he bought a couple of Sun workstations to see what he’d missed. Then he kept going.

“It’s a hobby, but it’s also a problem,” he said with a laugh.

If the garage caught fire, the one machine he’d grab? A bright blue Silicon Graphics Indy, the 1990s workstation from the very era he missed growing up. It was the first SGI he bought.

“It showed me how this amazing workstation worked!” he said. He still fires it up from time to time just to enjoy its IRIX operating system.

Still on his wish list: a DEC VT520 terminal with an amber screen. Working units only seem to turn up on the East Coast, and he won’t risk shipping a fragile CRT across the country.

Before joining Amazon nearly a decade ago, he held system administration and engineering roles at companies including AT&T and Tipico. Now at AWS, his day job involves leveraging his experience to help developers and customers adopt and use AWS services via blogs, webinars, code, and technical advocacy. The garage doubles as his set: he records AWS videos and live streams there.

Mesaros moved to the Seattle area around the start of the pandemic, arriving with a couple of workstations, a monitor, and a handful of laptops before his collection expanded. He soon realized gear was far cheaper here than in Europe.

“Most of these things are older than me. Especially older than my tech career,” the 38-year-old noted. “Actually, all of these things are older than my career. However, again, I find it fascinating how computer history has evolved.”

His arrival timing meant he narrowly missed the chance to visit Seattle’s beloved Living Computers: Museum + Labs before it permanently closed its doors. But in his own home, he’s essentially built a private version of the late Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen’s former institution.

Beyond the vintage hardware, Mesaros’ appreciation for computing history is permanently etched onto his body.

He sports a collection of retro tech tattoos on his arms, including symmetrical DOS and Linux terminal prompts (C:\WINDOWS> and root@localhost), a classic Windows 95 logo, MS-DOS branding, a shebang and a quit command that mark the start and end of a script, and detailed microprocessors like the Zilog Z80 and MOS 6502 paired with assembly code — a setup he jokingly calls his literal “ARM CPUs.” He’s still waiting, he said, on his check from Microsoft.

“I only tattoo ancient technology on myself,” Mesaros said, noting that people frequently stop him at airports to take pictures. “Old technology will stay with me … I feel like this is an investment in my career.”

He has plans for more ink during his next trip home to Serbia.

For Mesaros, collecting vintage computers is about more than admiring silent plastic shells on a shelf; it’s about making them talk across the digital chasm. In his garage and upstairs office, he routinely bends ancient hardware to modern purposes — proving that an 8-bit machine or a mechanical teletype can still interface with the cutting-edge cloud.

Mesaros has chatted with an AI coding agent from a Commodore 64 and deployed Kubernetes via a 1986 Tandy 102 portable computer. He wired a 1984 DEC DECwriter IV teletype to Amazon’s Kiro coding agent, explaining in a blog post how he talks to Kiro through the clacking machine — and he’s even deployed AWS infrastructure from it.

“Should this be done? Probably not,” he said. “But is it fun? Absolutely.”

In a demo for GeekWire, we watched the teletype clatter out the agent’s answer to his query, “What is GeekWire?” After a few seconds of hammering, it printed: “Seattle-based tech news site covering Pacific Northwest startups, Amazon, Microsoft and the broader tech industry.”

Not bad for a machine older than its operator.

Mesaros also spent a weekend trying to get stuck in 1999, using a Windows 98 machine as his only computer and documenting it on a VHS camcorder.

“The only window into the future was Kiro,” he said.

While these stunts might sound like elaborate practical jokes, Mesaros believes they serve a vital purpose in the age of AI. In a modern development landscape where tools let programmers “build a to-do app and walk away,” he argues that developers are losing the art of paying attention.

He likes to point out that a 2017 Google paper titled “Attention Is All You Need” helped kick off today’s LLM boom. “Now in 2026, to be a really successful developer,” he said, “attention is all you need.”

“I think being curious and paying attention are the two most important skills for a developer these days because it is so easy not to do those things,” Mesaros said. “And if you want to be relevant, if you want to be good at your job, especially with AI, I think those are the two important things that are critical.”

When he isn’t troubleshooting an obscure serial card data-ready pin or attempting to hook up a military field phone from his native Serbia to an LLM, he balances his digital obsession with analog pursuits. He wrenches on an old Jeep TJ — parked in the driveway because there’s no room in the garage.

He’s not the only tinkerer in the house. His wife is a fashion designer who 3D prints clothing and purses on a farm of printers in one of the upstairs rooms.

“She’s more creative than I am,” he said. “I get to tinker with old things that don’t want to work, and she uses technology to actually make new things.”

When it comes to the tech he lets into his own life, Mesaros is choosy. He wears a classic analog Timex and bought Meta’s smart glasses when they came out — then sold them within a month. “I don’t like the camera on my face,” he said. (He does use a camera-free pair that can display real-time translated subtitles when he travels abroad.)

That same instinct is why he doesn’t see screens going away, even as AI agents take on more work. “We still have the need for control,” he said. “Being able to observe and see, visualize what’s happening is still important.”

Old tech offers something else modern tech rarely does: a slower pace. Computer manuals once took months to produce because they’d stay accurate for years. “Today, if you create a manual that’s printed, it’s going to be valid for a week,” he said.

That’s what makes his garage an antidote to the endless scroll, a place anchored to the glowing screens and clacking keys of computing’s past. The hobby is his “computer away from a computer.”

“I spend my days in the email factory … and then in the afternoon, I just come here and tinker with something ancient that shouldn’t work anymore,” Mesaros said.

New York City will give nonprofit housing groups a new advantage when some of the city’s most troubled apartment buildings go up for sale.

The City Council’s Housing and Buildings Committee voted on Thursday to approve the Community Opportunity to Purchase Act, or COPA, after the measure cleared the City Council’s Housing and Buildings Committee earlier in the day.

Realtor.com® previously reported on the proposal as lawmakers revived COPA after an earlier version stalled. Since then, lawmakers have narrowed which buildings would be affected and shortened parts of the purchasing process.

“Buying and preserving some of the housing we already have is the most immediate way to stop displacement, and that's what COPA does,” Council Member Sandy Nurse, the bill’s lead sponsor, said ahead of Thursday’s vote.

Nurse said lawmakers had to “really start over again” after she inherited the legislation roughly 13 months ago and negotiate “a bill and a program that is workable.”

Mayor Zohran Mamdani has championed the proposal, and his administration has described it as “an extra layer of protection” for tenants vulnerable to displacement or deteriorating conditions.

“Tenants and community organizations across New York have spent years watching their neighborhoods be priced out from under them while speculators reap the rewards,” Mamdani said in a statement when the revised legislation was introduced in May.

But the revised bill reaches only a small slice of the city’s apartment stock—applying only to buildings with four or more units that meet specific measures of distress, foreclosure, or repair actions, and those with persistent hazardous violations. Owner-occupied buildings with five or fewer units are exempt.

In September, the city's Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD) estimated that roughly 2,100 properties would currently qualify, and roughly 300 buildings sold last year would have met the criteria.

When one of those properties goes up for sale, the owner would have to notify HPD and qualified nonprofit buyers. Those groups would get 20 days to express interest and 70 days to make an initial offer.

The owner could reject it. But if the property were later sold to someone else, a qualified nonprofit could have an opportunity to match that buyer’s price and terms.

Supporters say the extra time could solve a basic problem: Community groups often learn a building is available only after a conventional buyer has already moved faster.

Jenny Dubnau, board co-chair of the Western Queens Community Land Trust, said tenants have approached the organization for help buying their buildings “only to have it sold out from under us in the open market before we could make an offer.”

But property owners argue the same delay could have the opposite effect.

“Any version of COPA is a disaster for affordable housing, renters, and owners,” Small Property Owners of New York board president Ann Korchak said in a statement.

Korchak argued that the process could discourage private buyers prepared to quickly acquire and rehabilitate troubled buildings.

“This would alienate and discourage conventional purchasers who have the financial wherewithal to immediately purchase and rehabilitate a failing property,” she said. “It also gives an unfair advantage to the city and nonprofits, which would be free to make purchase offers without the burdensome restrictions faced by conventional buyers.”

Experience elsewhere suggests giving mission-driven buyers more time can preserve housing—but only when they can also find the money to close.

Washington, DC’s Tenant Opportunity to Purchase Act (TOPA) gives purchase rights to tenants rather than nonprofits, making it different from New York’s proposal. Still, a 2026 Urban Institute analysis found that TOPA helped create or preserve 16,224 affordable units between 2006 and 2020. The program was also associated with significant renovations or repairs to 12% of DC's housing stock.

The researchers also emphasized a limitation that could become central in New York: Purchase rights work alongside financing and technical assistance, rather than replacing them.

San Francisco offers a smaller example. City financing records show nonprofits have used its COPA law to acquire individual multifamily properties and lock in affordability for decades. One eight-unit building purchased through COPA in 2022, for example, received city financing and was subsequently restricted as affordable housing for at least 75 years.

Thursday’s approval sets up the next test for New York. COPA promises nonprofit buyers more time and legal leverage when eligible buildings come up for sale—but whether they can turn that opportunity into thousands of preserved apartments will depend on what happens after they get to the front of the line.

#4782. IBM puts governed context at the center of production AI

Governed context is becoming a practical requirement for artificial intelligence agents expected to make decisions across a business. As enterprises move beyond personal productivity tools, those agents need information that reflects changing conditions across the systems they use.

IBM Corp.’s approach connects that data foundation to the broader challenge of putting AI into production. A large language model’s reasoning capabilities depend on the information available to it, making data delivery an operational concern, according to Shaun Clowes (pictured), general manager of Confluent and Data at IBM.

“You wouldn’t give your accounts receivable work to a nuclear physicist that worked on your energy production side of your business,” he said. “So, why would you take a very, very smart model, give it all of the possible information about your business and say, ‘Figure out accounts receivable?’ You need the right context for the job.”

Clowes spoke with theCUBE Research’s Dave Vellante and John Furrier for IBM’s AI Operating Model interview series, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed real-time data, agent specialization and production governance ahead of IBM TechXchange 2026. (* Disclosure below.)

Enterprise data architectures have evolved around changing storage, computing and application requirements. Agents introduce a need to combine information across operational systems, such as travel bookings, baggage tracking and rental cars, while that information is still relevant to a decision, according to Clowes.

“Now we need a new type of architecture, a type of architecture that can bring together all the state of our business right now and present that directly to LLMs,” he said. “We call that the streamhouse architecture.”

IBM Confluent brings together streaming, connectivity, processing, governance and querying capabilities to support that approach. Rather than requiring all information to be centralized first, the platform supports the preparation of usable data products can happen across the environments where business information originates, Clowes explained.

“Our argument would be that you can actually move all of that very, very close to where the data is first produced, as close as you possibly can,” he said. “The earlier you govern it, the better. The quicker you produce the high-value data, the better. Then you need to just connect up to all the relevant places.”

Delivering current, governed data is only part of the challenge. Agents also need context tailored to the work they’re performing. Different teams may use the same business term to mean different things. Preparing context for an agent therefore involves establishing the definitions that apply to its task, according to Clowes.

“You can describe, ‘Hey, in this business process, what I mean is X,’” he said. “Then, you can have these governed metrics and these governed datasets that effectively represent the foundation upon which the model will reason.”

Enterprises can begin with high-value tasks and improve individual agents as they gain experience. Once those agents reliably execute established business processes, the opportunity expands to examining how the work itself is organized, Clowes explained.

“Then you can say to the supervising agent, ‘Do I even need these processes? Should I just do something totally different?’” he said. “The best bit about this whole system is it can learn at every layer.”

As agents take on more responsibility, access to current business information becomes increasingly important. Moving from human-assisted decisions to autonomous operations changes how businesses must deliver information. Agents working continuously need access to operational data without waiting for a person to gather it, Clowes explained.

“People used to talk about data as the new oil, but what they really meant was [that] data, in some sense, existed and was used by humans to make good decisions,” he said. “Businesses were run by humans … [and] that’s how you win.”

Information may originate on oil rigs, cruise ships or other remote systems. Enterprises must connect those sources into a usable operational view without assuming one centralized solution will contain everything, according to Clowes.

“You should always be asking yourself, ‘How can I know the state of my business right now?’” he said. “Because that is the world in which you make decisions. That is the world in which humans would make decisions, too.”

Nearly 90% of respondents — 89.6% — said CMAs will become more relevant in the future, up from 67% in 2020. At the same time, 83.2% said they were very or somewhat likely to use AI-assisted CMA tools.

For Greg Robertson of Giant Steps Advisors, who co-founded W+R Studios, the company behind Cloud CMA, results reinforce the importance of the agent’s role as consumers encounter an expanding array of automated home valuations.

He said the growing availability of automated estimates may help explain why agents see greater value in a CMA.

“I made a joke and said once, ‘Zillow can’t smell the cat,’” Robertson told HousingWire. “What does that mean? An algorithm can’t hear the dog bark next door. They can’t see the neighbor with the mattress in their yard. It takes a human being to do that.”

Neighborhood conditions, renovations, location and other details can influence what buyers are willing to pay, even when an automated estimate suggests otherwise.

The survey found that 90.8% of respondents trust the multiple listing service (MLS) above other sources for comparable-property information. Public portals, including Zillow, received less than 1% in the survey’s measure of the most trusted source.

The distinction between research and presentation matters because pricing disagreements remain a major obstacle to winning business, survey findings show.

Roughly two thirds of agents identified pricing as the leading seller objection — up from 52.7% in 2020. Another 33.6% said they had lost a listing because they and the seller could not agree on the price.

Meanwhile, 47.9% of respondents said they now prepare their CMAs at least one day before a listing presentation, compared with 35.3% in 2020.

“Research that I’ve done had about 50% of agents showing the listing price in their CMA, and about 50% would not because they wanted to have that conversation first,” Robertson said. “I realize some agents have a strategy of just showing comps, and then, hopefully, they can guide their seller to that and change their frame of mind.”

The survey identified several areas where agents want AI to help — including finding comparable properties, calculating adjustments and writing market commentary.

Researchers also found that agents continue to spend roughly the same amount of time researching comps as they did in 2020.

Robertson pointed to a constrained supply of comparable sales as one reason the work remains demanding. When fewer homes sell, agents may need to expand their research or look more closely at differences among properties to arrive at a defensible price, he said.

The survey found that agents are using fewer comparable properties in their CMAs — with the average maximum number of comps falling from 11 in 2020 to 8.7 in 2026. The average minimum dropped from five to 3.7.

That decline does not necessarily indicate less research, the survey said. Agents cited low inventory, unique properties, incomplete MLS data and difficulty accounting for renovations as persistent challenges.

In some rural and slow-moving markets, respondents said they had to expand their search radius or look further back in time to find usable sales.

Technology can help identify trends in available data, Robertson said, but agents still need to understand the neighborhoods they serve and the differences that matter to buyers. He also emphasized the importance of preparation before meeting a prospective client.

“You ask AI something, and then it’s like a Carfax,” Robertson said. “It just puts all this data on a piece of paper or several pieces of paper…It takes a human being, and I think the right software, to have a narrative.

“I think at the end of the day, a good CMA is really not a research tool; it’s a presentation tool, it’s a persuasion tool. All those things that have to be built into it — and that’s not coming from an AI sort of thing.”

For an industry increasingly surrounded by AI-generated information, the CMA’s enduring value may rest not on producing a number faster, but on helping an agent explain why that number makes sense.

#4774. How Can Coaches Use Supervision to Strengthen Their Practice?

Coaching can look very different from one client relationship to another. A coach may spend one session helping a leader work through a difficult decision, then help another client build confidence or manage a major career change.

That variety can be rewarding, but it also raises questions. Is the coach responding in the most useful way? Could a different approach have helped? How might the coach handle a similar situation next time?

Coaching for coaches, also called coaching supervision, gives coaches a dedicated space to step back and reflect on their work. It helps them consider what they could do differently and supports ongoing professional growth. It also gives coaches a chance to examine the choices, assumptions, and reactions that can shape a coaching relationship.

Coaching supervision is a reflective process that helps coaches examine their practice with support from a trained supervisor. The conversation may cover a challenging client situation, an ethical concern, a recurring pattern, or simply something that left the coach wondering what happened.

The goal is not to provide a script for every difficult conversation. Good supervision encourages coaches to think for themselves. A supervisor may ask questions that help the coach look at a situation from another angle, notice something they had missed, or recognize how their own reactions influenced the session.

That outside perspective can be valuable. Coaches spend much of their time listening closely to other people. Supervision gives them a chance to pause and examine their own work with the same level of attention.

Even experienced coaches can fall into familiar habits. A particular question might work well with several clients, so it becomes easy to reach for it again. A coach may also develop assumptions about what a client needs based on previous experiences. Reflection creates space to challenge those habits.

For example, a coach might leave a session feeling that a client was resistant to change. During supervision, the coach could explore that interpretation more closely. Was the client actually resistant, or were they unsure about the direction of the conversation? Did the coach move too quickly toward a solution?

Questions like these can lead to better self-awareness. They can also change how the coach approaches future sessions.

The benefits of supervision extend beyond the coach. Reflection can influence the quality of the coaching relationship itself.

A coach who recognizes a personal trigger may become more aware of how it affects their listening. Another coach may realize they tend to fill silences because they feel pressure to keep a session moving. Someone else may discover that they are avoiding a difficult topic with a particular client. These observations give coaches something practical to work on.

Supervision can also help coaches think through boundaries and ethical questions. Coaching relationships involve trust, confidentiality, expectations, and professional judgment. When a situation feels unclear, having a confidential space to examine it can help a coach make a more considered decision.

Supervision becomes more useful when coaches bring real situations to the conversation. That does not mean every session needs to involve a major problem. A small moment that felt unusual can sometimes reveal more than a dramatic client challenge.

#4771. Etzioni on AI: The colorful and cranky language of the AI era, from slop to slap

by Oren Etzioni & Daniel S. Weld on Oct 16, 2026 at 1:01 pmOctober 17, 2026 at 8:20 am

We’ve all encountered the phrase AI slop, but what’s an AI slap? A meat proxy? Chatfishing? Or agentsomnia? Following up on my glossary of AI, which demystified AI jargon, Prof. Dan Weld is joining me to shine a light on the nontechnical lingo of the AI era. We also sneak in a few phrases that should exist to capture the AI zeitgeist, circa 2026.

We introduce more than 35 phrases, so we’ve divided them into sections to keep the broligarchy from crowding enshittification.

“Digital content of low quality that is produced usually in quantity by means of artificial intelligence,” which is how Merriam-Webster defined slop, its 2025 word of the year. Shrimp Jesus is the specimen, Christ fused with crustaceans, posted to Facebook by the thousands.

AI cream is our name for the rare opposite, superb writing authored with the help of AI. We hope that over time the cream will rise to the top and the slop will sink.

Until that happens, we have the meat proxy, “a person who forwards AI-generated text, code, or other output without reading, understanding, or validating it.” We call the action of sending AI-generated text blindly an AI slap. It stings.

Your AI Slop Bores Me is a website where you type a prompt and a randomly chosen human answers it while pretending to be a chatbot. Fifty million views in a week, which tells you how badly people wanted to say something back.

AI suspicion is our name for the nagging feeling that what you’re reading was AI generated. You could also call it Turing tingle or AI-dar.

AI shaming is the accusation that follows, attacking someone’s work by insinuating a machine wrote it. The paper that named it is subtitled “Birth of a Classist Slur in Knowledge Work,” and it argues that the accusations fall hardest on racial minorities and on writers whose first language isn’t English.

See also: tasteslop, workslop, slopaganda.

A video or recording altered by machine learning so that a real person appears to say or do something they never said or did. Named in late 2017 for the Reddit user who called himself deepfakes. The word we all use for AI deception descends from one anonymous pornographer’s handle.

The liar’s dividend is the payoff a real wrongdoer collects once any authentic recording can be waved off as a fake. The damage isn’t the fake video. It’s that real video stops counting.

Counterfeit people was Daniel Dennett’s last argument, that machines able to pass as human are “the most dangerous artifacts in human history, capable of destroying not just economies but human freedom itself.” He wanted them outlawed and watermarked like banknotes.

Tilly Norwood is one of them, an AI actress unveiled at the Zurich Film Festival in September 2025. Her makers said talent agents wanted to sign her. Emily Blunt, shown a picture, said “Good Lord, we’re screwed” and told the agencies to stop.

Most of the damage doesn’t need AI at all. A cheapfake is manipulation made with cheap software or none at all. Chatfishing is using AI to write your messages on a dating app, where the con used to be a fake photo and is now a rented personality.

A name for the Silicon Valley billionaires and venture capitalists who think democracy is finished and want corporate rule in its place. Gil Durán’s book, subtitled Silicon Valley Fascism and the War on Democracy, traces the idea to a 1997 book, The Sovereign Individual, and to Peter Thiel’s circle, which Durán says believes “technology means democracy is an obsolete software system.”

Durán didn’t coin the phrase. Someone in the crypto world told him they’d been using it for years.

Durán’s target is the broligarchy, “a small group of extremely wealthy men, particularly those in technology, who wield significant political and economic power.” The word sat on Twitter from 2009 and went nowhere until Carole Cadwalladr wrote about “a new super-class of broligarchs” in July 2024.

The mental deterioration that comes from consuming too much junk online, chosen by Oxford University Press as its 2024 word of the year. Thoreau got there first, in Walden in 1854, complaining that England was busy curing the potato-rot while nobody was curing the brain-rot.

Dead internet theory is the supply side, “a theory that a lot of the internet is no longer created by real people but rather by bots and other AI tools.” It started as a forum post in January 2021 and is quietly turning into a description.

Enshittification is Cory Doctorow’s three-stage decay: “first, they are good to their users; then they abuse their users to make things better for their business customers; finally, they abuse those business customers to claw back all the value for themselves.”

AI fatigue is what’s left over, “the exhaustion or the sense of being overwhelmed by the rapid proliferation and constant engagement with artificial intelligence technologies.” The phrase has been drifting through the trade press since 2023 with no author attached; the definition above comes from Route Fifty, a trade publication for state and local government.

Andrej Karpathy’s February 2025 coinage, crowned Collins Dictionary’s word of the year for 2025, is “fully giving in to the vibes, embrac[ing] exponentials, and forget[ting] that the code even exists”—accepting whatever diff the AI hands you without reading it.

Promptatory is our word, styled on “purgatory,” for the state of being trapped in an unending edit-and-re-prompt loop with an AI system. You fix the bug the AI introduced, and in fixing it, the AI introduces two more, each smaller and more absurd than the last. Progress is hard earned: you close in on “done” like the runner in Zeno’s paradox, forever eating the remaining distance in half. Promptatory is vibe coding’s hangover: the vibe wears off, and the rereading begins.

A related condition we dub agentsomnia is the chronic inability to rest or disconnect from work, driven by the haunting feeling that your autonomous software or AI agents are stalled without your feedback. The Wall Street Journal calls it agent FOMO.

An OpenAI rallying cry. AGI is the point where a machine can do any intellectual task a person can, and the phrase became a general exclamation for the moment a model does something that rearranges your sense of what’s coming. Ilya Sutskever used it inside the company and posted it publicly in 2022. It works as celebration and as diagnosis.

P(doom) runs in the other direction: “your personal probability that AI ends the world, stated as a number so the vibe feels like math.” It started as shorthand among a small online community in 2022 and now gets asked at dinner parties.

Talk that outruns the technology comes in other flavors too.

Bragawatts, a unit of measurement for boasting, is “the practice of AI companies boasting about very large projects to create excitement and attract investment, even though many of these projects are never completed,” as Britain’s Cambridge Dictionary put it in January 2026.

Vibe revenue does the same thing to the income statement, “money or funding that an AI company is given because people are excited about the potential of AI, rather than because the company’s products are already successful.”

A one-sided relationship with someone who doesn’t know you exist. Donald Horton and Richard Wohl coined it in 1956 about television viewers, and Britain’s Cambridge Dictionary made it the 2025 word of the year, citing celebrities, influencers, and chatbots. A seventy-year-old term about Ed Sullivan turns out to describe your relationship with ChatGPT.

AI addiction is what happens when the one-sided relationship takes over. A 2026 study sorted it into three kinds: escapist roleplay, where people come to prefer the world the chatbot builds; pseudosocial companionship, where it replaces the relationships; and the epistemic rabbit hole, compulsive question-asking that crowds out everything else.

The researchers blame the design and call the chatbot an AI genie, a thing that gives you exactly anything you want with minimal effort.

AI psychosis is the far end, “a phenomenon wherein individuals reportedly develop or experience worsening psychosis, such as paranoia and delusions, in connection with their use of chatbots.” The Danish psychiatrist Søren Dinesen Østergaard suggested the term in a 2023 editorial in the journal Schizophrenia Bulletin. It isn’t a recognized clinical diagnosis, and we should say so.

Nearly every phrase above names a problem. Slop, shaming, deepfakes, brain rot, psychosis: what the AI era has added to English so far is a vocabulary of complaint. #4765. The EOR Compliance Checklist for Companies Building Global Teams in 2026

Four. That’s how many of the EU’s 27 member states had fully turned the Pay Transparency Directive into national law when the June 7, 2026 deadline passed. Four out of 27. If EOR compliance is on your list this quarter, that number says a lot about the year you’re in.

The rules show up late. They show up in bits. A few of the ones that will decide how you pay your people in Germany or Spain? Still sitting as drafts on some ministry website.

Ask a founder who deals with all of it and the answer is usually “our EOR does.” Partly true. On paper the Employer of Record is the employer. In real life you picked the person. You set the pay, you run their week, and one day you’ll be the one deciding they should go. Regulators look at all of that, not just the payslip.

FinSMEs wrote earlier this year about distributed teams powered by an EOR. The idea holds up fine: hire abroad now, open a local company later, maybe never. The trouble starts when the provider contract gets read as “risk, gone.” It isn’t gone. It just moved partly.

The EOR’s half is paperwork. Important paperwork – local contract, payroll, tax filings, social security, the benefits the law says you owe. A decent provider is good at this. It’s what you pay them for.

Your side is smaller and messier. Is this role actually a job, or a genuine contract? Who tells the person what to do each day? Who owns the code they write? Could their sales calls give your company a tax presence in that country? And every promotion, move or firing still starts with you. Small list. Big bills when it goes wrong.

This is when you have the most say. Once fifteen people are on someone’s payroll, switching providers is a headache nobody volunteers for.

Coverage maps look impressive and tell you very little. Native Teams says it runs on its own legal entities in 95+ countries – G-P advertises 180+ markets. Fine. What you actually want to know is narrower. In the three countries on this year’s hiring plan, does the provider own the local company, or is a partner doing the work?

A partner isn’t automatically a problem. It is one more name in the chain, though. Better to learn that name now than when your new engineer in São Paulo asks who exactly is paying her.

Nobody reads this bit. Do it anyway. Who pays if a tax office reopens two years of contributions? Is the indemnity capped, and how low? What happens to the employee files if you leave? How is your deposit held, and how fast do you get it back? Vague wording here stays vague later, usually at the worst possible moment.

Pay transparency makes a nice test. Back in July, King & Spalding’s review had Germany, Spain and Ireland still on early or partial drafts. Austria, Belgium and Portugal hadn’t put anything out at all.

So pick one of your countries and ask the provider straight: what’s coming here, and when do my contracts and job ads change? If they answer with dates, good. If they send a newsletter link, less good.

Native Teams has an EOR Compliance Checklist covering registration, localised contracts, statutory benefits and IP clauses, and Deel and Remote have guides along the same lines. Every vendor leans toward what it’s good at. Of course it does. So open two or three in separate tabs and see where they agree, because that overlap is pretty much your shortlist.

Contractors are fast to sign and look cheaper. That’s the trap. If someone keeps your hours, reports to your manager and works on your core product with no end date, a labour inspector will probably call them an employee, whatever the contract says.

Running a gig economy or marketplace business? Circle December 2, 2026. By then EU countries are meant to have the Platform Work Directive in national law, and under it, if your platform directs and controls the work, the person is presumed to be an employee. You then have to prove they’re not.

Nobody quite knows yet what each country’s version will look like – Littler’s analysis is pretty frank about that. Odd as it sounds, that’s the reason to check how you hand out and rate work now, not in January.

A translated template is not a local contract. Notice periods differ. Probation limits differ. In some countries an employee’s inventions don’t pass to the employer unless the contract spells it out.

With an EOR, IP usually moves twice: employee to EOR, then EOR to you. Check that both steps are written down. The second gets forgotten more than you’d think.

Here the EOR can’t help you at all. Say your new head of sales in Munich starts negotiating and signing customer deals for you. German tax authorities may decide your company has a permanent establishment there, and that’s a corporate tax issue, not a payroll one. Until a local adviser has looked at it, keep signing authority at home.

Every payroll run ships personal data somewhere else. Often there and back. Coruzant’s piece on the hidden HR challenges of scaling international teams notes how much privacy rules differ by region, and the GDPR sits at the strict end.

Before payday one, know where the data lives. Know who on your side can open it. And know what legal basis covers moving it. Dull? A bit. Cheap to fix in week one, though, and not cheap later.

Global payroll rarely blows up in one go. A pension payment slips in March. A new joiner gets the wrong tax code. Someone’s allowance never gets updated after a raise. Eighteen months later there’s a letter, with interest.

Treat the EOR invoice like any big bill. FinSMEs’ advice on protecting a growing company’s finances as it scales talks about separating duties, and it fits here: the person who approves the invoice shouldn’t be the only one looking at the payroll data behind it.

As each pay transparency law passes you’ll see salary ranges in job ads, limits on asking candidates what they used to earn, and gender pay gap reporting once an employer passes 100 workers. Your EOR deals with its own side of that as the legal employer. The job ad you wrote, though, and the pay bands behind it? Those are on you.

Hiring gets the attention. Leaving is where it tends to go wrong.

Lisbon to Barcelona sounds like a change of address. It isn’t. New country, new contract, maybe a different provider entity, and for a non-EU national a permit question too. Make moves something people ask about first. Otherwise you’ll hear about it from a beach photo in Slack.

Local law is toughest here. You might need documented cause. You might need to consult a works council, or give months of notice, and people on parental leave often have extra protection. Your EOR handles the process. The reason comes from you, and it needs to survive a challenge.

Past a handful of people in one country, your own entity often starts to make sense on cost. If you switch, carry over service dates, leave balances and benefits. Tell people early, too. Nobody likes learning their employer changed from a new payslip.

Native Teams, Deel and Papaya Global all keep contracts, payslips and payments in one place, so getting a quarter’s records should be quick. If it takes a week of emails, that’s worth knowing.

For hiring abroad before you’re ready to set up locally, they’re still about the easiest route going. It’s just that the provider does the paperwork and you make the calls, and the calls are where things go sideways. So be honest about classification. Actually read the liability terms. Look at payroll every month. And slow down on exits, more than you do on hires.

#4760. Amazon’s new Bee AI devices have a bystander problem, says former AWS security engineer

by Todd Bishop on Oct 16, 2026 at 4:02 pmOctober 19, 2026 at 6:42 am

Amazon’s new Bee AI devices ask the wrong person for consent, according to a privacy and security veteran who previously worked as a senior principal security engineer at Amazon Web Services.

In a LinkedIn article, Jeff Williams writes that Bee’s consent safeguards are aimed at the wearer, while the people being recorded have no practical way to say no.

Amazon unveiled the devices Thursday, the first new Bee hardware since it acquired the San Francisco startup last year. The $74.99 Bee Frontier goes on the wrist or clips to clothing, and the $99.99 Bee Eclipse snaps onto the back of a phone.

How Bee handles consent: Amazon says users are prompted to get permission from call participants each time they start a recording, and the devices show an indicator light while in use.

The problem, Williams says, is that neither one reliably tells the other people in a conversation that they’re being recorded. He writes that the prompt appears only on the wearer’s phone, and nothing enforces it.

“A prompt instructing the user to obtain permission is a liability allocation,” he wrote, arguing that Amazon is shifting responsibility for obtaining consent onto the wearer.

The light is easy to hide under a sleeve or in a pocket, he notes, and a device on the wrist or phone sits outside the frame on a video call.

Williams points to Meta’s Ray-Ban glasses as an example. In September, the data protection authority in Hamburg, Germany, found the glasses’ recording light hard to see outdoors and concluded that a recording indicator alone doesn’t establish informed consent under Europe’s GDPR.

In the U.S., about a dozen states, including Washington state and California, generally require consent from everyone before a private conversation can be recorded.

Whose data is protected: Williams gives Amazon credit for Bee Private Compute, which processes audio in an isolated, encrypted environment built on AWS Nitro and then deletes it. Amazon says only the customer can access the resulting data, not Amazon or Bee.

But that protects the wearer. The words of the people around the wearer still end up in transcripts and summaries they can’t see or remove. Amazon says those speakers aren’t identified by name unless they complete a voice training exercise with the device’s owner.

Williams notes that Amazon has changed comparable privacy commitments before, such as ending the “Do Not Send Voice Recordings” option on some Echo devices last year.

Williams also advises companies to keep the devices out of client meetings and privileged discussions, since the transcripts end up in an employee’s personal account, where the company can’t see or manage them.

A proposed fix: Williams says Bee should leave other people’s voices out of transcripts by default, unless they’ve enrolled their voices and agreed to be recorded. Before the acquisition, Bee indicated plans to record only the voices of people who had verbally consented, according to TechCrunch.

“I know first-hand that Amazon has the engineering talent to ship it,” he wrote.

Amazon’s response: The company didn’t comment directly on the critique but pointed to safeguards built into the devices.

One is an automatic shutoff: the devices turn off if they don’t detect the owner’s voice for 5, 15, 30 or 60 minutes, depending on the user’s setting. Amazon says that reinforces that Bee is “a personal AI assistant, not a device designed to passively pick up audio throughout the day.”

The company says it educates customers about responsible use in the Bee app, and that Eclipse will send push notifications encouraging users to let people around them know they’re using Bee and to ask for permission.

#4758. Treasury blueprints carve-outs from ROAD’s Wall Street BTR ban

Treasury issued a proposed rule to exempt certain purpose-built BTR communities from the ROAD Act ban on large institutional investor purchases. The proposal uses an 18-month post-occupancy window, addresses institutional to institutional transfers, and sets civil penalties up to $1 million or 3x purchase price.  AI Summary

The devil in the details of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, America’s most ambitious bipartisan housing policy measure in a half-century, amounts to a highly complex, high-stakes, high-wire act for both global capital investment and residential development players navigating the law’s black-and-white and grey areas for the near- and longer-term future commitments in the space.

On Thursday, the U.S. Department of the Treasury released a notice of proposed rulemaking that would grant build-to-rent (BTR) communities a range of notable exemptions from the institutional investor ban lawmakers included in the bill.

The final version of ROAD explicitly stated that investors who own 350 or more single-family homes will not be eligible to acquire additional properties, while exempting newly built BTR communities from that restriction. The ban takes effect on January 7, 2027.

However, industry advocates suggested that Treasury could interpret the law differently. In July, three days after ROAD went into law, a coalition of rental housing organizations issued an open letter asking that Treasury signal its intention to issue regulations exempting build-to-rent, as intended in the housing legislation.

Treasury’s proposed rulemakings address those concerns, at least for now.

The proposed rulemaking comes after the BTR industry faced months of uncertainty earlier this year, when earlier versions of ROAD included a broader investor ban with few BTR exemptions, leading developers and investors to warn that project financing was drying up and projects were at risk.

However, the Trump administration now appears to be signaling growing support for BTR. Treasury’s proposal follows a move last week by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) to make BTR communities eligible for financing insured by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) through its multifamily mortgage programs, signaling that the agency intends to treat the housing type similarly to traditional multifamily housing.

Treasury’s proposed framework would exempt purpose-built BTR communities that haven’t been owner-occupied and are purchased within 18 months of receiving a certificate of occupancy from the institutional investor ban. The homes must explicitly be for rent to individual renters, and not be “for resale, land banking, speculative appreciation or other purposes” to qualify, according to the document.

The exemption, as Treasury notes, covers not only communities made up exclusively of renter-occupied single-family homes, but also communities that include both owner- and renter-occupied homes. That distinction is important because many homebuilders sell only a portion of their completed communities to institutional investors for rental use, while selling the remaining homes to individual buyers.

In effect, this 18-month window would allow homebuilding firms that build BTR projects to sell their property to a large investor. However, under the proposed framework, if a developer misses that 18-month deadline, they may lose that ability.For example, let’s say a homebuilder delivers a 200-home build-to-rent community in 2027, after the proposed rule takes effect, and the firm decides to hold it for several years before selling. The homes would be outside Treasury’s proposed 18-month window, so a large institutional buyer that owns at least 350 homes would not be eligible to buy the community.

Without qualifying for the BTR exemption and a separate institutional-to-institutional transfer exemption, the remaining options may be to sell homes to an individual buyer or sell the entire community to a smaller investor who doesn’t meet the definition of a large institutional investor.In other words, the proposed rule could create an exit constraint for developers who build BTR, hold the community for more than 18 months, and subsequently choose to sell it to a large institutional investor.

However, Treasury appears to be open to amending this framework or providing further clarification. Treasury asked for public comment on whether 18 months is sufficient, so this is a proposed parameter, not a finalized rule.

Treasury considered allowing older homes in existing purpose-built BTR communities to qualify for the BTR exemption in the institutional investor ban, but rejected that approach in the proposal.

“Interpreting ‘newly constructed single-family home’ to include older homes that have been renter-occupied for potentially decades appears to be inconsistent with the statutory text and purpose,” the document reads. “Because ‘newly constructed’ and ‘originally constructed’ are not equivalent, such an interpretation would effectively render ‘newly constructed’ meaningless.”

This means older BTR communities would not qualify for the BTR exemption. However, Treasury’s proposal includes a separate pathway that could allow those properties to continue trading between large institutional investors.

Under the separate institutional-to-institutional exemption, a large institutional investor can purchase a single-family home from another large institutional investor if the seller acquired the home in compliance with the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. Treasury says that includes homes acquired before the investor became subject to the ban.

“Therefore, under the proposed rule, a single-family home purchased in compliance with the Act would remain eligible for subsequent purchases by large institutional investors, provided that each transfer satisfies a purchase exemption,” the document clarified, noting that this would allow for multiple future transfers of ownership between institutional investors.

Treasury further added that “a contrary interpretation could impair market liquidity and orderly disposition of single-family homes without materially advancing the purposes of the Act.”

Essentially, Treasury’s proposal would prevent a large institutional investor from using the BTR exemption to sell older, purpose-built rental communities. It would, however, allow an institutional investor to buy that property assuming the prior acquisition complied with the ROAD to Housing Act.

However, the proposal is not yet final. Treasury is seeking comment on whether its interpretation is appropriate and whether the final rule should clarify how it applies to purchases made before the effective date, excepted purchases and previous purchases made under the institutional-to-institutional exemption.

In addition to the BTR provisions, Treasury also clarified that renovate-to-rent exemptions will remain intact, allowing qualifying institutional investors to buy and rehabilitate neglected homes, provided that they spend at least 15% of the purchase price on improvements.

The proposal also clarified exemptions for homeownership programs, including rent-to-own arrangements. Requirements include meaningful financial support from investors, positive rent reporting and, for certain programs, a right of first refusal and a 30-day “first look” period for renters.

Notably, Treasury noted that it will establish clearer documentation and eligibility standards for an entity claiming it is merely a passive investor rather than exercising investment control over the homes.

For passive investors, Treasury would define when an entity can invest in another company that owns homes without being treated as controlling those properties. This clarification could help certain investors remain below the 350-home threshold.

While the proposal isn’t an outright ban, under Treasury’s guidance, a prohibited purchase by a large institutional investor could trigger a civil penalty of up to $1 million or three times the property’s purchase price, including the land.

Enact is developing AI models optimized to control robots. Such algorithms were historically tailored to specific robot form factors. As a result, a model built to power a humanoid robot may not be capable of reliably operating a standalone robotic arm. Enact’s algorithms, in contrast, are designed to support a wide range of robot designs.

Early-stage startups often use customized versions of open-source models to power their software. A recent job posting indicates that Enact is pursuing a different approach. The company is hiring an AI researcher with expertise in pre-training, a term for developing AI models from scratch. That task can be significantly more difficult than customizing open-source models.

Enact says that it’s developing not only AI models but also physical robot components, most notably actuators. Those are mechanical subsystems that act as a robot’s muscles. An actuator combines an electric motor with a chip that controls its motion and certain other auxiliary parts.

Enact’s actuators not only produce kinetic energy but also act as sensors. It says they “sense force and touch,” which presumably means they measure the amount of pressure a robot applies to objects when it picks them up.

The company didn’t specify how the technology works. However, earlier attempts to build actuator-based sensors may offer clues. Google LLC and publicly traded chipmaker Allegro MicroSystems Inc. are among the companies that have invested in such projects. Both companies used an approach called Back EMF to turn actuators into pressure sensors.

Running a current through an actuator causes its built-in motor to spin. The rotation of that motor, in turn, generates a second current that flows back into the actuator. Back EMF technologies measure that second current, or counter-electromotive force as it’s most commonly known, to understand what the host system is doing.

If a robotic arm’s actuator is active but not generating any counter-electromotive force, engineers can deduce that the arm is holding a heavy item its actuator is unable to move. By contrast, the presence of a strong counter-electromotive force indicates that the actuator is spinning freely. That often means the robot arm isn’t holding anything. Back EMF sensors can also collect data about a range of other robot activities.

A job posting indicates that Enact plans to use field-programmable gate arrays, or FPGAs, to power its robots. An FPGA is a chip that users can customize more extensively than a central processing unit or a graphics card. That makes it possible to achieve better performance and power efficiency.

Enact will reportedly use its funding round to hire more engineers and enhance its technology.
