#99. Alberta voters overwhelmingly reject plan to leave Canada in referendum

Result is a victory for prime minister Mark Carney and supporters of Canadian unity

Voters in Canada’s Alberta have overwhelmingly rejected a proposal to begin the process of independence, delivering a decisive victory for supporters of Canadian unity and deepening divisions within the separatist movement.

With nearly 1.9m votes counted, roughly 76% people supported remaining in Canada, while 24% backed beginning a process toward separation, according to unofficial results from Elections Alberta.

The Canadian prime minister, Mark Carney, who grew up in Edmonton, Alberta’s capital, celebrated the result.

“Albertans made a clear choice. They chose unity, to build together, and to take care of each other. Above all, Albertans chose Canada.”

He said the result would allow Canadians to move forward together and accelerate efforts to diversify trade, develop major infrastructure projects and make Canada a global energy powerhouse, with Alberta at its centre.

The results showed an overwhelming majority of Albertans had voted to remain and one radio show host posted: “Someone owes Albertans $130m for this theatrical waste of taxpayers’ money.”

Pollster David Coletto told the Guardian that residents felt the referendum was “a distraction away” from what they wanted their government to focus on.

“There was almost a general sadness about the wasted energy spent on this rather than the more real threats they see, including the Trump administration,” he said. “Most Albertans feel deep grievances with the federal government while also being proudly Canadian. The marriage may be rocky at times, and most Albertans want to see some thing changed, but very few are willing to see the marriage end.”

The province’s separatists, however, saw a silver lining. “We don’t have the final results. I think the numbers are going to fluctuate, but it is fair to say that at least a quarter of Albertans feel that Alberta needs to be treated better,” said Keith Wilson, a leader of the separatist movement.

Others took a different view of the results. “The result is bleak. Independence feels far away and we should not pretend this was not a defeat, however, it changes nothing about the reality of the circumstances Albertans find themselves in,” David Parker, a longtime far-right organiser linked to a massive breach of voter information, wrote on social media.

The Alberta premier, Danielle Smith, welcomed the early results, saying they showed widespread support for remaining in Canada. “Albertans have voted for a stronger Alberta in a united Canada,” Smith posted on social media.

Even though the vote was not explicitly about independence – a point that frustrated separatists – the ballot question nonetheless exposed divisions in Alberta’s politics and raised questions about the durability of national unity.

Delays at roughly 100 of the 1,400 at polling stations across the province pushed results back by more than two hours, but shortly after polls closed on Monday evening the results suggested a decisive win for those looking to remain in Canada.

The separation question – whether to remain in Canada or begin a legal process that could lead to a second vote – was one of 10 put to Albertans on Monday. The others covered issues including immigration, healthcare, education and the province’s constitutional powers. Elections Alberta said the remaining results could take days to count.

In the days leading up to the vote, provincial leaders and federal politicians crisscrossed the province to make the case for a unified country.

Carney warned against a vote from residents of the oil-rich province simply looking to “send a message” to the federal government. “A vote against Canada is a dangerous bluff that will lead to years of deep uncertainty and lost opportunities for Albertans and Canadians,” he told an energetic crowd last week.

The Conservative leader, Pierre Poilievre, whose parliamentary seat is in the province, told a crowd of supporters that Albertans were not “quitters”.

“To those people who are thinking about taking out their frustration on the current government by voting for separation in the referendum, I say this: Canada is more than a government,” he said. “It is a people, a flag, a land, a history and a promise.”

Data from Elections Alberta indicated roughly 43% of eligible voters – or 1.3 million Albertans – had already cast ballots before Monday’s vote through advance voting and mail-in ballots. That figure pointed to a potentially higher turnout than the province’s last referendum in 2021.

Smith’s tenure as leader of the United Conservative party is due in large part to separatists within the party who ousted her predecessor, Jason Kenney. In recent months she has tried to placate both the separatists and federalists in the party, in a province where a majority is not interested in secession.

The referendum became a political liability for Smith’s party, whose popular support has tumbled in recent months, including with the loss of a one-time conservative stronghold in a by-election.

Still, members of the separatist movement told the Guardian they would continue their push for independence. And in the aftermath of the results, one prominent separatist leader said he would push for a special general meeting of the party to remove Smith.

Jeffrey Rath told Global News that the Alberta independence will remain nothing but a dream long as Smith, whom he called an “avowed federalist” remains in power. He said it was time for separatists to “take back ownership” of the UCP.

In an interview before the results, the Calgary mayor, Jeromy Farkas, a staunch opponent of the independence movement, said the city’s “best future is here, as a Canadian city”, amid fears that uncertainty could lead to capital flight.

“I am Canadian because I’m Canadian, not in a transactional way and regardless of the flavour of prime minister,” Farkas said. “And I’m going to continue to make the case for how our future is strongest together, because no result will permanently put this to bed. “The separatist movement sees this as a long-term project.”

#98. Three things you should know about permafrost

A primer on this crucial and frankly mysterious piece of climate science.

Maybe, like me, you’ve been aware that this layer of frozen soil is thawing, that it’s important in the global puzzle of climate change … and that’s about the extent of it.

The great news for both of us is that my colleague James Temple just published two in-depth stories about permafrost. His reporting took him to the boreal forests of Alaska, where he helicoptered into the wilderness to learn more about how this material could affect our climate both now and in the future.

I’d recommend you give both of James’s stories a read. There’s this feature about efforts to tackle the intensifying fires that are threatening permafrost, as well as this look at how researchers are trying to understand the complicated tangle of potential climate effects. Here are three things I took away.

Permafrost is the frozen layer of soil that’s under about 15% of land around the Northern Hemisphere. While technically material needs to be frozen for only two consecutive years to count as permafrost, most of our current permafrost formed during the last ice age.

That means there’s organic material that’s been frozen, and essentially locked away, for thousands of years. But as global temperatures tick up and this layer thaws, microbes are able to break down that organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide and methane—powerful greenhouse gases that drive climate change. Those emissions form a feedback loop, accelerating warming further.

The steady rise in global temperatures has already shrunk the footprint of the Arctic permafrost by about 12% since 1850, and the pace has been picking up in the last 50 years.

The wild thing is, only a minority of the models that have been used in UN climate reports actually take into account the gradual loss of permafrost. And none of them include the abrupt thaw and collapse that can happen after events like heat waves and fires (more on those fires in a moment). There are efforts to make sure future UN reports reflect the growing science, but for now, all this means that we’re probably underestimating how much and how fast the planet is going to warm.

Fire is a natural part of boreal forests’ life cycle, but they’re getting more intense because of climate change. And crucially, fires can accelerate the thawing of permafrost. By the end of the century, this effect could release the equivalent of 80 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide.

Today, the policy across remote parts of Canada and Alaska is generally to let wildfires burn as long as they’re not threatening human habitats. But some researchers say we should explore fighting these fires to save the carbon trapped in the forests and the permafrost beneath them.

Researchers at the Woodwell Climate Research Center are developing a map of burn risk and carbon vulnerability across Alaska, essentially highlighting areas where fires are most likely to spread and which areas are home to certain types of permafrost that would release the most emissions. (Yes, apparently there are different types of permafrost. Yedoma is a particularly deep, carbon-dense form from the late Pleistocene era.)

That’s all just the beginning—once we figure out potential targets, the question is what to do about it. Some suggest setting more prescribed fires, something Indigenous groups have done for thousands of years, partly to reduce the danger of massive blazes. Or resources could go to early detection and efforts to put out fires before they grow.

There’s about 1.5 trillion tons of carbon locked away in permafrost, about twice the amount in the atmosphere. We know that permafrost thawing can release greenhouse-gas emissions and drive climate change. But there are complications.

One particularly big wrinkle: Other effects from permafrost thawing and fire recovery might counteract the carbon lost from those events. For example, as temperatures warm, plants can gradually move north and take hold where they couldn’t have before. Those plants soak up carbon from the atmosphere as they grow, at least partly locking away what was emitted.

Researchers are working to better understand this ecosystem. And the more we learn, the more complicated the picture looks. How much carbon is lost from permafrost, and how much a given area will recover its carbon, depend on a huge array of factors. How severe are the fires that have accelerated local thawing? What’s the local climate and environment? And what kind of permafrost is at stake?

The higher we push the planet’s temperature, the more we mess with complicated natural phenomena that we’re only beginning to understand.

#92. A New Ice Age Was Beginning. Then Humans Started Burning Fossil Fuels

Burning fossil fuels may have stopped the Earth from entering another ice age, a new study suggests. The research, published Thursday in the journal Science, uses samples of ancient vegetation and rock records to analyze the growth—and sudden retreat—of an ice cap that is the last vestige of an ice sheet that once covered much of North America.

The findings shows the Barnes Ice Cap, which covers parts of Canada’s Baffin Island, started growing a few thousand years ago before rapidly reversing course beginning in the 1960s as carbon pollution ramped up.

“A question that’s in the popular consciousness is, have we caused enough global warming to stop the next ice age from happening?” says Andrew Wickert, an associate professor of environmental science at the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, who was not involved in the research. The new research shows that “even as we were just starting to ramp up emissions, we’ve already done enough damage to cause the major North American ice sheet to already start retreating. That’s significant.”

The planet regularly goes through phases where ice extends down from the high latitudes—what we colloquially call "ice ages"—due to natural shifts in the Earth's rotation and orbit, known as Milankovitch Cycles. Those are followed by interglacial periods where ice retreats toward the poles.

The last ice age ended 12,000 years ago. At its peak, what's known as the Laurentide Ice Sheet covered all of Canada and extended as far south as New York and the Midwest. It retreated to form the Barnes Ice Cap, which covers an area the size of Delaware on Baffin Island.

Unlike glaciers, which can move, the Barnes Ice Cap is frozen to the bedrock, preserving the vegetal record of what grew around it under the ice. That quirk allowed the scientists leading the new study to glean information about how much it has grown—and shrank.

To determine when it first reached its modern size, they used an exposure dating technique to calculate roughly how long rocks several meters from the glacier’s edge had been exposed to the sky. Using measurements of Beryllium-10, an element that forms on the surface of exposed rock, the authors calculated that the ice sheet first reached the size it is today around 3,600 years ago.

Next, they wanted to see how much the ice cap had regrown from that point. To do this, they turned to the humble denizens of the tundra in front of the glacier: mosses and lichen.

“After the ice cap retreated, mosses eventually colonized this newly exposed land,” says Nicolás Young, a paleoclimatologist at the Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory at Columbia University and lead author of the new paper. “These mosses are living there, and things are normal—then, eventually, the ice cap starts to re-expand. The ice covers the moss, and kills it.” The traces of the dead moss are preserved in newly reexposed land, allowing Young and his team to piece together a timeline of how the ice has moved across the landscape.

Thanks to these records, Young and his coauthors estimate that the glacier started growing again around 2,000 years ago. This may have signaled the start of another glaciation period.

But starting in the 1960s, the ice cap has done something that wouldn’t correspond with another ice age: It started getting smaller again. The team used historic aerial photos that showed that the ice has reversed course, retreating significantly back from its new margins. The Barnes Ice Cap’s edges have retreated an average of 4 meters per year since the 1980s. That coincides with a period of rapidly increasing carbon emissions, which has caused ice to go into retreat worldwide. The ice cap offers a particularly stark example, though, given the growth prior to the 1960s.

“Modern retreat of this glacier has reversed 2,000 years of net expansion—that’s a pretty decent chunk of time,” Young says.

Meredith Kelly, a professor of earth sciences at Dartmouth, says the data gathered in the study is “awesome” and that the study shows a “clear pattern of what the ice cap was doing.” However, Kelly says that she would like to see a comparison of some of the data to other sites around the world to more fully grasp how, exactly, the Earth was moving into its glaciation period before the effects of human-caused warming kicked in. While we have a broad understanding of what kicks off periods where ice can grow, the exact mechanisms are still something that require more research.

The evidence presented in this paper, Young says, could also validate a decades-old hypothesis that has long been held up by climate deniers as “evidence” climate change is a hoax. In the early 1970s, scientists gathered for a meeting at Brown University to discuss hypotheses of when the next ice age might happen. (Young says that two of his coauthors on this paper were at that meeting.) The scientists concluded that a glaciation period was so imminent that they sent a warning letter to President Richard Nixon.

“I think this study actually helps show that scientists were right about a pending glaciation,” Young says. “

Media coverage of the meeting, as well as a colder period of weather in the 1970s, have long been pitched by climate deniers as “proof” that global warming isn’t actually happening. It very much is, though, and the new research actually adds to the evidence.

#91. Making the case for context-aware AI

Moving AI beyond experimentation requires connecting agents to operational context and identifying the business processes where they can deliver the greatest value.

Enterprise AI can generate recommendations, automate tasks, and help organizations make decisions faster. But without an understanding of how a business actually operates, even powerful models can miss the constraints and relationships that determine whether a decision is the right one. As companies look to move AI from experimentation into everyday operations, the challenge ahead is giving technology the context to act usefully within complex business processes.

For Devavrat Shah, chief scientist of enterprise AI at Celonis, that context is central to what he calls “decision intelligence.” AI agents can support individual tasks within larger decision-making processes, he argues, but they need to understand the role each task plays and the business conditions surrounding it. “You can't just do that in a vacuum,” he says. Without the right operational context, agents risk overlooking important constraints, making costly mistakes, or consuming excessive computing resources as they try to work out what they need to know.

The solution begins with connecting AI to the processes and data enterprises already use. A contextual layer can help agents understand workflows, operate within defined guardrails, and produce recommendations that people can review and approve. Shah advises organizations to start with a specific business decision, identify its most difficult steps, and test whether AI can improve them. Measuring gains in speed and value can then help determine whether an application is worth scaling.

This approach could also change how employees spend their time. Automating repetitive, manual work and coordinating tasks across teams could free people to focus on more complex strategic decisions. Realizing that benefit, however, will require investment in workforce education, so employees can understand AI outputs and their implications instead of just handing decisions over to agents.

Looking to the future, Shah sees opportunities extending from routine operational tasks to enterprise-wide planning in areas such as finance, workforce management, marketing, and sales. With the right context, AI could help organizations evaluate options and make consequential decisions more quickly. As Shah puts it, “Now, maybe [resource planning] should not take that much time, and people are actually making the right choices quickly.”

While unofficial results show both the separation and constitutional questions will fail, the ones on immigration are likely to succeed.

“I love Calgary and I love Alberta,” said Ortiz. “But when I hear people asking whether people should get access to these services – I mean, that was my daughter’s education. That was my family’s healthcare.”

The referendum amplified the grievances of fringe separatists, but it also exposed a central tension in Alberta’s approach to immigration. Even as the oil-rich province seeks newcomers to fill shortages in the job market, the provincial government wants to limit services available to some of those same workers.

The governing United Conservative party blames the rapid population growth for overcrowded and underfunded hospitals and schools. But critics say the proposed solution – restricting access to healthcare, education and social supports – could leave workers and their families more vulnerable without fully addressing the underlying causes of the crisis.

The referendum asked if the government should introduce a law ensuring only Canadian citizens, permanent residents and “individuals with an Alberta-approved immigration status” can access healthcare and education. Another asked if newcomers should have lived in Alberta for 12 months before qualifying for certain social support programmes and allowing the province to charge some temporary residents fees for healthcare and education.

Earlier in the year, the Alberta premier, Danielle Smith, blamed the immigration policy of Justin Trudeau’s Liberal government for the resource strains in classrooms, emergency rooms and social support systems. Canada’s federal government has since trimmed immigration targets and placed restrictions on its temporary foreign worker program amid criticisms it was being abused by large companies. But the western province’s economy relies on workers from abroad to fill jobs in sectors, like agriculture, food services and tourism, where employers struggle to recruit or retain enough staff.

Ortiz, who works with the non-profit Asociación de Mexicanos en Calgary, worries mounting skepticism towards migrant workers will push workers into the shadows and make them more vulnerable to labor exploitation.

“I can’t imagine the scale of the harm that a policy like this might create,” she said, noting that thousands of farm workers in the province don’t receive healthcare.

Ortiz has seen views shift on immigration in recent years and says the results of the referendum reflect a population that is “squeezed” economically.

“Scapegoating non-permanent residents is not going to lower the cost of living. That’s not going to fix our classrooms. They keep essential services open. In many cases, they are frontline workers. And now they’re being treated as disposable.”

#79. Designing an AI serving harness built to last

The engineering system surrounding an AI model increasingly determines whether its intelligence can be delivered reliably, economically, and at scale.

The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it.

That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?”

Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability.

For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it.

There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem.

A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures.

The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture.

That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic.

A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't.

The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost.

Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency.

Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance.

GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive.

The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat.

Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements.

For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value.

Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances.

That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy.

Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset.

Increasingly, enterprises have access to many of the same models. Sustainable differentiation comes from how effectively an organization turns that intelligence into business outcomes.

The model provides the intelligence. The harness determines whether the enterprise can deliver outcomes reliably, economically, and at scale.

The AI era has changed how fast startups can grow. Startups can grow so fast that they hit high annual recurring revenue (ARR) numbers, only to crash and burn a few months later. At this year’s TechCrunch Disrupt, Lovable go-to-market lead Luke Arno, Ben Broca of the AI platform Polsia, and Theory Ventures’ Tomasz Tunguz gave founders some tips on how to set their companies up for success in those first 90 days.

Broca, whose company Polsia uses AI agents to build and run businesses with almost no human staff (Broca says he is its only employee), said that product-market fit is one of the hardest things to find right now, especially in an era when so many products can easily be copied by competitors. In response, Arno said that durable go-to-market strategies are those that can be reliably repeated.

“The most critical thing for founders or earlier-stage companies to do is develop learning density or pattern recognition for where your customers are,” Arno said. “Don’t rush to build out a top-down go-to-market motion if you’re not there yet.”

That’s why, in those first 90 days after launching a commercial product that’s ready to sell, founders should not mistake early, fast revenue for a sign that the startup is ready to scale, Arno said.

Once a startup finds use cases that work, it will only be ready to build out a sales team and go for growth after it can attract new customers by repeating what has worked. If every sale requires new tactics, the startup hasn’t truly hit product-market fit.

“If I bring on 10 new customers in 10 different ways to solve 10 different problems, sure, I brought revenue in, but I don’t have a way to go scale a go-to-market,” Arno said. “If I bring three customers in with a similar motion to solve a similar problem, now I have high confidence that I can go scale.”

Lovable, the AI app-building startup, set a lot of those early benchmarks with how fast it grew, and now founders everywhere are feeling the pressure to do the same. Lovable is valued at more than $13 billion by its investors, says its ARR has passed $600 million, and has raised more than $700 million in funding to date.

“The expectations for overall execution speed are absolutely tremendous,” Tunguz said.

But Tunguz said growth like Lovable’s is still the exception, though founders should still move fast. The pressure they feel is real, he said, because far more money flows through the startup ecosystem at every stage than when he began investing in 2008. Today, a seed deal brought in by one of his partners could just as easily be a $5 million raise, a $50 million raise, or a $500 million raise.

The winners all focus on one thing: understanding their customers well enough to know what will keep them around even after any hype subsides.

“Community building is very important and being close to customers is very important,” Broca said. He said founders can send emails and throw events, and he personally likes to talk to a lot of his customers: “The general rule is always to be talking to customers.”

#60. RFK Jr’s ‘deities of vaccine research’ raided by police in Guinea-Bissau

Police seize doses of hepatitis B vaccine from people at center of dispute over research on African newborns

Controversial vaccine researchers who have been praised by Robert F Kennedy Jr, the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) secretary, had their offices in Guinea-Bissau raided by judicial police, who seized doses of hepatitis B vaccines, officials said on Facebook.

The researchers have been at the heart of an ongoing dispute over research on African newborns, supported by $1.6m from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and matched by the Pershing Square Foundation and the Bluebell Foundation for a total of $3.2m.

The police search took place on Monday, involving “investigative procedures aimed at clarifying the circumstances related to the said batch of vaccines”, according to the social media post, which was translated from Portuguese. The announcement noted that seizing the immunizations did not point to “irregularities in the quality, safety, or effectiveness of the vaccines”.

The Bandim Health Project recently posted a letter of approval for the trial from Guinea-Bissau’s national ethics committee at the ministry of public health, dated 14 July and signed by the committee’s president, Mouhammed Djicó Ould Ahmed. A Danish research ethics committee, which had to change its rules to consider a clinical trial, also approved the study earlier this month.

Quinhin Nantote, the health minister of Guinea-Bissau’s transitional government, disputed reports that the trial would move forward.

“This is not even remotely true,” Nantote told the Guardian in Portuguese on Thursday.

The study was redesigned over the summer, but it would still deny hepatitis B vaccines to 7,000 newborns in the first six weeks of life, in a country where about one in five adults have the virus. Hepatitis B kills 15% to 25% of people with chronic infections, causing cancer and liver failure, and newborns are particularly vulnerable – but vaccination within 24 hours of birth is safe and highly effective at preventing illness.

Any study that would deny the vaccine to participants was “unethical and exposes newborns to serious and potentially irreversible harm”, said Tarik Jašarević, a spokesperson for the World Health Organization (WHO), calling hepatitis B vaccination at birth an “essential public health intervention”.

Through its country office in Guinea-Bissau, the WHO and its partners have been advising the ministry of health on the planned introduction of the hepatitis B vaccine and the potential impact of this study, Jašarević added.

The Bandim Health Project was founded by anthropologist Peter Aaby in 1978, and it is now co-led by his wife, Christine Stabell Benn, an epidemiologist.

Kennedy has repeatedly praised their work, calling them “the deities of vaccine research” at a recent anti-vaccine conference. He has several times referenced the results of a study they conducted on the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP), which the same researchers later conducted again and found completely different results. Even so, Kennedy called Aaby a “very famous” researcher on Joe Rogan’s podcast in 2023 and cited his research when he halted US funding to Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance.

Stabell Benn was a member of the hepatitis B working group of the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), helping present evidence to the US committee that voted to end the universal birth recommendation before such decisions on vaccines were stayed by a federal judge. Stabell Benn also hosted a podcast with Tracy Beth Høeg, a former top official with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) who said she wanted to end “unnecessary” vaccines.

Aaby, Stabell Benn and Frederik Schaltz-Buchholzer, the lead researcher on the project, did not respond to the Guardian’s questions about the trial and the police search.

The Bandim organization is deeply enmeshed in Guinea-Bissau but has done little to improve the country’s health and research capacities in five decades of operation, according to a report from the non-profit Stand Up for Science (SUFS). The organization is now fundraising to help roll out the vaccines at birth to all newborns in the country.

“Our investigator encountered significant distrust of Bandim by the citizenry of Guinea-Bissau,” said Colette Delawalla, an American health researcher and founder of SUFS. “However, because of the lack of public health infrastructure, Bandim is often the only option in situations where people are desperate to access medical care of any kind.”

The police raid underlines the fact that Guinea-Bissau is a “fragile” nation that experienced a military coup less than a year ago, Delawalla said. The country lacks the stability needed for clinical trials, she added.

“Guinea-Bissau is in the difficult situation of trying to protect their youth, their future, from exploitation by two powerful western nations and they are using what leverage they have to do so,” Delawalla said.

The Browser Company is sunsetting its design-forward Arc browser on December 22, 2026, and directing users to Dia, its AI-first browser. The company announced the decision on Thursday, citing the rising cost of keeping a browser secure in the age of AI.

“Keeping a browser secure takes constant, dedicated work that is only increasing in the age of AI,” the company wrote.

“Dia was built from the ground up to meet today’s security standards, so that’s where our engineering and security teams will focus moving forward.”

The wind-down is staged into three parts. As of Thursday, new Arc accounts can no longer be created. On November 23, Arc Search on iOS and Android will lose its AI features, including “Browse for Me.” Users will no longer be able to sign in or sync tabs, though the app will continue functioning as a basic local browser.

On December 22, Arc will stop working entirely on Mac and Windows. Accounts will be deactivated, and personal data will be deleted within 30 days. Arc Search will also be removed from the Google Play Store.

While The Browser Company claims Dia now surpasses Arc in speed, stability, and security benchmarks, user reactions on social media were largely disappointed, with many reviving requests for the company to make Arc open source.

But CEO Josh Miller previously pointed out that Arc is built on the Arc Development Kit (ADK) — a proprietary internal SDK that is also the core engine powering Dia. Open-sourcing Arc would mean giving away Dia’s intellectual property.

“While we’d love to open-source Arc someday, we can’t do that meaningfully without also open-sourcing ADK,” Miller previously noted. “And ADK is still core to our company’s value.”

For users making the switch, Dia will carry over equivalents of several core Arc features. Users can still swipe between different work and personal environments using Dia’s “Profiles” (formerly Arc’s Spaces). Arc’s signature vertical tabs will remain as an optional sidebar layout, while Folders have been rebranded as “Pinned Tab Groups.”

Passwords, bookmarks, extensions, and history will seamlessly transfer to Dia. Arc features will remain free in Dia, with AI optional.

The shutdown officially closes a chapter that began in 2025, when the startup stopped active feature development on Arc. The Browser Company — which was acquired by Atlassian for $610 million in 2025 — conceded at the time that while early adopters loved the browser’s novel interface, the “novelty tax” and complexity limited mainstream adoption. Arc was fiercely beloved by a dedicated group of enthusiasts, but it never quite became the mass-market Chrome alternative its makers once envisioned.

The Google Gemini–powered smart speakers have apparently been downloading huge amounts of data to the device over the past two weeks, which could put a serious damper on your Wi-Fi network.

While it isn't happening to everyone, after checking my own home internet usage, I found out that I have been experiencing the same issue. My Google Home Speaker has used 487 GB of data in the past 10 days, using almost as much as the entire rest of my home between August and September.

Google confirmed this bug has only affected some devices and that it has rolled out a solution. A spokesperson from Google told WIRED: “A server misconfiguration caused some Google Home Speakers to repeatedly download a software update. This issue has now been resolved.”

#55. Germany sit out FIFA Under-15 World Cup as Russia return

Germany is one a handful of FIFA's 211 nations to refuse an invitation to play at the inaugural Under-15 World Cup. The tournament will also mark Russia's return to the FIFA fold.

With nearly 1,000 matches and 189 teams, the Under-15 World Cup in Azerbaijan is bloated even by FIFA's usual standards. It marks a change in its attitude to children's international tournaments, which have not existed on anywhere near this scale before.

For Germany, it is too much too soon. The German FA (DFB) told DW that it had "consciously decided not to participate." The statement from the organization suggested the DFB — whose development of young players is under scrutiny after a run of poor performances by the men's national team — felt such a tournament was not appropriate for players so young.

"At this age level, our identification and development process is still underway, and we therefore do not believe it is appropriate to nominate a fixed squad for an international tournament at this stage," it read.

"More broadly, the DFB's approach to talent development is based on the principle of supporting young players in a long-term and age-appropriate manner. We carefully assess at which stage international competitions and development measures are beneficial for each age group.

"In our view, young talents should not be exposed too early to the accompanying aspects of professional football. These include increased expectations and pressure, public evaluation of their performances, and early interest from agents and the football market."

The refusal to play in the tournament, which began this week, is consistent with Germany's more cautious approach to youth development. While in some leagues, like the Premier League in England, there is no regulation about youths playing professionally, in Germany's top flight, the Bundesliga, players must be 16. In fact, before the 2020-21 season, players could not be granted a pro license before they were 17 years and six months old.

That change enabled Youssoufa Moukoko to make his Bundesliga debut for Borussia Dortmund a day after his 16th birthday in 2020, becoming the league's youngest player in the process.

But, some would argue his is a cautionary tale. Hyped wildly due to his goalscoring exploits at youth level, Moukoko was fast-tracked by Dortmund and Germany, who called him up for the 2022 World Cup. Now, still just 21, he is struggling for game time and goals on loan at Corum, in the Turkish Super League, from Danish side Copenhagen.

Players under 16 are also forbidden by FIFA from having an agent. But this is poorly regulated in some regions of the world. That led Bundesliga club St. Pauli, then in the second division, to announce they would no longer work with player agents or private agencies in their youth system in 2023.

"We want to focus on partnership and dialogue with the players, their families and their relatives," explained the head of the club's youth academy, Benjamin Liedtke, as St. Pauli officially positioned themselves "against the commercialization of youth football."

Germany are one of 22 nations to refuse the invitation to compete in the tournament, 18 of whom are from UEFA, Europe's confederation. The USA are also one of the prominent refusals alongside Ukraine, whose FA told German public broadcaster Sportschau that they initially refused on sporting grounds before claiming that "other countries indicated that they would compete but did not want to play against Russia." DW contacted a number of FAs to clarify this point but is yet to receive a response.

FIFA President Gianni Infantino, who has become a notorious figure thanks to a number of scandals over the past year, told Sky in February: "We have to [look at reinstating Russia], definitely, because this ban has not achieved anything. It has just created frustration and hatred. Having girls and boys from Russia being able to play football games in other parts of Europe would help."

Eight months later, he has his wish. The ongoing tournament, billed as the "FIFA U-15 World Cup & Festival" comprises eight-a-side games with 20 minute halves, a smaller pitch and a number of other technical tweaks. FIFA say the 2027 edition will be for girls teams only.

"Young players need meaningful matches, different opponents and different football experiences in order to develop. Talent exists everywhere, but the opportunities available to young players are not always the same," said FIFA Chief of Global Football Development, Arsene Wenger, likely referencing that teams from Bhutan and Seychelles to the Bahamas and Vanuatu are present.

"This tournament gives players from across the world the chance to compete, to learn and to grow."

FIFA are yet to respond to a DW request for further comment.

The round robin stage, according to FIFA, has placed teams in "groups that provide diverse sporting and regional representation" but also appears to have, at least initially, kept apart nations in conflict with each other.

For example, Israel and Palestine or Kosovo and Serbia are not in the same groups but could, in theory, meet in the later stages of the tournament. This would not be allowed in senior football, thanks to various bans and workarounds.

FIFA are vague about how the tournament works after the round robin, saying only that it will consist of "four to five additional matches per team, determined by results and subsequent tier placement. [There will be] one winner for each of the six tiers." It also said that the winner of the top tier will be crowned champions but all teams will play matches until the tournament concludes on October 30.

But, given that they have been banned from international football since invading Ukraine in 2022, it is the presence of a Russian national team at a FIFA tournament that will likely heap the most controversy on to young shoulders. Though a Russian presence was not the reason for German refusal, not attending for sporting reasons may also turn out to be a smart piece of sport politics.
