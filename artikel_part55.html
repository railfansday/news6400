#3700. DayZ creator Dean Hall says "our industry is rotting from its core" because of big-company "greed", aiming shots at Epic's Unreal and Unity

"Developers have surrendered an extraordinary amount of control."

UPDATED 3.30PM BST: Dean Hall has explained more about his idea in an email exchange with Eurogamer, clarifying issues such as whether it uses or welcomes any form of generative AI, while offering some more strongly worded criticisms about Epic's Unreal engine and Unity.

Primarily, I wanted to clarify what Brutal's relationship was with generative AI, if any, because it's the tech topic of the moment and something both Epic and Unreal, and Unity, are all-in on. In this regard, Hall answered: "There is no generative AI inside it, no tooling or scripts for making generative AI assets. Anyone can see for themselves as the repository is public. The framework is a tool for putting a game or engine together, not for generating anything."

Hall then tackled generative AI more broadly, particularly as it relates to Unreal and Unity, and said he finds it "confusing" that both engines focus "so much effort" on it. They are human-centric workflows, as he calls them - presumably meaning designed to be easily digestible by humans. "What, specifically, is it about Unreal - which has long focused on visual scripting, blueprints, and such - that would make it good for AI work?" He asked. "If we take all the promises as true, why would a human-centric workflow without the human be better than a language-centric workflow provided to a language model?"

I don't understand some of that but the "inconsistency" bothers Hall. "Let's take their arguments as gospel and say that AI is the way for them," he went on. "What happens when the cost of making those very tools yourself is approaching zero? Why pay to work around someone else's enormous, human-centric workflow when you can increasingly make the exact tools you need? The technology they're embracing might actually be undermining the very reason developers needed them in the first place."

It reminds him of the dot-com era "when companies tried to slap a coat of paint on themselves and suddenly become internet companies". "Unity and Unreal may be accelerating the adoption of the very technology that could undermine their business models," he said. "Pretty ironic."

Think about the control companies like Epic with Unreal, and Unity with its engine, have over a game developer's project, and what happens if they make a change or perhaps introduce a fee a developer is not happy with. What can the developer do? Because those are issues people face. "Is that going to get better?" Hall asked.

"And I want to be clear," he added, concluding. "I am not saying my Brutal framework is the winner." Instead, it's meant to be a start. "I want to say developers will tear down Unity and Unreal, but it is Unreal and Unity who are tearing themselves down. Their greed and lack of accountability to developers has left them completely unable to reconcile their need to appear relevant. They have to slap AI on everything while being an encumbrance for using the very thing they are pushing."

Incidentally, on the topic of Icarus using Unreal Engine 4: Hall and RocketWerkz have no plans to remove it. The studio has apparently "sunset" Unreal as an engine it uses but switching engine for Icarus would require a lot more work. "Migrating Icarus would be very disruptive," Hall said, "and our players are loyal and we've earned their trust through patches every single week for years now since launch, not missing one. An engine switch to Brutal would disrupt that."

ORIGINAL STORY 2PM BST: DayZ, Icarus, and Kitten Space Agency creator Dean Hall has issued a fiery assault against some of the world's biggest game companies, calling out a "greed" that's "rotting" our industry to its core. He took particular aim at engine-makers Epic (Unreal) and Unity, while simultaneously backing his words by launching an open-sourced, free alternative of his own: a game-making framework called Brutal.

"Our industry is rotting from its core," Hall wrote in an expansive post on Reddit, "outwards in every direction, and it has been for some time. Driven by greed, driven by a race to the bottom on price. It is my position that the games industry was already heading somewhere unsustainable before reverse-engineering and powerful agentic processes became widely available. These have pushed things over the edge.

"But I think one of the biggest problems is something we don't discuss enough. Developers have surrendered an extraordinary amount of control over their work to companies whose interests aren't necessarily aligned with their own. So we're doing something about it."

Brutal is a framework rather than a game engine, so it allows freedom to either make a game with it - as Hall's RocketWerkz studio has with Kitten Space Agency - or use another engine with it, like RimWorld's developer Ludeon has with its game engine Waylith.

"We're making the Brutal framework publicly available, with its source code, and we're making it free to use, including for commercial games, subject to an attribution requirement," Hall wrote. "There are no royalties and we don't want a percentage of your game's revenue."

Hall's post shares a lot of detail about the origins of Brutal, about what it is and who's working on it. He also admits and even underlines that Brutal won't be for everyone. "I do not believe this is the tool that everyone should use to make a game," Hall states. "We are not trying to make something that can do everything for everyone."

One particular anecdote that stands out to me is about where the spark came from to do this, and it sounds like it came from asking Epic boss Tim Sweeney a question during a Game Developers Conference 2025 talk.

"Tim was, for a long time, a hero of mine," Hall shared. "A powerhouse of a programmer with a mind I admired growing up. But over time I became frustrated with how much of the discussion seemed to revolve around the latest buzzwords. The metaverse one year, AI the next. Meanwhile developers were struggling with performance, stability, and the practical consequences of decisions made by engine vendors."

The question that Hall asked Sweeney about was whether Unreal was accountable to developers regarding those issues, to which Sweeney apparently replied something about "making more demos and putting them on social media to counter people describing games as 'Unreal slop'". Hall countered by asking whether making more things more quickly wasn't actually part of the problem, creating false expectations about how easy games are to make.

"I was not satisfied with his answer," Hall went on, "and I continue to believe Unreal is not accountable enough to developers. Particularly when it can take a five percent royalty on game revenue above its licensing threshold. I don't think developers should have to simply accept that relationship without questioning it."

Hall believes more frameworks like Brutal will emerge after this, not because everyone will "abandon" popular engines - Hall's Icarus uses Unreal Engine 4, incidentally - "but because the cost of making alternatives is going to fall dramatically". Brutal, he hopes, might provide developers a "lifeboat" to survive on.

"This technology is a small step towards changing the balance of power," Hall concluded. "If the tools are public, if you have the source code, and if you're free to build your own systems on top of them, you have options. And if one day we stop being accountable to developers ourselves, they should have the ability to fork the framework and go their own way. That's important to me. You are only as independent as your tools are. This is how we fight back."

I spoke at length with Dean Hall nearly a year ago, finding out how life had been for him more than a decade after leaving the project he's best known for - multiplayer survival shooter DayZ. The years have been fraught with ups and downs, with disorientation caused by success and then the many challenges faced running a studio of his own and navigating projects that don't immediately work out.

Icarus is a co-op survival crafting game that's been around for a few years on PC, but that launched on PS5 and Xbox Series S/X earlier this year. Kitten Space Agency, meanwhile, is an unashamed successor to Kerbal Space Agency, made by the creator of Kerbal, Felipe Falanghe. Kitten Space Agency was released in pre-alpha for free last autumn, via RocketWerkz' own distribution platform Ahwoo, with an optional donation business model - another of Hall's experiments. And it's raised more than $230,000 so far that way.

#3699. The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (2026) Preview - A Beautiful Step Back In Time

For nearly 20 years, The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time was my favorite game. While it was eventually supplanted by The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, Ocarina of Time remains a formative, treasured gaming experience that laid the foundation for the wider 3D action genre. However, I am the first to admit that while the original Nintendo 64 version – the only one available on Switch and Switch 2 – has aged better than most games of its era, much like its iconic hero, it doesn't take long to realize it's from a bygone era.

For years, I wanted Nintendo to bring the 2011 3D remaster to Switch, but it's doing me one better: a full, from-the-ground-up remake with lush visuals, several quality-of-life improvements, and even voice-acted cutscenes.  We are just weeks away from the launch of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time on Nintendo Switch 2, and ahead of its November 5 release, I spent over an hour exploring Hyrule as both Young and Adult Link.

My time begins the moment I take control of Link after Navi wakes him up. Following a brief conversation with Navi and Saria, who also ran up to Link's treehouse, it's time to retrieve the Deku Shield and Kokiri Sword so Mido will let me past to see the Great Deku Tree. As increasingly endearing as Saria is thanks to her improved facial and body animations, as well as her more fleshed-out dialogue, Mido is just as increasingly obnoxious; I was definitely happy to make him eat his words after he mocked Link for previously being the only one without a fairy.

Running around Kokiri Forest is nearly enough to sell me on this remake. The visuals are absolutely stunning. The new jump button and ability to sprint out of a roll make it feel like an all-new game. The freely controlled camera adds to this feeling, even with some relatively basic level design. And while I never had a problem with how Young Link looks in this remake, I'm even more impressed and comforted by seeing him in action in the actual game. In fact, everything surrounding Link also looks great; Kokiri Forest is much more bustling than its previous forms, light fog dissipates as you run through it, and swinging your sword looks better than ever before. Though I love Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom's stylish anime-inspired art style, the sheer amount of detail present in each and every character, enemy, and environment accentuates the masterfully executed maximalist approach Nintendo took with this remake.

After finding 40 rupees to buy a Deku Shield, I crawl through the tunnel to the area where you get the Kokiri Sword. This time, however, there's added verticality as you dodge the rolling boulders to reach a ladder, which you climb and then jump across a gap to reach the chest that contains the blade. This is indicative of small changes dedicated fans will find scattered throughout; I didn't uncover anything monumentally different from the original game, but eagle-eyed viewers of last month's Nintendo Direct will notice that Lon Lon Ranch is now on level with the rest of Hyrule Field rather than up on a plateau, and that Kakariko Village is now slightly rearranged. Outside of some small upgrades to the diversity and placement of foliage, I didn't notice many more differences, but it's also been several years since I last fired up either the N64 or 3DS version of Ocarina of Time, so it's entirely likely they were subtle enough that I didn't notice.

After entering the Great Deku Tree and completing the first part where you jump off the ledge and break through the spiderweb, I must jump to the next part of the demo. Still as Young Link, I watch as Saria gifts Link the Fairy Ocarina through a touching, fully voiced (outside of Link, obviously) cutscene. From there, I'm dumped into Hyrule Field. It doesn't feel nearly as impressively endless as it felt in 1998, but it is definitely way more beautiful. After a brief conversation with the owl, Kaepora Gaebora, I sprint across Hyrule Field, catching passing yet impressive glimpses of landmarks like Lon Lon Ranch and Death Mountain. One new addition is apparent as I navigate the menus: The Threads of Time. Here, a tapestry tells the story of the Hero of Time in real time; I can look back at what has already happened in Link's adventure, as well as see how much is still on the horizon. Additionally, if you get lost, the tapestry can give hints for where you're supposed to go.

After a brief exploration of Hyrule Castle Town, including a short tour of the Temple of Time, it's time to jump ahead to the final segment of my demo: the Forest Temple. This section lets me try out Adult Link, who is equipped with the hookshot, and later, the Fairy Bow. This was the one place where my memory felt fuzziest, as I struggled to speedrun the puzzles that I last played through more than a decade ago.

Fighting Wolfos, Stalfos, and Skulltulas is more straightforward than most modern third-person fights, which may be the one area that fell slightly short of my admittedly lofty expectations. Using the lock-on targeting allows Link to dodge and counterattack, but I didn't find a simple way to swap targets when you're fighting multiple enemies at once. Instead, I had to release the left trigger, move the camera, and squeeze the trigger again. I get that this is how it worked in the original game, but I would have liked switching to the next target without going through that. Maybe there is an easy way to accomplish that, but I didn't find it in my nearly 90 minutes fighting through Hyrule.

Regardless of that one reservation, there is no doubt in my mind that The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time will consume my life when it comes out. And with October already halfway in the rearview, we really don't have long before a whole new generation gets to experience what could very well be Link's greatest adventure. This remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time comes to Switch 2 on November 5.

#3698. Just 4 minutes a day can dramatically improve strength after 65

A daily strength training routine lasting just four minutes can significantly improve strength, balance, and mobility in older adults, according to new research led by Penn State College of Medicine. The findings suggest that maintaining physical independence with age may require much less exercise than many people assume.

Published in the journal PLOS One, the study found that adults aged 65 and older experienced meaningful improvements in physical performance after only 12 weeks. These gains are especially important because muscle strength and mobility influence the risk of falling, the ability to live independently, and overall health and longevity.

For older adults, being able to move comfortably and perform everyday activities is essential for maintaining quality of life. Simple tasks such as standing up from a chair, climbing stairs, and walking can become more difficult as strength and balance decline.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, unintentional injuries, including those caused by falls, are among the leading causes of death in adults aged 65 and older.

Despite the importance of physical fitness, many older adults believe that meaningful improvements require long, demanding workouts. Christopher Sciamanna, professor of medicine and public health at Penn State College of Medicine and lead author of the study, says the new findings challenge that assumption. Even a brief daily routine can improve important measures of mobility and physical function.

"The human body is designed to improve very quickly," Sciamanna said. "And just a few repetitions of an exercise performed regularly can lead to huge improvements. Exercise is about forward thinking -- think about what you want to be able to do and train for it."

Why Shorter Workouts Could Make Exercise Easier

Strength training, also known as resistance training, involves making muscles work against some form of resistance, such as body weight or elastic bands. Research has shown that it can substantially improve muscle strength within a few months.

However, fewer than one in five older adults meet the recommendation to perform muscle strengthening activities at least twice a week. Common obstacles include discomfort, limited time, and the physical demands of conventional exercise programs.

Another challenge is that planning an effective workout can feel overwhelming, particularly for people who are unfamiliar with exercise.

"Exercise is actually really complicated, because you have to decide how many repetitions, how far, how many sets, how much rest, and how many times per week," said co-author Smita Dandekar, associate professor of pediatrics at Penn State College of Medicine. "It's hard work, so there's huge problems with people wanting to do exercise. If we can make it short, we're part way there."

The researchers had already seen encouraging results from an earlier experiment called FAST (Functional Activity Strength Training)-1. In that smaller study, 24 older adults performed just 30 seconds of push-ups and squats each day. After six months, their ability to perform squats had improved.

Other research has similarly suggested that performing only a few sets of exercises each week can produce strength improvements approaching those achieved with considerably longer workouts.

These findings led Sciamanna and his colleagues to investigate whether an especially brief exercise program could produce measurable improvements in older adults.

Testing a Simple Four-Minute Exercise Routine

For the new study, the Penn State researchers developed a program called FAST-2 to evaluate whether a short daily workout could improve mobility and physical ability in adults older than 65.

The trial included 97 participants whose average age was 74. They were randomly divided into two groups, with one following the exercise program and the other receiving no intervention.

Before the study began, participants reported exercising for an average of only about 18 minutes per week. That was far below the general recommendation of at least 150 minutes of moderate physical activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity each week, Sciamanna explained.

Participants received four elastic resistance bands and an adjustable-height stepper, along with written instructions explaining how to perform each movement. The researchers also provided easier variations to accommodate different physical abilities.

For example, participants could perform push-ups against a wall or countertop rather than on the floor. Those who found chair stands challenging could use their hands on their knees for additional support.

As their strength improved, participants were encouraged to make the exercises progressively more difficult. Someone performing modified push-ups might advance to the standard movement, while another participant might increase the height of the stepper.

To evaluate the program, researchers measured how quickly and effectively participants could rise from a chair and how long they could balance on one leg. These assessments were conducted at the beginning, midpoint, and end of the 12-week study.

Such tests are valuable because they reflect movements people rely on throughout the day. Performance on these assessments can also help predict future mobility problems, fall risk, and the potential need for additional care.

"These indicators predict your future ability to go into a nursing home, your future likelihood of falling and of developing difficulty walking," Sciamanna said. "They give you a sense of whether or not you're going to be able to be active in the future."

Although the study measured improvements in physical performance rather than directly testing whether participants experienced fewer falls or avoided nursing home care, the findings suggest that short workouts could help preserve abilities closely tied to independent living.

A Workout Older Adults Can Actually Stick With

Beyond the improvements in strength and mobility, the researchers identified another major advantage of the four-minute routine: participants were able to follow it consistently.

Throughout the study, they completed their exercises on 81% of days.

That level of participation suggests that a short, straightforward workout may be easier to incorporate into daily life than a longer exercise program. It could also help people overcome obstacles such as limited time, discomfort, or difficulty maintaining a regular fitness routine.

For older adults who are largely inactive, even a small amount of consistent resistance training could therefore provide a practical starting point for improving physical function.

The researchers say their results reinforce the idea that exercise sessions do not need to be lengthy to produce meaningful benefits. By focusing on movements that support everyday activities, a few minutes of daily effort may help older adults maintain strength, mobility, and independence.

For Sciamanna, the ultimate purpose of exercise extends beyond simply becoming physically stronger. It is about preserving the ability to participate in life and continue doing the things that matter most.

"Exercise is the key to freedom," he said. "Freedom is the ability to be able to do what you want to do, and I would say that if you can't do what you want to do, you're not free. I approach exercise by thinking of what I want to be able to do in 20 years, and then I train to do that."

Proton Pass is the password management app from privacy-focused Proton (whose suite of tools also includes Proton Mail and Proton VPN). Whether you're already a Proton user or are looking for a new password manager to securely store your credentials and other data, these are the tips and tricks that will help you get the most out of Proton Pass.

Password managers offer a secure alternative to writing down wifi passwords and other logins or sending them via text. In Proton Pass, you can create a separate vault specifically for your home and share a limited list of credentials—such as guest wifi, smart lock codes, and streaming information—with visitors. Vaults can be set as view only, and you can revoke access at any time. Users without a Proton Pass account receive a shared link, which can be set to expire after a specific amount of time or number of views.

Another use case for separate vaults is as an "in case of emergency" selection of items that you can share with trusted contacts. For paid users, Proton Pass has a dedicated emergency access feature, which lets up to five contacts of your choice get into your account after a dedicated waiting period in the event of your illness or death. Alternatively, you can set up a vault that contains only specific items, such as logins or secure notes, and share it with those who may need the information if you are incapacitated or unreachable. Remember that guest access can be set to expire or revoked at any time.

When you create an item in Proton Pass, you have the option to add custom fields, which allow you to save additional information relevant to your credential, such as text notes and 2FA codes. You can also select a Hidden field, which masks the text by default until you reveal it (similar to how passwords are masked). This prevents someone from looking over your shoulder and catching sight of sensitive information, such as security question answers and PINs. Custom fields are available on paid Proton plans.

A Proton Pass selling point is its hide-my-email alias feature, which generates a unique email address when you sign up for a new account. Email aliases forward to your main inbox, but they prevent you from having to share your real address, essentially creating burner identities that you can use for online shopping, newsletters or promotional lists, and other activity that you don't want tied to your personal email. Free users get 10 hide-my-email aliases, while those on paid plans have an unlimited number. While Proton Pass doesn't yet have an integrated virtual card option like 1Password, you can use a service like Privacy.com or Revolut to create single-use or merchant-locked payment cards that protect your real account information.

Another benefit of hide-my-email aliases is that you can find out who is selling your data (again, without compromising your personal email address). When you generate an alias, add a custom text field with notes on exactly what the address is being used for. If you start receiving marketing emails or spam to that alias, you'll know where that compromise came from. You can then disable the alias instantly within Proton Pass.

Some employers, universities, and service providers have multiple URLs for accessing online portals, but you use the same credentials across all of them. Instead of cluttering your vault with duplicate entries, you can add multiple URLs to an item so Proton Pass will autofill that item across all of them. This also prevents an issue where you have to update a password and then remember to sync it across duplicate items. When you edit an entry, simply tap Add in the Website box.

Proton Pass stores a history of your saved items so you can see previous passwords and information that's since been changed. This makes it easy to revert back to earlier versions if you encounter an error when updating a credential. You have access to the last 50 versions of an item on paid plans, and you can restore earlier versions to be the current version directly from the item history.

When you create a new credential, Proton Pass can randomly generate a strong password to match site requirements. Alternatively, you can opt for passphrases, which are strings of words separated by hyphens (or another symbol). These are still highly secure but much easier to remember and type than a random string of letters, numbers, and symbols. Passphrases can be useful on accounts for which you have to manually enter your credentials.

#3689. Natural compound could fight rheumatoid arthritis at its source

Scientists have identified a natural plant compound that could offer a surprising new approach to fighting rheumatoid arthritis. In experiments with rats, the substance reduced joint swelling, protected cartilage, and calmed harmful immune activity. Researchers also discovered that it works through an unexpected mechanism involving the way the body processes fats.

The findings, published in the journal Engineering, center on a compound called obakulactone (OL), a naturally occurring tetracyclic triterpenoid extracted from Phellodendri cortex, a type of plant bark. Tetracyclic triterpenoids are plant chemicals characterized by a structure containing four interconnected rings.

The researchers found that obakulactone helps restore balance to unsaturated fatty acids, which play important roles in cellular function and inflammation. It does this by promoting the breakdown of a protein called acyl coenzyme A thioesterase 1 (ACOT1), using the cell's natural protein disposal system, known as the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway.

By identifying this previously underexplored molecular target, the study suggests that changing how cells process fatty acids could become a new strategy for treating rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Natural Compound Reduces Arthritis Inflammation and Joint Damage

To investigate the compound's effects, scientists created a rat model of rheumatoid arthritis using complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA), a substance that triggers a strong inflammatory response resembling certain features of the disease.

The animals received obakulactone for 21 days at three different daily doses: low (50 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), medium (100 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), and high (200 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹). These amounts were calculated according to body weight.

The results were encouraging. Rats treated with the compound experienced significantly less joint swelling, while the structure of their cartilage and synovium returned toward normal. The synovium is the thin membrane lining joints that produces lubricating fluid and can become severely inflamed in rheumatoid arthritis.

The treatment also improved disease-related changes in the thymus and spleen, two organs that play important roles in the immune system.

Beyond these physical improvements, researchers observed major changes in immune activity. Obakulactone reduced elevated levels of CD3⁺ T cells and CD68⁺ macrophages in joint tissues. These immune cells normally help defend the body, but they can contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue damage when the immune system becomes overactive.

The compound also shifted macrophages away from an inflammation-promoting M1 (CD86) state toward a more inflammation-resolving M2 (CD206) state. At the same time, it suppressed the development of CD4⁺ T cells into Th17 cells, a type of immune cell associated with autoimmune inflammation.

Blood tests provided further evidence of these effects. Obakulactone reduced several inflammatory signaling proteins, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α. These proteins, known as cytokines, help immune cells communicate but can also intensify inflammation when produced in excessive amounts.

The treatment also lowered rheumatoid arthritis related blood markers, including rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (CCP-Ab), C-reactive protein (CRP), and matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3). The reductions became more pronounced as the dose increased.

Scientists Uncover a Surprising Link Between Fat Metabolism and Arthritis

One of the study's most intriguing findings involved fatty acids, molecules that are commonly associated with nutrition and energy storage but also influence inflammation throughout the body.

Using several advanced analytical methods, including metabolomics, MALDI mass spectrometry imaging, and proteomics, the researchers examined how obakulactone affected the chemical processes inside tissues.

These techniques allowed them to investigate changes in small molecules, map their distribution within tissues, and measure differences in protein levels.

The analysis revealed that the compound helped correct abnormalities in the production and breakdown of unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, and α-linolenic acid).

These fatty acids are important components of cell membranes and can serve as raw materials for molecules that regulate inflammation. Arachidonic acid, in particular, is involved in producing several powerful inflammatory signals.

In the rats with arthritis, obakulactone helped bring these disrupted metabolic processes back toward a healthier balance. This suggested that its benefits extended beyond simply suppressing immune activity.

A Hidden Protein Emerges as a Potential Treatment Target

To understand exactly how the compound worked, the scientists conducted additional laboratory experiments involving rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (SFs).

These cells help maintain the tissue lining the joints. However, in rheumatoid arthritis, they can become abnormally aggressive, multiplying excessively, releasing inflammatory substances, and contributing to cartilage damage and tissue scarring.

Obakulactone slowed the growth of these cells, reduced their production of inflammatory cytokines, and encouraged apoptosis, the controlled process through which the body eliminates unwanted or damaged cells.

The researchers then investigated which protein the compound was acting on directly.

Using cellular thermal shift assays, microscale thermophoresis, and surface plasmon resonance, they identified ACOT1 as a direct molecular target of obakulactone.

The binding measurements produced a dissociation constant (Kd) of (6.18 ± 0.26) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by microscale thermophoresis (MST)) and (6.34 ± 0.38) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR)).

These closely matching results provided evidence that the compound physically interacts with ACOT1. A dissociation constant describes how strongly two molecules bind to one another, with lower values generally indicating tighter binding.

Importantly, obakulactone did more than bind to the protein. It promoted ACOT1's destruction through the ubiquitin‒proteasome system, a cellular mechanism that marks proteins for removal and breaks them down.

This process reduced the activity of several downstream mechanisms associated with inflammation and abnormal cell behavior.

How Targeting One Protein Can Disrupt Inflammation

When ACOT1 was broken down, levels of another enzyme called stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) also decreased. This enzyme helps regulate the production of certain unsaturated fatty acids.

The changes subsequently suppressed two major cellular signaling networks: Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT).

These pathways act like internal communication systems, carrying signals that influence how cells grow, survive, and respond to their surroundings. When their activity becomes abnormal, they can contribute to inflammation and excessive tissue growth.

By interfering with these signals, obakulactone reduced inflammation and fibrosis, a process involving excessive buildup of scar-like connective tissue, in rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts.

Additional experiments, including rescue tests and studies using molecular inhibitors, strengthened the evidence that ACOT1 was central to these effects.

The researchers confirmed that the compound's ability to reduce inflammation, slow abnormal cell proliferation, and encourage apoptosis depended on its influence over ACOT1, the arachidonic acid pathway, and the downstream JAK-STAT/PI3K-AKT signaling pathways.

Together, the findings point to a potential way of disrupting the biological processes that sustain arthritis inflammation rather than focusing exclusively on suppressing immune responses.

A Potential New Direction for Rheumatoid Arthritis Treatment

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues, particularly the joints. It affects approximately 1% of people worldwide and can cause persistent pain, swelling, stiffness, and progressive joint damage.

Because it is a systemic disease, rheumatoid arthritis can also affect organs and tissues beyond the joints.

Although existing medications can reduce symptoms and slow disease progression, they do not work equally well for everyone, and some can cause serious side effects. This makes the search for additional treatment approaches especially important.

The new findings suggest that obakulactone could become a candidate for future rheumatoid arthritis drug development. They also identify ACOT1 as a potentially valuable treatment target and highlight the importance of unsaturated fatty acid metabolism in controlling inflammation.

However, the results remain preclinical. The compound has been tested in rats and laboratory cells, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether it is safe and effective in humans.

Even with that limitation, the discovery opens an intriguing possibility: targeting the metabolic machinery that helps drive inflammation could provide another way to combat rheumatoid arthritis and potentially protect joints from lasting damage.

#3681. Coffee calories have jumped nearly 40%. Sugar isn't the biggest culprit

Americans are getting more calories from their daily coffee, even though the amount they drink has barely changed. Researchers at the Gerald J. and Dorothy R. Friedman School of Nutrition Science and Policy at Tufts University have identified a surprising reason for the increase: non-dairy creamer, which has overtaken sugar and milk as the largest contributor to added calories in coffee.

The study, published online in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, examined dietary information from 30,000 American adults aged 20 and older. Between 2003 and 2018, average coffee consumption stayed relatively constant at approximately 13 ounces per day. However, the average number of calories consumed from coffee increased from 43 to 60 per day, a rise of nearly 40%. By 2018, the leading sources of added coffee calories were non-dairy creamer, sugar or syrup, and dairy milk.

"Coffee consumption has been associated with several health benefits. However, coffee is often accompanied by the addition of sugar and cream, which can contribute to an increased consumption of calories from added sugar and saturated fat," said Fang Fang Zhang, senior author of the study and Neely Family Professor at the Friedman School. "The more we know about what people are consuming, the better we can understand the link between nutrition and overall health."

How Researchers Tracked Changes in Coffee Habits

To investigate how Americans' coffee habits evolved, the research team examined eight consecutive cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), covering 2003 through 2018. The nationally representative dataset included 30,000 adults aged 20 and older, each of whom completed two 24-hour dietary recalls.

Researchers separated coffee beverages into two broad groups: non-combination beverages and those containing additional ingredients. They then classified the additions into several categories, including dairy milk, half-and-half, dairy cream, non-dairy milk, non-dairy creamer, sugar/syrup, sugar substitutes, and other ingredients such as alcohol and cocoa powder.

Some participants reported consuming pre-mixed coffee beverages/powder, making it necessary to estimate the amounts of individual ingredients they contained. To do this, the researchers used the USDA Food and Nutrition Database for Dietary Studies corresponding to each survey cycle, along with linked Standard Reference codes.

Non-Dairy Creamer Consumption More Than Doubled

The analysis showed that Americans' overall coffee intake, including coffee consumed without additions, remained largely unchanged between 2003 and 2018. What changed was the amount and type of ingredients being mixed into those beverages.

In 2018, dairy milk was the most commonly added ingredient by weight, averaging 21.4 grams per day. Non-dairy creamer followed at 8.1 grams per day, while sugar accounted for 4.4 grams per day. Consumption of dairy milk, sugar, and sugar substitutes showed no statistically significant changes over the study period.

Non-dairy additions, however, followed a very different pattern. Their average consumption rose from 3.65 grams per day in 2003 to 10.5 grams per day in 2018. Much of this growth came from non-dairy creamer, which increased from 3.37 to 8 grams per day. Non-dairy milk consumption also climbed sharply, rising from 0.27 to 2.45 grams per day.

Other coffee additions increased slightly as well, with an average gain of 0.5 grams per day.

Why Non-Dairy Creamer Now Adds More Calories Than Sugar

Although dairy milk remained the most common coffee addition by weight, non-dairy creamer had become the leading source of added calories by 2018.

On average, non-dairy creamer contributed 18.5 calories per day, compared with 16 calories from sugar and 11 calories from dairy milk. In 2003, non-dairy creamer had contributed just 7.71 calories per day, illustrating how dramatically its role in Americans' coffee consumption had changed.

"We found that non-dairy creamer being added to coffee has now passed both sugar and dairy for its contribution to the total energy consumed from coffee. Non-dairy creamers can be high in saturated fat, which is calorie dense. This could explain the increased calories that non-dairy creamer is contributing," said Yongyi Pan, the study's lead author and a Ph.D. candidate at the Friedman School. "Even though it has been on the market since the 1950s, non-dairy creamer has increased in popularity in recent years as consumers seek plant-based or dairy alternatives."

Sleep, Work Hours, and Coffee Consumption

The researchers also investigated whether coffee consumption differed across groups of adults based on their daily routines and overall diet quality.

They found that people who slept fewer hours generally drank more coffee than those who slept longer. A similar pattern emerged among adults who worked longer hours, who tended to consume more coffee than those with shorter work schedules.

However, coffee consumption was comparable among people with higher and lower overall diet quality, suggesting that drinking more or less coffee was not strongly associated with how healthy their broader diets were.

The researchers acknowledged that the study relied on participants recalling what they had eaten and drunk, which can introduce measurement errors. Because food and beverage consumption can vary considerably from day to day, self-reported dietary information does not always perfectly reflect people's usual habits. The research team took steps to improve the reliability of its estimates.

#3671. Scientists discover a hidden immune weapon against deadly lung cancer

Scientists have identified a previously overlooked type of immune cell that can detect and destroy small cell lung cancer, the most aggressive form of lung cancer worldwide.

The discovery, reported in the journal Cancer Cell by researchers at WEHI, revealed that patients with greater numbers of these specialized immune cells tended to survive longer. The findings point to a previously unrecognized immune defense that could provide a new way to attack tumors that are notoriously difficult to treat.

The research team also demonstrated that medications already in use could enhance the ability of these immune cells to kill cancer. This approach could eventually improve existing treatments and increase the number of patients who benefit from immunotherapy.

Why Small Cell Lung Cancer Is So Difficult to Treat

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is among the deadliest cancers, with fewer than 7% of patients surviving for five years after diagnosis.

Immunotherapy has dramatically improved treatment for several types of cancer, but its success against SCLC has been much more limited. Only a small proportion of patients experience lasting benefits.

A major challenge is the cancer's ability to escape immune surveillance. SCLC tumors frequently avoid detection by the conventional immune cells that many immunotherapy treatments depend on, allowing the disease to persist despite the body's natural defenses.

The findings published in Cancer Cell suggest there may be another way for the immune system to recognize and attack these tumors. By identifying a previously overlooked immune pathway, researchers have uncovered a potential strategy for overcoming the cancer's ability to remain hidden.

Professor Kate Sutherland, the study's senior author and a laboratory head at WEHI, said the discovery could help address a longstanding challenge in lung cancer research: finding ways to make immunotherapy effective for more patients.

"For decades, we've known that small cell lung cancer is exceptionally good at hiding from the immune system," Prof Sutherland said.

"We've found an immune cell that appears to be able to see through that disguise.

"Strikingly, patients whose tumors contained more of these specialized immune cells often had better treatment outcomes.

"This is the strongest evidence yet that gamma delta T cells are present in small cell lung cancers and appear capable of fighting the disease.

"It suggests these cells may offer an alternative immune attack route for tumors that are able to evade conventional cancer-fighting immune cells, opening up new possibilities for treating a cancer that has seen few advances over the past decades."

Miniature Tumors Reveal a Powerful Immune Response

To investigate whether gamma delta T cells could directly attack lung cancer, the scientists developed miniature tumors in the laboratory using tissue samples donated by patients. The samples were obtained through the Victorian Cancer Biobank in partnership with the Royal Melbourne Hospital.

These miniature tumors, often called "mini tumors," provide one of the most realistic laboratory models of SCLC created so far, allowing researchers to study how cancer behaves and responds to potential treatments.

The team then introduced gamma delta T cells collected from the same patients' blood into the tumor models. The results showed that these immune cells were highly effective at identifying and destroying SCLC cells.

The scientists went a step further by testing whether two medications already approved for other uses could make this immune response even stronger. The drugs were tarlatamab (a cancer treatment approved for SCLC) and zoledronate (approved for osteoporosis and cancer-related bone disease).

Each medication contributed in a different way. Zoledronate increased the number of available gamma delta T cells and improved their ability to identify the lung cancer tumors. Tarlatamab helped bring the immune cells and cancer cells together, effectively directing the immune attack toward the tumors.

When used together, the drugs substantially increased the ability of gamma delta T cells to kill cancer cells in laboratory experiments. The findings suggest that combining existing treatments could provide a new strategy for strengthening the immune system's response to SCLC.

Could Existing Drugs Lead to New Lung Cancer Therapies?

Dr. Jin Ng, the study's first author, said the discovery could also support a treatment approach known as adoptive cell transfer. This involves collecting immune cells, multiplying them outside the body, and then delivering large numbers of those cells back into a patient to help fight cancer.

Because methods for expanding and reinfusing gamma delta T cells have already been developed, the researchers believe their findings could help accelerate the development of new treatments.

"For patients with lower gamma delta T cell levels, therapies such as zoledronate may offer a way to boost their cancer-fighting immune cells and responsiveness to treatments," Dr. Ng said.

"As therapies that expand and reinfuse gamma delta T cells already exist, our findings raise the exciting possibility of rapidly translating this discovery into new treatment strategies.

"This could open an entirely new avenue for personalized treatment approaches in small cell lung cancer and other solid cancers that are often missed by conventional immune cells."

Clues From Major Lung Cancer Immunotherapy Trials

The investigation began with an examination of data from two influential clinical trials, IMpower133 and CASPIAN. Both studies helped establish immunotherapy as a standard treatment option for patients with small cell lung cancer.

Researchers noticed evidence pointing toward an important role for gamma delta T cells and used sophisticated genomic and computational techniques to investigate further.

Analyses conducted by WEHI's genomics and bioinformatics teams revealed that patients with stronger gamma delta T cell signatures were among those who benefited most from immunotherapy.

Together with the laboratory findings, these results strengthen the evidence that gamma delta T cells play an important role in the immune response to SCLC and could become useful targets for future therapies.

The WEHI team is now working with scientists at the Doherty Institute to build on the discovery, with the goal of translating the findings into a new class of immunotherapy drugs for small cell lung cancer.

The research received support from Cancer Council Victoria, the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), The CASS Foundation, and The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer.
