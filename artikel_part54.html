#3750. "We were wrong": Wardogs devs end Season 2's team-specific cash and XP bonus experiment after a single, tumultuous day

Season 2 of Wardogs, the multiplayer sandbox FPS currently blasting and/or politely driving its way through early access, introduced an un usual "social experiment" when it launched yesterday. In an attempt to rehabilitate the reputation of its Lonestar faction, a.k.a the infamous Blue Team, developers Bulkhead began granting bonus XP and cash – the latter being the upgrade-focused game’s lifeblood – to Blue players alone. The hypothesis, apparently, was that more skilled players would give Lonestar a chance, helping boost its dismal winrates and perhaps improve its tarnished image.

One day in, then, how’s it going? “We were wrong. We fucked up,” says Bulkhead CEO Joe Brammer in a video announcing the bonus scheme’s immediate cancellation. Ah!

It turns out that while the promise of free money and experience did indeed incentive a wave of Blue Team picks, some players – to use Brammer’s given term, "tryhards" – were earning far more lucre than Bulkhead anticipated. Given the bonuses were a mere 5% to cash and 2.5% to XP, the developers’ expectation was that the experiment’s incentives were "not, for the median player, substantial enough to actually matter." Wardogs’ "top" players, however, have been taking home thousands upon thousands of war dollars in those bonuses alone, potentially upsetting the shooter’s progression balance.

The bonus-bribed rush to join Lonestar during match starts also, it seems, caused issues, as players would ditch severs once the blue crew filled up to go try their luck joining the faction elsewhere. Without enough players to slum it on the red and green teams, matches were delayed until all three teams were sufficiently manned.

Thus: one cancelled experiment, a few thousand players with freshly swollen pockets, and likely zero people having switched to the belief that Blue Team is filled with scholars and gentlemen. Still, the video suggests more conventional rebalancing techniques, namely moving spawn locations on the Bakuriani map, have been more effective and improving the troubled faction’s win percentages. Such changes will stay, even if the XP bonus has already been hotfixed out of existence.

Next on Bulkhead’s hitlist is a widely-used vehicle XP exploit, which I haven’t seen in action, but apparently involves repeatedly driving in and out of a faction’s HQ to rapidly amass minor experience rewards. With the help of Season 2’s more generous XP payouts for logistical feats, among which safe 'round trips' are counted, perpetrators have been prematurely unlocking late-game vehicles like tanks. The temporary fix, in place for now, is the slowing of vehicle progression rates to their Season 1 levels.

#3749. Phantom Blade Zero May Have The Best Ladder Climbing In All Of Video Games, But I Promise This Hands-On Preview Talks About More Than That

I played Phantom Blade Zero at Gamescom in Cologne, Germany, back in August, which, at the speed of the internet, means it feels like I had my hands on the controller about five years ago. The memory is vague and dreamlike, but it’s a strong one that left an undeniable impression. I also made sure to take thorough notes because I knew it would be some time before I could write about it.

For my Phantom Blade Zero play session two months ago, developer S-Game Studio offered up the opportunity to basically play from the beginning of the game, encouraging a long play session. I played through the intro and initial tutorials, meeting protagonist Soul, and learned some of his backstory.

A major selling point for Phantom Blade Zero since its reveal has been its use of motion capture for its animation. When our own Wesley LeBlanc visited the studio in China last year, they put him in the motion capture rig to showcase how important the motion capture process was to the game’s development. My admittedly dismissive reaction to this selling point has always been… all modern triple-A games use motion capture for gameplay and cutscenes. I have seen video game performers like Troy Baker dressed in grey leotards more than I have seen him dressed in clothes whenever behind-the-scenes footage of game development is shared. What’s the big deal?

I am not fully removed from that camp, if I am being honest, but I admit that getting my hands on Phantom Blade Zero and controlling the movement and action did highlight the overall smoothness of playing what is essentially a Chinese martial arts film. It really does feel like you’re playing a game that borrows the beauty of human movement showcased in movies like Ang Lee’s Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon. Acrobatic dance applied to the art of swinging a sword.

After completing an opening combat encounter, it is revealed that Soul suffered a heart wound and a healer cryptically named The Flesh Sculptor gave him a replacement: a tainted heart that will only let him live for 66 days. And then I am whisked away to a high-speed carriage ride where the charismatic old man driver warns me that no one visits my destination because of a plague.

Enemies with chain and claw weapons ambush the carriage and Soul ejects the driver from the cart before things get too dangerous, as the driver's yell of, “You still owe me for the ride!” echoes in the distance. This whole sequence is very "style over substance." Pulling off counters and delivering sword slices to enemies as they leap from the nearby cliff face walls isn’t particularly challenging, but it looks impressive while feeling closer to a series of QTE moments. There is no QTE, however, to save the horses as the carriage careens off a cliff and crashes in a valley. When Soul comes to following the crash, I am surprised to see the dead horses hanging from the trees. A gruesome image now etched onto the map screen presumably serves as a memorable visual reminder of this location, but I can’t help but feel bad for the poor animals.

The whole sequence impressively sets up a chance to finally explore without training wheels, and I am happy to report that the level design is interesting and circuitous. With such a focus on combat and combat choreography, I was worried the action would be a series of arena fights. But Soul can sneak up on enemies, open shortcuts, and find characters that need help and offer missions. I hesitate to place the game in the Souls-like genre – it seems generally more forgiving – but the level layout recalls that structure in a way I like.

Soul may also be the fastest ladder climber I have ever controlled in a video game. Ladder climbing may be an odd mechanic to highlight, but I have climbed up and down thousands of ladders in my video game career and they always bring pacing to a standstill. You push up on the control stick and wait. If you’re lucky, sometimes you can press the sprint button to make your character climb a little faster, or slide down. That’s been the best-case scenario for decades. Soul, however, bounds up ladders like Spider-Man sprinting up the side of a building. Much like the player eager to get to the next memorable moment, Soul doesn’t have time to gingerly step on or grab each rung one by one. The UI loudly proclaimed that Soul officially only has 65 days to live once he woke up from the crash. There is no time.

And speaking of no time, mine was also literally limited during my Gamescom appointment, so S-Game Studio fast-forwarded me to the incredibly impressive Lion Dance boss. The Lion Dance is a traditional Chinese dance where two performers wear a joined lion costume and perform acrobatic feats often on high pillars. Here’s a good example. Seeing the real-world version of the dance is entrancing, and for Phantom Blade Zero’s Lion Dance boss, the team motion-captured real performers.

Just seeing the technically-two characters move around in the boss arena is impressive, but the second stage of the boss fight (which you jump to if you die – no need to replay the first stage of the boss fight) is where it gets really impressive. Pillars appear from the ground and the Lion dances among the tops of them while shooting fireballs at you. And while that is happening, small lion head minions appear to distract you. However, defeating the last minion means you can grab a lion mask, jump on the pillars yourself, and shoot your own fireballs at the boss.

The reports from everyone who has had a chance to play Phantom Blade Zero have basically been universally positive, but I still went into the demo expecting a potentially forgettable, but well-executed action game. It ended up being one of my favorite hands-on experiences at Gamescom, despite the long wait to talk about it. The game feels great to play; I am eager to explore the levels; and the Lion Dance boss, in particular, showed me that the boss encounters (unless the Lion Dance is the notable exception) will be full of unexpected surprises and impressive animation.

#3746. I Tried 'PhotoCraft,' the AI Clone of Photoshop, and It's Almost Exactly Like the Real Thing

David Nield is a technology journalist from Manchester in the U.K. who has been writing about gadgets and apps for more than 20 years.

He has a bachelor's degree in English Literature from Durham University, where he also spent a term as editor of the award-winning student newspaper Palatinate. His journalism career started in print media, where he contributed to and edited several technology magazines and bookazines sold in the U.K. and internationally. More recently, he has worked as a freelancer for some of the biggest technology publications on the web, covering everything from on-the-ground reporting about product launches, to detailed explainers and how-to guides on apps, gadgets, and platforms. His expertise covers broad areas of consumer tech, including smartphones, laptops, wearables, and AI.

Love it or hate it, AI is changing tech at a rapid pace. Case in point: AI is now being used to reverse-engineer big-name software packages. Once complete, developers upload the cloned versions online as open-source tools that anyone can use for free. It seems no company is immune from these dupes, no matter how big it happens to be. That includes Adobe, and its legacy software like Photoshop, which now has a free-to-download AI clone called "PhotoCraft." I gave this clone a shot, and while it isn't perfect, it's remarkable how similar it is to the real deal.

One of the developers leading this particular charge is Brandon Thomas, a seasoned software engineer who has used Claude Opus 5.5 to build copies of Adobe Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere, Lightroom, Acrobat Pro, After Effects, and InDesign in the Rust coding language. They all get new names—Photoshop is PhotoCraft, Premiere is FilmCraft—but they're very similar in terms of features and layout to what Adobe puts out and charges a monthly fee for. It's another seismic industry shift driven by AI, and Thomas is apparently building clones of Microsoft Office apps next.

According to the developer, Thomas "got bit by the dark pattern 'cancellation fee' one too many times," which prompted the start of the project. "AI lets us take back tech and set the clock back to the pre-shitty era," says Thomas. "But now you can just create 1:1 functional equivalents, clean room, that are highly performant and cross-platform. Nothing is stopping you." In another comment, he declares, "Software is over."

It's not clear exactly how Thomas completed this project, but he has used the term "vibe coding," so it sounds like some kind of AI-powered observe-and-copy is going on here, even with his programming experience. The developer has also suggested this is a more worthwhile endeavor than supporting open source apps such as LibreOffice and GIMP as, in his view, "There is no reason to save broken software."

As Ars Technica points out, reverse engineering is usually tolerated as long as no code is directly copied, but if the new suite of tools too closely matches the look and feel of the apps they're cloning, that could mean legal trouble. The truth is, no one's sure yet—and it brings up again the legality of the AI models themselves, which have been trained on vast amounts of human-made code to get to this level.

Adobe is no doubt taking a very close look at this, but while it's still available, I decided to give the software a try myself. You can download the apps from the ArtCraft website, though they're labeled as "early alpha" and "in development" for now. According to Thomas, 99% feature parity with the Adobe equivalents should be "measured in months and not years".

The ArtCraft web portal offers a launcher app through which you can manage the entire 'Craft' selection of tools, but I focused on PhotoCraft as Photoshop is the only Adobe program I really make much use of. My first impressions: It definitely looks like a Photoshop rip-off.

PhotoCraft has the same color scheme, the same layout of panels, and the same menus in the same order—with the exception of Plugins, which I suppose will take a while. The keyboard shortcuts are the same, as far as I could tell, and the various preferences screens are very similar too—both in terms of the options you have and how they're laid out.

Most of the Photoshop work I do involves cropping and combining images, tweaking their colors and balance, and getting them in fixed formats for articles. I was able to transform my workflow from the Adobe app pretty much wholesale: The same panels, the same dialogs, and the same tools (from the clone stamp to the magic wand).

Plenty of image editors ape Photoshop, of course, because it's the industry leader—Pixlr, anyone?—but this seems on another level entirely. PhotoCraft is also fast and slick, and as far as I could tell, free from bugs. The only bumps I came across were some clunky snapping and selecting operations, which could definitely be improved upon but didn't actually break anything.

I'm by no means a power user of Photoshop, and people with more experience than me have found plenty of issues under the hood. I suspect that when it comes to playing around with more advanced tools and layer functions, cracks might start to show in the way you would expect from AI code trying to copy what another program does. I'm not sure "watch this program and make something that behaves the same" is the best way forward for software development.

That said, for me, PhotoCraft did everything I needed it to in terms of image manipulations, adjustments, and layer control, and did it well. How the app carries on long-term remains to be seen, as AI-written bug fixes and feature upgrades will presumably be piled on top of existing AI-written code—and that's assuming Adobe doesn't get involved.

Personally, I'm not going to cancel my Photoshop subscription: I can afford it, I use it, and I'm not yet ready to trust my projects to something that's been coded by AI. But this is the era we're in now, where AI can look at something—a painting, a website, a movie, a sprawling Adobe software application—and give you a very convincing imitation in not much time at all.

#3742. RuneScape: Dragonwilds Review - An Adventure In Busywork

RuneScape: Dragonwilds strikes a respectable balance between a fantasy RPG and survival crafting game. The variety of environments and hidden lore for those who want it give depth to an otherwise nondescript setting and offer many hours of content for those who can tolerate its crafting system. But, without having an attachment to the RuneScape series, or a love for chopping wood or mining ore, it’s difficult to enjoy past the opening chapter. Though there is fun to be had, I wish certain parts of it didn’t feel as tedious or locked behind a grind for materials.

Dragonwilds expands the RuneScape world and brings back familiar faces to ground players in the dragon-infested continent of Ashenfall. It revolves around the history of Ashenfall’s early settlers and the takedown of Dragon Queen Kuldra, told indirectly through glowing blue books and side quests. Those stories of grief and greed are interesting, but mostly a backdrop for common tasks like plundering temples and stabbing rats.

Most activities tie back to the crafting system, whether that’s foraging for food or smithing swords. You can build tools, weapons, armor, jewelry, furniture, structures, and more. Dragonwilds lets you spend as much time as you want farming materials, though some, like ores, are more necessary than others. Magic spells can speed up some of the monotony, but that also requires leveling up your skills enough to learn those spells.

Even with crafting at its core, Dragonwilds is bloated with workstations and items that are more laborious than fun to collect. I blame the increasingly high number of materials needed to craft higher-level weapons and armor, and the multiple station types that could have been combined into one. As you get stronger, the number of materials you need for your gear increases, too. For example, my gear now needs Fine Cloth woven from threads and gold bars. By the time I reached the halfway point of the game, I had separate stations for tools and weapons, armor, jewelry, bows and arrows, and magical weapons like wands.

Classes don’t really exist in Dragonwilds. Instead of explicitly choosing a class like in a traditional RPG, you swap between roles by switching your gear – like swapping from armor to robes to switch from melee to magic. Changing classes felt pointless because I could force my way through most challenges with a sword, and bows and wands are less effective given the circumstances. Many confrontations involve flanking from multiple sides, so long-range combat isn’t ideal. #3741. Why painful arthritis can return stronger after cancer treatment

For some cancer patients, the effects of immunotherapy can linger in an unexpected place: their joints. Painful arthritis may return repeatedly, sometimes becoming more severe with each episode. Now, scientists have uncovered a possible explanation involving the immune system's ability to remember previous attacks.

Researchers at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center have found evidence that arthritis triggered by certain cancer immunotherapies behaves like a disease driven by immune memory. Rather than launching an entirely new inflammatory response during each flare, the immune system appears to reactivate cells involved in earlier episodes.

The discovery could eventually help doctors identify patients at risk of recurring arthritis and develop treatments that control joint inflammation without interfering with the immune system's ability to fight cancer.

The study, published in Cancer Immunology Research, was led by scientists at UT MD Anderson and Yale University. The research team included Roza I. Nurieva, Ph.D., professor of Immunology; Synat Keam, Ph.D., a postdoctoral fellow in the Nurieva Laboratory; and Yuanteng Jeff Li, M.D., assistant professor of General Internal Medicine. Sang Taek Kim, M.D., Ph.D., an assistant professor at Yale University, also co-led the investigation.

"Cancer immunotherapy works by creating a lasting immune response against tumors, but that same persistence may also contribute to recurring inflammatory side effects that can be debilitating for patients," Nurieva said. "By identifying the immune cells that drive recurrent arthritis, we can inform treatment strategies that reduce the risk of future flares while preserving the therapy's effectiveness against cancer."

Why Cancer Immunotherapy Can Trigger Painful Arthritis

Immune checkpoint inhibitors are widely used cancer treatments that help the body's natural defenses recognize and attack tumor cells. These drugs are a form of immunotherapy that works by removing some of the molecular restraints that normally keep immune activity under control.

Although this approach can help the immune system destroy cancer cells, it can sometimes cause immune cells to attack healthy tissues as well. When the joints become targets, patients may develop inflammatory arthritis, a condition that causes pain, swelling, and inflammation.

For some patients, the problem does not disappear after the first episode. Approximately 20% to 50% of patients who develop this complication experience repeated arthritis flares, and symptoms can persist for months or even years after immunotherapy ends.

These recurring episodes are often more intense than the initial attack, raising an important question: Why does the immune system continue to produce inflammation, and why can its response become increasingly aggressive?

Scientists Discover Immune Cells That Remember Previous Attacks

To investigate, the researchers examined fluid taken from the joints of six patients during both their first arthritis episode and a later recurrence.

Their analysis revealed two distinct populations of immune cells that appeared during both flares. One consisted of inflammatory CD8 T cells, while the other was a specialized group of CD4 T cells expressing two markers known as PD-1 and CXCL13.

T cells are immune cells that help coordinate the body's defenses and respond to potential threats. Some can also persist after an initial immune response, allowing the immune system to react more quickly when it encounters a familiar target.

The presence of the same immune cell populations during separate arthritis attacks suggests that recurring inflammation may arise from cells already involved in the earlier episode. Instead of starting from scratch, the immune system appears to reactivate an established inflammatory response.

This immune memory mechanism could help explain why arthritis can repeatedly return even after cancer treatment has stopped.

Why Arthritis Flares May Become More Severe

The researchers also found that the recurring immune cells appeared more active during subsequent arthritis episodes. They produced increased amounts of inflammatory signaling molecules, which immune cells use to communicate and influence one another.

Another finding was particularly unexpected. Regulatory T cells, which normally act as a braking system to prevent excessive immune activity, also began producing inflammatory molecules during recurring flares.

This suggests that some of the cells responsible for keeping inflammation under control may instead contribute to it, potentially weakening the body's ability to shut down the response.

The researchers also identified extensive communication networks connecting inflammatory cells within affected joints. These signaling networks became more pronounced during the second arthritis episode.

Together, the findings suggest that recurring flares involve a coordinated and increasingly active inflammatory environment, rather than just a handful of immune cells acting independently.

Could Immune Memory Help Predict Future Arthritis Flares?

Despite growing recognition of inflammatory arthritis as a complication of cancer immunotherapy, scientists still do not fully understand why it affects certain patients while others never develop the condition.

The newly identified immune cell populations could provide an important starting point. Future studies may determine whether these cells can serve as biomarkers, meaning measurable biological indicators that help doctors recognize patients who are more likely to experience recurring arthritis.

Understanding which immune cells sustain the inflammation could also guide the development of more precise treatments. Ideally, such therapies would prevent or reduce painful arthritis flares while leaving the beneficial cancer-fighting effects of immunotherapy intact.

Because the investigation examined samples from only six patients, additional research will be needed to confirm the findings and establish whether the identified immune cells can reliably predict future episodes.

Still, the results offer a promising explanation for a difficult complication of cancer treatment: an immune system trained to remember and fight tumors may also retain inflammatory responses that continue to harm the joints.

#3733. Scientists discover a way to kill dangerous gut bacteria without killing the good

A dangerous intestinal infection may soon have a more targeted treatment option. Scientists have identified a natural compound that can kill the bacteria responsible for severe gut infections while leaving much of the beneficial gut microbiome unharmed.

The bacterium Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile) is naturally found in the intestines of many people and generally causes no problems when present in small amounts. However, antibiotics that target a wide range of bacteria can upset the delicate balance of microorganisms in the gut. This disruption allows C. difficile to multiply rapidly, potentially causing severe intestinal inflammation that can become chronic.

C. difficile infections (CDI) are among the leading causes of diarrhea associated with antibiotic treatment. The bacteria produce toxins that can trigger dangerous and sometimes life-threatening inflammation in the intestines. Because these infections frequently return after treatment, scientists are searching for more effective ways to eliminate the pathogen without causing additional damage to the gut microbiome.

A team led by Prof. Rolf Müller, department head and scientific director at HIPS, has now identified a promising drug candidate that specifically targets C. difficile while preserving much of the gut's beneficial microbial community. HIPS is a site of the Helmholtz Centre for Infection Research (HZI) that operates in collaboration with Saarland University.

Natural Compound Targets Dangerous Gut Bacteria

The researchers found that argyrin B, a naturally occurring antibacterial substance, was highly effective against C. difficile in laboratory tests and experiments involving infected mice. Importantly, the compound showed little activity against the beneficial microorganisms that help maintain a healthy digestive system.

"We were able to show that the natural compound argyrin B is highly effective against CDI: both in laboratory experiments and in a mouse model of the infection, where it significantly reduces the bacterial load," says Dr. Jennifer Herrmann, senior scientist in the department "Microbial Natural Products" at HIPS. "An antibacterial target of the argyrins had previously been identified and characterized in the notorious hospital-acquired pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Now we have been able to show that argyrin B also acts on this target in C. difficile and exhibits particularly high efficacy in doing so. In contrast, the argyrins have virtually no effect on the beneficial microorganisms in our digestive tract. The gut microbiome could therefore be spared during treatment with argyrin and restored to a healthy state more quickly."

A Bacteria-Killing Substance Found in Soil

Argyrins were originally discovered in myxobacteria, microorganisms that inhabit soil. These bacteria produce natural substances that help them attack other microbes and use them as a source of food.

Although argyrin B has demonstrated promising antibacterial activity, researchers must also determine whether its pharmaceutical properties make it suitable for use as a medicine. That includes understanding how the compound behaves inside the body and whether it could cause unwanted effects.

One particularly encouraging finding emerged when the team examined what happens after mice receive argyrin B orally. Very little of the compound entered their bloodstream. Instead, most of it remained in the large intestine, where C. difficile infections occur.

By concentrating in the gut rather than circulating throughout the body, argyrin B could potentially reduce the risk of side effects affecting other organs. Together with its selective activity against C. difficile, this characteristic makes the compound a promising candidate for a treatment that may be better tolerated.

Could It Help Prevent Recurring Gut Infections?

One of the biggest challenges in treating C. difficile is its tendency to return. Patients who experience multiple relapses are often treated using fecal microbiota transfer (commonly known as a stool transplant). This procedure aims to restore a healthier balance of gut microorganisms, but it can be complicated to perform and unpleasant for patients.

A medication developed from argyrin could eventually provide another treatment option. By eliminating the harmful bacteria without extensively disrupting the rest of the microbiome, such a therapy might reduce the likelihood of further infections and relapses. That could also help some patients avoid the need for stool transplants.

More research is needed before argyrin B can be considered for clinical use. The scientists plan to investigate which dosage produces the strongest antibacterial effect and use additional experimental models to determine whether the compound can also combat recurring C. difficile infections.

Researchers think they might have pinpointed the exact night sky that we see depicted in Vincent van Gogh's iconic painting "The Starry Night."

Perhaps one of the most famous paintings in modern history, Van Gogh's "The Starry Night" carries a legacy that continues to inspire and mystify generation after generation. But while you might see in its swirls of color and bright spots of starlight a night sky derived purely from imagination, some scientists think it represents an actual view — one that includes a waning crescent moon, Venus and the Hyades cluster. And they may know exactly when this sky appeared..

In a new study, researchers suggest that "The Starry Night" depicts the pre-dawn sky seen around July 22, 1889 from Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, where Van Gogh painted it. But while this sky happens to perfectly match the one depicted in the artwork, art historians have long thought that Van Gogh painted this piece in June. Could these findings shift the date of this legendary artwork?

This investigation started decades ago with art historian Albert Boime and astronomer George Abell at UCLA in 1984 and continued in the following years with astronomer Charles Whitney at Harvard University. However, these earlier analyses did not agree with one another, and there was no true consensus about exactly when the sky matched with the painting.

So the question remained, lingering in the minds of those with a shared love for both art and space. And a team led by Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim, the CEO of South Korean robotic telescope company SLLAB and a Ph.D. candidate at Kyung Hee University's School of Space Research, picked up the search once more.

"When they see a fragment of a starry sky, observational astronomers like me and amateur astronomers alike will want to solve the puzzle. I am simply someone who pursued that process a little more actively," Kim told Space.com.

But how did Kim's team come to their conclusion, after others had failed to nail down a date over the decades? In short, "we analyzed the painting astronomically, analyzed documents about his situation in July 1889, and then carried out ecological and meteorological investigations," Kim said.

To start with, the team analyzed the sky from the exact location in Saint-Rémy where Van Gogh is said to have painted "The Starry Night." They then used observational geometry to map the historical night sky.

"Modern telescopes are equipped with a function called astrometry, which compares an image of the night sky with celestial coordinates stored in a computer and automatically extracts the coordinates of the image," Kim said. "We used Procrustes analysis, a method that compares only the 'shape' of two sets of points. After matching their position, size and rotation, it measures how similar the two shapes are from the differences that remain (the residuals)."

"Because a star cluster on the canvas may be larger or smaller than in reality and slightly tilted, this method was the right fit. It allowed us to explain, in quantitative terms, what earlier studies had described only qualitatively as 'resembling a certain constellation,'" Kim added.

But this quantitative analysis wasn't enough to say for sure that this was the right-fitting sky, as different constellations could fit to a similar degree, Kim explained. To get closer to their answer, the team combined their analysis with a deep dive through history.

"We also checked whether the stars were actually in that part of the pre-dawn sky on that date, whether they fall within the painter’s field of view, and whether the appearance of the moon and Venus matches the painting. It was the Hyades that satisfied all of these conditions," Kim said.

As mentioned, the date of this matching sky comes in late July, a month after art historians think Van Gogh painted "The Starry Night." But the researchers suggest that their findings could provide evidence to support the idea that the work was actually painted in July.

Letters written by Van Gogh as early as 1888 describe his desire to paint a star-filled sky. A letter dated April 12, 1888 reads: "A starry sky, for example, well — it’s a thing that I’d like to try to do, just as in the daytime I’ll try to paint a green meadow studded with dandelions."

Kim followed these letters through the months to try and find more information that could potentially alleviate the discrepancy between the matching skies and the actual month the painting was created. Kim pointed to a letter from between late May and early June 1889 that reads: "This morning I saw the countryside from my window a long time before sunrise with nothing but the morning star, which looked very big."

Kim drew on "personal imagination" to find ties between Van Gogh's sky observations in these months and what they might mean for the painting's timeline, Kim shared, so this doesn't definitively change the view of this painting's history. However, Kim suggested that these records could indicate that Van Gogh was repeatedly looking up and watching the pre-dawn sky, which could have led to him seeing this exact sky on July 22.

The painting itself even ignites uncertainty among art historians. As the researchers cite in their new paper, leading art historians see "The Starry Night" as stylistically different from Van Gogh's other works during the month of June, drawing further questions over the date painted.

"What our study shows is that the sky in the painting matches, in both observation and shape, the real pre-dawn sky around July 22. So, what we propose is the possibility that Vincent conceived the idea in June and completed the painting after July 22," Kim said.

However, "I think further research in art history and conservation science could shed more light on this question," Kim added.

This work was described in a study published Oct. 22 in the journal Scientific Reports.

#3727. Five Garmin Data Screens That Helped Me Improve My 'Running Economy'

On most runs, I prefer to pay attention to pace and heart rate data from my Garmin, and ignore the rest (even though I love playing with the data later on). But I also believe in trying a fun experiment now and then. So, for my most recent training block, I’ve retooled my watch's data screens around several of the more nitty-gritty metrics available to me— cadence, stride length, vertical oscillation, ground contact time, and more—to see what useful insights I could gain.

First, a quick definition, because "running economy" gets thrown around a lot: Running economy is the energy (oxygen) cost of running at a given speed. Two runners can hold the same 8:00 pace, but the one who uses less oxygen to do so is more “economical.” That runner can run the same pace at a lower heart rate, hold it longer, or go faster for the same effort. It's one of the big levers for performance, alongside VO2 max and lactate threshold, and it's one you can improve with training, strength work, and technique.

Technically, your watch can't measure running economy directly and give you one neat number. What it can do is show you how you're producing your speed, so you can spot inefficiencies and then test whether fixing them lowers your heart rate at the same pace. That's the experiment I’m running (pun intended). After my next marathon, I'll break down how my running economy actually improved, and in what ways.

Garmin groups these metrics under "running dynamics." Depending on your watch and accessories, they include cadence, stride length, ground contact time, ground contact time balance, vertical oscillation, vertical ratio, running power, and step speed loss. In all the examples below, I'm using a Garmin Forerunner 970; note that not every Garmin can show all of these data fields.

Cadence works on nearly every Garmin running watch.

Stride length is available on many mid-range and higher watches, sometimes from the wrist and sometimes only with an accessory.

Ground contact time, ground contact balance, vertical oscillation, and vertical ratio have historically required a compatible chest strap, but many newer watches now measure these from the wrist.

Running power has its own requirements, depending on your watch and whether you use an accessory. Check Garmin's running power device requirements.

Step speed loss and step speed loss percentage require an HRM 600 chest strap paired to a compatible watch. They aren't measured when you're moving at a walking pace during a run.

Check your watch's owner's manual (the "Running Dynamics" page) to see what metrics are available for you to rebuild your own data screens.

Keep in mind that while these metrics are useful, there is no universally "correct" cadence or vertical oscillation number. Chasing the oh-so-coveted 180 steps per minute can do more harm than good if you end up overstriding at a fast turnover or running stiff and choppy. Think of everything below as a diagnostic, not a target. Some more tips to make the most of your Garmin data screens, without succumbing to information overload:

Change one variable at a time. I spent a few weeks laser-focused on cadence before getting stressed over anything else.

Use the new data screens on easy runs. Hard workouts are for hard efforts; easy runs are where you can afford to observe more.

Review after the run, not during. Staring at vertical oscillation mid-stride is a great way to trip and fall. Feel free to glance, but don't let the numbers take over your run.

Watch the trends, not single numbers. Again, focus on personal patterns, not any specific number targets.

Keep screens sparse. Three or four fields per screen is plenty. Anything denser is hard to read mid-stride.

Consider keeping your base screen first in the rotation and add the experimental screens after it, so you can flip over on easy runs without losing your usual setup.

"Run at 180 steps per minute" is one of the most repeated pieces of running advice, and one of the most misunderstood. Cadence naturally varies with pace, so chasing one magic number regardless of how fast you're going can actually hold you back. Pace is the product of cadence and stride length. Seeing those two variables together can help show you how you're getting faster paces (or not).

What I noticed immediately: on easy runs, I naturally fall into a longer stride and lower cadence. It felt relaxed, but it also meant a lot of braking on each landing. Over the past several weeks, I've been nudging my cadence up by about 3 to 5 steps per minute, which shortens my stride slightly at the same pace, and my heart rate at that pace has come down a bit (although cooler weather certainly helps there, too).

The goal is to find your own cadence range and shift it slightly higher, not to force a fixed number. When in doubt, aim for a cadence at each pace that keeps your foot landing close to your body rather than reaching out ahead of it.

This screen needs compatible hardware, and if you do have it, it's my favorite for spotting fatigue.

Ground contact time (GCT) is how long your foot stays on the ground each step. Generally, shorter is associated with a springier, more efficient stride, and it tends to shrink as you get faster. GCT balance shows the left/right split.

Step speed loss is the newest of these metrics, and the one most directly tied to economy. Every time your foot lands, it acts a bit like a brake, and your forward speed dips before you push off again. Step speed loss measures how much speed you give up during each footstrike, and the percentage version expresses that loss relative to your running speed. Lower numbers mean less braking and more of your energy going into moving forward instead of fighting yourself.

It captures overstriding. Reaching your foot out ahead of your body increases braking, and this metric shows you the cost. That makes it a great companion to the cadence screen: if raising cadence brings your landing closer to your body, you may see step speed loss drop.

It's a cleaner efficiency signal than bounce. Vertical oscillation tells you how much you move up and down. Step speed loss tells you how much you're slowing down horizontally, which is closer to what actually costs you energy.

It shows fatigue. Like GCT, it can creep up late in long runs as form degrades.

Two other uses I found valuable on this screen:

Fatigue tracking. In long runs, my GCT always creeps up in the final third, well before my pace or heart rate tell me I'm utterly cooked.

Asymmetry flags. A consistent tilt away from 50/50 isn't automatically a problem, since a small imbalance is common. But if yours shifts noticeably on days something feels off, it's worth listening to.

Again, don't stress about small imbalances. Look for changes from your normal.

Vertical oscillation is how much you bounce with each step. Vertical ratio is that bounce relative to your stride length, which makes it easier to compare across paces and across runners. Lower generally means less energy spent going up rather than forward.

Personally, this was the most misleading screen in the beginning. I saw my number, decided it was too high, and tried to "run flatter." That just made my stride feel forced and awkward. What worked better was treating it as a trend. The plan is that when I improve cadence and let my stride shorten a touch, vertical oscillation naturally drops on its own. Strength work and a slightly higher cadence should do more for me than any overly contrived form cue about "floating."

The economy framing here is simple: compare power to heart rate over time. In fact, I'm planning to go in and put heart rate on my screen alongside power. Because power responds instantly to changes in effort and terrain, it's a better gauge of your economy compared to pace on hills or extra windy days.

If you can hold the same wattage at a lower heart rate than you could two months ago, that's a real improvement in fitness and efficiency, regardless of what pace says. Just be aware that different power systems calculate the number differently, so stick with one source and don't compare across devices.

Finally, the screen I never removed: pace, heart rate, and lap or average pace (plus a timer and time of day, since this is still my watch, after all). For the sake of these runs, I could judge my form changes by whether my heart rate at a given pace went up or down. At the end of the day, this is how you find out whether any of the tweaks above are making a difference.

The findings were first identified using data from DZNE's Rhineland Study and then independently confirmed in another population. The research was published in Nature Communications.

"Our study suggests that healthy eating goes hand in hand with slower biological aging. The effects are not massive, but they are measurable and relevant for prevention. By slowing the aging process, the risk of age-related diseases such as dementia or cardiovascular disorders can be reduced. Thus, healthy eating contributes to healthy aging," says Prof. Monique Breteler, Director of Population Health Sciences at DZNE and head of the Rhineland Study. "Importantly, there is probably no single 'correct' diet. According to our data, various dietary patterns are associated with slower aging, some more, some less. That is an encouraging finding, because it leaves room to tailor healthy eating to personal and cultural preferences, budget and taste."

To investigate the connection between diet and aging, the scientists analyzed dietary information and blood samples from roughly 7,500 men and women. The initial analysis included 6,470 participants in DZNE's Rhineland Study in Bonn, Germany. The researchers then tested their findings using data from another 1,034 people enrolled in the EPIC-Potsdam Study, conducted by the German Institute of Human Nutrition Potsdam-Rehbrücke (DIfE).

Rather than relying only on participants' chronological ages, the team looked for molecular signs of aging in their blood. Specifically, they measured DNA methylation, a process involving chemical modifications to DNA that help regulate gene activity and influence how cells function.

These chemical patterns change in relatively predictable ways as people grow older, allowing scientists to estimate biological age. Unlike chronological age, which simply measures how long someone has been alive, biological age offers clues about how the body is aging at the molecular level.

"We applied three different approaches, so-called epigenetic clocks, to read biological aging from DNA methylation," explains Juliana Tavares, a doctoral researcher at DZNE and lead author of the current publication. "To this end, we leveraged state-of-the-art technology, which allowed us to cover about 850,000 sites in the DNA. This is roughly twice as many as in most previous studies."

The next step was to determine how closely each participant followed the 10 dietary patterns. Researchers compared reported food consumption with the recommendations for each diet and assigned scores reflecting how well individuals followed those guidelines.

Because the diets emphasize different foods and nutritional goals, a person could receive a high score for one eating pattern while scoring poorly on another. In fact, participants who met one definition of healthy eating often did not meet the requirements of other diets.

"One and the same person could therefore rank high on one diet score and low on another. In other words: People who ate healthily by one standard were often not the same people who ate healthily by another. According to our data, hardly anyone counts as a healthy eater by every standard at once," says Tavares.

Despite those differences, the overall pattern was remarkably consistent. Better adherence to every one of the 10 diets was associated with slower biological aging. Some eating patterns showed stronger associations than others, but the absolute differences were relatively small.

"While there are measurable differences between the diets, they are modest in absolute terms. It's the big picture what matters," says Tavares. "In summary: When it comes to slowing down the aging process, there isn't just one healthy diet, but indeed a whole repertoire of possible diets with a positive effect."

One unexpected result emerged when researchers examined the association between diet and aging among smokers. The link between healthier eating and slower biological aging was stronger in smokers than in nonsmokers, even though smokers did not generally have healthier diets.

The researchers have not yet determined what biological processes might explain this difference. One possibility is that the extensive damage caused by smoking leaves greater room for measurable improvements associated with healthier eating. However, the finding does not lessen the well-established dangers of tobacco use.

"Smoking is very harmful to health," says Tavares, "One explanation for our findings might be that there is just more health benefit to gain for smokers by eating healthy because of the enormous detrimental effects of smoking."

Different Diets Share Common Aging Pathways

The scientists also investigated which biological processes might connect the different diets to slower aging. Although each eating pattern was associated with changes at a distinct collection of DNA methylation sites, the underlying biological pathways were surprisingly similar.

More than 70% of the affected pathways were shared across all 10 diets. These processes help regulate essential functions such as cellular structure, communication between cells, and metabolism. They also play important roles in aging and the development of chronic diseases.

The researchers identified some differences that reflected the specific goals of individual diets. For example, the DASH diet was associated with pathways related to heart health, while the MIND diet, which was developed to support brain health, showed distinct associations with pathways involved in memory and cognitive function.

"More than 70 percent of the affected biological pathways were shared across all diets. These involve cell structure, cell signaling and metabolism -- processes central to aging and chronic disease. Therefore, it is understandable that the dietary patterns we examined were all linked with a slowing of biological aging," says Tavares. "A few pathways were diet-specific, and they matched each diet's main objectives: heart-related pathways were unique to the DASH diet, and pathways related to cognition and memory were unique to the so-called MIND diet, which aims to support brain health. You don't need a perfect diet -- that may be the most practical message here. But moving forward, the diet-specific pathways point to future research on targeted dietary interventions. This could be helpful in designing targeted health recommendations."

It’s a testament to One More Level’s chapter design, as each takes you to a new location that builds on the last while leaving its own unique mark on William’s adventure.

I definitely died more than 100 times from start to finish. I wish a good portion of those deaths were fairer – I would have liked to learn from them rather than feel my annoyance mount higher. When Valor Mortis’ various systems coalesce into a French army-uniformed package in the second half, however, it becomes a game that demands a lot from you and returns your efforts in kind with satisfying rewards, or at the very least, the lantern to rest at you’ve been begging to see. One More Level’s first go at the Soulslike falls short of its premise, but it’s a confident step into the genre that leans on the studio’s prior expertise to deliver something tense and novel.

#3717. Massive study of 460,000 people challenges ultra-processed food cancer fears

A massive study involving more than 460,000 Americans is challenging assumptions about ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer. Researchers found that eating more ultra-processed food (UPF) was not associated with a higher overall incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC). However, the findings revealed an important distinction: Some types of ultra-processed foods were linked to lower cancer rates, while others were associated with higher rates.

The research, led by Erikka Loftfield of the National Cancer Institute and published October 15 in the open-access journal PLOS Medicine, suggests that the health effects of ultra-processed foods may depend heavily on what those foods actually contain.

Ultra-processed foods account for more than half of the calories consumed by American adults. These products have attracted increasing scrutiny because of their potential connections to chronic diseases, including colorectal cancer (CRC). Yet the category includes a remarkably diverse assortment of foods, making it difficult to judge their health effects as a single group.

For example, some ultra-processed whole grain and dairy products have been associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer, while processed meats have been linked to a higher risk. This raises an important question about whether the level of processing alone is a useful indicator of a food's potential health effects.

A Massive Study Tracks Cancer Risk for Nearly 19 Years

To investigate these differences, researchers examined health and dietary records from 461,682 Americans who were between 50 and 71 years old when they joined the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study in 1995 and 1996.

At the beginning of the study, participants completed questionnaires describing how frequently they consumed different foods. Researchers used these responses to estimate each person's intake of ultra-processed foods.

Participants were then tracked through 2018, with a median follow-up period of 18.8 years. This allowed the team to examine how dietary patterns were associated with the development of colorectal cancer over nearly two decades.

The results showed no overall association between total ultra-processed food consumption and colorectal cancer incidence. In other words, participants who consumed more ultra-processed foods did not, as a group, experience higher rates of colorectal cancer.

However, a closer examination of individual food categories revealed a much more complicated picture.

Some Ultra-Processed Foods Linked to Lower Cancer Risk

The researchers found that higher consumption of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium from ultra-processed food sources was associated with a lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

Meat from ultra-processed sources showed the opposite pattern, with greater consumption associated with higher cancer incidence.

The team also examined what might happen when ultra-processed foods were replaced with less processed alternatives. Their analysis found that substituting minimally processed foods for 10% of ultra-processed food intake by weight was associated with a 2% lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

These findings suggest that the nutritional characteristics of individual foods may be more informative than simply classifying everything as ultra-processed. Foods placed in the same broad category can have very different associations with cancer risk.

Why the Findings Come With Important Limitations

Despite the study's large size and lengthy follow-up period, the researchers caution that its conclusions may not apply equally to everyone.

Most participants were older, white Americans, meaning the findings may not accurately reflect the experiences of younger adults or more racially and ethnically diverse populations.

Another important limitation involves how dietary information was collected. Participants reported their eating habits only once, at the beginning of the study. Researchers therefore could not account for changes in individual diets over the following decades.

The availability and variety of ultra-processed foods also increased during that time. As a result, the initial dietary assessments may have underestimated participants' actual consumption during the study period.

Because the research was observational, it also cannot establish whether particular foods directly increase or decrease cancer risk.

Rather than treating all ultra-processed foods as equally harmful, the findings support evaluating foods according to their nutritional content and specific health associations.

The results also align with existing dietary recommendations for colorectal cancer prevention, which encourage eating whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium-rich foods while limiting processed meat.

"In this large population of older US adults, we did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence; however, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention such that higher whole grain and dairy but lower meat intakes were associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence. "

Ultimately, the study highlights why the label "ultra-processed" may not tell the whole story. When it comes to colorectal cancer, the kinds of foods people eat appear to matter more than their processing category alone.

It's a solid premise, and while the narrative is not particularly complex, Rose's character arc has standout moments that will stick with me.

For newcomers, Belmont's Curse stands alone perfectly fine, though it is technically a sequel to Castlevania III, which the first two seasons of Netflix's Castlevania are based on. Arguably, it's a better sequel to the show than the game; the art style and character designs have clear anime inspirations, and some voice actors reprise their roles. I enjoyed seeing them in a new light, especially as they passed the torch to Rose.

The best example of this torch-passing is a deck of tarot cards, gifted to Rose by her late mother, Sypha. It's my favorite mechanic in Belmont's Curse. Each is a specially crafted vessel to hold the power of Paris' most powerful figures, friend or foe. As you approach each target, Rose will gather clues from her surroundings to aid in hunting them down or besting them in battle. This investigation ranges from just-okay, linear moments in the early game to cryptic, map-spanning missions in the late game. Each card also unlocks a spell, ranging from fireballs and a magic cross to a blast turning foes to stone. They also come with a list of Works of Mercy: achievements that upgrade your spells upon completion. These range from "Use This Spell 20 Times" to "Kill 44 Skeletons" or "Find All The Rooms In The Clock Tower." It provides a fun reason to use abilities you might not otherwise, which is especially nice when you become overwhelmed by options in the game's closing hours. I was swayed on more than one spell after achievement-hunting for upgrades.

Speaking of combat, the action in Belmont's Curse is magnificent. Rose is light on her feet and only becomes lighter as she gains new abilities, becoming a whirlwind of death by the game's conclusion. Between six weapon categories, a variety of perk-granting relics, and your deck of cards, players are flooded with choice in how to approach encounters. I constantly switched up my loadout, often mid-fight, to adapt to the enemies that appeared. Fights were tough enough to keep me on my toes but never insurmountable, and a forgiving checkpoint system keeps repeated deaths from becoming frustrating. Inventive and intense boss battles against Dracula's coterie of minions are also a major highlight.

After 16 hours, I ended my playthrough with 92 percent completion. I just couldn't resist doubling back to earlier zones to see the new areas I could access with my newfound traversal abilities, and Belmont's Curse never failed to reward me. Between two dozen weapons to find, upgrades to health and mana, and even some huge, optional areas, I'm excited for the satisfaction that awaits me as I dive back in to reach 100 percent.

Belmont's Curse is a strong reminder of why the Castlevania series is so influential, with a rich world, tense combat, and a map chock-full of secrets. While this latest entry certainly pays homage to the Castlevanias of yore, it also breathes fresh life into the franchise, making me excited for its future. Castlevania is back, and between Rose Belmont and Evil Empire, it's in great hands.

Why Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Can Be So Difficult to Treat

Carpal tunnel syndrome develops when the median nerve, which extends from the forearm into the hand, becomes compressed as it passes through a narrow channel in the wrist called the carpal tunnel. Pressure on this nerve can cause pain, tingling, numbness, and muscle weakness, sometimes making everyday activities difficult.

The condition is commonly associated with repetitive hand and wrist movements. Studies suggest that approximately 5% to 10% of people develop carpal tunnel syndrome during their lifetime, and the number of cases is increasing.

One common treatment involves injecting corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and relieve pressure on the nerve. Although these injections can ease symptoms, the benefits often fade, and repeated treatments may be necessary.

"Traditional steroid injection for carpal tunnel syndrome often provides only temporary relief, thus requiring repeats of the procedure," said study co-author Anupama Tandon, M.D., professor in the Department of Radiodiagnosis and Imaging at University College of Medical Sciences and Guru Teg Bahadur Hospital in Delhi, India. "Further, steroids carry risk of localized skin changes, fat thinning and tendon weakening."

A Simple Procedure That Frees a Compressed Nerve

Researchers investigated an alternative technique known as nerve hydrodissection. Rather than relying on medication to reduce inflammation, this approach uses carefully placed fluid to separate the affected nerve from nearby tissues that may be restricting its movement.

Doctors use real-time ultrasound imaging to see the nerve and guide the injection precisely. The fluid helps loosen adhesions, which are areas where tissues stick together, allowing the nerve to move more freely.

Hydrodissection is often performed alongside steroid treatment. However, researchers have had limited evidence about whether the procedure can provide lasting relief without adding steroids.

To investigate, Dr. Tandon joined study lead author Anindita Bose, M.D., assistant professor in the Department of Radiology at Mahatma Gandhi Medical College and Hospital in Jaipur, India, and other researchers in conducting a randomized controlled trial involving 63 patients.

All participants had confirmed carpal tunnel syndrome and had continued experiencing symptoms despite conventional treatment.

The researchers randomly assigned patients to three equally sized groups. One received ultrasound-guided hydrodissection using normal saline, a sterile saltwater solution. Another underwent the same procedure with both saline and corticosteroids. The third received an ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injection without hydrodissection.

Carpal Tunnel Relief Lasted Longer Without Steroids

Researchers tracked each patient's progress using standardized measurements of pain, symptom severity, and hand function. They also performed ultrasound examinations to measure the size of the median nerve, an indicator of swelling.

These evaluations took place four weeks, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks after treatment.

After four weeks, all three groups showed significant improvements. However, differences became increasingly apparent as the study continued.

Patients who underwent hydrodissection continued to experience improvements in their symptoms and hand function. Those who received steroid injections alone initially improved but began experiencing recurring symptoms during later follow-up visits.

"At 12 weeks and 6 months, both hydrodissection groups demonstrated further improvement in clinical symptoms and function, whereas patients who received corticosteroid injection alone experienced a recurrence of the symptoms, suggesting that the initial benefit was short-lived," Dr. Tandon said.

Surprisingly, adding corticosteroids to the hydrodissection fluid did not produce a statistically significant additional long-term benefit. Using ordinary saline alone appeared to work just as well over the six-month follow-up period.

Ultrasound measurements provided additional evidence of the treatment's effectiveness. In patients who underwent hydrodissection, the cross-sectional area of the median nerve decreased by approximately 43% to 45%. Among those treated with corticosteroid injections alone, the reduction was only about 15%.

The greater decrease in nerve size suggested that hydrodissection was more effective at reducing swelling around the compressed nerve.

"Most of our patients had been suffering from carpal tunnel syndrome for months to years and did not get much relief despite trying various treatments like pain medications, splints, lifestyle modification and corticosteroids," Dr. Tandon said. "It came as a pleasant surprise to find that a simple procedure of dissecting the median nerve under ultrasound guidance with normal saline provided them long-term relief."

A 15-Minute Treatment That Could Help Patients Avoid Surgery

Beyond providing sustained symptom relief, hydrodissection offered several practical advantages.

The procedure lasted just 15 minutes on average and was generally well tolerated. Researchers observed no significant complications, either immediately afterward or during the follow-up period.

Patients also reported high satisfaction with their treatment. The procedure was inexpensive, required neither anesthesia nor hospitalization, and allowed participants to return to work within an hour.

These advantages could make hydrodissection an attractive option for people seeking relief without undergoing surgery or receiving repeated steroid injections.

The findings are particularly relevant as carpal tunnel syndrome becomes more common worldwide.

"Overall, the study suggests that ultrasound-guided saline hydrodissection is a safe, minimally invasive treatment that can provide sustained clinical and morphological improvement in patients with refractory carpal tunnel syndrome," Dr. Tandon said. "Thus, hydrodissection merits to be a minimally invasive option for mild to moderate carpal tunnel syndrome and can defer and even alleviate the need for surgery."

Larger Studies Will Test How Long the Benefits Last

Although the findings are encouraging, the researchers emphasize that additional studies are needed before the treatment's long-term effectiveness can be fully established.

The trial included only 63 participants, and patients were monitored for six months. Larger studies involving multiple medical centers and longer observation periods could help determine how consistently hydrodissection works and whether its benefits continue beyond the initial follow-up period.

The research team has already begun another investigation comparing different fluids used during hydrodissection, including saline and dextrose, a form of sugar.

The goal is to identify which approach produces the most durable improvements for people with carpal tunnel syndrome.

"This trial features an extended follow-up period to track long-term patient outcomes and establish the true durability of the treatment," Dr. Tandon said.

The study, "Efficacy of US-guided Hydrodissection for Carpal Tunnel Syndrome: A Prospective Randomized Controlled Trial," was conducted by Anupama Tandon, M.D., Anindita Bose, M.D., Gopesh Mehrotra, M.D., Siddharth Maheshwari, M.D., and Ayush Khandelwal, M.D.

#3703. Why the Treadmill Can Feel So Much Easier Than Running Outside (but It Actually Isn't)

Is the treadmill harder or easier than running outdoors? Survey runners, and you’ll get plenty of different opinions on which feels harder or easier, but the basic physics of running are the same on both. (I promise.) So why do people who are used to treadmills find that they’re suddenly slower when they run outdoors? I’m going to run through the factors that are at play here, and talk about how to adapt if you want to be able to enjoy both.

If you find treadmill running harder, you probably already know the reason: It’s boring. You have nothing to distract you from your own effort and the glowing numbers telling you how little progress you’ve made. This is a problem that we can train our brains to solve for us over time, whether with distractions, mindfulness, or simply being grateful that we’re not outdoors in the bad weather.

Physics-wise, running on a treadmill is pretty much identical to running outdoors in identical conditions. It is not true that the treadmill "moves your feet," as I have seen runners claim. Running is the action of pushing off the ground to move yourself forward relative to the ground. If the “treadmill moves your feet” theory were correct, would we not have to consider the rotating Earth its own sort of treadmill? And thus it would be 2,000 times harder to run west than to run east?

In reality, you have to spend just as much effort to stay in place on a treadmill going 6.5 miles per hour, as you do to move forward at 6.5 miles per hour on flat, steady ground. In other words: in both scenarios, you are asking your muscles to push off with a force that will keep you moving 6.5 mph faster than the ground. Physics is not the reason why the treadmill feels different.

Then there’s the issue of wind resistance. Some runners will say you need to set the treadmill’s incline to 0.5% or 1% to mimic outdoor air resistance. Even on a calm day, your body has to push into the air to keep moving. Adding a small incline to the treadmill is supposed to mimic that extra effort.

But that is only true if you run at a pace of 7:30 per mile (8.0 mph) or faster. Below that, “the difference is so small as to be meaningless,” a scientist who studied the question told Runner’s World. So if you’re jogging at 6 mph, you don’t have to worry about accounting for wind resistance. Now that we understand the physics, let’s talk about why treadmill runs often feel easier than outdoor runs.

Pacing is probably the biggest factor (aside from weather) in why outdoor running feels harder for a person who is used to treadmill running. On the treadmill, you decide on a pace, punch it in to the treadmill, and then you'll keep going at that pace whether you're thinking about it or not.

But outdoors, you don't know when you start what pace you're running. Even if you're relying on a watch to tell you your current pace, it can take a moment to catch up and give you the correct numbers. It's easy to take off a little too fast, especially if you have a good song playing or you're excited to get the workout going (or over with).

So you, the treadmill runner, head off on an outdoor run without a good sense of how fast you’re going, and maybe you find yourself tired out a few minutes in. Meanwhile, outdoor runners learn to develop a sense of pace out of necessity. They know you have to listen to your body, not just look at a number, to know whether you're running at a pace you can sustain for the whole workout. Fortunately, you can learn this skill! Just run outdoors at least some of the time, and pay attention to your breathing and effort level.

If all your treadmill runs are inside of a 68-degree gym, they’ll all feel pretty similar. But the great outdoors is fond of blessing us with heat, wind, humidity, rain, snow, ice, and similar complications.

Heat slows us down a lot, especially if we aren’t used to it. (You do build up some heat adaptation throughout the summer.) Humidity, in combination with heat, makes this even worse. Your body can’t cool itself as well through sweating, so you get hot and stay hot. It’s normal for your pace on a hot day to be anywhere from a few seconds to a few minutes slower each mile.

Heavy winds can also slow you down (when you’re running into the wind) or speed you up (when you’re running with the wind at your back). Ice can make you slow down to watch your footing. Snow can make you work harder as your feet sink into the ground and you have to fight to pull them back up again.

On the other hand, a cool, dry day is better for your performance than the conditions inside a sweaty gym. Perfect running weather is (in my opinion) around 50 degrees, calm, and overcast. Most people will run a lot faster and feel better in those conditions than on a treadmill at room temperature.

If you live in a pancake-flat part of the country, you can skip this section. But many of us live where there are hills. Big ones, little ones, maybe some mountains. On the treadmill, you get to choose what incline to run with. Outdoors, your choices may be limited.

I live in a hilly place, so even my “flat” outdoor routes aren’t entirely flat. A regulation running track is my only truly flat option. Even gently rolling hills can add up over the course of a long run, making you work harder on the uphills without ever fully giving you that speed back on the downhills.

A treadmill only has one surface. Every step meets flat ground. Every step is the same softness or hardness. Outdoors, there’s so much more variation.

Even on a simple city run, you’ll find yourself traversing curbs, slightly tilted sidewalk slabs, cambered edges of roads, pebbles, stray garbage, and occasional patches of grass or dirt. Take your run to the trails and you’ll also hit packed dirt, mud, soft grass, leaf litter, rocks, sticks, logs, little streams you have to hop over, ruts carved by mountain bike wheels—you get the idea. Your feet have to land and push off just a little differently for each of these.

The variety in outdoor running is good for your feet, but it can be fatiguing on the small muscles of your feet and lower legs if you’re not used to it.

It’s OK to do plenty of your training on a treadmill, even if you're training for an outdoor race, and in some situations it may be necessary. The treadmill can let you get your training in when the weather is bad, when you can’t line up child care at your running times, or any number of other reasons.

The important thing is to still run outdoors at least sometimes. If you’re training for a marathon, try to do your long runs outdoors, even if some of your shorter runs and speedwork have to be on the treadmill. Get outside when you can. That way, you’re adapting to the weather, training your feet on different surfaces, and building the muscles and mindset necessary to tackle hills.
