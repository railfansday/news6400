#850. Germany's far-right AfD forges a pro-Russia alliance with the BSW

In Saxony-Anhalt, the AfD and BSW are joining forces to push for negotiations with Moscow. What is driving this rapprochement?

The state parliament of the eastern state of Saxony-Anhalt is calling for an immediate end to all German financial and military aid to Ukraine. The motion was passed during a special parliamentary session with the votes of the far-right populist Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) which combines left-leaning economic positions with right-wing populist positions on immigration. Both parties are urging Saxony-Anhalt's current state premier Sven Schulze, of the center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU), to advocate in the Bundesrat, Germany's upper house representing the federal states, for peace negotiations with Russia.

However, as the federal states have no specific powers in the field of foreign policy, the motion will have no direct impact on Germany's Ukraine policy.

Speaking on behalf of the BSW in the state parliament, legislator Claudia Wittig called on the premier to take action: "It is your responsibility to finally use every available avenue to work for peace."

The proposal drew sharp criticism from all other parties. "Anyone who demands a ceasefire only from the side that has been attacked is not calling for peace, but for capitulation," said CDU lawmaker Stefan Ruland.

The special session took place against the backdrop of ongoing negotiations over the formation of a new state government in Saxony-Anhalt. The AfD emerged as the clear winner of the September 2026 election, securing just under 44% of the vote. However, it fell short of an absolute majority in the state parliament and will need the support of other parties if it is to govern.

The BSW, which narrowly secured representation in the state parliament, is the only party willing to hold formal talks with the AfD on forming a government. Because the AfD is classified by the security authorities in the eastern German state as a "confirmed right-wing extremist" party, all other parties have ruled out any cooperation.

Observers view the joint motion by the AfD and BSW as a sign of closer and potentially enduring cooperation between two parties rooted in very different ideological traditions.

"Their common ground lies in their rejection of the political establishment, their pro-Putin stance, and their criticism of Germany's support for Ukraine," political scientist Armin Pfahl-Traughber of the Federal University of Applied Administrative Sciences said in an interview with DW.

The BSW was founded in 2024 as a breakaway from the socialist Left Party. Its founder, Sahra Wagenknecht, after whom the alliance was originally named, had long been a controversial figure on the German left and advocated far-reaching changes to Germany's economic policy. She was the Left Party's most prominent representative of the so-called "Communist Platform", which maintained close ties to Russia.

Over time, however, her socially conservative positions and criticism of Germany's immigration policy came under increasing scrutiny within the party. These growing tensions ultimately led to her departure from the Left Party and the founding of the BSW.

The AfD, too, has repeatedly expressed sympathy for Putin's system. Shortly after the start of Russia's war against Ukraine, Björn Höcke, the influential AfD state chairman in Thuringia, described Russia as a "champion of a world of free and sovereign states."

The growing rapprochement between the two parties made headlines following a meeting in early October. At the invitation of a far-right publishing house, representatives of the AfD and BSW met in Saxony-Anhalt for what was described as a confidence-building dinner. Also in attendance were controversial figures from both the far right and the dogmatic left. According to their own accounts, participants from both sides explored the possibility of closer cooperation in a cordial atmosphere and in a spirit of mutual trust.

Jürgen Elsässer, the host of the event and editor-in-chief of the right-wing magazine Compact, has for years promoted the idea of a so-called "cross-front" alliance (Querfront) between the far left and the far right in opposition to Germany's political establishment. He has long sought to build bridges between the two camps.

Pfahl-Traughber does not regard the cooperation between the AfD and BSW as a "cross-front" alliance: "I am not even sure that the BSW can still be classified as part of the political left." While the party's founder, Wagenknecht, has her roots on the left, she and her allies position themselves on the economic left but the cultural right, the political scientist argued.

The prominent German historian and author Ilko-Sascha Kowalczuk likewise does not consider the BSW as a left-wing party. In an interview with DW, he argued: "The BSW was never left-wing; it was always reactionary."

Kowalczuk considers both the AfD and the BSW as threats to democracy: "The BSW and the AfD are two sides of the same coin. Both are mouthpieces for the Kremlin, both pursue similar domestic political objectives, and both seek a comparable anti-democratic transformation of the state into an authoritarian system."

Although the recent resolution passed by the Saxony-Anhalt state parliament is unlikely to have any immediate impact on Germany's policy towards Ukraine, Kowalczuk warns of its broader implications: "For now, the AfD and BSW remain isolated, but with positions like these they are appealing to large sections of society, particularly in eastern Germany. The erosion of solidarity with Ukraine will continue and will soon spread to larger segments of both the SPD and the CDU."

#849. How Brazil’s election result could shape women’s football in the country

Female players including Carlinha and Tayla Carolina have spoken out against the far right, who they fear will harm the women’s game as well as wider society

The day before the first round of Brazilian elections, the Estádio do Morumbi hosted the second leg of the Brazilian Women’s Championship final, in which São Paulo drew 1-1 with Corinthians to win 3-2 on aggregate and secure the Brasileirão Women’s title.

It had been an incredible final but after the game the goalkeeper Carlinha used an interview to appeal to her fans: “Never again Bolsonaro in power,” she shouted to a record-breaking TV audience of 23 million people.

Having lost her grandmother during the Covid-19 pandemic, Carlinha recalls the challenges faced by hundreds of thousands of Brazilian families when the far-right president Jair Bolsonaro spread fake information about the virus while ignoring public appeals for the government to buy and distribute vaccinations. More than 700,000 Brazilians died during the pandemic. Now his son, Flávio, is standing against the 80-year-old incumbent leftwing president, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva.

“I didn’t just think of [my grandmother] when I gave that interview, I think about her and miss her every single day,” Carlinha tells Moving the Goalposts. “I felt relieved after talking about my personal opinion in such an important moment of Brazilian democracy. I’m a journalism student and I know the impact if the right wins the elections.”

Carlinha is one of several female football players who have used social media and their visibility to speak up before and during the presidential election, which will come to an end on Sunday with a second-round vote. The father of the Bolsonaro dynasty is currently under “humanitarian house arrest”, serving a 27-year sentence for a coup attempt.

Carlinha’s club and international teammate, Tayla Carolina, went viral the weekend after discussing her family’s background and how public policies implemented during Lula’s government were important for her mother and grandmother, who have worked as cleaners throughout their lives and have since passed. “I’m from a very humble family, I’m a Black woman. I’ve studied in public schools and used the national healthcare system … my mum, dona Dora, worked for over a decade for someone who never paid her labour rights … and she worked until she couldn’t walk any more due to the cancer that took her from us. She never saw me play because she never had the time, she never rested,” she wrote. Her story resonates with many Brazilians who fought for shorter working weeks, approved earlier this week in congress.

Tayla elaborates her point: “I’m from a country where most of the people are poor but the country is not seen as poor. It shows there are huge inequalities, which come from exploitation. Years have passed and there’s still a lot of inequality and I come from it … as a public figure, and someone who has lived similar lives to the majority of Brazilians, I feel the responsibility to speak up.”

The player organisation Fifpro has just released a new study showing that nearly half of female football players think of early retirement owing to the psychological and financial pressures of the women’s game (800 professional players took part). With the Women’s World Cup being staged in Brazil next year both Carlinha and Tayla feel the election will have a huge impact on the game going forward.

“There is one side that makes it quite clear how they feel about women’s rights,” says Carlinha, referring to, among other things, politicians such as Eduarda Campopiano, one of the most voted members of the São Paulo Assembly, who calls herself an “enemy of feminists” and has said she does not believe in femicide – that women and girls can be killed because they are women. Tayla also feels the need for policies to support the women’s game at a crucial moment. “We still need government incentives, so it may not be immediate, but the game will be affected, positively or negatively, depending on how the elections go.”

Lula approved the World Cup law in June 2026, which regulates many aspects of the tournament, including how ministries, the Brazilian FA (CBF), state federations and clubs will commit to developing a legacy and supporting the women’s game in the country. The legacy plan, divided into four strands, proposes supporting access and permanence of girls and women in football, developing and professionalising the game, fighting gender- and racial-based discrimination, and enhancing the economic and cultural aspects of women’s football in Brazil. The commitment to the tournament is stated in Lula’s government plan. Bolsonaro’s proposals for women’s sport do not mention the World Cup; instead, he proposes “ensuring that women’s categories, from youth to elite, are played by athletes of the female sex, protecting women and tournaments from ideological agendas”.

Brazilian football, men’s and women’s, has been heavily politicised for decades, and the rise of social media has led to an increase in players publicly supporting their political candidates. In the men’s game, the far right is often championed by the Santos and Brazil forward Neymar, who has showed support for the Bolsonaro family on many occasions. Tottenham’s Richarlison, meanwhile, is positioned on the left and this week accused Flávio Bolsonaro of “taking possession” of an island he says he bought a few years back.

The country’s female players do not have the same number of followers on social media and cannot influence a big audience in the same way as the men. However, it is true that the result of the election is likely to have more of an impact on the women’s game than on the men’s.

Kely Nascimento, the daughter of late football legend Pelé and founder and executive director of the Nascimento Foundation, says: “Female players, female athletes, they don’t have the protection of wealth. What feeds the brilliance of Brazilian football are the players, and they mostly come from communities, favelas and other places that are going to be hugely affected by these elections. Women are more affected by all this, right?”

Carlinha, who played in one of the two recent friendlies against Argentina, says playing for the national team at home is “one of the best feelings ever”. At both stadiums, in Porto Alegre and Recife, the crowd predominantly chanted in support of Lula, which could be heard over the TV commentary during the broadcast.

The politicisation of women’s football is not new, as Brazilian women’s football historian and author Aira Bonfim explains. “This is a hundred-year-old history, because we have episodes of women playing football in Brazil since 1915, and the women’s game reveals different perspectives of gender inequalities, racial inequalities, class, territory, which will affect the progress of the women’s game,” she says. “By knowing women’s football history, we can look at episodes which are equally as political, such as the ban on the women’s game [between 1941 and 1979], the persecution of women, and lies that were told so that female players would give up on it.”

One of the pieces of history that she has uncovered in her research became a short film, which debuted earlier this month at the Rio Film Festival, The House of Dona Carlota, produced by Mymama Entertainment and directed by Lili Fialho and Bruna Linzmeyer, which tells the story of a woman who was arrested and persecuted because she led a women’s football team, Primavera Atlético Clube.

It shows women’s football as a space of socialisation, sport and resistance to the Estado Novo dictatorship in the late 1930s and early 1940s. The women’s game was banned in Brazil for decades, and the World Cup in 2027 is where many hope its renewed development will be consolidated.

“What’s at risk [in this election] is a waste of all of the human investments in building social legacy plans. There is also the risk of dismantling all political communication that has been done to support [the World Cup] through the ministries, cities and state governments. If you elect someone who did not make any commitments towards it, who did not commit to the World Cup or what it represents to women, it can be a huge loss,” Bonfim says. “Just the fact that during Jair Bolsonaro’s government he removed the status of the ministry of sports reveals how he feels about the sector.”

The hosting of a Women’s World Cup is key in developing public policies and attracting investment and support. As Nascimento says: “There are girls all over Brazil for whom football is not just their dream, but it’s also how they get out of very awful situations. And there are amazing people doing God’s work all over Brazil just to get girls to play, to be educated in another way … for some reason, Brazil just makes football magic, and that’s not gender-specific. And there’s an enormous Brazil that is being ignored and taken advantage of.”

There are many reasons for the international community to look closer at the second round of Brazil’s elections on Sunday, from how it can affect climate change, foreign policy and financial markets to many other issues. Women’s football, and all it represents, is one of them.

#843. Scientists who discovered a third form of magnetism win major physics prize

Scientists have uncovered a previously unknown form of magnetism, overturning an understanding of magnetic behavior that had stood for more than a century. The discovery, known as altermagnetism, has revealed a third fundamental category of collinear magnetism and opened new possibilities for quantum materials, advanced electronics, and future computing technologies.

Three physicists responsible for the breakthrough have been named recipients of the 2026 Europhysics Prize, one of Europe's most prestigious honors in condensed matter physics. The European Physical Society (EPS) Condensed Matter Division selected Professor Jairo Sinova of Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz (JGU), Dr. Libor Šmejkal, and Professor Tomas Jungwirth for their work establishing altermagnetism as a distinct form of magnetic order alongside ferromagnetism and antiferromagnetism.

"This award recognizes a fundamental discovery that challenged one of the most established paradigms in condensed matter physics," said Professor Jairo Sinova, Director of the Spin Phenomena Interdisciplinary Center (SPICE) at Mainz University. "Discovering that an entirely new magnetic phase had remained hidden for more than one hundred years demonstrates that even the most mature scientific fields can still hold fundamental surprises."

The breakthrough emerged from years of collaboration between Sinova's research group at Mainz University and Jungwirth's team at the Institute of Physics of the Czech Academy of Sciences in Prague. Šmejkal, who also maintained an affiliation in Prague, spent eight years at Mainz between 2016 and 2024, first pursuing doctoral research and later working as a postdoctoral scientist in Sinova's group.

During that period, Šmejkal played a leading role in developing the theoretical framework behind altermagnetism. By combining advanced mathematical approaches to symmetry with spintronics, which studies how electron spin can be used in electronic devices, the researchers identified a previously unrecognized pattern of magnetic behavior.

Their predictions soon attracted experimental teams around the world. Measurements of electronic properties and electrical transport in multiple materials provided evidence supporting the new magnetic classification. Altermagnetism has since grown into one of the most active areas of condensed matter physics, with research efforts expanding across Europe, North America, and Asia.

A Third Form of Magnetism Rewrites Physics

For more than 100 years, physicists generally divided collinear magnets into two fundamental categories: ferromagnets and antiferromagnets.

Ferromagnets have an overall magnetic alignment that produces a net magnetization. These materials are essential to many magnetic memory technologies used today. In conventional antiferromagnets, opposing magnetic moments cancel one another, leaving no overall magnetization and producing electronic behavior very different from that of ferromagnets.

Altermagnetism introduces a surprising third possibility.

Like antiferromagnets, altermagnets have magnetic moments that cancel out, resulting in no net magnetization. However, their electrons can exhibit properties previously associated with ferromagnets.

This unusual combination allows altermagnets to support electrical currents with strongly polarized electron spins while also displaying extremely rapid magnetic dynamics. Those characteristics could make them valuable for a new generation of spintronic devices, potentially advancing the speed and capabilities of future information technologies.

The significance extends well beyond electronics. Altermagnetism represents a previously unrecognized symmetry class of matter and has revealed unexpected connections to several major areas of modern physics, including topological phenomena, unconventional superconductivity, and quantum materials with strong interactions between electrons.

The path toward this discovery began with theoretical investigations into unusual ways that electricity moves through magnetic materials. Among the early advances was the prediction of the crystal anomalous Hall effect, a phenomenon involving electrical transport in certain crystal structures.

Initially, these unexpected behaviors might have appeared to be unusual features of individual materials. However, the researchers eventually recognized that they were connected by a broader principle involving symmetries of electron spins.

Rather than representing isolated exceptions to established magnetic behavior, these materials belonged to an entirely different magnetic category.

A major milestone came in 2022, when the team published a comprehensive symmetry classification of altermagnetism and identified hundreds of materials that could potentially exhibit it.

Experimental physicists subsequently tested these predictions using several complementary methods. These included angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES), which reveals how electrons behave inside materials, along with measurements of electrical transport.

The results confirmed altermagnetic behavior in several materials, helping establish the new classification experimentally.

Interest in the discovery has expanded rapidly. Hundreds of scientific papers have appeared within just a few years, and altermagnetism has become a prominent subject at international physics conferences. The growing research community is now investigating both its fundamental properties and its potential technological applications.

Discovery Earns Prestigious 2026 Europhysics Prize

The Europhysics Prize recognizes major advances in condensed matter physics, a field concerned with the physical properties and behavior of materials. Established in 1975, the award honors discoveries that have significantly advanced scientific understanding, with a substantial share of the recognized research conducted in Europe.

The 2026 prize recognizes the theoretical and experimental advances that established altermagnetism as a new fundamental form of collinear magnetic order.

The award ceremony was scheduled for September 2026 at the 32nd General Conference of the European Physical Society Condensed Matter Division (CMD32) in Graz, Austria.

Professor Jairo Sinova is a physics professor at Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz in Germany and director of the university's Spin Phenomena Interdisciplinary Center. His research investigates magnetism, spintronics, and quantum materials.

Dr. Libor Šmejkal conducted much of the crucial theoretical work that led to the identification of altermagnetism while working at Mainz University from 2016 to 2024. His current affiliations include the Max Planck Institute for the Physics of Complex Systems, the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Physics of Solids, and the Institute of Physics of the Czech Academy of Sciences.

Professor Tomas Jungwirth is affiliated with the Institute of Physics of the Czech Academy of Sciences and the University of Nottingham. He has collaborated extensively with Sinova's Mainz research team and played a central role in establishing the theoretical foundations of altermagnetism.

#831. Magic mushroom ingredient takes the fight out of aggressive fish

Psilocybin, the psychedelic substance found in more than 200 species of mushrooms, appears to have an unexpected effect on aggressive fish. Most mushrooms containing this compound belong to the gilled mushroom genus Psilocybe. In mammals, psilocybin interacts with serotonin receptors in the brain, influencing emotions and behaviors such as mood, appetite, and aggression. However, scientists still know relatively little about how the substance affects social interactions among animals.

To investigate this question, researchers in Canada examined the effects of psilocybin on the amphibious mangrove rivulus fish (Kryptolebias marmoratus). Their findings, published in Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience, suggest that even a brief exposure to a low dose can significantly reduce aggressive behavior without disrupting normal social interactions.

"We show that an acute, low dose of psilocybin significantly reduces activity and aggressive attack behavior during social interactions in adult mangrove rivulus fish, a species that is naturally highly aggressive," said first author Dayna Forsyth, a research associate and former MSc student at Acadia University in Nova Scotia.

"These findings provide the first evidence that psilocybin can selectively reduce escalated aggression in a vertebrate model without suppressing social interaction," added senior author Dr. Suzie Currie, a biologist at The University of British Columbia.

Why Scientists Studied Aggressive Mangrove Rivulus Fish

Mangrove rivulus fish are naturally combative, particularly when they encounter another member of their species. Their aggressive responses are easy to recognize and measure, allowing researchers to detect even small changes in behavior.

These fish also have an unusual reproductive ability. They can fertilize themselves, producing offspring that are genetically identical. This makes them especially useful for experiments because scientists can minimize genetic variation and more confidently attribute behavioral differences to psilocybin rather than inherited traits.

For the experiment, the researchers worked with three genetically distinct lines of fish bred in the laboratory. One group received psilocybin, another provided the fish used to provoke social interactions, and the third helped researchers measure how much psilocybin entered and accumulated in the animals' bodies.

The researchers first established a baseline for each fish's behavior. They placed a fish into a tank already occupied by another fish, with the two separated by a fiberglass mesh barrier and an opaque cover. The mesh prevented physical contact while allowing the fish to see and smell each other once the cover was removed.

After allowing five minutes for the fish to adjust to their shared environment, the team removed the opaque cover and recorded their interactions.

The following day, 24 hours after the initial observation, the researchers placed the same fish in water containing dissolved psilocybin. Following 20 minutes of exposure, they returned it to the tank with the same fish it had encountered previously. The opaque cover was then removed again so the researchers could observe any changes in behavior.

Magic Mushroom Compound Reduces Aggressive Attacks

The results revealed a striking change. Fish exposed to psilocybin moved around less and displayed fewer bursts of aggressive swimming than fish that had not received the substance.

To assess these changes, the scientists measured overall activity (time spent moving) and several signs of aggression (including swimming bursts). These rapid bursts are particularly important because they indicate that an encounter is escalating toward an attack, even when the fish cannot make physical contact.

"Swimming bursts are high-energy attack behaviors that represent an escalation of aggression towards the stimulus fish without making physical contact," explained Currie. "Other types of aggressive behaviors, like head-on displays, are more about communication and social assessment and require very little energy."

Interestingly, psilocybin did not suppress every form of aggressive or social behavior. While the fish became less likely to engage in intense, energy-demanding attacks, their less physically demanding social displays remained largely unchanged.

"Psilocybin's calming effect appears to selectively reduce energetically costly, escalated behaviors while lower-energy social display behaviors remained largely unchanged," said Forsyth. "This suggests that this compound can selectively dampen escalated social conflict rather than shutting down behavior altogether."

The compound also reduced general activity. When placed alongside another member of their species, fish treated with psilocybin spent less time swimming than untreated controls.

Together, these findings suggest that psilocybin may influence specific aspects of aggressive behavior rather than simply making animals less responsive to their surroundings.

What the Findings Could Mean for Psychedelic Research

The researchers believe animal studies like this one could eventually help scientists better understand how psychedelic compounds influence behavior and identify possible directions for therapeutic research.

By examining which social responses are most sensitive to psilocybin, researchers may be able to uncover biological mechanisms that are difficult to investigate directly in people. Such insights could ultimately inform studies of potential clinical applications.

However, the team emphasized that the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a treatment for aggression in humans. The experiment involved fish, did not evaluate any clinical intervention, and cannot establish whether similar effects would occur in people.

There are also important questions about how long the calming effect lasts. The researchers examined only single doses and short exposure periods. They did not investigate repeated treatments, long-term behavioral changes, or whether the fish might adapt to the substance over time.

Additional experiments will be necessary to determine whether psilocybin's ability to reduce aggressive attacks can persist beyond the brief period examined in this study.

"Future studies can build on this work to explore how psilocybin alters neural signaling, which serotonin pathways are involved, and why some aspects of social behavior are affected while others are not," concluded Currie. "These are questions that are difficult or impossible to answer directly in humans."

#821. Modern neuroscience is rediscovering a surprising idea freud had 130 years ago

What if the human brain works in ways that Sigmund Freud began describing more than 130 years ago? A new scientific analysis suggests that some of his ideas about the mind share surprising similarities with one of the most influential theories in modern neuroscience.

Published in the scientific journal Entropy, the paper explores connections between Freud's original theories, later developments in psychoanalysis, and a modern approach to brain research known as the prediction paradigm.

This approach describes the brain as a prediction machine. Rather than simply responding to information from the outside world, the brain is constantly anticipating what will happen next. It then compares those expectations with incoming information from the senses and works to reduce any differences between what it predicted and what actually occurred.

Scientists believe this ongoing process plays a central role in how people perceive their surroundings, make decisions, behave, and regulate emotions.

Researchers Erik Stänicke, Bendik Hovet, and Line Indrevoll Stänicke, together with colleagues, argue that this modern understanding of brain function has important parallels with ideas that psychoanalysts have been developing for more than a century.

"For over 130 years, psychoanalysis has developed psychological theories about how predictions take place at a subjective level, which cognitive neuropsychology is now studying at a physiological level."

How Freud's Ideas Connect to the Predictive Brain

Although neuroscience and psychoanalysis approach the mind differently, the researchers suggest that both may be describing related processes.

Neuroscience examines the physical mechanisms behind thought and behavior, often using mathematical models to explain how the brain processes information. Psychoanalysis, which grew out of Freud's work, focuses more on unconscious mental processes, emotional experiences, and the ways past relationships influence present behavior.

In other words, one field investigates what happens inside the brain, while the other explores how those processes are experienced by the individual.

A particularly interesting connection involves the psychoanalytic concept of projection and the neuroscientific concept of prediction.

In psychoanalysis, projection involves attributing feelings, intentions, or qualities to someone else. The researchers suggest that this resembles how the brain uses previously learned expectations to interpret new experiences.

"When we attribute qualities, intentions or feelings to other people, our brain shapes our experience of the world in line with established expectations," says Stänicke.

For instance, someone who has repeatedly experienced rejection may become especially sensitive to signs that other people are rejecting them. Even an ambiguous remark or facial expression might be interpreted negatively because past experiences have shaped what the person expects.

Over time, these expectations can influence how someone approaches unfamiliar situations and forms new relationships.

"This corresponds to the neuroscientific distinction between changing one's own predictions, perceptual inference, and the attempt to make the world conform to them, namely active inference."

Perceptual inference refers to the way the brain interprets sensory information and updates its expectations in response. Active inference, by contrast, involves taking actions that help bring experiences into line with predictions or reduce uncertainty.

Together, these concepts help explain why people do not always experience the world as it objectively is. Their expectations can influence what they notice, how they interpret events, and how they respond.

Why the Brain Holds On to Negative Expectations

The researchers believe the similarities between Freud's ideas and modern neuroscience could offer valuable insights into mental health disorders, particularly those involving deeply established patterns of thinking and behavior.

Both approaches portray the mind as constantly seeking stability and predictability. This tendency is related to homeostasis, the process through which a system maintains a relatively stable internal state.

In the predictive brain model, reducing uncertainty is an important part of maintaining that stability. The brain relies on familiar expectations to make the world easier to understand, even when those expectations are inaccurate or no longer helpful.

"Psychoanalysts refer to the tendency in the mind to recreate familiar relational patterns, even when these are poorly adapted," says Stänicke.

This could help explain why people sometimes repeat harmful patterns in relationships or continue interpreting situations negatively despite evidence that challenges their assumptions.

"Rigid and persistent symptoms, such as paranoid ideas or an internalized critical voice, may be stable but not very flexible prediction models," says Stänicke.

"For example, there may be people who automatically expect criticism, rejection or hostility from others, and therefore interpret situations through this filter despite the fact that reality does not warrant it."

Such expectations can become deeply entrenched because they make experiences feel familiar and predictable. Even when the resulting interpretation is distorted, maintaining an established mental model may feel less uncertain than changing it.

According to Stänicke, this offers a possible explanation for why some psychological symptoms can persist for years and why changing longstanding patterns of thought and behavior often requires considerable time.

The connection is particularly interesting because it suggests that some mental health difficulties may involve more than consciously held beliefs. They may also reflect deeply learned expectations that automatically shape emotional responses and interactions with others.

How Psychotherapy Could Help Change Deeply Rooted Patterns

The researchers also highlight an important distinction between what people consciously believe and what they have learned through experience.

"In addition, both models give us insight into how parts of our expectations of the outside world are not only anchored cognitively, but in procedural memory that is expressed in relational ways of being," he says.

Procedural memory is a type of long-term memory involved in learned habits and skills. It allows people to perform familiar actions without consciously thinking through every step.

In the context of relationships, the researchers suggest that a similar form of learning may help explain why certain emotional reactions and patterns of interaction become automatic.

Someone might intellectually understand that other people are not necessarily hostile or critical, for example, yet still instinctively respond as though rejection is inevitable.

These expectations are not simply thoughts that can be changed through reasoning. They can also be embedded in habitual ways of reacting to people and navigating relationships.

This distinction may help explain why psychotherapy sometimes needs to address personal relationships and emotional experiences rather than focusing only on conscious thoughts.

"Therefore, psychotherapy sometimes has to work relationally. For example, new experiences in the relationship between therapist and patient can gradually help to change entrenched relational patterns."

Through repeated experiences that challenge familiar expectations, patients may gradually develop different ways of interpreting situations and relating to other people.

Bringing Freud's Psychoanalysis and Neuroscience Together

The researchers argue that connecting psychoanalysis with modern predictive brain theory could benefit both fields.

Neuroscience may provide a biological framework for understanding processes that psychoanalysts have long described through subjective experience. At the same time, psychoanalytic theories may offer more detailed explanations of how expectations are experienced emotionally, interpreted personally, and expressed through relationships.

The proposed similarities do not establish that Freud's broader theories were correct. Instead, they suggest that two very different traditions of studying the mind may offer complementary ways of understanding some of the same psychological processes.

By examining both the brain's physical mechanisms and the individual's inner experience, researchers hope to develop a more complete understanding of human behavior and mental health.

"Bringing these two fields together can open up for a more holistic psychology, in which both neurological mechanisms and subjective experience are included. In this way, we can understand subjectivity in a more scientific manner."

#808. "Mind-blowing" Alzheimer’s breakthrough: Two hours of sleep restored without clearing brain plaques

Scientists have discovered an unexpected reason why Alzheimer's disease disrupts sleep, and their findings suggest that the problem may be reversible. In experiments involving mice with Alzheimer's-like brain changes, researchers restored more than two hours of sleep per day without removing the amyloid plaques associated with the disease.

The surprising discovery points to an overlooked culprit: the brain's own immune cells. Rather than simply protecting the brain from damage, these cells can trigger inflammation that interferes with healthy sleep.

Researchers at the University of Kentucky found that temporarily eliminating most of these immune cells allowed the mice to sleep longer and spend more time in the deep, restorative stages of sleep. The results suggest a potential new approach to treating one of the most disruptive symptoms of Alzheimer's disease.

The findings, published in the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia, were reported by a team led by Shannon L. Macauley, Ph.D., an associate professor of physiology at the University of Kentucky College of Medicine, and first author Nicholas J. Constantino, Ph.D., a recent doctoral graduate.

Macauley described the discovery as "paradigm-shifting" because it challenges the prevailing understanding of why people with Alzheimer's often experience severe sleep problems.

How the Brain's Immune System Can Disrupt Sleep

To understand what the researchers uncovered, imagine a small fire breaking out in a kitchen. Instead of containing the flames, the building's sprinkler system activates everywhere, flooding the entire house and causing widespread damage.

Something similar may happen in the brain during Alzheimer's disease.

Amyloid plaques are deposits of sticky protein fragments that accumulate between nerve cells. They are one of the characteristic features of Alzheimer's and have long been investigated for their role in the disease.

The brain also contains specialized immune cells known as microglia. These cells normally protect the nervous system by removing debris, responding to injuries, and helping maintain a healthy environment for neurons.

However, when microglia detect amyloid plaques, their defensive response can become excessive. Instead of simply addressing the threat, they release inflammatory signals that may interfere with normal brain activity.

Scientists have traditionally linked sleep problems in Alzheimer's primarily to damaged neurons or the accumulation of amyloid plaques. The new findings suggest that the immune system's reaction to those plaques plays a major role.

"Basically, we showed that it is not the plaques themselves, or solely dysfunctional neurons, that cause sleep loss but actually microglia," said Macauley. "Microglia are immune cells that, when they respond to plaques, kick off this elaborate cascade of inflammation, as if the microglia are partying all night, and keeping the brain awake."

In other words, the brain's response to Alzheimer's pathology may create additional problems beyond those caused by the plaques themselves. The researchers compared this widespread reaction to a "whole house response," in which a defense mechanism ends up causing more disruption than expected.

Tracking Alzheimer's Sleep Changes Inside the Brain

To investigate the connection between microglia and sleep, the scientists studied two groups of mice. One group was genetically predisposed to develop amyloid plaques, while the other consisted of normal "wild-type" mice that experienced typical aging.

The researchers examined the animals at two ages: six months, when plaques first appeared in the genetically modified mice, and 18 months, when the disease model showed more advanced pathology.

Comparing these groups helped the scientists distinguish changes associated with Alzheimer's pathology from those that naturally occur as the brain ages.

The team used several advanced technologies to measure sleep and examine what was happening inside the brain.

Each mouse was fitted with a small device that recorded electroencephalography (EEG) and electromyography (EMG).

EEG measures the brain's electrical activity, including the patterns and rhythms produced when networks of neurons communicate. These electrical signals change depending on whether the brain is awake, dreaming or experiencing deep sleep.

EMG measures electrical activity in muscles. Combining the two techniques allowed researchers to identify different sleep stages and determine how long the animals remained in each one.

The scientists also wanted to visualize the relationship between amyloid plaques and immune cells throughout the brain.

For this, they used light-sheet microscopy, an imaging technique that makes biological tissue transparent and illuminates it with a thin layer of laser light. The resulting images can be assembled into detailed three-dimensional maps, allowing scientists to examine structures that would otherwise be difficult to see.

This approach revealed the distribution of plaques and microglia across the brain, helping researchers understand where immune cells were responding to Alzheimer's pathology.

Scientists Temporarily Remove 87% of Brain Immune Cells

To determine whether microglia were actually responsible for disrupted sleep, the team conducted an experiment designed to temporarily eliminate most of these cells.

The researchers administered a drug called Pexidartinib (PLX3397), which was originally developed for cancer research. It blocks a cellular signal that microglia depend on for survival.

After the mice received the medication in their food for 14 days, approximately 87% of their microglia had been depleted.

This allowed the scientists to test whether sleep would improve when the cells driving the inflammatory response were largely absent.

The researchers also performed a detailed analysis of the animals' brain activity using a mathematical method called Fitting Oscillations and One Over Frequency (FOOOF).

This technique separates electrical signals into periodic activity (the rhythmic waves we usually think of as brain waves) and aperiodic activity (the background electrical noise).

By examining these different components, the team could identify changes in how the brain was functioning, including whether it remained unusually active during periods when it should have been resting.

The process is somewhat like monitoring a car engine to determine whether it continues running at high speed even when the vehicle is supposed to be idle.

A Surprising Discovery About Alzheimer's Brain Plaques

Macauley called the results "mind-blowing and unexpected."

One of the most surprising findings was that sleep problems did not continue worsening as amyloid plaques accumulated.

The researchers initially expected that increasing plaque buildup would lead to progressively more severe sleep disturbances. Instead, the mice developed substantial sleep problems relatively early, and those problems remained largely unchanged even as plaque levels increased dramatically.

"I expected that as plaque burden became more severe, sleep disruption would also worsen," said Constantino. "The disruptions in sleep and cortical EEG activity that occur at 6 months, when plaques first emerge, did not worsen by 18 months, despite more than double the amount of plaque burden."

The team described this pattern as a ceiling effect. Essentially, sleep disruption appeared to reach a certain level early in the disease process rather than increasing in direct proportion to the amount of amyloid in the brain.

This suggests that the initial inflammatory response triggered by plaques may be particularly important. Once microglia become activated and disrupt sleep, additional plaque accumulation may not necessarily produce an equivalent increase in sleep loss.

The discovery offers an important clue as to why sleep disturbances can emerge relatively early in Alzheimer's disease.

Alzheimer's Disrupts a Different Type of Sleep Than Normal Aging

The study also revealed a notable difference between sleep changes caused by normal aging and those associated with Alzheimer's pathology.

Sleep is not a single, uniform state. Throughout the night, the brain moves through several stages that perform different functions.

Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is strongly associated with vivid dreaming and plays an important role in processing memories. The researchers found that normal aging primarily reduced this type of sleep.

Alzheimer's pathology, however, had a different effect. It selectively disrupted non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, which includes the deepest and most physically restorative stages of sleep.

During NREM sleep, the brain performs essential maintenance functions that support learning, memory, and recovery. Deep sleep is also associated with processes that help clear metabolic waste products from the brain.

"That restorative sleep is super important for physical repair, learning and memory and washing out the toxins of the day," Macauley said.

Losing this important stage of sleep could have consequences beyond daytime fatigue.

Because healthy sleep helps maintain the brain's internal environment, disruptions may interfere with processes that normally remove potentially harmful waste. Researchers suspect this could contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle in which Alzheimer's pathology disturbs sleep, and inadequate sleep may further aggravate the disease.

Breaking that cycle could therefore become an important treatment strategy.

More Than Two Hours of Sleep Restored Without Removing Plaques

The most striking result emerged after the researchers temporarily removed most of the microglia from the brains of mice with Alzheimer's-like pathology.

The animals gained more than two hours of sleep per day.

They also experienced longer periods of restorative NREM sleep, giving them more opportunities to transition naturally into REM sleep, which is important for memory formation.

Even more surprisingly, these improvements occurred without any reduction in amyloid plaque levels.

The plaques were still present, but the mice were sleeping substantially better.

This finding provides evidence that the inflammatory response driven by microglia contributes directly to sleep disruption and that suppressing this response can reverse at least some of the associated sleep problems.

It also suggests that researchers may not need to eliminate amyloid plaques to improve sleep in Alzheimer's disease.

Although these findings are promising, the experiments were conducted in mice, and the same benefits have not yet been demonstrated in people. Temporarily removing most of the brain's immune cells is also not an established or practical treatment for Alzheimer's patients.

The researchers are now interested in whether the beneficial effects can be reproduced by adjusting microglial activity rather than eliminating the cells themselves.

If such an approach eventually proves successful in humans, it could provide a way to improve sleep and potentially reduce some of the additional stress that poor sleep places on the brain.

The work was conducted in Macauley's laboratory in the Department of Physiology at the University of Kentucky's Sanders-Brown Center on Aging.

Macauley credits the achievement to a "beautiful partnership" with students and researchers who were willing to challenge established ideas about Alzheimer's disease.

"I love people who take initiative, find their passion, are curious, and keep pushing to find an answer," Macauley said.

She encourages members of her laboratory to be "calculated risk-takers," emphasizing that meaningful discoveries often require scientists to investigate possibilities that may initially seem unlikely.

A quote from hockey legend Wayne Gretzky hangs in her office: "You miss 100% of the shots you don't take."

Constantino, who recently completed his doctorate at the university, said the laboratory's collaborative environment encouraged him to pursue questions that crossed traditional scientific disciplines.

"Dr. Macauley has also taught me to embrace uncertainty and failure as part of the scientific process," Constantino said. "Some of the most interesting studies I have been a part of emerged because our original hypothesis was wrong."

Macauley reinforces this mindset with a simple instruction to her team: "Follow the data, ask better questions, and figure out what is actually happening."

That approach helped the researchers move beyond the traditional emphasis on neurons and amyloid plaques to examine the broader role of the brain's immune system.

Could Existing Medications Help Restore Sleep in Alzheimer's?

The research team is now exploring ways to translate these findings into treatments and diagnostic tools that could eventually benefit people with Alzheimer's disease.

One promising direction involves calming overactive microglia without removing them.

Because microglia perform essential protective functions, eliminating them is not an ideal long-term strategy. Instead, the researchers hope to modify how these immune cells respond to amyloid plaques and regulate inflammation.

The laboratory is investigating existing medications, including the diabetes drug Metformin and the anti-seizure medication Stiripentol.

These drugs are being studied for their potential to alter how microglia produce and use energy, a process known as cellular metabolism. Changing that energy use could potentially reduce excessive immune activation and its harmful effects on sleep.

The hope is to prevent the brain's inflammatory response from becoming so intense that it disrupts normal sleep activity.

Researchers are particularly interested in whether intervention during the earlier stages of Alzheimer's could improve sleep and quality of life before substantial memory loss develops.

"If we can target that process, it might help with quality of life, attention, cognition and confusion," Macauley said.

The proposed drug strategies remain experimental for this purpose, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether they can safely produce similar benefits in humans.

A Simpler Way to Detect Early Alzheimer's Changes

Beyond identifying a potential treatment target, the study uncovered electrical activity patterns that may help distinguish Alzheimer's pathology from normal brain aging.

The researchers believe these patterns could eventually make EEG a useful tool for detecting and monitoring changes associated with Alzheimer's disease.

Unlike more expensive imaging procedures, EEG records electrical signals through electrodes placed on the scalp. It is relatively affordable, does not require surgery, and can be repeated to follow changes over time.

The team envisions EEG becoming a "readily accessible, affordable and longitudinal biomarker of Alzheimer's disease."

A biomarker is a measurable biological sign that provides information about a person's health or a particular disease. A longitudinal biomarker can be tracked repeatedly to monitor how a condition develops or changes.

"Portable EEG systems could allow us to monitor people in their home environments and potentially screen for changes associated with an Alzheimer's disease, without the initial need for expensive or invasive tests," Macauley said.

If future human studies confirm the usefulness of these electrical signatures, portable EEG devices might eventually help doctors identify people who need additional testing.

This could be particularly valuable in rural communities across Kentucky, where patients sometimes must travel long distances to reach specialized medical centers.

Instead of requiring every potentially at-risk patient to undergo advanced testing immediately, local clinics might use simpler monitoring tools to identify individuals who would benefit from further evaluation.

Although this application still requires validation, it offers a possible path toward more accessible Alzheimer's screening and monitoring.

Ultimately, the study suggests that treating Alzheimer's may require looking beyond the plaques themselves and examining how the brain responds to them.

By identifying the immune cells responsible for much of the sleep disruption in their mouse models, the University of Kentucky researchers have uncovered a potential way to restore an essential brain function without first removing the underlying amyloid deposits.

The next challenge is determining whether calming these cells can deliver similar benefits in people, potentially improving sleep, cognition and quality of life long before the most severe symptoms of Alzheimer's develop.

#803. Brazil's elections matter for the planet. This is why

Brazil is heading back to the polls, but the impact could be felt worldwide. With the Amazon vital to the health of the planet, the election could help shape its future.

Few national elections carry as much environmental weight as Brazil's presidential race. And this one is being watched from far beyond the country's boundaries.

Home to the largest section of the Amazon rainforest, Brazil is guardian of a carbon sink of vital importance, making the country's conservation policies globally relevant.

Environmentalists have already voiced alarm at Flávio Bolsonaro — son of right-wing former president Jair Bolsonaro — coming out 2% ahead of incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in the first round of elections.

"It's going to be a second coming of Bolsonaro, therefore a more radical Bolsonaro," said Claudio Angelo, international policy coordinator at Climate Observatory, a network of Brazilian NGOs.

The Amazon rainforest stores huge amounts of the carbon dioxide (CO2) released into the atmosphere from the burning of fossil fuels. As a result, it helps regulate the Earth's climate.

For decades, the tropical forest has acted as one of nature's most powerful defenses against climate change. But it has also suffered from widespread deforestation.

During Jair Bolsonaro's presidency, from 2019 until 2023, environmental agencies were weakened and enforcement efforts curtailed, meaning tropical forest was cleared at unprecedented rates.

"The environment agenda was disregarded and the level of control the government had on pollution overall, but specifically on deforestation, was reversed," said Claudio Forner, head of climate policy at Berlin-based think tank Climate Analytics.

Over Bolsonaro's four-year term, an estimated 45,586km2 of the Amazon was destroyed, an area bigger than the size of Denmark. And the forest is not as resilient as it once was, with scientists warning that parts of it are now emitting more carbon than they absorb.

Lula came to office with a message of protection for the Amazon. Besides calling for an end to deforestation by 2030, he increased monitoring of the forest and strengthened Brazil's environmental enforcement agency IBAMA.

Deforestation has plummeted under his leadership, with 2026 set to be its lowest level in a decade. That represents a 41% drop overall compared to Jair Bolsonaro's tenure.

Experts have already criticized Flávio Bolsonaro's record on forest protection. As a senator in 2019, he authored draft legislation that could have stark impacts for the Amazon. While landowners are currently required to preserve 80% of their property for the growth of native plants, the bill would allow them to use their holdings as they please.

"The signal from the president will say 'now you can deforest' or 'it's harder to deforest,'" said Ana Yang, director of the Environment and Society Center at London-based think tank Chatham House.

"The moment that you decrease enforcement capability, deforestation grows," she told DW.

Bolsonaro has also pledged to end illegal deforestation by 2029, a year before Lula. But Claudio Angelo is critical of that message.

"He will end illegal deforestation not by enforcing the law, but by making it legal," he said.

More generally, environmental experts fear Bolsonaro might dismantle many of the protections strengthened under the current president.

"Unfortunately, climate change has become a partisan issue, so rather than offering a solution from their perspective, the right pictures climate change as an invented problem...that regulation goes against economic growth or it goes against competitiveness, that it's the aim of the left to control the means of production," Forner said.

In an interview with Brazilian media in August, Bolsonaro said he regarded extreme weather events to be cyclical and did not believe they were solely linked to human influence.

A Bolsonaro win is likely to see Brazil move away from its position as a climate leader, a role the country embraced last year as it hosted the COP30 climate conference in the Amazonian city of Belem.

But even a Lula victory would not represent a straightforward climate success story, say experts. While the current administration has overseen significant reductions in deforestation, it has also backed plans to expand oil production.

Angelo said the decision to allow exploratory drilling was a "betrayal" of Lula's mandate but added that the president's overall record on climate had been "quite impressive".

"Lula is the world's worst climate leader, except for all the others," he told DW.

The current president will face a runoff election with Bolsonaro on October 25. But while experts say the outcome could determine the fate of the Amazon and the wider efforts to curb global warming, for many Brazilians climate is not high on their list of priorities.

"Ultimately what people care about is access to education, sanitation and health," said Yang. A road built through the Amazon to access those services, while criticized from outside Brazil for destroying parts of the rainforest, would be desirable to voters, she added.

"They don't have time to care. Not because they don't want to. They care about their own survival," she said.
