#2550. France is flashing euro crisis signals. This time, the fault lines are different

As France's economic and political woes mount, investors are asking whether the country's debt problems risk contaminating the rest of the euro zone.

Markets are flashing alarm bells over the euro zone's second-biggest economy. The euro has remained under pressure, falling almost 5% against the dollar since the start of the year.

France's benchmark borrowing cost, the yield on its 10-year bond, rose above 5% for the first time since 2002 this week before moving sharply higher.

Meanwhile, the spread between French and German 10-year bond yields — a closely watched metric of distress — reached more than 170 basis points on Thursday for the first time since the euro zone debt crisis of the early 2010s. The cost of insuring the French government against default, as measured by credit default swaps, is also the highest since that period.

The big concern is fragmentation, when countries that share a currency face sharply different borrowing costs, compounding market stress and risking spiraling debt repayments for the worst-impacted countries.

As the euro zone sovereign debt crisis of the early 2010s showed, market stress in one country can spread rapidly through economies that share a currency and a central bank but retain separate fiscal systems. The crisis began in Greece before spreading to countries including Portugal, Ireland, Italy and Spain.

Governments were expected to rescue struggling banks, which held large amounts of sovereign debt, creating a vicious cycle.

The crisis only eased after rescue programs tied to sweeping austerity measures, and ECB President Mario Draghi pledged in 2012 to do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro.

Economists are warning that a new vicious cycle is being created — not only in France but in big economies from the U.S. to the U.K. — as investor concern over rising government debt pushes up yields and interest costs, which in turn add to the debt burden.

A slowdown in bond purchases raises "the specter that the government will be forced into default because it simply doesn't have the cash to pay interest and principal on its debt," Nobel laureate Paul Krugman wrote in a blog on Oct. 8.

"Fear of default then leads to even more capital flight, which increases fears of default and the interest rate," he said.

France in particular has one of the highest deficit-to-GDP ratios in the 21-nation euro area and a growing national debt pile. Its economy has slowed to a crawl, with the government cutting its 2026 growth forecast to just 0.5%.

Those fiscal strains come as political volatility and social unrest raise doubts about the government's ability to deliver measures aimed at stabilizing the public finances.

"We are deep into French crisis territory," Carsten Brzeski, ING's global head of macro research and chief euro zone economist, said by email.

"There is no risk of an imminent liquidity issue for the French government but the lack of trust of bond markets and the ongoing crisis should finally bring a high sense of urgency to Paris."

John Stopford, head of multi-asset income at Ninety One, said France has gone from being a core or semi-core European country for fixed income investors to increasingly being considered on the periphery, which he labeled "bizarre" given the history of France in relation to the EU and its economic power in the continent.

"We as investors, have just chosen for the time being to pretty much avoid all of the non-core countries in Europe – it's too fluid a situation with too much uncertainty to really want to get involved," Stopford said on a call.

Nonetheless, analysts CNBC spoke to broadly agreed that reforms meant that even if the market is behaving in similar ways to the post-financial crisis period, the contagion risk remained limited this time around.

Ninety One's Stopford said spreads would likely need to be trading "in the 200s" before the situation is broadly perceived as a genuine crisis — but, he added, that the situation in France "doesn't look great."

"French spreads are now wider than they were at the peak of the peripheral crisis of the early 2010s, but they're still much narrower than the extreme levels that we saw in Spain, Italy, Greece and Ireland back then, so... I think the market's assumption is that things will need to get worse before we get to a point where EU institutions start to look to try and limit further damage," he told CNBC.

BlackRock strategists noted the current environment is "materially different" to the one that preceded the 2011 to 2012 euro zone debt crisis, with the region now better equipped to weather financial stress, but they added that "further volatility and spillovers remain possible."

"The French bond sell-off illustrates how competition for capital is reshaping fixed income markets amid a global rate reset," they wrote. "Rising government borrowing and AI-related investment are pushing up financing costs, exposing borrowers with deteriorating fundamentals to greater scrutiny."

George Lagarias, chief economist at Forvis Mazars, said France was already in a debt crisis, citing its debt-to-GDP ratio of 119% at the end of the second quarter of 2026 — close to Greece's of 128% in late 2009.

A spillover would mean a wider French-German yield spread followed by higher credit default swap prices – the cost of insuring debt, he said.

"Italy, Spain, would see higher borrowing costs, and have to curtail fiscal expenditures, possibly raising political backlash in their own countries," he told CNBC. "The European periphery would come under tremendous pressure, even if their finances are in much better order than a few years ago."

However, Lagarias said crucial structural differences minimize the risk of a wider spillover akin to the early 2010s, which he estimated at 10% to 20%.

"Greece had a very bad trade deficit. France also has a trade deficit, but the numbers are much more benign," he said by email.

He added that the Insee, France's national statistics body, was "not likely to lie about the deficit" and contrasted this with Greece.

In 2009, Greece sharply revised its projected budget deficit from 3.7% of GDP in the spring to 12.5% by October, undermining confidence in the reliability of its public finances and helping accelerate the crisis.

"[French] debt levels are worrying but known. Markets can deal with known risks. They hate surprises," Lagarias added. "What sunk Greece was a deficit that was suddenly revealed to be five-fold versus the official estimate. This catalysed a crisis."

Alex Everett, investment director for rates management at Aberdeen Investments, agreed that the current environment should be treated "primarily as French risk rather than a systemic euro-area risk."

"Compared to the early 2010s, the Eurozone has stronger banks, greater integration and numerous tools designed to limit the risk of sovereign fragmentation," he said. "I anticipate contagion to be short-lived."

#2549. Giants’ Matt Nagy ready to open up playbook for J.J. McCarthy’s first start

The Giants' ever-changing quarterback situation is testing offensive coordinator Matt Nagy's math skills.

Nagy estimated that the Giants lost about 30 percent of their playbook — namely, the dual-threat quarterback section of zone reads and RPOs — when Jaxson Dart was injured in Week 2 and replaced by pocket passer Jameis Winston for four starts.

The latest variable in the equation is the introduction of J.J. McCarthy, who leapfrogged Winston to get the next start Sunday against the Texans.

"Maybe now 20 percent of that 30 that was gone is back," Nagy said before Thursday's practice. "For us, what does he do well? You've seen some of the packages he's done, but that can become predictable. We don't want to be predictable. Now that he's the starter, it probably benefits us some that he's just not always coming in to do predictable QB-driven runs."

J.J. McCarthy (18) makes a throw in the second half of the Giants' loss to the Saints on Oct. 18, 2026. AP Photo/Adam Hunger

Making just his second appearance since he was acquired in a Sept. 28 trade from the Vikings, McCarthy produced a historic fourth quarter — 243 passing yards and three scoring drives — off the bench last Sunday in nearly bringing the Giants back from a 24-point deficit in a 34-27 loss to the Saints.

The performance backed up the coaching staff's belief that McCarthy turned a corner last week and was ready for a bigger role.

"Just the confidence of him being able to, in practice, talk to us about the scheme, where his eyes are going in the pass game, where he's snapping the ball with a motion or a shift," Nagy said.

"If there was a shoestring with Jameis or he got hurt, he could go in and function. Whereas the first two weeks, I had a highlighted [play] call sheet that, if something happened and he was in, these were the plays. It was probably 50 percent. Last week, it was probably 90 percent. Now this week, it's going to be 100 percent."

New York Giants Offensive Coordinator Matt Nagy speaks at a press conference as the New York Giants practice on Thursday, Oct. 15, 2026, at Quest Diagnostics Training Center in East Rutherford, N.J. Robert Sabo for NY Post

Tight end Isaiah Likely said it is the responsibility of the other 10 offensive players to make McCarthy "as comfortable as can be [for] a quarterback in a new offense."

McCarthy has at least two pass catchers who believe they are always open. So, he'll throw his 50 mph fastballs — among the fastest quarterback velocity in the league — downfield (12.4 career yards per completion) against a notoriously ball-hawking secondary.

"Letting him know that as long as the ball's in the air and in my vicinity, it's a great pass either way," Likely said. "It doesn't matter if somebody's on me or if I'm wide open. Just throw the ball, trust me, and I'm going to make the play for you."

New York Giants quarterback J.J. McCarthy (18) runs with the ball during the fourth quarter of an NFL football game against the New Orleans Saints in East Rutherford, N.J., Sunday, Oct. 18, 2026. USA TODAY Network via Reuters Connect

McCarthy is the eighth quarterback that Malik Nabers has caught a pass from in 25 career games. His strategy for adapting to quarterback change is simple.

"Get open; just get open," Nabers said. "They're going to find you, and they know who their guys are. And when you talk to them, just give motivation to them. Tell them, 'I know you're just coming in, you don't know me, really … but just know on certain plays, just give me an opportunity. Even when I'm covered, just give me an opportunity.' "

McCarthy aced the no-huddle part of the playbook against the Saints. But what about managing a four-quarter game?

"His operation is really, really good," Nagy said. "It starts with listening to the play call in your headset, being able to say it and/or read it on the wristband, get to the line of scrimmage, and then be a quarterback: see the formation, motion shifts, see the play clock, make your ID checks or whatever you do with the center, and then make a play. What he's doing is amazing. He wants as much as he can get to digest within our offense."

The Giants could have not thrown McCarthy into a hostile road environment as 9.5-point underdogs and given him two weeks to prepare to make his first start after the bye. Why wait?

"We would not have had the discussion if we didn't think he could do it," Nagy said. "We fully feel as a staff [quarterbacks] coach [Brian] Callahan has done an amazing job working with him and getting him ready. We feel confident about it."

#2547. "Sad" Vinicius Jr criticised by Spanish media as his Real Madrid struggles continue vs RB Leipzig

By Lewis Blain | 22 Oct 2026 07:22

Real Madrid maintained their perfect start to the Champions League with a convincing 4-1 victory over RB Leipzig on Wednesday evening, although the result did little to silence questions over Vinicius Junior's recent form.

Bernardo Silva and Arda Guler put Los Blancos in control before Kylian Mbappe and substitute Yan Diomande added further goals, with the latter producing a spectacular volley to cap off an impressive cameo.

Vinicius, however, endured another frustrating evening, struggling to influence proceedings before being substituted in the 70th minute amid whistles from the Bernabeu crowd.

The Spanish media were largely critical of the Brazil superstar, with some questioning his confidence and others highlighting the stark contrast between his performance and Diomande's impact.

AS were particularly critical, publishing the headline "Vinicius sigue negado", which translates as Vinicius remains unable to find his form.

The newspaper described his first half as "bastante frío" - fairly cold - noting that he barely attempted anything during the opening 25 minutes. Although he subsequently showed some defiance and forced two saves from Leipzig goalkeeper Maarten Vandevoordt, AS also highlighted that he had barely touched the ball after half-time before being replaced by Diomande to further whistles.

GOAL Espana handed Vinicius 6/10, labelling it a discreet performance by his own high standards. They noted that he struggled to establish his usual rhythm against Leipzig's compact defence, with Mourinho ultimately turning to Diomande for additional attacking impetus.

VAVEL Espana also awarded the Brazilian 6/10, describing him as "apagado", or 'subdued,' and expressing concern about his confidence when taking on opponents.

The outlet outlined his no attempted dribbles - a particularly striking statistic for a winger whose ability to beat defenders has traditionally been one of his greatest strengths. VAVEL also highlighted his serious demeanour when leaving the pitch.

El Pais were similarly unimpressed, saying that Vinicius was displaying "una timidez y una cautela muy desacostumbradas" - an unusually 'timid' and cautious approach. The newspaper felt Madrid's attacking play gravitated towards Guler on the opposite flank, leaving Vinicius increasingly peripheral to proceedings.

EFE delivered perhaps the harshest assessment, publishing the headline "La intrascendencia de Vinicius vs. el brillo de Diomande" - Vinicius's insignificance versus Diomande's brilliance. They argued that the Brazilian had wasted another opportunity to reassert himself, contrasting his limited influence with Diomande's immediate impact after coming on.

Meanwhile, Real Madrid-focused publication La Galerna gave Vinicius an "aprobado", meaning a 'pass', but offered a poignant observation. "Le ocurrió algo que para él es peor que jugar mal: estar triste." In English, something happened to him that was worse than playing badly - being 'sad'.

Vinicius's struggles have become increasingly difficult to separate from a turbulent few months.

Arsenal were heavily linked with the Brazilian during the summer transfer window while negotiations over his Real Madrid future remained unresolved, before he eventually agreed a new long-term contract running until 2032 in August.

While there is no definitive evidence that the transfer speculation alone explains his downturn, his performances, the increasingly audible frustration from supporters and the emergence of Diomande as a genuine alternative have created a more complicated situation for the 26-year-old so far in 2026-27.

Following the Leipzig match, Cadena SER's Javier Herraez described him as "está en la lona" - or 'on the ropes' - after he went straight down the tunnel rather than celebrating with his teammates.

There was nevertheless a note of encouragement from Mbappe, who publicly backed his teammate and urged him to remember his quality. Former Real Madrid striker Pedja Mijatovic also suggested that Diomande's breakthrough could actually help Vinicius rediscover his best form by reducing the pressure on him.

The Brazilian still has time to turn things around, but with the Bernabeu crowd becoming increasingly impatient, he will need to rediscover the direct running and threat that made him one of Europe's most feared attackers.

Wednesday's victory offered Madrid plenty of reasons for optimism, but Vinicius, however, remains a significant concern.

Investors may be wary of the volatility hitting parts of the bond market right now, but they have an "historic" opportunity to grab attractive yields in high-quality credit, Rick Rieder, BlackRock's chief investment officer for global fixed income, told CNBC in an exclusive interview.

Duration is a measurement of a bond's price sensitivity to fluctuations in rates, and issues with shorter maturities tend to have lower duration.

"Not only is it historic in that these yields are where they are, but the only time these yields are ever here is when there's some problem, like an economic problem or default problem," Rieder said in an interview about his fourth-quarter outlook, released Wednesday.

That isn't the case now, where the economy remains strong, he said. "So you don't really have to worry about defaults of any significance."

While Rieder didn't specifically identify the credits in the aforementioned portfolio, the iShares Aaa - A Rated Corporate Bond ETF (QLTA) has a 30-day SEC yield of 5.68% and an effective duration of 6.48 years — higher than Rieder's low duration focus.

The iShares Flexible Income Active ETF (BINC), which is managed by Rieder, has a 5.66% 30-day SEC yield and three-year effective duration, but its portfolio extends beyond investment-grade credit.

Rieder sees the best opportunity right now in investment-grade corporate bonds — which he pointed out he hasn't said "in a really long time." Investment grade bonds are rated AAA through BBB- by S&P and Fitch, while Moody's rates them Aaa through Baa3.

That includes hyperscaler bonds, which have flooded the market with issuance this year as they ramp up their AI infrastructure. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta Platforms and Oracle have issued a total of about $228.4 billion in bonds year to date, as of Oct. 20, according to LSEG.

"They're very low leverage. They have tremendous amounts of cash flow coming in," Rieder said of the hyperscalers in general. "You're now getting to buy these entities which are higher quality, paying you a very attractive yield to get it."

Rieder's iShares Flexible Income Active ETF has upped its allocation to investment-grade corporates to 14.3% of its portfolio. In July, that stood at 8.5%.

That said, investors need to be patient and sit tight since rates could still move higher from here, he advised.

"Your starting point is by far your best barometer of what your return is going to be," he noted. "So if you get seven and a quarter, maybe you get eight, maybe you get six, but ... if your holding period is a year or longer, you're going to get that yield."

Caution should also be exercised outside of investment-grade credit, which requires more selectivity and deep research, he warned. That includes AI names.

"There's parts of the data center supply that's not hyperscaler guaranteed or hyperscaler backed," he said. "That we've been more cautious around, and we'll continue to be."

The bond market, particularly high-yield credit, has been rocked as investor concern grows over the massive amount of debt some companies are taking out to fund their artificial intelligence build-out.

Junk-rated CoreWeave has become the poster child, seeing its stock drop, bond yields jump and credit-default spreads widen. Oracle, which is sitting in the lower end of investment grade and is seen as a bellwether on the broader AI market, has also taken some hits.

Rieder is also keeping an eye on the Federal Reserve, which is expected to raise its federal funds rate again in December to combat inflation. The market is currently pricing in a nearly 90% probability that the central bank will hike at that time, according to the CME Group FedWatch tool.

"They're not borrowing off the overnight funds rate," Rieder noted. "There's a lot of floating rate finance in housing, credit card, auto loan finance, and you're seeing those areas already under pressure."

Instead, the market is and will continue to price the AI supply coming into the market, he added.

Rieder sees a number of possible risks ahead, including rising levels of government debt across the globe. In the United States, the budget deficit has grown to $2 trillion for fiscal 2026.

"The more the central banks raise the rates, the cost of debt to these to already indebted governments is something we've got to be careful about over time," he said.

Bond supply coming from AI companies should also be watched.

"With the amount of supply that's coming — data center, hyperscaler — there's some crowding-out effect that's taking place," he said. "With government rates moving higher, that is something that we just got to keep an eye on."
