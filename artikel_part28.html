#5049. Smarter Cooling, Stronger Reliability: Advancing Wind Turbine Gearbox Performance

As the wind energy industry matures, operators and service providers are increasingly focused on maximizing turbine availability while controlling long-term maintenance costs. Although major drivetrain components often receive the spotlight, one overlooked system can have an outsized impact on turbine reliability and performance: gearbox oil cooling.

Heat exchangers play a critical role in maintaining proper oil temperature and viscosity within wind turbine gearboxes. When cooling systems become clogged with airborne debris, dust, or organic material, oil temperatures rise, lubrication performance declines, and component stress increases. Over time, these conditions can contribute to reduced gearbox efficiency, elevated maintenance demands, and avoidable downtime.

“Wind operators today are balancing increasing performance expectations with the realities of aging fleets and rising maintenance costs. Technologies that improve cooling efficiency and reduce service exposure can have a meaningful impact on long-term turbine reliability and operational profitability,” according to Mike Erickson, HYDAC’s Market Manager for the Wind Industry.

Traditional “panel cut” heat exchanger designs have long been susceptible to clogging due to their tightly packed fin structures. In many wind environments — particularly agricultural, dusty, or high-pollen regions — debris accumulation can severely restrict airflow. Oil leaks can further compound the issue by trapping contaminants within the exchanger core, making cleaning more difficult and labor intensive.

This operational challenge has driven the need for more resilient cooling technologies designed specifically for real-world wind farm conditions.

HYDAC’s new “Square Wave” heat exchanger design represents a significant advancement in addressing these reliability concerns. Engineered to allow debris to pass more freely through the exchanger fins, the design minimizes clogging while improving cooling efficiency under demanding operating conditions.

The importance of effective cooling cannot be overstated. Wind turbine gearbox oil typically operates within an optimal temperature range of approximately 45°C to 65°C. Even modest temperature increases can significantly impact oil viscosity. According to field data, a 12°C rise in oil temperature can reduce viscosity by nearly 28 percent — affecting lubrication quality and accelerating wear on critical drivetrain components.

Field trials have demonstrated measurable operational benefits from the Square Wave design. During a one-year trial on GE 1.x turbines, the upgraded exchanger consistently reduced clogging and lowered maintenance requirements compared to traditional panel cut cores. Operators reported reduced up-tower cleaning time and fewer temperature-related cooling faults.

Field validation across multiple turbine platforms has further reinforced the operational advantages of the Square Wave design. In one case study involving legacy 1 MW-class turbines operating in the Pacific Northwest, a major renewable energy operator installed pilot sets of Square Wave heat exchangers as part of a cooling performance evaluation. Installation teams reported the upgraded exchangers integrated seamlessly into the existing platform with no retrofit complications.

After initial operating data demonstrated improved cooling consistency, the operator expanded the evaluation program with additional units. Long-term SCADA analysis later showed turbines equipped with Square Wave technology operate approximately 20 to 25 percent cooler than comparable turbines using conventional heat exchanger designs under similar operating conditions. The data also highlighted another important advantage: the Square Wave cores continued to maintain airflow and cooling efficiency in debris-heavy environments where traditional exchanger designs became increasingly restricted over time.

The successful deployment also generated interest from additional wind service organizations seeking retrofit-ready cooling upgrades that could improve turbine reliability while reducing maintenance burdens across aging fleets.

“What operators are increasingly recognizing is that small improvements in thermal management can create meaningful gains in turbine reliability, maintenance efficiency, and long-term asset performance,” said Erickson. “The field data we’re seeing continues to validate the value of designing cooling systems specifically for real-world wind operating environments.”

More recently, long-term SCADA data from Mitsubishi platforms further validated the technology’s performance. After two years of operation, turbines equipped with Square Wave heat exchangers operated approximately 19 to 25 percent cooler than reference turbines using standard exchanger designs under comparable operating conditions.

#5047. Tesla now says it needs its own maps for FSD after years of dismissing maps

Tesla says it’s building its own maps to fix some of the most common problems with its “Full Self-Driving” system, including reading speed limits, parking, and choosing routes. The company’s head of AI described the effort on Tesla’s Q3 2026 earnings call.

It’s a notable shift for a company whose CEO spent years arguing that self-driving systems relying on maps are brittle. And it shows Tesla’s fleet is now doing the same job Waymo’s cars do: mapping the roads so the self-driving system knows what it can’t reliably figure out on its own.

Tesla fans argue that Waymo’s approach differs because it creates HD maps with lidar, but ultimately both are creating their own maps; if your argument is that Tesla’s maps are less well-defined and needed for driving for different reasons than Waymo’s, you don’t really have an argument.

The question came from Tesla’s shareholder Q&A. A retail investor wrote that mapping “continues to be a problem” for FSD, listing speed limits, parking and typical routes as the three biggest issues, and asked why Tesla relies on third-party maps instead of its own.

Tesla’s VP of AI, Ashok Elluswamy, agreed that “we shouldn’t be depending on external people’s maps.” He said Tesla is building its own maps using a new AI-based mapping and navigation architecture, fed by what the fleet collectively learns, that will also learn individual drivers’ preferences and respond to voice commands.

He said the technology will start handling the beginning and end of trips with FSD v15, and over the following months take over navigation, parking and destination handling.

Tesla has gone back and forth on this before.

In 2015, when it launched Autopilot, Tesla told Fortune it had started building high-precision maps with “100 times the level of granularity” of standard navigation, using data from its customer cars.

At its Autonomy Day in 2019, Elon Musk said Tesla had tried high-precision lane maps and realized it was “a huge mistake.” He called relying on them a “really bad idea” that makes a self-driving system “extremely brittle,” in the same presentation where he called lidar a “fool’s errand.” The message was clear: Waymo needed lidar and detailed maps, and Tesla would solve driving with cameras and AI alone, anywhere.

Seven years later, Tesla is building a map layer from its own fleet to fix the things its vision system gets wrong.

To be fair to Tesla, this isn’t an HD map in the sense Musk criticized. Tesla has always used navigation maps, and building its own instead of licensing them isn’t a contradiction on its own.

But look at the problems Tesla is trying to fix. Speed limits, parking and routes are all about understanding the environment, and FSD still often fails at it, especially with speed limit signs.

Last month, a Belgian road safety group found that FSD sped in 55% of the 30 km/h zones it tested in Brussels, averaging 44 km/h, partly because the car’s screen showed a 50 km/h limit in most of those zones. In the US, NHTSA is weighing an investigation into claims that Tesla Robotaxis speed through active school zones, covering about 1,000 Model Y and Cybercab vehicles.

A speed limit sign is about as simple as road information gets. If an end-to-end vision system can’t reliably read and apply it, and the fix is a better map, then vision alone isn’t doing the job Tesla said it would.

That brings Tesla closer to Waymo than it would like to admit.

Waymo maps a city by driving its sensor-equipped vehicles through it before launching service, and keeps those maps updated as its fleet drives. Tesla is now describing the same idea: its cars collect what they see, and that data feeds a Tesla-built map that helps every car drive better. The main difference is that Waymo’s cars have lidar on top.

It also fits how Tesla’s Robotaxi service actually operates: city by city, inside small geofenced service areas, with Tesla handling the fleet operations. Tesla isn’t talking about centimeter-level HD maps today, but a fleet-built map that keeps getting more detailed could easily head that way.

There’s nothing wrong with using maps. Every serious self-driving program does, and a map built from millions of Tesla cars could be a real advantage. If it fixes FSD’s speed limit problems, that’s good news for everyone sharing the road with it.

Add this as another reason why unsupervised FSD will not be going out to consumer cars in Q2 of 2027. There will be more reasons forthcoming, but not for some time as the main focus will be raking in more cash for those payday goals.

What’s wrong is the years of messaging that Tesla’s approach didn’t need any of this. Musk sold FSD as a general solution that would work anywhere through vision alone, while dismissing Waymo’s maps and lidar as crutches. Now Tesla is adding the map, it already operates in geofences, and the AI still can’t reliably read a 30 km/h sign in Brussels.

Waymo built its maps and has been running a driverless service for years. Tesla is building its maps now and has pushed unsupervised FSD in customer cars to Q2 2027, and only inside its Robotaxi fleet. At what point do we admit that both companies are solving the same problem in very similar ways, and only one of them has lidar?

We are a decade into Tesla’s self-driving effort and it is doing exactly what Musk used to say doesn’t count as a “true self-driving system”: geo-fenced areas using maps.

#5046. DOJ streamlines voluntary self-disclosure of healthcare fraud

The Department of Justice and the Department of Health and Human Services Office of Inspector General have unveiled new policy directing healthcare organizations to voluntarily self-disclose any potential criminal fraud to the former agency alone.

The change aims to remove obstacles that could keep organizations from disclosing fraud, the DOJ said in its Thursday announcement. Both agencies have had policies in place that reduce repercussions for organizations that voluntarily self-disclose a fraud arrangement that was previously unknown to the government.

For DOJ, that could result in declined prosecution of corporate criminal conduct (referred to as a declination), while HHS-OIG could reduce civil monetary damages, expedite its resolution of the matter and forego the need for a corporate integrity agreement.

Because a single fraud arrangement could involve criminal, civil or administrative repercussions (such as program exclusions) for an organization, forcing organizations to decide where and how to self-report any fraud arrangements. Streamlining the process to a single criminal disclosure with the DOJ, while still receiving the benefits of HHS-OIG’s process, removes a barrier that could keep a healthcare organization from disclosing, DOJ said.

“Companies that discover fraud within their own operations should not have to navigate a maze of agencies before reporting it,” Assistant Attorney General Colin M. McDonald, of the Justice Department’s National Fraud Enforcement Division, said in the announcement. “Today, together with our partners at HHS-OIG, we are eliminating unnecessary bureaucratic barriers that have caused confusion about whether companies must make multiple disclosures of potentially criminal conduct.

“There is now one clear point of contact: DOJ’s Fraud Division,” he continued. “If a company comes forward, cooperates fully, and remedies the misconduct, the benefits are clear and predictable. If a company conceals what it knows, it should expect a very different conversation.”

HHS-OIG’s own update to its Health Care Fraud Self-Disclosure Protocol similarly outlines the directive, and specifies that a disclosure to HHS-OIG alone will generally not qualify for declination from the DOJ.

Law firm Womble Bond Dickinson, in an online post outlining insights from its White Collar Defense and Criminal Investigations Team, emphasized that the “one-way street” directing disclosures to DOJ alone still warrants careful determination among organizations. Conduct that is initially reported to HHS-OIG could later be determined to be potentially criminal, meaning that the company “may have forfeited the opportunity for a declination” from DOJ.

Additionally, the firm wrote, a declination doesn’t exclude a company from having to conduct “a thorough internal investigation” in line with the agencies’ unchanged expectations.

“Companies should scope the investigation from the outset to address the requirements of both regimes, including full cooperation, identification of responsible individuals, appropriate remediation and quantification of damages,” the firm wrote.

Womble Bond Dickinson as well as Sidley, another firm, both noted that the policy update stands apart from the False Claims Act’s liabilities and potential protections. Sidley’s takeaways described exclusion of both the Act and its enforcement team, within the DOJ’s Civil Fraud Section, from the policy as “a significant gap for the Department to address if it wants to encourage self-disclosures by healthcare companies.”

The DOJ and other enforcement agencies have signaled healthcare fraud as a key focus, with administration officials often highlighting key investigations and actions in press events during the run-up to Congressional midterms. DOJ launched its National Fraud Enforcement Division in April, which includes a Health Care Fraud Section currently comprised of nine strike forces.

#5042. Scientists may have overlooked a hidden Alzheimer's culprit for decades

For decades, researchers have invested billions of dollars in Alzheimer's treatments designed to target amyloid beta, a peptide that forms harmful deposits in the brain. But a biochemist at the University of California, Santa Cruz, believes scientists may have overlooked another potential contributor to the disease: a smaller, closely related peptide that was long considered harmless.

In a new commentary published in ChemBioChem, UC Santa Cruz professor Jevgenij Raskatov highlights research suggesting that this lesser-known peptide, called P3, can form microscopic clumps and threadlike structures known as fibrils. Evidence also suggests that P3 may interact with amyloid beta (Aβ), potentially influencing how it accumulates in the brain and how toxic it becomes. These findings raise the possibility that P3 contributes to the nerve cell damage associated with Alzheimer's disease.

"The P3 peptide is, most likely, not the innocent bystander it was commonly thought to be. There's still more research to be done. But this could turn Alzheimer's research on its head," said Raskatov, whose laboratory investigates amyloid peptides in hopes of identifying ways to prevent their harmful effects and develop better Alzheimer's treatments. "P3 is a distinct aggregating peptide that is itself potentially neurotoxic and may be contributing to Alzheimer's disease."

Why Alzheimer's Treatments Have Had Limited Success

Alzheimer's is the most widespread neurodegenerative disease, affecting approximately 35 million people worldwide and generating annual costs exceeding $800 billion. The number of people living with the condition is expected to double by 2050.

Despite decades of intensive research, developing effective treatments has proved extraordinarily difficult. More than 400 clinical trials have investigated potential Alzheimer's drugs, with most focusing on amyloid beta. Many of these efforts have failed, while others have produced only modest benefits. Some treatments have also been associated with serious complications, including brain hemorrhages and strokes.

Amyloid beta originates from a much larger protein embedded in cell membranes called the Amyloid Precursor Protein (APP). Two enzymes, β-secretase and γ-secretase, cut this protein in sequence, releasing amyloid beta peptides of different lengths.

Researchers have concentrated particularly on two versions, Aβ40 and Aβ42, which contain 40 and 42 amino acids, respectively. Aβ42 has attracted the greatest attention because it is especially prone to sticking together and forming toxic aggregates. As a result, it has remained a central target of Alzheimer's drug development for decades.

Existing medications, including cholinesterase inhibitors and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonists, can temporarily ease certain symptoms but do not stop the disease from progressing. More recently, antibody treatments such as Lecanemab and Donanemab have been approved to help remove amyloid beta from the brain.

However, Raskatov argues that the benefits achieved so far remain limited, underscoring the need to investigate other biological processes involved in Alzheimer's.

"Progress has been extremely slow, and the current state of the art in Alzheimer's therapy leaves much to be desired," he said. "We need fundamentally new approaches to the problem."

The Overlooked Peptide That May Also Damage Brain Cells

Unlike amyloid beta, P3 forms when Amyloid Precursor Protein is processed through a different sequence of molecular cuts. In this pathway, an enzyme called α-secretase acts first, followed by γ-secretase.

Raskatov has proposed calling this peptide "Amyloid α," or Aα, to better distinguish it from amyloid beta and reflect its ability to form aggregates.

For years, scientists largely assumed that P3 was incapable of forming amyloid deposits, was not toxic to cells, and would remain soluble in water. Under that interpretation, the peptide would simply dissolve rather than accumulate in the brain.

These assumptions were never adequately verified, according to Raskatov. Nevertheless, they helped establish the view that P3 was unlikely to matter in Alzheimer's disease, leaving it largely absent from research efforts.

Raskatov, a specialist in peptide chemistry, and researchers in his laboratory decided to test those long-standing beliefs.

Over the past five years, the team has published three major studies demonstrating that P3 can form amyloid deposits at least as readily as amyloid beta. In fact, their experiments showed that P3 produces these aggregates more rapidly.

The researchers also found evidence that P3 itself may be harmful to neurons, although its toxicity appears lower than that of amyloid beta.

According to Raskatov, an independent research group in the United Kingdom has confirmed and expanded upon his team's findings. Other laboratories are now beginning to investigate how P3 and amyloid beta interact, including whether their relationship affects the accumulation and toxicity of these peptides.

Together, the results challenge the long-held assumption that P3 is biologically harmless and irrelevant to Alzheimer's. However, further research is needed to establish whether and how it contributes to the disease in patients.

Challenging Decades of Alzheimer's Research

For much of the past several decades, amyloid beta has dominated scientific explanations of Alzheimer's disease. David Teplow, an emeritus professor of neurology at UCLA and a prominent Alzheimer's researcher, said the peptide has long been regarded as a central cause of the condition.

But an independent evaluation of Raskatov's research led Teplow to conclude that scientists are beginning to reconsider this narrow focus.

"This reevaluation has far-reaching consequences for both basic science and clinical research into the causes and treatment of Alzheimer's disease," said Teplow, a founding editorial board member of the Journal of Molecular Neuroscience and the American Journal of Neurodegenerative Disease, as well as editor-in-chief of Progress in Molecular Biology and Translational Science.

Raskatov has also encountered a troubling problem while examining how other scientists have interpreted his team's discoveries.

He identified at least four papers published in respected, peer-reviewed journals that cited his laboratory's research as evidence that P3 was harmless and unable to form amyloid deposits. Yet his team's experiments had demonstrated precisely the opposite.

"This is exactly the opposite of what we have actually shown," Raskatov said. "We remain in the dark on how this sort of grand confusion may have come about. Clearly, there is more work ahead of us."

The emerging evidence suggests that understanding Alzheimer's may require looking beyond amyloid beta alone. By examining P3 and its interactions with other amyloid peptides, researchers hope to gain a more complete picture of the processes that damage brain cells and potentially identify new directions for treatment.

#5028. New knee arthritis treatment delivers lasting pain relief without surgery

A minimally invasive procedure that targets abnormal blood vessels in the knee could provide lasting relief for people struggling with osteoarthritis, offering a potential alternative to joint replacement surgery.

In a study involving 194 patients, researchers found that a treatment using tiny particles that dissolve within hours was associated with substantial reductions in knee pain and improvements in mobility. The benefits persisted for at least 12 months, with 80% of patients assessed at the one-year follow-up reporting clinically meaningful pain relief.

The approach, known as genicular artery embolization, aims to interrupt the inflammation and abnormal blood vessel growth that contribute to chronic knee pain. Rather than replacing the damaged joint, doctors use a thin catheter to deliver microscopic particles directly into specific blood vessels.

The findings, published in the journal Radiology, suggest that this technique could provide another option for patients who have exhausted conventional treatments but cannot or do not want to undergo knee replacement surgery.

Why Knee Arthritis Is So Difficult To Treat

Osteoarthritis is the world's most common form of arthritis and a major cause of disability. According to the World Health Organization, knee osteoarthritis affects more than 365 million adults worldwide.

The condition develops as cartilage, the protective tissue that cushions the ends of bones, gradually deteriorates. But osteoarthritis involves more than cartilage damage. Inflammation within the joint, changes in surrounding tissues, and increased sensitivity of pain signaling nerves can all contribute to persistent discomfort.

For many people, the consequences extend far beyond occasional knee pain. Everyday activities such as walking, climbing stairs, exercising, or standing for extended periods can become increasingly difficult.

Treatments typically begin with physical therapy, medication, and injections into the affected joint. When these approaches stop providing adequate relief, knee replacement surgery may eventually be considered. However, surgery is not appropriate or desirable for everyone.

"For many patients with knee osteoarthritis, there is a real treatment gap today," said Florian Nima Fleckenstein, M.D., deputy head of Interventional Radiology Campus Mitte, Charité -- Universitätsmedizin Berlin. "Conservative measures such as intra-articular injections no longer provide sufficient relief, but joint replacement is not an option for medical or personal reasons."

A Different Way To Fight Knee Arthritis Pain

The treatment being investigated, genicular artery embolization (GAE), approaches knee arthritis from an unusual direction. Instead of focusing directly on damaged cartilage, it targets the abnormal blood vessels that develop around an inflamed joint.

These vessels can form within and around the synovium, a thin membrane lining the inside of the joint. As the vessels multiply, they can contribute to inflammation and encourage the growth of sensory nerves involved in transmitting pain.

GAE is designed to interrupt this process by reducing blood flow through the abnormal vessels while preserving the knee's normal circulation.

During the procedure, an interventional radiologist inserts a thin catheter into an artery and carefully guides it toward the affected blood vessels. Tiny particles are then injected to block selected vessels, with the goal of reducing inflammation and pain.

The technique is called superselective embolization because doctors target specific small arteries rather than blocking circulation throughout the joint. It does not require an open surgical incision or replacement of the knee.

Researchers have previously investigated both temporary blocking agents and permanent microscopic particles. Each approach has potential drawbacks, including differences in how precisely the particles can be delivered and how long they remain inside the blood vessels.

For this study, the team examined rapidly resorbable, gelatin-based microspheres. These precisely sized, spherical particles are engineered to dissolve within hours, potentially combining the advantages of temporary agents with the controlled delivery of permanent microspheres.

"GAE is a whole new treatment regimen that targets abnormal hypervascularity around the joint and, in turn, modulates the pathological neurovascular environment," Dr. Fleckenstein said.

Hypervascularity refers to an unusually high concentration of blood vessels. The pathological neurovascular environment describes the abnormal interactions between those vessels and pain signaling nerves in the affected tissue.

"By reducing both inflammation and pain, GAE with resorbable microspheres may be the first procedure that alters the course of the disease, slowing its progression."

Whether the procedure can actually slow osteoarthritis progression remains an open question. Although reducing inflammation could theoretically influence the disease, the study did not demonstrate that GAE prevents cartilage deterioration or reverses existing joint damage.

Nearly 200 Patients Underwent the Procedure

To evaluate the approach, researchers conducted a prospective single-center study involving 194 patients with osteoarthritis-related knee pain. The participants included 114 women and 80 men, with a median age of 69 years and a median body mass index of 28.4.

All had continued to experience symptoms despite at least three months of conventional care, including physical therapy, anti-inflammatory medications, and injections into the knee.

"We believe these results carry real weight because they come from real-world data," Dr. Fleckenstein said. "With this broad, inclusive study design, our participants are exactly the patients that physicians encounter every day in their practices."

The procedures took place between July and November 2024. Forty-five participants (23%) had osteoarthritis affecting both knees and received two separate treatments, with the second performed within four weeks of the first.

Altogether, the researchers carried out 239 GAE procedures.

Doctors used real-time X-ray imaging, known as fluoroscopy, to guide the catheter and deliver the particles to the targeted arteries. All 239 procedures were technically successful, meaning the intended blood vessels were successfully treated.

The safety findings were also encouraging. No moderate or severe adverse events were recorded, and mild, temporary complications occurred in 6.7% of procedures.

These included brief skin discoloration near the treated area and one superficial collection of blood near the catheter insertion site. All resolved without lasting complications.

Knee Arthritis Pain Dropped and Stayed Lower for a Year

The researchers monitored pain, movement, and quality of life before treatment and at several intervals afterward. Assessments were conducted at six weeks, three months, six months, and 12 months. An orthopedic surgeon also examined patients in person at the six-month follow-up.

Participant retention remained relatively high throughout the study. Follow-up rates were 94% at six weeks (183/194), 89% at three months (172/194), 89% at six months (171/194), and 79% at 12 months (154/194).

The most striking finding was the sustained reduction in knee pain.

Using the Numeric Rating Scale (a 0-to-10 measure of pain intensity), median pain scores fell from 7 before treatment to 4 after six weeks. By six months, the median score had declined to 3, and it remained at that level at the 12-month follow-up.

That represents a reduction of more than half from the original median pain score, with benefits that were still apparent a full year after treatment.

"In our cohort, we saw a significant drop in pain and a significant increase in function, including sports and recreation and daily activity," Dr. Fleckenstein said. "Most importantly, their quality of life significantly increased."

Patients Also Reported Better Mobility and Quality of Life

The improvements extended beyond pain reduction. Researchers used the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score, a standardized questionnaire that evaluates how knee problems affect symptoms, mobility, recreation, and everyday life.

Across all five areas assessed, patients reported meaningful progress during the year following treatment.

Median scores for daily activities increased from 53 to 71.5, suggesting that routine movements such as walking and climbing stairs became easier. Sports and recreation scores climbed from 15 to 36, indicating improvements in more physically demanding activities.

Scores measuring osteoarthritis symptoms rose from 51 to 68, while the pain category improved from 44 to 65 (where 0 indicates extreme knee pain and 100 indicates no pain at all).

Quality of life scores also increased substantially, rising from 19 before treatment to 40 after 12 months.

To determine whether these changes were large enough to matter to patients, the researchers applied established thresholds for clinically meaningful improvement.

Previous studies have identified a reduction of at least 2.0 points on the Numeric Rating Scale and an increase of at least 10 points in the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score categories as meaningful improvements.

These thresholds are known as the minimum clinically important difference. In simple terms, they distinguish changes that are likely to be noticeable in daily life from smaller statistical differences that may have little practical significance.

At 12 months, 80% of participants who completed the pain assessment exceeded the clinically meaningful improvement threshold.

"Our study demonstrates that GAE using rapidly resorbable gelatin-based microspheres is a safe, minimally invasive therapy that provides meaningful pain relief and functional improvement in participants with osteoarthritis-related knee symptoms for at least 12 months," Dr. Fleckenstein said. "By embolizing the pathological vessels, we're able to normalize the vessel structure -- and, in turn, the neuronal structure of the knee."

What Other Studies Reveal About Knee Artery Embolization

The results add to a growing body of evidence investigating whether blocking abnormal knee blood vessels can offer sustained relief from osteoarthritis.

A randomized study published in Clinical Radiology in 2026 provided encouraging findings. Researchers compared GAE combined with standard care against standard care alone in 50 patients. After one year, pain scores had improved by 72.7% in the embolization group, compared with 24.2% in the standard care group. The treated patients also reported better knee function.

However, that study used polyvinyl alcohol particles rather than the dissolving gelatin particles examined by Fleckenstein's team. It also did not include a sham procedure, making it difficult to separate the treatment's physical effects from patients' expectations.

Other research has raised important questions about how much of the improvement is caused by embolization itself.

A randomized, sham-controlled study published in European Radiology in April 2026 followed 58 patients for 12 months. Both the GAE and sham treatment groups experienced sustained reductions in pain, but researchers found no statistically significant difference between them. MRI examinations also failed to identify a significant reduction in joint inflammation attributable to embolization.

Similarly, an analysis appearing in the September 2026 issue of the European Journal of Radiology examined 22 studies involving 633 patients. Although pain scores often improved substantially after treatment, the researchers found no statistically significant advantage over sham procedures when results from three randomized trials were combined.

A separate June 2026 analysis of 45 studies involving 2,205 patients also documented improvements in pain and function, but judged the overall certainty of the evidence to be low or very low.

Together, these findings suggest that GAE is associated with promising improvements, particularly in observational studies, but more rigorous trials are needed to establish how much benefit comes directly from blocking the blood vessels.

Could This Treatment Help Patients Avoid Knee Replacement?

Despite the remaining uncertainties, researchers and medical specialists are increasingly exploring GAE as a possible option for patients who have limited alternatives.

In a 2026 position statement, the Society of Interventional Radiology supported offering the procedure to appropriately selected patients whose symptoms persist despite conservative treatment, particularly those who cannot undergo or wish to delay knee replacement surgery.

The society also emphasized the importance of specialist training, careful patient selection, and continued clinical trials to establish the treatment's effectiveness and long-term safety.

The study led by Fleckenstein provides particularly valuable information about rapidly resorbable gelatin microspheres. With nearly 200 participants, it represented the largest published clinical investigation of this specific approach at the time.

Still, because the study did not include an untreated or sham treatment comparison group, it cannot establish how much of the observed improvement resulted directly from the procedure. The researchers also did not evaluate whether the treatment slows cartilage loss or prevents patients from eventually needing joint replacement.

Longer follow-up and larger controlled trials will be essential to determine how durable the benefits are, which patients respond best, and whether dissolving particles offer advantages over other embolization materials.

For patients whose knee arthritis continues to interfere with everyday life despite medication, therapy, and injections, the approach nevertheless represents an increasingly studied possibility.

"This lets us speak about safety and efficacy with real confidence," Dr. Fleckenstein said. "For the right patient, it can mean lasting relief from a single, minimally invasive procedure -- a meaningful new option between injections and joint replacement."

The rent control ordinance was a big step toward affordability and stability for Portland families, according to Strimling. By basing rent increases on the area’s inflation, tenants can plan for reasonable increases alongside wage adjustments, he argues.

“We could not have gotten here as an individual here, an individual there. #5017. Epic launches Connectors to link its AI tools with third-party apps

Epic launched a new capability that lets its artificial intelligence agents access information and perform tasks in third-party applications.

Through "Connectors," developers can connect their apps to Epic's AI tools—patient-facing AI assistant Emmie; clinician AI assistant Art and Penny, the company's revenue cycle and operations AI. Connectors will also work with custom AI that healthcare organizations create in Agent Factory, the company announced Wednesday morning ahead of its Open@Epic conference.

As one example, Epic said a Connector can pull arrival time from an ambulance app so a hospital emergency department can prepare for the patient. A hospitalized patient could ask Emmie for a meal, and Emmie could place the order directly in the hospital’s dietary management app.

"The goal is to take what is sometimes a fragmented experience today, where patients, clinicians and healthcare system staff need to be going between multiple different systems or gathering data from multiple different places and bringing that experience together," Johnston Thayer, vice president of health informatics at Epic, told Fierce Healthcare in an interview. "Similar to other types of integrations in the past, where data needs to flow between systems, this represents capabilities that bring those same types of connections to the AI and agentic space."

Emmie could use a Connector to bring hospital wayfinding instructions to the patient, Thayer noted. "For the surgical team, Epic could check a medical equipment app to find information about assistive device availability and help the care team bring together the devices needed for the patient’s recovery.”

Epic's Agent Factory, which officially debuted at its User Group Meeting in August, is a platform for building, running and monitoring AI agents that can reason and act across workflows. Agent Factory launched with over 100 pre-built AI features that organizations can shape with their own local policies, clinical protocols and guidelines, Epic executives said.

Epic's AI assistants already retrieve information from within the EHR, but with the development of the Connectors capabilities, Epic is positioning its Agent Factory and its AI assistants as an orchestration layer that can coordinate workflows across systems, including third-party apps. Epic's AI tools also can use several Connectors to complete a multi-step workflow across multiple applications, Thayer said.

Thayer points to one use case in which Epic's Emmie helps arrange a follow-up appointment for an older adult who doesn't have access to transportation. As the AI agent considers transportation options, it also pulls information about the weather forecast for that day. Or, the agent could gather relevant information from other healthcare systems.

Epic already integrates with outside capabilities through API-based interfaces, and Connectors extends that work to agentic AI, Thayer said.

He compared assistive AI to lane-assist technology and agentic AI to a driverless car. Assistive AI helps people perform familiar tasks, but autonomous agents need tools designed specifically for how they operate. "Agents and software can still show up and work through some of the same processes that people do, clicking the same buttons, going through the same processes. But it's actually not the best way for them to operate," Thayer said.

Epic’s Connectors provide purpose-built infrastructure so agents can work more effectively across software applications, he noted.

Healthcare organizations will be able to find developer-created Connectors on Epic Showroom starting in March 2027, the company said.

The goal is to keep Connectors open and standards-based, Thayer noted. The company will initially use the Model Context Protocol (MCP) as the standard for building Connectors.

"There may be a different preferred standard in the future. The goal is to use some of those existing standards so that folks know how to develop according to those specs, and where we don't have to be the ones who decide what's valuable," he said.

Epic aims for third-party developers to build capabilities and individual health systems to determine which ones meet their needs, Thayer said.

Before enabling a Connector, healthcare organizations can review what it can do, what information it can read, and what it can change.

"Permissions are all handled at the health system level," Thayer said. That review process includes health systems' "security, legal and compliance teams taking a look at what type of information will be flowing across, confirm that it is a safe and appropriate use of details to be pulling in, and all of that is in control of each health system," he noted. "There is some work that goes into it from our teams, just in terms of standing up the infrastructure to make those connections available and respond," Thayer said. "We want it to be something that represents a value-add both ways, where it makes sense for them to be connecting and making that information available. "We have thousands of test cases and evaluations that we run on a regular basis to ensure that Emmie is performing safely and within the specified guardrails that we have put in place," Thayer said.

Connectors won’t give these AI copilots new autonomous capabilities, and the agents will continue to operate within established guardrails even when connectors bring in information from third-party apps, he asserted.

"When we're looking at agentic AI, and especially as it gets closer to autonomous AI, it's really important that we put in place the right safeguards to prevent these things from doing the unexpected or something that might not be safe," Thayer said.

"The types of things that Emmie can do, such as helping to book an appointment or direct you to a place in MyChart, are things that we have worked with the community to determine are safe at this point in time. #5015. A 47-year study reveals the age when your body starts losing strength

Aging may begin to affect your physical abilities earlier than you expect. A remarkable 47-year Swedish study found that fitness, muscle endurance, and physical performance generally begin declining around age 35, even among people who remain active.

But the findings also offer an encouraging message. Although exercise cannot completely prevent the physical changes associated with aging, becoming more active during adulthood can still produce meaningful improvements in fitness and strength.

Researchers at Karolinska Institutet in Sweden spent nearly five decades tracking how people's physical abilities changed as they grew older. Their findings, published in 2025 in the Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle, help explain when physical decline begins, how it progresses, and why staying active remains important throughout life.

A 47-Year Study Reveals When Physical Fitness Peaks

The research was part of the Swedish Physical Activity and Fitness study (SPAF), which followed 427 randomly selected men and women born in 1958. Participants underwent physical assessments over 47 years, beginning at age 16 and continuing through age 63.

Unlike most earlier research on physical aging, which compared people of different ages at a single point in time, this project repeatedly examined the same individuals as they grew older.

That distinction matters because physical abilities vary considerably from person to person. Following individuals over decades allowed researchers to observe how fitness changed within the same population rather than relying on comparisons between different generations.

The assessments included aerobic fitness, muscular endurance, and muscle power. Aerobic fitness describes how effectively the body uses oxygen during exercise, while muscular endurance measures how long muscles can continue working. Muscle power reflects the ability to produce force quickly, which is important for activities such as jumping, climbing stairs, and maintaining mobility.

The results revealed a consistent pattern. Aerobic fitness and muscular endurance generally reached their highest levels between ages 26 and 36, with physical performance beginning to decline around the mid-30s. Some abilities, particularly explosive muscle power, peaked even earlier.

The decline was initially modest but became progressively faster with age. By age 63, average performance across the measurements had fallen by roughly 30 to 48 percent from peak levels.

Importantly, the pattern appeared in both men and women. Although individual fitness levels differed, the overall rates of age-related decline were similar between the sexes.

Exercise Can Slow Physical Decline, Even After 35

One of the most striking findings was that regular physical activity did not entirely prevent the decline associated with aging. Even people who remained physically active showed evidence of diminishing performance as they grew older.

However, exercise made an important difference in how well people maintained their abilities.

Participants who became physically active during adulthood experienced improvements in physical capacity of approximately 5 to 10 percent. Those who were already active during adolescence also tended to maintain better fitness, endurance, and muscle power throughout adulthood.

These findings suggest that physical decline is not simply the result of becoming less active with age. Biological changes also appear to contribute, although exercise can help reduce their impact.

"It is never too late to start moving. Our study shows that physical activity can slow the decline in performance, even if it cannot completely stop it. Now we will look for the mechanisms behind why everyone reaches their peak performance at age 35 and why physical activity can slow performance loss but not completely halt it," says Maria Westerståhl, lecturer at the Department of Laboratory Medicine and lead author of the study.

The distinction is important. Reaching an average physical peak in the mid-30s does not mean everyone suddenly becomes weaker at that age. People can continue improving their individual fitness and strength through training, even after the age when population averages begin to decline.

Scientists are still investigating exactly why physical performance begins deteriorating relatively early in adulthood.

Several biological processes are thought to contribute. As people age, changes in muscle cells, the nervous system, and the body's ability to produce energy can gradually reduce physical performance.

One possible contributor involves mitochondria, the tiny structures inside cells that convert nutrients into usable energy. Changes in mitochondrial function may affect how efficiently muscles produce the energy required for movement.

Over time, muscles can also lose mass and become less responsive to the signals that trigger growth and repair. More advanced muscle deterioration can contribute to sarcopenia, an age-related condition involving the loss of muscle strength and mass that can make everyday activities increasingly difficult.

Although these processes often become more noticeable later in life, the Swedish findings suggest that some underlying changes in physical function begin decades earlier.

The researchers hope to determine which biological mechanisms drive this decline and why physical activity can delay, but not completely prevent, the loss of performance.

Follow-Up Research Links Early Fitness to Heart Health

The importance of maintaining physical fitness throughout adulthood extends beyond muscle strength.

In a 2026 follow-up study published in Scientific Reports, researchers examined additional health outcomes using the same Swedish cohort. They found that people with better aerobic fitness at ages 34 and 52 tended to have less arterial stiffness at age 63.

Arterial stiffness occurs when blood vessels become less flexible, making it harder for them to expand and contract as blood moves through the body. Greater stiffness is associated with increased cardiovascular risk.

Notably, the relationship between earlier fitness and later artery health remained significant even after accounting for several established cardiovascular risk factors, including obesity, smoking, and blood pressure.

The results do not establish that better fitness directly prevented arterial stiffening. However, they suggest that maintaining aerobic capacity during early and middle adulthood may have benefits that extend well into later life.

More Evidence That Staying Active Pays Off

Other research has reinforced the idea that exercise remains valuable even when physical performance inevitably declines with age.

A 2026 study published in GeroScience followed 82 older adults who continued cycling over a nine-year period. Despite maintaining active lifestyles, participants experienced an average 19.7 percent decline in peak aerobic capacity.

Yet their fitness remained remarkably high. Nearly all participants who completed the exercise test performed at or above the 90th percentile for their age when compared with reference populations.

The findings suggest that although regular exercise cannot eliminate biological aging, it can help people maintain unusually high levels of physical function as they get older.

Another 2026 study in The American Journal of Medicine followed 49 men ages 60 to 82 who had not previously participated in structured exercise training.

Over four years, participants who performed weekly resistance exercises improved their upper and lower body strength. Those who combined resistance training with aerobic exercise also improved their aerobic fitness.

Although the study was relatively small and participants chose their training programs, its findings support the idea that meaningful physical improvements remain possible well into older adulthood.

It's Never Too Late to Improve Your Fitness

The original Swedish research is continuing, with investigators planning another round of assessments when participants reach age 68.

The next stage is intended to explore how long-term changes in physical performance relate to lifestyle, overall health, and the biological processes responsible for aging.

Together, the findings point to an important distinction between getting older and becoming physically inactive. Some loss of fitness and strength appears to be a natural part of aging, but the extent of that decline can vary substantially.

While the body may begin losing some of its physical capabilities around age 35, those changes do not eliminate the benefits of exercise. Whether someone begins exercising in their 30s, 50s, or later, becoming more active can still help improve physical performance and preserve the abilities needed for a healthier, more independent life.

The Independent Payment Advisory Board, which was supposed to force automatic Medicare cuts if spending exceeded target growth rates, was repealed in 2018 without ever becoming operational.

The “Cadillac tax” — an important way to discourage excessively generous insurance and a reform I vigorously supported — was repeatedly delayed and ultimately repealed in 2019.

As lawmakers chipped away at the ACA, the political push to find new ways to spend health care dollars more wisely seemingly began to flag. Efforts to move health care away from fee-for-service models and to encourage greater efficiencies had mixed results at best.

But the big mistake, ironically, was the ACA’s lack of focus on affordability. The ACA did nothing to slow — and indeed likely accelerated — the trend toward consolidation in health care, which drove prices higher. The ACA’s design and compliance costs favored large insurance carriers, and the ACA exchanges’ narrow-network model gave insurers reason to bulk up for negotiating leverage against providers. The ACA goal of improving care coordination helped hospitals justify stepped-up mergers and acquisitions of physician practices, thereby strengthening market power.

As costs kept climbing, many health economists were reluctant to probe the wasteful spending that accompanied the consolidation frenzy. This work was chilled by the ascendent political viewpoint that attempts to rationalize expensive end-of-life care would result in “death panels” — a term effectively coined and leveraged by former Alaska Gov. Sarah Palin (R).

It embodied a distrust of government involvement in medicine and a suspicion that experts would value some lives more highly than others. But that is already what we do. Some medical treatments are more valuable than others. I wrote a New York Times opinion piece in 2009 titled “I am a Death Panelist. I Am Not the Problem.” That essay has aged better than my letter to Congress. In it, I argued that cost containment efforts must prioritize high-value, life-improving treatments over endless, ineffective interventions.

Seventeen years and trillions of dollars later, that idea has never been more important.

Health care reform proposals like “Medicare for All” are doomed to fail if we can’t contain costs. The current administration’s focus on rooting out fraud is a good start. But more needs to be done. Armed with new tools to better measure what patients want and identify valuable innovation, we can discourage the 30% of care that does no good without heavy-handed government interventions such as rationing or price controls.

The USC Schaeffer Center for Health Policy & Economics and the Aspen Institute jointly recommended in 2021 that Congress establish an independent Institute of Health Technology Assessment. The institute would bring together leading scientists, economists, physicians, and, importantly, patients to evaluate new and existing evidence on the effectiveness and value of devices, medicines, procedures, and other innovations that end up on a medical bill. The idea would be to equip all parties, from payers to providers to patients, with insights into effectiveness, cost, and value. The independent, publicly funded institute should be set up without regulatory authority — for instance, it cannot set prices — but in a way that can nevertheless influence private and public decisions about clinical care.

The ACA also came up short here. It set up the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI), which has awarded more than $5 billion to support over 2,600 research studies and projects across the United States. One study showed that antibiotics could prevent half of all appendectomies, resulting in an estimated 27,410 fewer surgeries over five years.

Unfortunately, in the wake of the death panel debate, Congress was so fearful that PCORI could be used to ration care that it prohibited the institute from conducting formal cost-effectiveness analyses. It cannot try to quantify health benefits or rank treatments on a “cost-per-dollar” basis.

That restriction starkly illustrates the challenges that continue to bedevil the movement to put value at the center of health care policy. We are unlikely to get control of costs unless we can see what procedures and technologies are worth to patients and society in dollars over time.

I stopped endorsing legislation after the ACA passed. But if anyone introduces a bill to establish an Institute of Heath Technology Assessment, or to unleash PCORI to do cost-benefit analysis, I’m ready to sign.

Dana Goldman is the founding director of the USC Schaeffer Institute for Public Policy and Government Service.

#5004. Strokes have nearly doubled among younger adults, and scientists aren't sure why

Strokes are becoming increasingly common among younger adults, challenging the long-held belief that they primarily affect older people. A study examining nearly three decades of medical data found that stroke rates among adults ages 20 to 54 nearly doubled, even as the chances of surviving a stroke improved.

The findings, published September 23, 2026, in Neurology, the medical journal of the American Academy of Neurology, reveal a concerning trend in the Greater Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky region. Although more people are surviving strokes, the growing number of cases means that more younger adults could face lasting health complications.

"While stroke has traditionally been considered a disease of older adults, our findings show that stroke is becoming increasingly common in younger adults," said study author Emily R. Fisher, MD, of the University of Cincinnati. "At the same time, more people are surviving these strokes, which means more younger adults may be living with the long-term effects of stroke."

Stroke Rates Nearly Doubled Over Three Decades

To investigate how stroke patterns have changed, researchers examined cases recorded in Greater Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky between 1993 and 2020. They identified 2,076 adults between the ages of 20 and 54 who experienced their first stroke during the study period.

The results showed a striking increase. In 1993-1994, the stroke rate was 33.9 cases per 100,000 person-years. By 2020, it had climbed to 62.2 cases per 100,000 person-years, representing an increase of approximately 83%.

Person-years is a measurement that accounts for both the number of people being studied and the amount of time they are observed. It allows researchers to compare how frequently strokes occurred across different periods.

The increase was not spread evenly across all types of stroke. Instead, one particular form accounted for most of the rise.

Researchers found that ischemic strokes were largely responsible for the growing number of cases. This type of stroke happens when a blood vessel supplying the brain becomes blocked, cutting off oxygen and nutrients to brain tissue. Without a steady blood supply, brain cells can begin to suffer damage within minutes.

Among younger adults, the ischemic stroke rate rose from 23.8 cases per 100,000 person-years in 1993-1994 to 47.3 in 2020, nearly doubling over the study period.

Other forms of stroke did not follow the same pattern. Researchers found no comparable increases in intracerebral hemorrhage, which involves bleeding directly into the brain, or subarachnoid hemorrhage, which occurs when bleeding develops in the space surrounding the brain.

High Blood Pressure, Diabetes, and Other Risk Factors

The researchers also examined health conditions and behaviors among younger adults who experienced strokes. Over time, several potential risk factors became more common, including high blood pressure, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, and substance use.

Atrial fibrillation is a type of irregular heartbeat that can allow blood clots to form in the heart. If a clot travels to the brain and blocks a blood vessel, it can trigger an ischemic stroke.

One of the largest changes involved substance use. In 1993-1994, 4.6% of younger adults who experienced strokes had documented substance use. By 2020, that figure had risen to 40.2%, with much of the increase associated with marijuana use.

However, the study could not establish whether these changes were responsible for the rising stroke rates. The researchers lacked comparable health information from people who had not experienced strokes, making it impossible to determine how much the observed risk factor trends contributed to the increase.

Stroke Survival Is Improving, but Concerns Remain

Despite the troubling rise in stroke cases, the findings also offered some encouraging news. Younger adults who experienced strokes were somewhat more likely to survive than they had been nearly three decades earlier.

After accounting for differences in age, race, and sex, researchers found that the proportion of patients who died within 30 days of a stroke declined from 11.7% in 1993-1994 to 9.4% in 2020.

The improvement was most noticeable among patients with intracerebral hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage, two types of bleeding strokes that can be particularly dangerous.

Still, surviving a stroke does not necessarily mean making a complete recovery. Depending on the location and severity of the brain injury, survivors can experience lasting difficulties with movement, speech, memory, or other abilities. As strokes become more frequent among younger adults, more people may have to manage these challenges for decades.

"Our findings highlight the importance of identifying and managing stroke risk factors early in adulthood," Fisher said. "Additional research is needed to better understand what is driving these trends and how they can be reversed."

The researchers emphasized that further investigation is necessary to explain why stroke rates have climbed so sharply among younger adults. Because the analysis focused on one geographic region and lacked a comparison group without strokes, it cannot establish whether the same trends apply nationwide or identify the factors responsible for the increase.

The study was supported by the Greater Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky Stroke Study.
