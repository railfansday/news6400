#4598. Acquirers Are Snapping Up Boom-Era Startups That Haven’t Raised Funding In Years

When a venture-backed startup goes a few years without raising a new round, it’s common to presume its odds of a sizable exit have gone down as well.

But that’s often not the case, an analysis of Crunchbase data shows. This year, a number of larger acquisitions of private companies involved startups that haven’t disclosed a new investment for more than four years.

Several of these acquisitions are quite recent — and in some cases, quite large. The standout is Uber’s purchase earlier this month of ezCater, a platform for catering and workplace meals, for $2.3 billion in cash. The deal comes nearly five years after Boston-based ezCater raised its last reported venture funding round.

That’s not the only one. A number of companies whose names might feature on a playlist of 2021 boom-era unicorns have sold to acquirers in recent months, including one-time highfliers like Airtable, Headspace and Miro. In many cases, companies are selling at valuations which, while not puny, are still far below their former peaks.

To illustrate the extent to which these aging startups are finding acquirers, we used Crunchbase data to put together a list of 15 deals involving well-funded companies that haven’t raised equity funding for four or more years.

One big issue for older and middle-aged startups is that valuations hit a cyclical peak in 2021 and early 2022. Many companies that raised during that time had difficulty attracting fresh capital or were reluctant to close new rounds at reduced valuations. Even with the more recent AI boom, little went to this cohort.

Moreover, since the 2021 boom-era rounds were quite large, funded startups commonly had the option to cut operating costs and extend their runways with existing capital. As a result, quite a few companies that haven’t raised rounds in years are still chugging along, managing to survive and even grow without more venture funding.

EzCater is a case in point. In the year prior to its acquisition, the business generated more than $2.5 billion in gross bookings. It had an annual growth rate in the high teens, with average order sizes over $400.

That said, even that revenue and growth rate didn’t secure ezCater much of an acquisition premium from its last funding round. At $2.3 billion, Uber’s purchase price is 53% above the $1.5 billion pre-money valuation the catering startup secured in its last funding round in late 2021.

In many cases, even good-sized acquisitions of well-funded startups are occurring at valuations far below former peaks.

Take Miro, a provider of online collaboration tools that agreed to sell to Bending Spoons last month in a deal valued around $1.36 billion. That’s a huge step down from early 2022, when San Francisco-based Miro closed its last round at a $17.5 billion post-money valuation.

Or consider Bending Spoons’ prior purchase of data and workflow platform Airtable, for $1.28 billion in August. That’s barely over one-tenth the $11 billion valuation Airtable secured in its December 2021 Series F.

Fashion startup Everlane is another that lost its shimmer after securing a 2020 valuation in the hundreds of millions. It sold to fast-fashion leader Shein this spring for $100 million.

In the health and wellness sector, meanwhile, Headspace also saw its valuation plunge. The meditation and mental health app sold to Sword Health in a deal reportedly valued around $300 million. That’s well below its peak post-money valuation of around $2.6 billion.

While startup investors aren’t celebrating acquisitions made far under peak private valuations, they’re not the worst outcome. And for startups themselves, they’re also far from a catastrophic scenario.

Of course, every unicorn investor hopes for high-multiple returns. But for startups that go years without funding, the market seems to be telling us that’s not likely in the cards.

#4595. Dormant Bitcoin Wallet Awakens After 10.7 Years As 500 BTC Value Jumps 212x

A dormant Bitcoin (BTC) wallet transferred 500 BTC, worth about $41.54 million, to Coinbase Prime after 10.7 years of inactivity.

Whale Alert flagged the transaction Tuesday evening, noting the stash had increased in value more than 212 times since 2016.

Meanwhile, blockchain analytics firm Arkham Intelligence attributed the wallet to Irish cannabis cultivator Clifton Collins, whose reported 6,000 BTC fortune has been the subject of Irish authorities’ recovery efforts.

Arkham says the 500 BTC was transferred to an address identified as a Coinbase Prime deposit wallet. Coinbase Prime provides institutional cryptocurrency trading and custody services.

Notably, the transaction marks the fifth 500 BTC movement from Collins-linked wallets in 2026, bringing the total moved this year to 2,500 BTC, Arkham reported.

Irish authorities recovered 500 BTC in March, followed by further recoveries in May and July. Another 500 BTC moved to Coinbase Prime in August, preceding Tuesday’s transaction.

However, the latest transfer does not show whether the Bitcoin was sold or who initiated it. Identifying the destination as a Coinbase Prime address also does not confirm that the coins were liquidated.

Collins, a former beekeeper from Dublin, accumulated approximately 6,000 BTC in late 2011 and early 2012 through his cannabis business, when Bitcoin traded at just a few dollars per coin.

He reportedly divided the holdings among 12 wallets, each containing 500 BTC, to protect his fortune from hackers. Collins printed the private keys on paper and hid them inside the aluminum cap of a fishing rod case at a rented property in County Galway.

In 2020, police arrested him after discovering cannabis in his car, after which he served five years in prison.

During his imprisonment, authorities cleared out the rented property, and the fishing rod case containing the keys was reportedly discarded. The missing keys left the Bitcoin inaccessible, turning the fortune into one of Ireland’s most notable cryptocurrency recovery cases.

Ireland’s Criminal Assets Bureau announced in March 2026 that it had accessed a previously inaccessible 500 BTC wallet with assistance from Europol’s European Cybercrime Center.

The latest movement adds to the series of transactions involving Collins-linked holdings, although the circumstances behind Tuesday’s transfer remain unclear.

Of note, the 2,500 BTC moved this year represents roughly 42% of the reported 6,000 BTC fortune, leaving approximately 3,500 BTC potentially remaining across the wider stash.

The transaction also comes amid renewed activity among other long-dormant Bitcoin holders. A separate wallet transferred 411.45 BTC, worth about $34 million, on Sunday after more than a decade of inactivity. Two other wallets moved a combined 417 BTC a day earlier.

These transfers have accelerated since September and continued into October, raising the possibility that some holders are preparing to sell, adjusting custody arrangements, or moving funds between wallets for security reasons.

At press time, Bitcoin was trading at $84,669, up 2.62% in the past 24 hours.

#4592. What Solana’s Alpenglow Switch Actually Changes at Slot 459,653,000

AI-assisted summary, reviewed by NullTX editors.

For about 400 milliseconds this afternoon, Solana will stop landing user transactions. That isn't a failure.

It's the scheduled handoff between two consensus systems, and it's probably the only "400 ms" figure that matters today.

Many people heading into the Alpenglow launch still treat 400 ms block times and 12.8-second finality as the bar to beat. Those are the old chain's numbers. They describe TowerBFT, the system Alpenglow is replacing, so Alpenglow shouldn't be checked against them. If you go through Solana's own documentation, you'll find the right benchmarks before the first post-launch numbers arrive.

According to Solana's official Alpenglow page, the upgrade's feature gate activates at the start of epoch 1064 on Wednesday, October 21, at about 13:20 UTC. The Solana Foundation says to treat that time as an estimate and to watch the epoch instead.

The new consensus doesn't take over the moment the epoch begins. TowerBFT keeps running for the first 5,000 slots, and Alpenglow takes control at slot 459,653,000. Anza, the team behind the main Agave client, put that switch at around 14:00 UTC.

That time isn't fixed. Solana Compass calculated that with about 375,000 slots left before the switch, each millisecond of drift in average slot time moves the boundary by roughly six minutes. Using epoch 1062's pace, it estimated the switch at about 14:02 UTC.

The transition itself has a cost. Solana Compass reports a gap of roughly two slots, about 400 ms, during which blocks carry only votes and user transactions don't land. So the 400 ms everyone is watching for will turn up, just as a pause rather than a block time.

This is where much of today's coverage gets it wrong.

Block time first. Solana's page says slots moved from 400 ms to 200 ms in a separate "reduced slot times" upgrade, which finished this month. That change isn't part of Alpenglow. It was already running before today. Solana Compass measured epoch 1062 averaging 217.7 ms per slot over 26.1 hours. Checking Alpenglow against 400 ms would mean grading it against a number the network had already beaten before the switch.

Then finality. The 12.8 seconds is TowerBFT's figure. Under that system, a block became final only after enough votes built up over 32 slots. Alpenglow's stated target is about 150 ms, which Solana describes as 99% faster than TowerBFT.

Solana is careful about that 150 ms figure, and I think readers should be too. The Foundation calls it an expectation, not a constant, and says one-round finality comes in lower. Galaxy's engineers, quoted by Solana Compass, call it a design target rather than a measured result. As I noted when looking at how CertiK verifies a TPS claim, a target is a claim until someone measures it on the live network.

Alpenglow is Solana's first replacement of its core consensus since TowerBFT, and today's launch only covers part of it.

The first phase, Votor, ships in Agave 4.3. Validators now send votes directly to each other and combine them into certificates, instead of submitting votes as on-chain transactions. Per Solana's page, votes are no longer transactions and no longer appear in blocks. A block can finalize in one round if at least 80% of stake notarizes it. Otherwise, a second round uses 60% thresholds.

Proof of History no longer paces block production. Execution stays the same: the SVM, programs, transactions and fees don't change.

The second phase, Rotor, will replace Turbine, Solana's block propagation layer. Solana lists it as "not yet scheduled." If someone credits today's numbers to "Alpenglow" as a whole, half of the design hasn't shipped yet.

The security trade-off is also worth knowing. TowerBFT needed more than two-thirds of stake for confirmation. Alpenglow tolerates 20% malicious stake plus 20% offline stake. Solana Compass notes that slashing for double voting is defined but not enabled in this first version.

Validators also pay differently. TowerBFT voting cost about 1 SOL per epoch in vote transaction fees. Alpenglow replaces that with a flat 0.8 SOL per epoch Validator Admission Ticket (SIMD-0357), which is burned. A validator that can't pay doesn't vote or produce blocks in the next epoch.

In my view, this is the detail most likely to affect today's real numbers.

Alpenglow's fastest finality depends on the one-round fast path, which needs at least 80% of stake to notarize a block. On the morning of October 20, a tracker run by Pumpkin's Pool, cited by SolanaFloor and reported by Solana Compass, flagged 151 validators holding 20.3% of stake as unprepared. The day before, that figure was 45%.

The math is uncomfortable. If 20.3% of stake really couldn't vote, only 79.7% would be left, which is just short of the fast-path threshold. In that case, blocks would need the slower second round, and finality would land above the headline figure.

I expect that number to drop before the switch, since operators have been upgrading quickly. But it's the main reason I wouldn't trust any finality figure from the first hour without also checking how much stake is actually voting. Validators without a registered BLS key, or that miss the required client version, stop voting entirely. Their delegators also stop earning inflation rewards.

There's already one public reference point. Solana's page links to a community cluster explorer run by Valid Blocks, where Alpenglow has been running on Agave 4.3.2.

When I checked this morning, it showed an average finalization time of 131 ms over the past hour, ranging from 111 ms to 156 ms. Average slot time was 215 ms. That's inside Solana's target range.

That cluster had 62 active validators and about 10.2 million SOL staked. Mainnet is far larger, more spread out geographically, and runs under real load. Test-cluster results are a strong sign, but they aren't proof of how mainnet will perform.

You don't need to wait for a press release to know whether the switch happened. Solana's getAgGenesisCert RPC method returns null while the cluster is still on TowerBFT and returns a genesis certificate once Alpenglow is live. Nodes older than Agave 4.3 return a "Method not found" error, which shouldn't be confused with null.

The extrasolar planet, or exoplanet, under the watchful infrared eye of the JWST, is WASP-17b, located around 1,300 light-years away. The exoplanet has a mass around 0.8 times that of Jupiter, but it is around twice as wide as the solar system's largest planet, giving it a "super-puff" consistency comparable to the Earth-based treat. WASP-17 b is so close to its parent star that it completes an orbit in under four Earth days, with this proximity also making the temperature of this "hot Jupiter" skyrocket.

Given the extreme conditions of this exoplanet, it is little wonder that its atmosphere is pretty unusual too. Using a new technique called transmission strings, a team of scientists mapped the contours of the atmosphere of WASP-17b. They found that the atmosphere is shaped like a ring with prominent "gems" rising from it.

"Planets are temperamental, their atmospheres complex and chaotic," team member Hannah Wakeford of the University of Bristol said in a statement. "The fact that all of these numbers came together and showed us this very structured, dynamical picture of this giant world over 1,000 light-years away was just really exciting."

WASP-17b is so close to its parent star that it is tidally locked, meaning one side permanently faces the star; its dayside is constantly bombarded with stellar radiation. In contrast, the planet also has a cooler nightside, which faces out into space in perpetuity.

By creating incredibly comprehensive 3D views of this exoplanet's atmosphere, researchers found that WASP-17b's atmosphere has a chemical composition and temperature that shift between dayside and nightside according to theory and modelling.

This was possible because the transmission strings technique tracked WASP-17b as it crossed or transited in front of its star, capturing the composition of light around the edge of the planet's atmosphere, or limb, from different angles. This method trumps standard approaches because traditional modelling only maps light as an average across the entire limb.

"Rather than assuming your atmosphere is a circle with the same radius all the way around, our technique allows you to pull out different distances from the center to the edge of the planet," Lili Alderson, of Cornell University, said. "We're able to go around the edge.

"So, we've gone from a ring to understanding where on the ring the gemstones are, so to speak."

The modelling showed just such a "gem," that occurs where hot winds from the planet reach the equator and the nightside of WASP-17b, the atmosphere of the cooler side inflates.

"Theory predicts that the morning and the evening sides should have a strong temperature difference, and WASP-17b fits really nicely in that trend – pretty much bang-on what we thought it would be," Alderson said.

The successful application of the transmission strings method is an exciting step forward in the modelling of the atmospheres of the over 6,000 worlds in NASA's exoplanet catalog."This is proof of concept that planets are, in fact, 3D objects, and that, consistently, as they go around their star, what we predict should be happening is happening," Alderson concluded. Fusion startup Helion has been awarded $175 million in federal funding to research, develop, and manufacture advanced semiconductors and power electronics for its fusion power plants, the company announced on Thursday.

Currently, those devices lose about 5% to 10% of the electricity that flows through them. While those losses seem small, they can compound themselves in a complex system like Helion’s.

The new production line, to be built in Washington State, will be the first in the U.S. to make a class of semiconductor that is used not just in fusion power, but in manufacturing, wastewater treatment, medicine, and defense.

“There are no U.S. manufacturers right now that do it,” Kirtley said. “This is absolutely enabling going from zero to 100 for advanced, high-voltage, and pulsed semiconductors here in the United States.”

The three-year award was made by the Department of Commerce under the CHIPS Act, a Biden-era law that seeks to bolster semiconductor module research, development, and manufacturing in the U.S. Alongside the federal funding, Helion also announced a fresh $50 million private investment, though it didn’t disclose the investor. Kirtley acknowledged that the R&D funded by the award won’t make its way into Orion in time for the launch — federal contracts can take months to negotiate — but he said they promise to help improve the efficiency of subsequent Helion power plants, which would improve their competitiveness in cutthroat electricity markets.

Semiconductor efficiency is key to Helion’s approach to fusion, which harvests electricity directly from the fusion reactions rather than using them to heat steam to spin a turbine.

The startup is developing a reactor known as a field-reversed configuration. Fusion fuel gets injected into both ends of an hourglass-like chamber, where it’s heated to form a plasma. The plasmas are then accelerated toward each other using magnetic fields. When they collide, another magnetic field compresses the ball further, raising the temperature to over 100 million degrees C.

At those temperatures and pressures, atoms in the fusion fuel start fusing, releasing energy in the process, and pushing the plasma outward. This force pushes against the magnetic field, and that energy is captured by the magnets and capacitors and turned into electricity — think of how a hybrid car or EV recharges the battery by using electric motors to slow the vehicle.

Helion says that approach, known as direct energy conversion, will help future reactors produce more power than they need to operate, a milestone that any fusion power plant must reach if it is to be commercially viable. Efficient power electronics are a key part of the process.

“One of the enabling parts of our technology that allows us to do this direct electricity recovery — putting electricity into fusion and at a very high efficiency, recovering it at high speed — is the modern semiconductor,” Kirtley said. “We’re going to take that to the next level here, increase the efficiency.”

The defense industry is likely to be another beneficiary. Energy weapons have garnered fresh interest as drones have swarmed battlefields and overwhelmed existing air defense weapons. The Orionids peak around Oct. 21. Here's when and where to look for the best chance of spotting meteors.

If you love watching meteors, 2026 has given you plenty to enjoy. This summer, the annual Perseid meteor shower peaked on the day of a new moon — and a solar eclipse — so moonlight stayed out of the way. And there's more to look forward to: in December, the Geminids, the year's most prolific shower, will peak after a waxing crescent moon sets early in the evening, opening the door to excellent viewing.

Right between those two headline showers, the dependable Orionids take their turn, reaching peak activity around Oct. 21-22.

So, when and where can you catch the show?

The Orionids take their name from the spot where they seem to fan out: Orion's raised club, just above and to the left of the reddish star Betelgeuse. Orion is usually a winter favorite, so in early autumn the mighty hunter doesn't fully clear the eastern horizon until after 11 p.m. local daylight time. Don't rush outside just yet though. Wait until around 2 a.m., when Orion is well above the horizon. By about 5 a.m., just before dawn, Orion reaches its highest point in the southern sky — and the view should be at its best.

Orionid meteors tend to be faint, so city lights can easily wash them out. If you can, head to a safe, dark rural spot. Get comfortable in a reclining lawn chair or sleeping bag, give your eyes time to adjust, and enjoy the view overhead and toward the southern sky.

And what about the moon? A full moon arrives October 25-26, but there's still a welcome stretch of dark sky for several mornings before Wednesday.

By Oct. 21-22, the waxing gibbous moon will be resting among the dim stars of Capricornus, reaching its highest point around 8:45 p.m. local daylight time. Here's the good news: it will set shortly after 2 a.m., leaving roughly 3½ hours of dark sky before dawn. In that window, one observer may spot as many as 15 to 20 Orionids per hour. Because those hours line up with the shower's peak, the moon shouldn't steal the show this year.

So, where do the Orionids come from? Their story begins with Halley's Comet—the same famous visitor behind May's Eta Aquarids.

Meteoroids are tiny leftovers from a comet's nucleus. As a comet nears the sun, frozen material on its surface turns directly into gas, a process known as sublimation. The comet becomes a kind of cosmic litterbug, sprinkling dust and debris along its orbit. Those pieces are tiny and brittle, with the consistency of cigar ash, and every swing past the sun leaves more behind.

Comets are among the solar system's oldest and most fascinating objects. They formed alongside the sun and planets about 4.6 billion years ago, yet they have changed remarkably little. Think of them as time capsules from the solar system's childhood. Far beyond the outer planets, they spend most of their time in a deep freeze, where temperatures hover just above absolute zero, -459.67°F (-273.15°C). In that extreme cold, chemical reactions largely stop, helping comets preserve material from those earliest days.

Over the past 100,000 years, Halley's Comet has probably circled the sun well over a thousand times, leaving a fresh trail of debris on every visit. Each October and May, Earth brushes close to Halley's orbit. Some of that dust meets our atmosphere at a breathtaking 41 miles (66 km) per second, lighting up as the streaks we call "shooting stars."

The Orionids have another lovely feature: about half of these meteors leave glowing trails that linger longer than those of equally bright meteors. Halley's composition may be the reason. The particles begin burning up about 80 miles (130 km) above Earth, perhaps because they're made of lightweight material from the comet's diffuse surface rather than its core.

Perhaps you remember seeing Halley's Comet when it last swept through the inner solar system in late winter and early spring of 1986. With an orbital period of 75.4 years, this celebrated visitor is due back in late July or early August 2061.

For a little perspective, average life expectancy is 79.5 years in the United States, 82.2 years in the United Kingdom, and 83.4 years in Canada. So, if you were born after 1980, there's a fair chance you'll be here to welcome the "mother of all Orionids" home in 2061.

As for me, I saw Halley in 1986 at the "advanced age" of 30. The odds of another reunion are pretty long — you can do the math.

Until then, I'll be more than happy to step outside early on Wednesday morning and greet a few of "Halley's children." I hope you'll join me.

The consequence is that voting power is purchasable at any moment, including immediately before a contentious vote, and including by someone with no interest in the protocol beyond the outcome of that vote. Governance attacks of this shape have been executed repeatedly.

The alternative is a non-transferable staking derivative. Stake the liquid token, receive a governance token that cannot be sold, lent or borrowed. Voting weight then has to be accumulated by locking, which means it can only be held by parties who have accepted the opportunity cost of holding.

EVEDEX’s published design for $EVE follows this shape. Staked EVE converts one-to-one into stEVE, which is non-transferable and carries the voting rights in the EVEDEX DAO. It is a straightforward mechanism, and its main effect is to make governance capture expensive in time rather than merely expensive in capital.

The second change is in how unstaking works, and it is more consequential than it sounds.

Lockups have traditionally been binary. Funds are locked for a term, then they are not. This produces the unlock-cliff dynamic familiar from every vesting chart: nothing happens for months, then a large tranche becomes liquid on a known date, and the market prices it in advance.

The newer approach makes exit continuously available but progressively priced. Under the $EVE design, unstaking runs linearly across a year, updating continuously, and holders can withdraw the unlocked portion at any point. Exiting early carries a penalty of up to 50%, declining linearly to zero as the period completes.

The third shift is the most important and the least visible in marketing material.

Emissions-funded staking rewards are circular. The protocol pays stakers in newly issued tokens, the yield looks attractive, more tokens are staked, and the source of the yield is dilution of the same holders receiving it. The advertised APY is a number the protocol chooses, not a number it earns.

Revenue-indexed rewards work differently. The $EVE design explicitly does not express staking rewards as a fixed annual rate; returns are tied to protocol fee generation and to each staker’s share of total staked supply. That produces a lower and more variable headline number, which is commercially unhelpful and analytically honest. It also means the token’s return profile is a function of whether the exchange actually does business — which, for a venue reporting more than 74,000 registered traders and monthly volume above $15B, is at least a testable proposition.

None of this addresses the central asymmetry, which is allocation.

$EVE has a fixed supply of 100 million, with 31% to treasury and DAO, 18% to the core team, 15% to partnerships, 12% to liquidity provision and vaults, 10% to seed and private rounds, 8% to network incentives and 6% distributed retroactively to early users based on accumulated points and badges. That 6% figure is roughly in line with what comparable programmes have distributed, and it is worth stating plainly: retroactive community allocations in this sector are small relative to insider allocations, and elegant staking mechanics do not change that arithmetic.

The other unresolved question is timing. The $EVE token generation event is scheduled for a window of approximately six to eighteen months after mainnet launch — a range wide enough that the market conditions at TGE are unknowable, and unlock schedules for team and investor tranches are the detail that will determine how the design performs in practice.

The direction of travel is real, though. Exchange tokens are being built to reward staying rather than to reward arriving. Whether the mechanisms hold when the first serious drawdown tests them is the part nobody can model in advance.

The astronauts of SpaceX's Crew-12 mission to the International Space Station wish that we could all see Earth as they just did.

An orbital view reveals the immense beauty of our blue planet, the only world we know of that hosts life. But it also highlights how fragile that life is, tethered to our lonely Earth in the vast void of space, according to Crew-12 commander Jessica Meir of NASA.

"You see this very thin, tenuous band of an atmosphere, and you know that that is all that is supporting life, and we really need to do our best to protect it," Meir said on Thursday afternoon (Oct. 15), during a press conference that discussed the recently concluded Crew-12 mission.

"I wish that I could bring all the eyes of all the humans to look back at the Earth in this extraordinary way that we are so privileged to have," she added. "I think it really does change you philosophically and [in] how you see each other, how you see yourself, how you see the human race when you look on it down from the vantage point that we have from above — you know, [it] gives everybody this understanding that we need to protect our planet, that it's not only so beautiful, but it's so precious."

From the International Space Station (ISS), Meir said, it's clear that everything on Earth is connected — and that the borders we've laid down between states and nations are artificial constructs.

"You realize that we really are one human when you are in space," she said. "I wish I could share that perspective with all the eyes of all the humans. I think it would be a very healthy thing for everyone to understand.

Crew-12 mission specialist Andrey Fedyaev, of the Russian space agency Roscosmos, voiced similar sentiments, though he would turn an ISS visitor's eyes inward, to focus on the crew.

"I always say that the life of crewmembers on board the space station is the best example of how people need to live on Earth," Fedyaev said during Thursday's briefing (in Russian; in interpreter translated his words into English in real time)."

"We don't have borders. We are all friendly. We're just one crew, and the safety of the crew, the safety of the station, depends on the actions of the entire crew," he added. "So, if humankind does arrive at the same conclusion — that our Earth, our planet, is one and the same space vessel that flies through space — we need to take care of it and not to destroy it. Meir and Fedyaev are far from the only ones to have their perpsectives shifted by a space mission. Pretty much every astronaut experiences this phenomenon, which is known as the overview effect, a term coined in 1987 by space philosopher Frank White.

The quartet launched toward the ISS on Feb. 13 atop a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket. Their Crew Dragon capsule, named Freedom, arrived at the orbiting lab a day later.
