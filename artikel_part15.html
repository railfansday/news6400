#5699. Greenland’s ice vanished 416,000 years ago, and ancient sediment reveals why

Ancient sediment from beneath Greenland's ice reveals how much warmer summers were 416,000 years ago, offering new clues about the ice sheet's future.

Sediment pulled from beneath the Greenland Ice Sheet at a former U.S. military base has yielded the first direct measurement of summer temperatures at the site during an ancient warm period.

Summers were 4.7°C warmer than today, and the ice was gone.

The study was conducted by John Michael Aguilar, Elizabeth Thomas, and colleagues at the University at Buffalo and the University of Vermont.

Camp Century was built by the US Army in the 1950s-1960s under the cover of a research station.

Its secret purpose was Project Iceworm – installing nuclear-armed missiles inside tunnels drilled through the ice sheet.

The drilling program failed, but it did produce one unintended scientific legacy.

A core of sediment extracted from nearly a mile beneath the ice represented material that had been sitting at the ice-bed interface since before the ice arrived.

Those samples were brought to the University at Buffalo in 1975 by Chester Langway. He was the glaciologist who led the drilling operation.

After his retirement in the mid-1990s, they were transferred to the University of Copenhagen. There they sat largely forgotten until a chance freezer cleanout in 2018 returned them to scientific attention.

Thomas and her team have been working through them ever since.

This study is the first using the Camp Century sediment to reconstruct actual summer temperatures during the ice-free period.

The technique relies on lipid biomarkers – fatty molecules produced by bacteria and preserved in the sediment over geological time.

The types and proportions of these molecules shift depending on the temperatures at which the bacteria were living.

By comparing them against equivalent molecules found in modern Arctic lakes, the researchers were able to work backwards to a temperature estimate.

“The types and proportions of these molecules change depending on the temperature,” Aguilar said.

“By comparing them with similar molecules found in modern Arctic lakes, we were able to estimate how warm it was at Camp Century.”

The result, for the period approximately 416,000 years ago known as Marine Isotope Stage 11, was 4.7°C warmer than modern summers, with an uncertainty of ±3.2°C.

For the site to have been ice-free, the Greenland Ice Sheet would have had to retreat more than 60 miles from its present edge in that region.

The result came as something of a surprise, and not entirely for the obvious reason. Greenland experienced similarly warm summers roughly 5,000 to 10,000 years ago.

During that interval, Camp Century remained covered by ice. Yet 416,000 years ago, at similar temperatures, the ice was gone.

The researchers’ explanation points to time. The warm conditions of Marine Isotope Stage 11 may have persisted for tens of thousands of years.

That would give the ice sheet far longer time to respond than the relatively brief Holocene warm period provided.

Ice sheets don’t melt on the same timescale as temperatures rise; they need sustained heat to lose mass at scale.

“It’s striking that a period of moderately warmer temperatures coincided with such a substantial retreat of the ice sheet,” Aguilar said.

The warm period 416,000 years ago was driven by slow, long natural orbital cycles. The warming underway today is not.

“Today’s human-caused warming is more intense than the warm period 400,000 years ago,” Thomas said.

“And climate projections suggest that this warmth could persist for hundreds or thousands of years. Both the magnitude and duration of warming are important to understanding how the ice sheet will respond.”

The Greenland Ice Sheet holds the equivalent of about 7.4 meters of global sea level rise. It is not going to melt overnight, and the Camp Century findings don’t predict a timetable.

What they do is establish a data point. Under conditions of sustained moderate warming the ice sheet in northwestern Greenland was capable of substantial retreat.

That’s a constraint on how the models should behave, and what they should be prepared to accommodate.

The analysis itself, though based on techniques labs use routinely, turned out to be anything but routine.

Comparing temperatures across such different time periods required accounting for changes in land elevation as the ice sheet grew and melted. Such a correction that took years to work through properly.

“What may look like a routine analysis actually took years to figure out,” Thomas said. “It’s been quite a journey, not unlike the journey these sediment samples took to get here.”

The samples were recovered during a failed military program, forgotten in a freezer, and rediscovered by accident decades later.

They have now provided scientists with the first quantitative estimate of land-based summer temperatures in Greenland during one of Earth’s most important ancient warm periods.

The ice sheet’s future depends on understanding its past and recognizing that what looks like moderate warming, given enough time, can move a lot of ice.

The study is published in the journal Climate of the Past.

Stimulating the brain with ultrasound could help counter the delusions people experience in schizophrenia, a small trial finds.

The trial involved transcranial ultrasound stimulation, in which a device placed on a patient's head delivers ultrasound waves through the skull and into the brain. Its goal is to change the activity in a targeted brain region.

Delusions are false beliefs that are very difficult to shake; for example, a person might believe they are being stalked when they aren't or think that a major disaster is about to occur without any evidence to suggest so. In the trial, researchers found that a 10-minute session of the ultrasound treatment reduced the severity of delusions for at least a week in patients with schizophrenia or a related disorder.

This targeted ultrasound treatment delivered directly to the brain could avoid some of the full-body effects of antipsychotic medications, such as weight gain and high blood pressure. That said, the new results, published Tuesday (Oct. 20) in the journal npj Acoustics, are a first step and will need further verification in larger trials.

"It's too early to know for certain what the therapeutic benefits will be, but it's very exciting," study co-author Dr. Donald Goff, a professor of psychiatry at the New York University Grossman School of Medicine, told Live Science.

The pilot study included 10 patients with either schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder. In both conditions, patients experience psychotic symptoms, including delusions and hallucinations. Schizoaffective disorder also includes mood symptoms, such as mania.

The researchers interviewed each participant to characterize the content of their delusions, and also documented their cognitive state and experiences with auditory hallucinations — perceptions of sounds that aren't actually occurring. The participants then received both an ultrasound treatment and a placebo treatment, spaced one week apart.

Other noninvasive therapies — such as transcranial magnetic stimulation, which uses magnetic fields — can also stimulate the brain. But the ultrasound technique differs from these therapies because ultrasound waves can penetrate deeper into the brain with greater specificity, study co-author Shy Shoham, a neural engineer at NYU Grossman, told Live Science. He compared the way a transducer directs ultrasound waves toward a given brain region with the way a lens focuses light.

In this case, the researchers focused this lens on a brain network called the cortico-striatal-pallido-thalamic circuit, which includes structures underneath the brain's outer cortex. The relationship between this circuit and schizophrenia symptoms is still somewhat unclear, partly because its deeper components are hard to study in adequate resolution with MRI, said co-author Dr. Wei Qi, a clinical assistant professor of psychiatry at NYU Grossman. However, some past studies have suggested that this network plays a part in schizophrenia.

The ultrasound treatment targeted a brain structure called the globus pallidus internus (orange).

Much of what scientists know about the network comes from research in movement disorders, such as Parkinson's disease, in which there is an imbalance between excitation and inhibition in the neurons that control movement. A similar dynamic could help to explain symptoms of schizophrenia, the scientists think.

"Our hypothesis is that, in schizophrenia, there is an imbalance between excitation and inhibition regarding what in the environment is actually important," Qi told Live Science. "The failure to inhibit irrelevant signals could be the basis of psychosis."

The researchers surveyed the study participants after each treatment, asking about the degree of belief each person had in their delusions and the amount of stress they caused. Compared with the placebo treatment, the ultrasound stimulation was associated with less-severe delusions over the following week. There was no significant change in patients' auditory hallucinations or overall cognition, though.

The ultrasound therapy came with mild side effects, such as fatigue and headache. Some patients had transient increases in delusions, which Qi attributed to normal fluctuations in symptoms that are expected in schizophrenia. These outcomes were just as common after the placebo treatment, so they may not be related to the ultrasound specifically.

The stimulation was aimed specifically at the right side of a brain structure called the globus pallidus internus (GPI). In a previous study, the authors found that greater connectivity between this region and another structure — the dorsal anterior cingulate gyrus — was linked to more-severe psychotic symptoms in schizophrenia. So, they made the GPI their target.

To date, "there haven't been many successful therapeutic attempts at targeting some of these deeper structures," said Dr. Deepak Sarpal, an associate professor of psychiatry at the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine who was not involved with the study. Ultrasound waves reach brain regions under the cortex in a less-invasive manner than deep brain stimulation, in which a surgeon implants an electrode in a patient's brain, Sarpal noted.

The researchers are not the only group using transcranial ultrasound to influence the cortico-striatal-pallido-thalamic circuit. Earlier this year, another group stimulated portions of the striatum, which is located directly under the cortex, in patients with schizophrenia. They evaluated hallucinations rather than delusions, finding that the treatment reduced symptoms for at least a week after the procedure.

In this line of research, the targeted nature of transcranial ultrasound presents advantages as a research tool as well as a treatment approach.

"If we find that a specific circuit or a node in a circuit seems to be closely linked to symptoms we're trying to treat, then it will be a matter of understanding what pattern of stimulation may allow us to correct that," Goff said. Qi, Goff and colleagues now plan to continue testing their technique on more patients and to experiment with different doses of ultrasound.

"A lot more work needs to be done," Goff said, "but we're really quite optimistic about its promise."

#5697. The Best Culinary Resorts in the United States

Where to go to rest, relax, recharge, and eat well while doing it

At some hotels, food is an afterthought: Breakfast buffets are bland, in-room mini bars are stocked with generic snacks, and lobby bars are lackluster. But other resorts understand that we don’t simply factor food into an itinerary; many people plan their entire trip around eating, hoping to get to the heart of local cuisine and dine at the area’s best restaurants without ever leaving the property.

These culinary resorts will help you build an entire vacation around great meals. If you want to get your hands dirty, resorts on working farms have guests harvesting produce that will end up on their dinner plates that night, and some properties send visitors off on fishing and foraging expeditions to find their own meals. Other spots keep it more low-key with award-winning restaurants, special wine tastings, and wellness-minded retreats serving homegrown juices. No matter how you want to engage with the food that will power your trip, these 10 culinary resorts are the best places in the U.S. to have a truly delicious stay.

Acura has always done things its own way. We believe the best experiences are created through intentional choices, thoughtful craftsmanship, and the confidence to pursue a different approach. This spirit is reflected in our philosophy of Precision Crafted Performance. Learn more.

Made up of 65 freestanding cottages, Wildflower Farms gives guests a taste of farm life — albeit an incredibly chic version. Guests can start the day with a complimentary tour of the 6-acre farm and a visit to feed the resort’s Icelandic sheep and chicken, then pick up a few eggs to bring over to the on-site restaurant Clay for an incredibly fresh breakfast. Other food-focused activities include suiting up in beekeeping gear to learn how to make honey, taking a guided foraging walk, or stirring up a cocktail using ingredients grown on-site in a class hosted in the resort’s cooking school barn.

Clay serves breakfast, lunch, and dinner in a cozy dining room that centers a massive wood-burning fireplace. The menu changes seasonally to feature lamb and pork raised just steps from the kitchen, plus a selection of the 14,000 pounds of fruit, vegetables, and herbs that the farm produces annually. The beverage program also has local roots: Wine director Vanessa Price has built a 7,000-bottle cellar that shines a light on New York’s often-overlooked Finger Lakes and North Fork wine regions, and ingredients like carrot eau de vie show up in martinis.

With just four cabins situated on 3,800 acres of working ranch, Quercus is a small culinary resort that punches well above its weight. Chiara Visconti di Modrone and her husband Angelos Pervanas originally launched Quercus as a regenerative farming project an hour outside Atlanta before teaming up with acclaimed Atlanta restaurateurs to morph it into a full-scale culinary escape. Now the all-inclusive experience features private saunas and cold plunge pools, holistic wellness consultations, and activities like horse training or kayaking in the Flint River.

Guests are encouraged to kick off their visit with a garden tour so they understand the property’s commitment to biodynamic farming principles and recognize the rare varieties of turmeric and uncommon peppers that will later appear on their plates. The resort offers three meals per day, along with snacks like beef jerky and gochujang okra chips stocked in each room. Each meal can be enjoyed in the restaurant spaces on the property or delivered in a wicker picnic basket through the resort’s charming take on room service. Uberto is the pinnacle of Quercus’s culinary experiences, a 10-plus course tasting menu helmed by chef Ryan Smith, whose Atlanta restaurant Staplehouse earned a Michelin star. The menu changes with the seasons, but mainstays like clarified curry made with lemongrass and galangal from the farm’s tropical greenhouse showcase an essential truth: Chefs spend all week in the kitchen and fermentation lab crafting each dish, but the garden has been working on dinner all year.

Mii Amo is an adults-only wellness resort tucked into the red rocks of Sedona’s Boynton Canyon vortex, which is supposedly one of the most powerful hubs for spiritual and healing energy in the area. It has everything one would expect at a luxury wellness retreat: lodging in one of 23 cozy casitas, a daily credit to book treatments at the 40,000-square-foot spa, an array of sound baths and meditations, and guided hikes and mountain bike rides through the canyon.

But unlike some other wellness resorts, Mii Amo has a top-notch culinary program as well. During all-inclusive stays ranging from three to 10 nights, guests get three meals per day at the on-site restaurant, Hummingbird, along with unlimited juice bar visits to pick up protein-packed smoothies, ginger shots, and sleep tonics. While menus are rooted in seasonal produce from the chef’s garden, the resort doesn’t believe in total restriction so sweet treats, alcohol, and caviar are available to add a little indulgence to your stay. Book one of the weekly nutrition classes or juicing demonstrations to learn how to recreate the nourishing fare served at Mii Amo after returning home.

On a hillside in the Catskills, Susan Manning and Doug Doetsch built a cidery, farm-to-table restaurant, and hotel on the same land that Doetsch’s family has farmed for five generations. Eight Shaker-inspired, apartment-style accommodations are built into two 19th-century historic buildings located just down the hill from the cidery on which the resort is centered.

The 12-acre orchard features rows upon rows of trees bearing 60 different varieties of apples and pears. The resort offers weekend tours of the orchard, plus cider tastings. Cider-maker Joshua Hinchman turns the fruit into an ever-expanding portfolio of traditional cider, while also experimenting with other beverages like fortified apple wines and cyser, a type of mead. All tastings are hosted in a barn that was built into the hillside using reclaimed wood that once supported the original Tappan Zee Bridge, and the structure is also passive house-certified, making it the world’s first cidery to receive that energy efficiency designation. Apples find their way onto executive chef and culinary director Edwyn Ferrari’s menu as well, so digging into custardy cider posset at the end of a meal in the resort’s restaurant feels full circle.

This Healdsburg hotel is the flagship property from Appellation, a hotel collection co-founded by chef Charlie Palmer and Christopher Hunsberger, who spent more than 35 years working for Four Seasons Hotels and Resorts. As a self-described culinary hotel, the 108-room resort set lofty expectations for itself, but Appellation Healdsburg delivers with three restaurants, a packed schedule of food-focused activities, and a premier location in the heart of Sonoma County.

Folia Bar & Kitchen is the resort’s premium restaurant, and it offers a three-course menu of local ingredients from purveyors like Hog Island Oyster Co. and Sonoma Bee Company. For guests who want to try their hand in the kitchen, the resort offers a variety of hands-on cooking classes, some of which are taught by Palmer himself. Since the hotel counts hundreds of wineries as neighbors, it invites a different winemaker to the property every Friday to host a guided tasting. But when it’s time to venture off the property, guests can also book with the concierge to get special access to Sonoma County producers like Knights Bridge Winery, Bricoleur Vineyards, and Dry Creek Vineyard for behind-the-scenes tours and tastings.

Los Poblanos is a historic inn dating back to 1932 where lavender grows in wide, fragrant rows and fruit trees line the paths between buildings. The property was designed by seminal architect John Gaw Meem in classic Santa Fe style with pitched tin roofs that nod to the site’s history as a dairy farm and the original 1934 hand-painted grain silos still stand on site.

Though it no longer produces dairy or serves as the headquarters of a larger ranch, a 25-acre organic farm is the heart of the property. Guests can take a formal tour of the greenhouses or wander the resort’s gardens, orchards, and cottonwood trees, which are fed by community-managed irrigation systems called acequias that are older than the state itself. All that benefits Campo, the property’s signature restaurant, where the flavors of the Rio Grande Valley appear on every plate. In addition to homegrown produce, the restaurant sources meat and other ingredients from local purveyors including Native American Beef, a program that supports Indigenous cattle producers who faced hurdles competing in the commercial meat industry. Don’t miss the Farm Shop with lavender-scented products, hot sauce from the garden’s peppers, and the estate’s handcrafted gin, which is produced in the resort’s distillery just down the road in downtown Albuquerque.

The Ojai Valley Inn has long been lauded as a wellness escape thanks to its 31,000-square-foot spa and location at the center of the hippie enclave of Ojai, but the resort is also a culinary destination in its own right. With six restaurants and bars on the property, options range from a casual pub serving quintessential burgers and local beer to a renowned four-course tasting menu at Olivella, where guests will eat dessert drizzled in honey from the resort’s apiary and order wine from the 10,000-bottle cellar.

In addition to the restaurants and bars, the resort recently poured $20 million into an epicurean center called the Farmhouse, where culinary ambassador and beloved California chef and restaurateur Nancy Silverton curates a slate of cookbook talks, cooking classes, and special tasting menu dinners with visiting chefs and buzzy winemakers. It’s also the site of the annual Ojai Food & Wine festival, held in March, which brings renowned chefs from around the world to the peaceful valley.

For travelers looking to be spoiled for choice, look no further than the Grand Hotel. Abundance is wrapped into every aspect of the resort, from its 660-foot porch that offers views of the idyllic car-free island to 388 Victorian-style guest rooms dressed up in maximalist candy-colored decor.

The property is not lacking in options on the food front either: It has 13 different bars and restaurants. The elegant main dining room serves breakfast, lunch, and dinner, but the peak time to visit is for nightly live entertainment from the Grand Hotel Orchestra and a five-course menu that rotates every three days to highlight a different type of cuisine. Other restaurants are just as grand; the Woods Restaurant serves cozy Bavarian dishes in a dining room that feels like a lodge with a roaring fireplace, while the Jockey Club serves seafood towers and steakhouse classics in a room outfitted in saddle leather. More casual picks are also available like Detroit-style pies at Mackinac Island Pizza Company or old-school sundaes made with scoops of Guernsey Farms Dairy ice cream at Sadie’s Ice Cream Parlor.

When visitors check into one of the rustic cabins at Windy Hill Farm and Preserve, a retreat and upland preserve in the foothills of the Great Smoky Mountains, there are plenty of ways to get involved in cultivating the food that will eventually end up on their plates. The all-inclusive experience features activities like beekeeping demonstrations, bourbon tastings, and cooking classes hosted by executive chef Ben Warwick, and every evening ends with an opportunity to make s’mores around a campfire. The resort has also recently launched themed dinners that offer built-in lessons on the surrounding Appalachian region. The first in the series is called Watershed, which tells the story of how water flows from the highlands surrounding the resort to the Gulf in 10 delicious courses.

But some of the most popular ways to spend a day on the 650 acres of rolling hills are more hands-on. Guests who opt to go crappie fishing on the Tennessee River will cast their reels in search of the native fish, and those who get lucky can have their catch cleaned and cooked at Wilder, the hotel’s restaurant. Quail hunting is also popular. Each hunt begins with a hearty breakfast at Wilder before guests head out with professional guides and sporting dogs. Proceeds from the hunting operation are used to fund large-scale land conservation and native habitat restoration.

Hidden Pond is a retreat where the quintessential clapboard cottages are as beloved as the property’s farm-to-table restaurant. Many of the accommodations were recently redesigned, and part of the revamp included outfitting the in-unit kitchens with top-of-the-line Breville appliances. However, you may not even bother firing up the appliances once you try the fresh-baked pastries delivered to each room by picnic basket, and dig into the menu at the on-site restaurant Earth.

The restaurant is popular with locals as well as hotel guests, and the menu is built around produce pulled from the resort’s organic garden. Summer brings heirloom tomatoes and micro basil while cooler months stock pumpkins and root vegetables like celeriac. It’s all paired with fresh-caught lobster and other local seafood, so you’ll also get a taste of the Atlantic that inspired the trip to Maine in the first place.

#5695. James Webb Finds a Tiny World’s Rings Changing in Opposite Ways

James Webb has revealed unexpected changes in Chariklo’s two rings, which appear to be evolving in opposite ways over only a few years.

For much of modern astronomy, rings were believed to belong only to the giant planets of the Solar System, including Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. That assumption changed in 2013, when astronomers discovered two dense rings around Chariklo, a small body only about 250 kilometers across that travels between Saturn and Uranus at nearly 17 times the Earth-Sun distance.

On October 18, 2022, researchers led by the Institute of Astrophysics of Andalusia (IAA-CSIC) used the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) to examine Chariklo’s rings during a stellar occultation. This method tracks how a star’s light dims as an object passes in front of it. A new study published in Science Advances, also led by the IAA-CSIC, now shows for the first time that Chariklo’s rings can change measurably over the course of only a few years.

“By comparing JWST observations with those obtained during other stellar occultations over the last decade, we discovered opposite changes in the two rings: while the inner ring shows significantly higher opacity, the outer ring shows lower opacity, ” explains Pablo Santos-Sanz, an IAA-CSIC researcher who leads the study.

The contrasting changes suggest that Chariklo’s rings are not static structures. Instead, the system appears to be active and may be influenced by physical processes that are more complicated than astronomers previously realized.

The observations also marked an important technical milestone. Chariklo’s passage in front of the star was the first stellar occultation that had been specifically predicted and planned for JWST and then successfully observed by the space telescope.

“Achieving this required knowing with extraordinary precision the orbit of Chariklo, the position of the star—thanks to ESA’s Gaia mission—and the trajectory of JWST itself around the L2 Lagrange point, a region of space located about 1.5 million kilometers beyond Earth, away from the Sun. JWST follows an orbit around this region that requires periodic corrections through station-keeping maneuvers,” notes Yücel Kilic, postdoctoral researcher at the IAA-CSIC and co-author of the study.

During the occultation, Chariklo was moving relative to JWST at only 2.5 kilometers per second. That unusually slow relative motion gave astronomers exceptional spatial resolution, allowing them to probe the fine structure of the rings in greater detail.

Chariklo’s rings are extremely narrow, and the object itself is so distant that the rings cannot be directly photographed, even by JWST or the largest telescopes on Earth. Stellar occultations provide another way to study them. As Chariklo and its rings pass in front of a background star, each ring produces a brief dip in the star’s brightness, revealing information about the ring’s structure and opacity.

Statement by Pablo Santos-Sanz (IAA-CSIC).

Scientists had generally assumed that rings around small Solar System bodies remain relatively stable over time. The newly detected changes in Chariklo call that assumption into question and suggest that such ring systems may evolve much more quickly than expected.

“Our results force us to rethink how they form, how they evolve, and what mechanisms maintain their stability. The ability to detect these changes opens a new window for understanding the evolution of these systems and, possibly, that of other ring systems in the Solar System,” says Santos-Sanz.

Exactly what caused the changes is still uncertain. They may reflect genuine evolution within the rings over time, differences caused by the filters used during separate observations, or some combination of the two.

The Institute of Astrophysics of Andalusia (IAA-CSIC) directed every major stage of the project, beginning with the scientific planning and prediction of Chariklo’s occultation for JWST and continuing through data analysis and interpretation.

Researchers at the IAA-CSIC also played a central role in modeling the rings and carrying out the statistical analysis that confirmed the observed changes are real. The work involved collaborators from Spain, Brazil, France, Hungary, and the United States.

#5694. Fingerprints Pressed Into China’s Terracotta Army Reveal a Secret Buried for More Than 2,200 Years

A new analysis found fewer than half the prints came from adult men, leading to a shocking realization.

When Emperor Qin Shihuang was carried to his tomb more than 2,200 years ago, he would not journey to the afterlife alone. Thousands of terracotta warriors would accompany him on the march into eternity. Possibly sculpted in the image of the men who served him in life, no two of the figures were alike—but whose hands brought this army to life? As it turns out, some of those hands left their fingerprints behind.

It was long assumed that only male artisans raised an entire army from clay. When archaeologist Xiaoxi Li of Emperor Qinshihuang’s Mausoleum Site Museum investigated with his research team, however, he found something astonishingly different. The fingerprints on the statues indicated that not all of the sculptors were adult men. An analysis of the prints, which were pressed into the clay while it was still wet and preserved forever once it was fired, suggested that the figures were made not only by men, but also by women and children. Earlier archaeologists had noticed the prints but had no way of learning anything more about them. Li’s team is the first to tease biological evidence out of them.

“These findings challenge the long-held assumption that Terracotta Army production relied exclusively on adult male labor, providing the first quantitative biological evidence for a diverse workforce including women and children,” he said in a study recently published in the Journal of Cultural Heritage.

The scale of the project helps explain why so many hands were needed. Alongside roughly 8,000 life-sized figures are 130 chariots drawn by 520 horses, plus another 150 cavalry horses. The Qin dynasty was known for pulling off undertakings of this magnitude, and past research has focused on how such an incredible feat was accomplished rather than on who accomplished it. The little that was known about the artisans came from their workshop marks—either the character “Gong” (宫) or “Xianyang” (咸阳)—and from differences in clay composition, which suggest that specific body parts were produced in separate workshops. Beyond that, the sculptors’ identities were a blank. And while scholars have generally assumed that only adult men were conscripted for such work in Qin China, inscriptions on lacquerware from the same period record the labor of women, boys, and girls.

To test that assumption, Li’s team focused on fingerprints found on the ceramic warriors in a single excavation unit of the tomb complex. The researchers photographed the prints, keeping only those that were neither distorted nor eroded and were therefore clear enough to analyze. The photos were then digitally enhanced to maximize the visibility of ridges and furrows so that details could be carefully measured. After this process, which also eliminated duplicate prints on the same body part of a statue (assumed to come from the same hands), the team was left with images of 79 prints on 36 figures. Most had been left on the heads and arms, probably while those parts were being attached to the rest of the body. Several others appeared on armor details, such as rivets and waistbands, that were pressed onto the surface.

Analyzing the magnified images, the researchers were able to identify certain fingerprints as belonging to laborers other than adult men. The key clue was the spacing between the ridges, which hints at age: because ridge spacing widens as the body grows, narrower spacing can give away younger hands. Still, the method leaves room for uncertainty. The morphology of some prints was consistent with either a juvenile boy or an adult woman, and it is possible that the clay shrank as it dried and was fired, shrinking the prints along with it, though by how much (if at all) is unclear. The location of the prints may reveal something else about the sculptors as well: less experienced workers may have been likelier to leave their marks behind, forgetting to smooth the clay after attaching heads and arms to a body.

Contrary to previous assumptions, fewer than half of the prints—just 45%—were linked to adult men. Nearly 33% appeared to belong to adult women. Children under the age of 15, the threshold of adulthood in China at the time, accounted for about 22%. These children may well have been apprentices learning the craft from masters, a reading supported by surviving Qin legal manuscripts that describe how long new apprentices needed to train before reaching artisan status.

Questions remain, however. Because only a fraction of the terracotta population was examined, the fingerprint distribution may not hold for the rest of this army, which took over a decade to build. Only by studying the remaining statues will researchers learn more about who created them.

“Notwithstanding these limitations, this study marks an important methodological breakthrough in Terracotta Army archaeology,” said Li. “It offers a new perspective into Qin state-run handicraft labor organization and validates [the methods used] for ancient craft studies.”

#5693. Under Extreme Pressure on Ice Giants, Water Ice Gets Weird

Every human being has an all-encompassing relationship with water that spans their entire lifetime. Our bodies are 50% water. We drink water, bathe in it, and swim in it. We skate on it, snowboard on it, and have snowball fights with it. We can take its familiarity for granted.

On Earth, water is the simplest compound to understand. It's either liquid, solid, or gas, and by high school, most of us know it can be separated into its constituent atoms. But science reveals the strange truth about water ice. The Solar System contains some strange worlds with strange environments. For water to be water, it always has to have two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. But new research shows that under extreme pressure, water ice becomes a bewildering shape-shifter that leaves its molecular identity behind.

The research is titled "Evidence for symmetry-reduction in solid H2O above 222 GPa," and is published in Communications Chemistry. The lead author is Ashkan Salamat, a physicist at the University of Nevada Las Vegas, and director of the university's Nevada Extreme Conditions Lab.

There are more than 20 kinds of ice, and each one forms at a different pressure and temperature. They're not governed strictly by temperature, like water is here on the surface of the Earth. Their naming convention is confusing for non-specialists, because they're named in order of discovery, not by ascending pressure and temperature, as one might expect. Among them, ice-X holds a special place.

Typically, a material under pressure has its atoms forced closer together, and as they pressure rices, they form arrangements with higher symmetry. That's what happens with ice all the way up to ice-X. Ice-X forms under extreme pressure that doesn't exist anywhere on Earth, even in Earth's mantle.

"Water (H2O), one of the most consequential compounds in the Solar System, governs the structure and dynamics of icy planets, the terrestrial water cycle, and through the pressure-induced transition from hydrogen to ionic bonding, it exhibits marked changes over the pressure range of planetary interiors," the authors write. Ice-X forms under immense pressure, the kind of pressure exerted in Uranus and Neptune, the Solar System's ice giants.

Under these pressures, the oxygen atoms shift. They're forced to sit exactly half-way between two hydrogen atoms, the hallmark of ice-X. This forms a cubic, symmetrical crystal lattice, as expected. This is an important transition, where water ice stops being molecular. But at the same time, it conforms to the rule that under increasing pressure, ice retains symmetry.

Though ice-X forms under the extreme pressures in Uranus and Neptune, the researchers wanted to see what happens under even greater pressure, the type of even more extreme pressure that exerts itself deep inside the ice giants. Would it exhibit the expected symmetry under these extreme pressures? What strange behaviour might it exhibit on these worlds?

They subjected the water to 35 million PSI, more pressure than it takes to form ice-X. This extremely high pressure is present in Uranus and Neptune, and the researchers wanted to see what happens.

The researchers used spectroscopy and x-rays to observe the structural arrangement of the hydrogen and oxygen atoms subjected to extreme pressure in the diamond anvil cell. The water did not reach higher symmetry as expected. Instead, it exhibited lower symmetry. "Here we report experimental evidence that water ice undergoes a transformation from ice-X to a loss of cubic symmetry above 222(12) GPa," the researchers write.

222 (+/- 12) gigapascals is an awesome amount of pressure. It's more than 2 million times greater than Earth's atmospheric pressure. "This transition marks the onset of a new regime of structural complexity in high-pressure H2O, redefining the upper stability limit of ice-X," the authors write.

Though the ice loses symmetry at these pressures, it still agrees with theoretical predictions, which takes some of the strangeness away. "The measured equation of state for ice-X and the evolution of the cell parameters of the newly observed phase align well with theoretical models," the authors write, adding that "These experiments highlight the complexities of compressing H2O under high pressures..."

Those aren't rock solid examples, but they're hints that remind us how different planetary environments can be. The lesson is that other planets don't necessarily conform to what we think is allowed based on our Earthly understanding.

This work shows that the same may be true of water ice. We can't assume that things like simple water will behave in predictable ways in the extreme environments elsewhere in the Solar System. Uranus, for example, has an icy-fluid mantle that may reach a whopping 700 gigapascals (GPa) of pressure.

#5689. Some polar bears are surviving bird flu, and scientists want to know why

Blood samples from East Greenland reveal evidence of H5 bird flu infections in four apparently healthy polar bears, raising new questions about disease in the Arctic.

A polar bear does not have to go looking for trouble. Sometimes, it can find it in its next meal.

A seabird, a clutch of eggs, or a dead seal may offer food to a hungry bear. But if that food carries bird flu, an ordinary search for dinner could also bring an infection.

That is how a disease named after birds can become a problem for one of the Arctic’s biggest predators.

Scientists have already reported polar bear deaths from bird flu in Alaska and Norway’s Svalbard islands.

Now, blood samples from East Greenland offer a more hopeful development.

Four bears carried signs of a past encounter with a dangerous type of bird flu, yet all looked healthy when hunters found them.

A bear can recover from an infection without leaving any obvious sign that it was ever sick. Its blood, however, may still hold evidence.

When the immune system responds to an infection, it makes proteins called antibodies. These can stay in the blood after the infection has ended, a little like footprints that remain after a visitor has left.

Kayla Buhler, a researcher at the University of Inland Norway and a postdoctoral fellow at Cornell University’s College of Veterinary Medicine, led the work.

“We’re not detecting active infections; we’re detecting a prior infection that has been cleared by the animal,” Buhler said.

The researchers could investigate that history because local Inuit hunters had helped collect samples for years.

Their partnership with scientists offered access to animals in a remote region where tracking wildlife disease is difficult.

The samples came from hunts near Scoresby Sound in East Greenland.

These hunts provide food, hold cultural importance for Indigenous communities, and follow annual government quotas.

Researchers at Aarhus University in Denmark, including Christian Sonne, worked with the hunters to collect heart blood from 109 bears between 2016 and 2025.

All of the samples came from the January-to-May hunting season.

The hunters also knew how the animals looked and behaved before the hunt. Every sampled bear appeared healthy, and the hunters had not reported seeing sick ones.

The team first checked for antibodies to the broader flu family that includes bird flu. None of the 81 bears sampled through 2023 tested positive.

Then the results changed. Three of 14 bears tested positive in 2024, followed by six of 14 in 2025.

Those nine positive results did not all point specifically to H5 bird flu.

More detailed tests at Canada’s National Microbiology Laboratory found antibodies linked to the globally spreading H5 group in four bears, all sampled in 2025.

“This is one of the first studies to show that bears can recover and do quite well after infection,” Buhler said.

The study did not establish how those four bears encountered the virus. The researchers consider eating infected birds or mammals one likely route.

That could happen when a bear hunts, scavenges a carcass, or feeds around nesting birds.

Other possible routes include contact with fluids from an infected animal’s nose or mouth, although the team did not confirm any route in these bears.

Migrating birds help explain how the virus could reach such a remote place. Every summer, birds travel north to breed, connecting Greenland with places far beyond the Arctic.

Once they arrive, many crowd into large nesting colonies where infections can spread.

Glaucous gulls travel between East Greenland and Svalbard, for example, and researchers have repeatedly found viruses from the same H5 group in those gulls on Svalbard.

For polar bears, this possible exposure comes as their search for food is changing. They hunt on sea ice, but as that ice disappears, they spend more time on land.

A bear looking for food ashore may turn to animals it would encounter less often while hunting on the ice. Bird colonies can become feeding grounds, bringing bears into closer contact with birds and anything those birds carry.

“On Svalbard, Norway, we see that polar bears are now taking down reindeer, which is something never seen before in the past. There and in many other parts of the Arctic, they are starting to raid bird colonies,” Buhler said.

The researchers did not show that ice loss caused the infections they detected. They describe a possible consequence of changing diets: new meals may bring new opportunities for disease to spread.

The researchers suspect the bears encountered the virus during the previous summer or fall, when migrating birds were present.

If so, they survived for months before hunters took them the following winter or spring.

But antibodies cannot tell researchers exactly when an infection happened or how ill a bear became.

A healthy-looking animal might have had a mild infection, or it might have recovered from a more serious illness.

The sample size was small, which means the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Researchers tested only 14 bears in each of the final two years, all from one hunting area, so the results cannot describe what is happening across the entire Arctic.

These samples also came from animals that lived until the hunt.

They cannot reveal how many other bears might have died, or whether surviving one infection would protect a bear the next time it encountered the virus.

Those four bears give scientists a reason to look more closely at animals that seem well.

Finding out why some bears survive could help researchers understand a disease they have also found in bears that died.

The team wants to know whether viruses circulating in Greenland carry changes that make them better at infecting mammals.

Another question concerns spread between bears, which sometimes gather around carcasses or garbage near human settlements.

“It’s important for us to monitor what new pathogens are becoming established in polar bears because we don’t know how they’ll be affected,” Buhler said.

Working with local hunters could help researchers monitor these changes and track the health of polar bears, which remain important to Indigenous communities for food and cultural traditions.

The study is published in the journal Emerging Infectious Diseases.

Around five bifolia would typically be nested together, creating what experts call a quire. Then, a stack of quires would be bound together along the spine to create a book. However, Davis and Layfield suggested that the Voynich manuscript was intended to be a stack of singulions — a series of loose bifolia never meant to be bound together as a quire or a book.

This is not the first time a researcher has suggested the Voynich manuscript has pages out of order. But Davis and Layfield analyzed the graphic properties of the untranslated document to see if their hypothesis made sense.

"We're viewing it as a text and assuming it has a message," Layfield told Live Science, which means it should be possible to statistically compare blocks of text to see if they're similar or disconnected. Layfield used a technique called latent semantic analysis (LSA), which is a natural language processing technique invented in the late 1980s to find hidden patterns in texts.

LSA is particularly useful for small sets of data, Layfield explained. "Modern AI techniques need ridiculous amounts of data to train on, which we just don't have. We've got just 35,000 or so words. That's it." The researchers' LSA analysis of the Voynich manuscript revealed that some pages had low similarity, suggesting they may not have gone together, while others had high similarity, bolstering the idea that they were supposed to be read together.

By looking at the Voynich manuscript as a collection of singulions, Davis and Layfield solved some of the nagging issues with the document.

"When you start to think about it in the context of the other strange features of the manuscript, all of those problems are resolved," Davis said. "It solves the problem of each scribe writing one sheet. It solves the problem of the images crossing the gutter."

This means that any previous work on the Voynich manuscript that assumes it was ordered correctly now needs to be reconsidered, she said, but it also means there is new progress on the book.

"This work helps us think about whether there's actual meaning underneath it all," Davis said. "It doesn't get us there," she said about a definitive translation, "but it moves the ball because you can't make sense out of a book where all the pages are mixed up."

Davis is optimistic that someone will crack the Voynich mystery, in part because a similar modern puzzle known as Cain's Jawbone has been successfully deciphered by a handful of people. This devilishly difficult linguistic puzzle from the 1930s consists of 100 pages of a murder mystery arranged in the wrong order. Due to the unique nature of the text and its vague, stylized language, even AI has failed to solve it.

"We have some ideas for other work we want to do," Davis said. For example, there's a large water stain in the upper margin of some of the pages of the Voynich manuscript. "The stain should be symmetrical from one page to the next but it's not. That also supports the idea that the manuscript is bound out of order."

Davis and Layfield are thinking about ways to compare the stain shapes across the pages, as that may help them reorder the incorrectly organized pages.

#5687. A Massive Volcanic Eruption Devastated Japan 7,300 Years Ago – but Survivors Found a Way To Thrive

Survivors of Japan’s ancient volcanic catastrophe may have found a lifeline along the coast, where communities grew even as more distant populations faltered.

Around 7,300 years ago, the Kikai-Akahoya eruption damaged ecosystems and made parts of Japan uninhabitable for the Jomon people, who lived there by hunting and gathering. Yet some Jomon populations continued to grow through the disaster and its aftermath, while population growth stalled or reversed in more distant areas.

For decades, archaeologists have debated whether the eruption brought an “apocalypse” that abruptly ended all human life closest to its epicenter or whether local populations survived even relatively nearby. A new international study led by Lund University finds that neither total collapse nor simple continuity fully explains what happened.

“This eruption must have been devastating, exploding out of the sea bed with little warning. It battered the Japanese islands with powerful earthquakes and enormous tsunamis, and then blanketed the landscape with meters of volcanic ash,” explains senior author Peter Jordan, professor of archaeology at Lund University.

The researchers found that populations declined as ecosystems deteriorated and areas became uninhabitable. In southern Kyushu, however, Jomon populations had been shrinking long before the eruption. The disaster struck a region that was already sparsely populated and visited only by highly mobile groups.

Northern Kyushu had a different population history. Its population was growing before the eruption, and that trend continued through the disaster and its aftermath despite extensive, lasting ecosystem damage. The authors suggest that “refugees” moving out of the worst-hit areas immediately after the eruption may have helped sustain that growth.

Survivors from inland areas appear to have moved away and clustered along northern coastlines, where marine ecosystems recovered quickly. New ways of drawing on the sea’s abundant resources may also have accelerated population growth.

“The disaster caused major loss of life. However, survivors migrated, forged new social relations, innovated new kinds of economy, and appear to have found creative ways to exist and later thrive in the new post-disaster conditions,” says lead author Enrico Crema, professor at Cambridge University.

In more distant parts of Japan, populations that had been growing before the eruption stagnated or declined afterward. These far-reaching effects had not previously been identified. They may have been linked to short-term climate change triggered by the eruption or to extreme tooth wear in the deer and boar that Jomon communities relied on for food. The animals may have suffered that dental damage while grazing in ash-covered landscapes. Other volcanic effects on human health and well-being could also have contributed.

For Jordan, the contrasting outcomes demonstrate how complex and unpredictable the aftermath of a major disaster can be.

“This study helps in creating a deeper understanding of the contingent ways in which cultures and societies cope with sudden shocks and catastrophes. Even in the most impossible situations, survivors and communities have choices, however limited: they need to act and make decisions, they need to adjust, collaborate and find new connections and social networks,” he concludes.

The Artemis II astronauts got views of our home planet and satellite that are normally impossible to see, such as the rugged moon and crescent Earth seen in this image taken on April 6.

Six months after the Artemis II mission sent four astronauts around the moon, the value of their closeup view is coming into focus. The first science results, released on October 7, reveal details of the moon’s array of colors, how often it gets hit by tiny rocks and lessons for squeezing as much science as possible out of future human lunar missions.

“We worked extremely hard to make sure we were able to maximize lunar science return from wherever we could in this mission,” said Artemis II flight science operations lead Kelsey Young at a virtual Lunar Surface Science Workshop meeting on October 7. “This is creating yet another tool in our toolbox as planetary scientists to understand our moon, our solar system, even our own planet.”

We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday.

NASA’s Artemis II mission sent four astronauts on a 10-day journey around the moon in April — the first time humans had been anywhere near our natural satellite in more than 50 years. Astronauts Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen spent about seven hours in close proximity to the moon taking scientific observations, describing the moon’s surprising colors and textures and witnessing a solar eclipse from space.

Almost all of that event was livestreamed. But the astronauts took thousands of photos, notes and audio recordings that weren’t immediately made public. Part of the science team’s job was to digest those observations and release the data to anyone who wants to study them within six months of splashdown on April 10.

With three days to spare, the team released three reports and a massive data archive on October 7, the 67th anniversary of when the Soviet Luna 3 spacecraft took the first photograph of the farside of the moon. The results offer a rare test of what trained human observers can add to lunar research and what NASA can learn for future missions to the lunar surface.

“We’re really excited to release all of this data,” earth scientist Sara Schmidt of NASA Johnson Space flight Cener in Houston said during the meeting. After six months of hard work, “this is enabling science to happen for the next, I don’t know, 60 years, if not more.”

Surprisingly, the most valuable data from the mission were not the mind-blowingly gorgeous photos, but the astronauts’ verbal and written descriptions of what they were seeing.

Before launch, the astronauts had extensive training in lunar geology and science observations. They were an extension of the science team, said field geologist Cindy Evans also of NASA Johnson Space Flight Center, one of the astronauts’ instructors.

“You could hear it in their voices. They understood the language of geology,” she said.

That training was meant to help answer a question Young hears often: What can humans add that a camera can’t?

During the flyby, the astronauts worked in pairs. Two of them spoke to mission control on the ground, giving periodic updates to the science team on what they were seeing. The other two recorded themselves describing their observations and reactions and used a stylus to annotate specially made maps of the moon that had been uploaded to their tablets during flight. Those notes, doodles and recordings were not made public until now.

The idea was to give the crew high tech field notebooks, Young says.

“If you have any field experience, you know your field notebook is your best friend,” she said in the meeting. On Earth, geologists capture nuance and context in written and spoken words, as well as sketches, she said. “We wanted to provide the ability to capture that nuanced information from the crew.”

One example of this was when Koch sketched her view of the Orientale basin, detailing differences in texture and layers around the crater.

“Sketches can be so powerful,” Young says. “They’re quick and dirty and that’s exactly the right thing to do. You’re capturing that nuance really quickly before it leaves your brain.”

Some of the most unique observations came during the 54 minutes when the moon eclipsed the sun, giving the astronauts a view of the solar corona, interplanetary dust and other stars and planets. That was a favorite moment for planetary scientist Jennifer Heldmann of NASA’s Ames Research Center in California.

“That audio showed their sheer joy and amazement in what they were seeing and where they were,” Heldmann says. “They were so blown away by what they were seeing, while also taking very relevant science observations.”

But some of the most affecting moments were when words failed. When Glover and Koch saw the Earth emerge from behind the moon, for a while all they could say was “Oh my gosh!”

“I guess I should be trying to describe it, but I mean, good grief,” Glover’s tablet recorded him saying.

“No, I tried, but it’s impossible,” Koch agreed. “You might be able to fly a spaceship and talk, but if you can see that and talk, you just ain’t right.”

Another thing the astronauts were able to see during the eclipse were impact flashes — tiny blips of light marking where bits of rock hit the moon.

“It’s a pinpoint of light,” Hansen said in his recorded observations. “It’s not like a meteor, you didn’t see anything else, it’s just like a bloop.”

Before launch, the science team identified and prioritized observations they hoped the crew would be able to make during the flyby. Impact flashes were at the top of the list. Seeing the flashes would give scientists a way to estimate how often the moon is struck by incoming rocks, and how large those rocks are. That’s an important thing to know for protecting future missions where astronauts will walk on the surface or build a long-term moon base.

Scientists didn’t know whether the astronauts would see any flashes at all. They saw five. The flashes were “pinpricks of light lasting less than a second, with no color associated with the flash,” Heldmann said at the meeting.

Comparing the number of flashes per minute to theoretical models of how many impacts the moon might experience, scientists determined that the incoming rocks were tiny, about 8.5 grams and just over 4.5 centimeters across — about the size of a golf ball.

“With these observations, we believe that the Artemis II crew set a record for the smallest meteorites ever optically observed to impact the moon,” Heldmann said.

The resulting craters are probably less than a meter wide, too small for cameras on NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter to see.

That’s good news for future astronauts. Artemis IV, planned for 2028, is supposed to bring astronauts to the lunar surface for the first time since Apollo. If successful, it will be a two-week mission using landers from spaceflight companies Blue Origin or SpaceX. The probability of dangerous impact during such a mission is less than 1 in 10 million, Heldmann said. For a larger moon base meant to last a decade, the probability is higher, but still low.

“Now we can start to quantify those impact probabilities, which turns out are very very low,” she says. “Crews will be very safe.”

The moon’s muted colors were another high-priority observation for the science team. Lunar hues and brightnesses can change with terrain roughness, surface temperature and mineralogy — and they’re much more discernable with human eyes than with cameras. The crew also saw how colors changed as the viewing angle and light levels changed.

“This is such a cool demonstration of the value of human eyes as scientific sensors,” said planetary scientist Ariel Deutsch, also of NASA Ames.

Combining the crew’s observations with other data going back to the 1970s let scientists connect colors to composition. Blues were associated with titanium-rich volcanic rocks. Browns reflect different iron levels. Greens are from glass formed during volcanic eruptions or asteroid impacts.

Future astronauts could use this information to create terrain maps and to find specific materials on the moon for sample collection or resources.

The crew also was enamored with the terminator, the boundary between light and dark, which revealed lunar topography in a new way.

“Even as rugged as the far side looks … man, when you look at the terminator, it is just, like, impossibly rugged,” Wiseman said during the flight. “And it reminds you that the whole farside is that way. It’s just the terminator is really bringing out the shadows and the hills and the valleys. And it is just — it’s unbelievable.”

The lighting at the terminator is similar to what future astronauts might expect at the moon’s south pole, where the sun will be low on the horizon and may cast dramatic shadows.

“We want to make sure we have a good handle on that for both science and operations and exploration,” Heldmann said.

With the lessons from Artemis II in hand, now it’s the next astronauts’ turn.

Some recommendations coming out of Artemis II focus on improving conditions in the spacecraft and weaving science into mission planning better. For example, due to time constraints, the astronauts’ cameras were not calibrated before flight. The team is now trying to calibrate them back on Earth, but it can’t re-create how the windows affected images because the windows were charred on the way back through Earth’s atmosphere.

Another takeaway from Artemis II is to continue to refine and train astronauts in descriptive notetaking. The audio notes will be even more important for the first landed missions, where astronauts in spacesuits will not be able to write in a notebook.

“We want to train astronauts to be field scientists, regardless of the landing site, regardless of the mission that they’re given,” Young says.

The buddy system of two astronauts at the windows, two talking to the ground, led to better science, the team concluded.

“The crew had scientific discussions and tried to understand what they were viewing,” Heldmann says. For surface missions, where time is limited and choices must be made quickly, that collaboration will be even more important.

The crew also commented on how helpful it was.

“When we actually do go down there to the surface, I know for safety reasons that we would never send someone alone, but I just want to really emphasize how important the discussion time was,” Glover said. “When we started to talk, we not only got better science discussion, we got better human connection.”

Coburn and their co-authors, describes how that serendipitous encounter allowed them to track the switchback back to its birthplace in the solar corona for the first time.

Switchbacks are important in our understanding of solar physics because of how they tie into the solar wind - the supersonic stream of charged particles that consistently blows from the surface of the Sun out into space. Modern spacecraft have shown that violent kinks in the Sunâs magnetic field, which they called âswitchbacksâ, directly affect the flow of these particles, and therefore, the exposure the wider solar system gets to the solar wind more generally.

Essentially, this preserves the heavy ions at the same state they were in when the plasma was formed.

According to the data from Solar Orbiter, it seemed pretty clear that the plasma it encountered in the switchback had been baked at over one million degrees Celsius - most likely in the Sunâs extremely hot corona. Coronal holes are darker, cooler regions where the magnetic fields from the Sun stretch out into interplanetary space. They contrast to coronal loops which are hot arcs of plasma that are anchored on both ends to the solar surface. Since the plasma was clearly formed in a closed loop, the researchers concluded that the switchback itself was launched by a process known as an interchange reconnection.

An interchange reconnection happens when the arc that makes up part of the loop, encounters an open magnetic field line. Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have found what may be one of the most distant supernovas ever observed. And in a stroke of cosmic luck, the team thinks they're seeing what the explosion looked like just days after it ignited.

The supernova, named AT 2026abck, dates to around 1.15 billion years after the Big Bang, when the universe was less than one-tenth of its current age and bursting with massive stars flickering into life. The massive exploding star violently released its light over 12.5 billion years ago, according to the researchers — so long ago that the distance between us has effectively ballooned sixfold due to the expansion of the universe.

The discovery was described in a paper that was uploaded to the preprint server arXiv Sept. 29 and has not yet been peer reviewed. According to the study authors, this ancient speck of light is stupendously bright.

"At its peak, the supernova is about 8 billion times the brightness of the sun … almost as bright as the whole Milky Way Galaxy," study co-author Xiaohui Fan, an observational cosmologist and professor of astronomy at the University of Arizona, told Live Science via email. "Over the course of the explosion, it radiates the energy that the sun will radiate over about 2 billion years."

The newly discovered supernova shines brilliantly at over 30,000 degrees Fahrenheit (16,000 degrees Celsius). Its incandescent shell of ejected material has billowed to a radius of approximately 46 astronomical units ‪—‬ about 46 times the distance between Earth and the sun, or about as far as Pluto swings at its farthest point from the sun.

The supernova's host galaxy, designated ID101411, holds about 6.3 billion solar masses and is "forming new stars more than ten times faster than our Milky Way," first author Mingyang Zhuang, an observational astrophysicist at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, told Live Science via email.

As with other early galaxies, ID101411 is deficient in metals, meaning any elements heavier than hydrogen and helium, because not enough time had passed to create those heavier elements in the forges of massive stars and their apocalyptic explosions.

The study authors estimate that they observed the supernova as it would have looked just four to 10 days after it exploded. Although this is relatively common, spotting one at such an incredible distance "is extremely rare," Zhuang emphasized. "You have to observe the exact same tiny patch of sky repeatedly with [JWST], and the explosion has to happen at the right moment. We were lucky."

There's another layer of luck that made the discovery even more remarkable: It was observed without the aid of a cosmological quirk called gravitational lensing — a phenomenon predicted by Einstein in which the gravity of a massive object in space bends and magnifies the light emitted by objects behind it.

"Most supernovas this far away were found with help from a massive foreground galaxy cluster acting as a natural magnifying glass," Zhuang explained. "It's the most distant one we've actually seen appear."

This supernova was discovered as part of the NEXUS Treasury program, in which JWST intermittently scours patches of the sky for ancient explosions. It had previously observed the explosion's host galaxy over the past few years and finally witnessed the supernova on Aug. 7 of this year.

Intriguingly, the star had not yet exploded in JWST's previous viewing, on May 28, 2026, meaning that 71 days passed on our Earthly clocks, even though only about 12 days passed in the supernova's own time frame.

This highlights a mind-bending cosmological effect: The universe's expansion doesn't just stretch space; it slows down time from our perspective.

"Because the universe has expanded since this light was emitted, everything about this explosion appears to us in slow motion, about six times slower than it actually happened," Zhuang said. "Changes that took one week at the supernova take about six weeks to unfold from our point of view."

Mysteries still remain about the nature of this ancient explosion that shone so brilliantly we can observe it across nearly the entire universe.

Rather than a conventional core collapse supernova ‪—‬ in which a star between approximately eight and 50 times the mass of the sun exhaust its nuclear fuel and suffers a rapid core collapse and subsequent explosion ‪—‬ the recently discovered supernova may be one of the universe's rarest, most powerful explosions, the study authors said. Known as a superluminous supernova, this type of explosion releases tens or hundreds of times more energy than conventional supernovas. Whether the explosion continues to heat up or starts to cool could reveal exactly what is happening in its environment and what caused it, the team said.

"Our interpretation is still preliminary," Zhuang concluded. "Follow-up observations will tell us more about exactly what kind of explosion this was."

#5676. Study Finds the Arctic Could Lose Far More Sea Ice Than Scientists Previously Predicted

Clues from a warmer Arctic 6,000 years ago suggest the region could lose a million square kilometers more winter sea ice than scientists previously expected.

The loss of Arctic sea ice has been linked to colder winters and heavier snowfall in the UK, the rest of northern Europe, and North America. The ice regulates how much heat and moisture the ocean releases into the atmosphere, so changes in its cover can influence weather far beyond the polar region.

Climate models disagree most about how much ice will cover the Arctic in winter, making its future particularly difficult to predict. Using evidence from a warmer Arctic 6,000 years ago, researchers have now revised those projections downward by about one million square kilometers, or roughly 386,000 square miles. The findings, published in Nature Geoscience, come after the Arctic recorded its lowest maximum sea ice extent since modern measurements began in March 2025, a record tied again this year.

Researchers from the University of Cambridge and the Centro de Investigación en Matematicas (CIMAT) in Mexico compiled the largest-ever database of a key substance produced by algae living in sea ice. Called IP25, this biomarker was measured in ocean sediment cores dating to the mid-Holocene, when subtle shifts in Earth’s orbit contributed to warmer Arctic conditions and reduced ice cover. Alongside IP25, the team measured complementary biomarkers from organisms that live in open water. Together, these traces allowed them to estimate how much sea ice covered the ocean thousands of years ago.

“The scientific community has amassed lots of paleoclimate records over recent decades, but it’s remained a challenge to directly link these records to future projections, since there is uncertainty at both ends,” said lead author Crystal Fu, from Cambridge’s Scott Polar Research Institute.

By comparing their reconstruction with simulations of the mid-Holocene from 27 climate models, the researchers could assess how closely each model reproduced the ancient ice conditions. They used a statistical method called Bayesian weighting to give greater influence to models that matched the evidence more closely. Those weights could then be applied to simulations of the recent past and future because the same physical processes govern sea ice across different climates.

Although the mid-Holocene warming had a different cause from today’s warming, Fu said the ice would respond in the same way. “It doesn’t matter if the warming is natural variation or caused by human activity: if it warms, sea ice is going to melt,” she said.

In the historical simulations, the weighting removed the models’ collective tendency to overestimate winter sea ice, while the range of uncertainty in the projections narrowed by as much as 85%.

“When we apply the model weights to historical simulations, we completely eliminated the positive bias,” said Fu. “The future projections similarly end up being lower than previous estimates, by about a million square kilometers.”

Applying the revised weights to precipitation projections also suggested that snowfall over southeast Greenland and rainfall in the Mediterranean may be underestimated. The researchers described these as hints that effects beyond sea ice could be larger than expected, with possible implications for the Greenland ice sheet’s stability and future flood risk in southern Europe.

“By using evidence from the past, we can now produce more reliable maps of where Arctic winter sea ice is likely to disappear,” said co-author Marco Aquino-López, of CIMAT. “These give us a much clearer picture of what the Arctic might look like in the coming decades, and how much uncertainty remains.”

#5673. Solar panels are bringing idle cranberry bogs back to life

Two Massachusetts cranberry bogs that had stopped making financial sense to farm are growing berries again – with solar panels overhead helping pay the bills.

The Black Cat project in Plymouth and the Ring Road project in Kingston combine cranberry farming, community solar, and battery storage. Distributed Energy Infrastructure (DEI), which built the projects, says income from leasing the land for solar has made it viable to cultivate the long-idle bogs again.

It’s a particularly challenging form of agrivoltaics because cranberry bogs have soft, peaty soil and established vines that need to survive construction. You can’t just roll in with heavy equipment and start installing panels.

So the solar had to fit the farming. In a September article in North American Clean Energy, DEI cofounder and executive vice president Prateek Tare described panels raised 10 feet above the ground, leaving room for farm machinery underneath. Rows have more than 18 feet of clear space between them, and solar panel angles let sunlight reach the cranberry bogs.

Farmer Iain Ward helped work out those details, along with how construction could proceed without damaging the bogs. Even the protective mats needed changing: Black ones absorbed too much heat, so crews used white ones instead.

That care added time. Tare said construction took around eight months – roughly twice as long as a comparable conventional solar project.

“The DEI team was phenomenal at understanding and adapting solar construction so that energy production could coexist without damage to the perennial cropping system,” Ward said.

Greenbacker Renewables owns both sites. Black Cat pairs 1 megawatt (MW) of solar with 2.7 megawatt-hours (MWh) of battery storage, while Ring Road has 4.4 MW of solar and a 13.5 MWh battery.

The money reaches both sides of the farming arrangement, too. The landowner receives solar land lease income, and Ward is paid to manage the bogs, in addition to his earnings from selling the berries.

DEI’s October announcement celebrates what it calls the first-ever agrivoltaic cranberry harvests, which Tare’s earlier account dates to fall 2025.

Considering how tough farming is in general for independent farms, they're in huge debt on average, getting renewables on their properties for another income stream seems like a no brainer in general. If having panels can help their plants grow better and/or reduce water usage, even better. With that amount of renewable onsite electricity, and the wild cost of diesel, switching to EV equipment also seems like a no brainer if they have to get new equipment anyway.

The announcement doesn’t say how many cranberries were picked or how yields compare with growing them without panels. Those numbers might help other farmers decide whether solar could help keep their own bogs in business.

Ceratopsians — horned dinosaurs like Triceratops — have been hypothesized to use their imposing headgear as armaments for battling with other members of the species.Fossilized facial injuries consistent with head-to-head violence have provided physical clues. “We know from many fossils that ceratopsian skulls tended to get banged up a little bit … in very particular spots on the frill or on the cheek,” says Andrew Farke, a paleontologist at Raymond M. Alf Museum of Paleontology in Claremont, California. But little else was known about how ceratopsians actually used their horns in battle.

Studying the biomechanics of the animals’ weaponized skulls could provide information on what movements and forces the dinosaurs evolved to tolerate. Such biomechanics research had previously been done on the bite stresses of theropod dinosaurs like Tyrannosaurus and on the forces felt by the club tails of armored dinosaurs, says vertebrate paleontologist Eric Austin Ames Jr.

Ames and his colleagues at the University of Bristol in England developed digital 3-D models of six skulls across five ceratopsian species spread across the group’s evolutionary tree. They simulated the combat stress experienced across the skull, varying the direction of attack and how the weaponry was used. Higher stresses in the skull meant a greater risk of mechanical failure, Ames says. The dinosaurs are more likely to have evolved to fight in a way that was well-tolerated by the skull’s mechanics.

The team found that early members of the group from about 125 million years ago — like the hornless, husky-sized Psittacosaurus — didn’t have skulls well-suited for combat of any type. The pig-sized Protoceratops had a skull that tolerated ramming fairly well. Larger, later-diverging species  like Centrosaurus and the three-horned Triceratops and Spiclypeus appeared adapted to a wider range of combat styles.

Centrosaurus may have been a specialized stabber, lunging its head to jab at opponents. “When we tested [the skull] with vertical forces, it actually handled the stresses really well,” Ames says. “But when we tried side-to-side, it did not perform very well.”In contrast, Triceratops and Spiclypeus had skulls that tolerate the forces generated by interlocking horns and wrestling.

Ames thinks ceratopsians’ horns and frills probably first evolved for social and sexual display, but were soon used for full contact purposes. “Throughout their evolutionary history, their [horns’ traits] started to really explode in diversity and that coincided with different intraspecific combat styles,” he says. “This is something we see in modern herbivores.”

Much like with the antlers and horns of modern deer, buffalo or antelope, these weapons could have easily been turned on predators as well. “These adaptations are kind of like a Swiss Army knife,” Ames says. “They can be used for multiple things.”

The team created its 3-D models from surface scans of fossilized skulls. More detailed models that better simulate the complicated internal structure of these dinosaurs’ immense, bizarre skulls could provide even more insight to this prehistoric fight club, Farke says.

#5669. The ‘Tavern-Style’ Pizza Takeover

It’s the year of the tavern — and that means pizza style, too

In the suburbs of Chicago where they both grew up, it was just pizza, according to Brittany and Brennan Grosvenor, the couple behind Proof in Portland, Oregon. Ordering came down to the question of “thin-crust, deep-dish, or double-dough,” Brittany recalls of her family’s Friday night tradition. The Grosvernors sell the former at Proof, though the language of “thin crust” has given way to the more nationally familiar phrasing of “tavern-style,” the pizza trend that has swept from coast to coast in recent years.

Outsiders might associate Chicago with its famous, tourist-beckoning deep-dish pizza, but Brittany says that’s just not reflective of reality, citing deep dish as a much less frequent treat than the Midwestern, thin-crust style that’s gaining popularity. “The pizza you get every week, or if you go to a kid’s birthday party, it’s always tavern-style,” she says.

“Tavern-style” pizza — which is prevalent across the Midwest, with Chicago, Milwaukee, Minnesota, and St. Louis touting similar pies — is known for its thin, cracker-like crust, cut into a lattice instead of triangular slices. Depending on the region and the pizza shop where one’s nostalgia lies, the sauce might lean sweet or spicy. Lightness and crispness, though, is the style’s unique sell. Writing for Esquire in 2023, Omar Mamoon called the variant, which was then still on the rise, “a foil” to famously thick Detroit-style pizza, which had its big moment in the late 2010s.

The beauty of the tavern-style pie is its shareability. A pizza yields more slices when cut into a grid than into triangles, explains Bradley Shorten, who’s behind both Kim’s Uncle Pizza in his hometown Chicago suburb of Westmont, Illinois, and the recently opened La Merna in Washington, D.C. Pizza cut into little squares makes for great drinking food, especially since it fits on a cocktail napkin. What makes a good pie, according to Shorten, is that every square has a little of every ingredient; the toppings go to the edge. And while the four, small corner slices might not be the “best” ones, Shorten says, they’re the ones “everybody goes for,” especially if they’re from Chicago.

Over the past few years, as “taverns” have become growing fixations for a cadre of restaurateurs, the “party cut” pizza and tavern-style pizza spots have also become ubiquitous, with openings like Keepers in New York city; One Trick Pony in Forth Worth, Texas; Tillie’s Tavern Pizza in Wheat Ridge, Colorado; and Zarella and Dimmi Dimmi Corner Italian in Chicago, where the style has always been abundant. For some Chicagoans, these openings across the country are an opportunity to correct the record about their city’s pizza culture, and for other Midwesterners to claim their stake in the region’s pizza, too.

When Mike Fadem opened the second location of his popular NYC pizzeria Ops — known for its wood-fired Neapolitan-esque pies with leopard-spotted crusts and pliant dough — he wanted to offer something new. Fadem is from St. Louis, where the city’s namesake pizza is super-thin. Despite his love for it, “it’s maybe not the best version of tavern pizza,” Fadem admits, noting the iteration’s many critics, some of whom take issue with its use of the gooey processed cheese called provel. The trajectory of tavern-style pizza has been like a game of telephone, he explains: St. Louis pizza is derived from thin-crust Chicago pizza, which itself came from Neapolitan pizza. Divergence has accompanied each jump.

One of Fadem’s employees at Ops, meanwhile, is from New Jersey, which claims its own thin-crust, tavern- or bar-style pizza tradition. (The bar pies of the Boston area also have their own devoted following.) Accordingly, “I had this interest in ‘can we make an Ops version of this?’” Fadem says. With Ops known for sourdough, Fadem also wanted to highlight the rich flavor of whole grains. And he wanted it to be really crispy, a quality he says draws in New Yorkers. Fadem’s take is on the menu as the regionally agnostic “tavernetta.” Though St. Louis and New Jersey served as initial inspiration, the best pizza from his research was actually in Milwaukee. The Tavernetta hasn’t displaced the other pies at the restaurant; it’s ordered in addition to them, with most groups getting at least one.

In June, Los Angeles cocktail spot Bar Next Door celebrated its third anniversary by adding food to its menu. Previously, the bar had allowed customers to bring in pies from Prince Street Pizza next door since Prince Street co-owner Lawrence Longo is a partner in it. “But, you know, after having a slice, sometimes you get a little full and you’re like, I want to go to bed,” says owner and bar director Brynn Smith. “That’s the whole goal for us: for people to stay a little longer, drink more. We thought the tavern-style pizza would be perfect.” The fact that the pizzas — they’re technically listed on the menu as “bar pies,” to better go with the establishment’s name — are made exclusively for the bar by Prince Street just adds another reason for people to go out, Smith says.

The idea of the tavern as a social and convivial space is inseparable from the tavern-style pizza. Shorten’s goal with La Merna — which he runs with his wife Meg Schmitt, who grew up in St. Louis — was the feel of a Chicago dive bar or a Wisconsin supper club: a place with wood-paneled walls and checkered floors, where people could slide into a red leather booth for pitchers of beer and pizza and hang out for a while. As my colleague Francky Knapp wrote last year, the rising appeal of the “tavern” vibe across restaurants nationwide is rooted in the sense of providing somewhere homey and lived-in. At taverns, diners are beckoned to sprawl out and stay awhile; to free themselves of pretense and to tap into something welcoming in its familiarity. This, for so many people, is exemplified by the archetype of hometown pizza shop, which is forever tethered in our cultural imaginations to the trappings of a previous, simpler era.

It’s for this reason that, in Oregon, the Grosvenors have started serving their take on Bosco sticks, the stuffed breadsticks of Midwestern cafeterias, and Italian beef with giardiniera. While they could find certain Midwestern hits around Portland, it sometimes frustrated them that they couldn’t find everything in one place. According to Brennan Grosvenor, “We’re just trying to embody the feeling: If you went into a tavern in Chicago, what could you order there?”

#5667. Forgotten Material From the 1960s Is Hiding Something Unexpected

A long-overlooked material has revealed a hidden atomic pattern that challenges conventional expectations about magnetism.

A uranium compound that has received little scientific attention for decades has been found to contain a previously unknown atomic structure with unusual magnetic properties. The discovery could help researchers identify materials capable of supporting faster, more resilient computer memory.

Scientists discovered a repeating, twisted arrangement of atoms known as a chiral superlattice inside uranium oxytelluride (UOTe). The structure appears to play a central role in how electrons move through the crystal, which exhibits an uncommon combination of two different types of magnetism. The findings were published online October 7 in Nature.

Dr. Mengke Liu, an assistant professor of physics at the University of Texas at Dallas, discovered the structure while examining crystals synthesized by Dr. Sheng Ran, an associate professor of physics at Washington University in St. Louis. Liu began studying the material while working as a Harvard Quantum Initiative Postdoctoral Fellow, before joining UT Dallas in 2025. Using transmission electron microscopy and scanning tunneling microscopy, she investigated the crystal at atomic resolution.

“I was originally studying this material for an entirely different reason,” Liu said. “When I examined it with high-resolution microscopy, I found a naturally occurring superstructure no one had recognized before.”

The newly identified superlattice consists of a larger, repeating atomic arrangement with a predominantly left-handed or right-handed twist, a property known as chirality. The researchers found that this organization influences electron behavior and is associated with the material’s unusual magnetic characteristics.

The crystal displays characteristics of both ferromagnetism and antiferromagnetism. In ferromagnetic materials, atomic magnetic moments align to produce an overall magnetization. In antiferromagnetic materials, neighboring magnetic moments point in opposing directions, typically canceling one another so that the material has no net magnetization.

“Finding a single material that combines both of these properties is interesting fundamentally,” Liu said.

A research team at Harvard University, led by Dr. Suyang Xu, the John L. Loeb Associate Professor of the Natural Sciences and a corresponding author of the study, was independently investigating the same compound. The teams compared results from complementary experiments and conducted additional measurements, confirming that the unusual atomic superstructure is an important factor controlling how electrons travel through the material.

Antiferromagnetic materials offer potential advantages for computer memory because they are generally less sensitive to external magnetic fields and can support faster operation than conventional ferromagnets. A material combining characteristics of both could provide new possibilities for magnetic memory technologies, although practical devices based on the discovery have not yet been demonstrated.

“If those advantages can be harnessed, memory devices could potentially become both faster and more robust,” Liu said.

Computational analyses also suggest that hundreds of related compounds might contain comparable superlattice structures. This expands the range of materials researchers can investigate for similar electronic and magnetic behavior.

“This study provides a new way of looking for materials with these unusual properties,” Liu said. “Instead of focusing only on the fundamental atomic arrangement, we can also now explore larger superstructures that might influence how electrons behave.”

Uranium oxytelluride was first known to scientists decades before the discovery of its unusual superlattice. Modern microscopy techniques have now made it possible to examine structural features that earlier investigations could not resolve.

“Uranium oxytelluride has been known since the 1960s, but it has been largely ignored in research since then. Now, with today’s techniques, we’re able to uncover and study properties that previously were hidden,” Liu said.

#5666. Neutrinos May Be The Key To Why We Are Surrounded By Matter

Neutrinos don't interact strongly with regular matter. Countless neutrinos stream through us all the time with no effect. But neutrinos weren't always so weakly interacting. In the earliest cosmic moments neutrinos still interacted strongly with protons, neutrons, and electrons. All of these particles were in thermal equilibrium for a time until the Universe could expand to a critical point. At about a second after the Big Bang neutrons decoupled from other material particles. They began to act similar to the neutrinos we see today.

These decoupled neutrinos still fill the Universe. They form a cosmic neutrino background. A particle echo of the Big Bang, similar to the cosmic microwave background. In the present epoch these neutrinos play no strong role in the evolution of the Universe, but some of them may be the reason we live in a matter-dominated universe.

One of the basic symmetries of the Universe is that every elementary particle has a twin. Electrons have the positron. Quarks have anti-quarks. Protons have anti-protons. Under the right circumstances you can create particles, but they always come in pairs. So one of the big questions in cosmology is why we live in a matter-dominated cosmos. If the Big Bang created an equal amount of matter and antimatter, where did all the antimatter go? The answer could be heavy decoupled neutrinos.

Although neutrinos don't have an electric charge, there are both matter and antimatter neutrinos. Rather than charge, they are distinguished by their helicity or handedness. Matter neutrinos are always left-handed, while anti-matter neutrinos are right-handed. In the modern Universe only left-handed neutrinos interact with matter. The right-handed ones have some teeny-tiny gravitational effect but are otherwise "sterile" neutrinos.

Neutrinos also come in three different varieties or flavors. For matter, the three flavors are associated with the electron, muon, and tauon, which is why neutrinos have three possible masses. Anti-neutrinos correspond to the anti-ons and also have three mass values. This latest study focuses on the anti-neutrinos with the greatest mass. What they call the decoupled heavy neutrino.

Decoupled heavy neutrinos could have dominated the Universe for a time. Credit: Roshan & Show

In their model, the authors note that heavy neutrinos can undergo decay. They argue that since the half-life of these heavy neutrinos is relatively long, they would have decayed well after things like electrons, protons, positrons, and anti-protons formed. When heavy anti-matter decayed, their by-products kept the Universe a bit warm and annihilated some of the antimatter particles to create neutral photons. As a result, the balance between matter and antimatter was shifted in favor of matter. The remaining antimatter of the cosmos was destroyed via regular particle interactions, leaving matter as the stuff we see today.

If this model is correct, the neutrino-dominated period would have a measurable effect on primordial gravitational waves. When gravitational wave observatories improve to the point where we can detect primordal waves, we could also test this model. There would also be a measurable effect on the cosmic neutrino background. So perhaps in the future we will discover that one of the strongest consequences of the Big Bang was caused by one of the Universes weakest interactions.

#5663. The Unwritten Rules of Traveling to Hong Kong—Including the Time of Year You Should Absolutely Avoid

Pocket-sized Hong Kong packs more of a punch than its 430-square-mile size might suggest. Split into three main areas—Hong Kong Island, the Kowloon Peninsula, and the New Territories—made up of 18 districts, each with their own rich history, culture, architecture, and foods, there's more than enough to keep the average tourist busy for months, never mind the usual weekend trip.

An amped-up version of London's Oyster card, Hong Kong's contactless Octopus card makes getting around the city a breeze. Download the app for free or pick a physical card at the Airport Express ticket desk in the arrivals hall or any MTR subway station (the 50 Hong Kong Dollars (or around $6.50) deposit is refunded when you return the card). Hop on the #6 double-decker from Central to Stanley beach, which lurches around Hong Kong Island's mountainous southern South China Sea coast (sit upstairs on the right-hand side). On the subject of public transport, there are certain rules which everyone follows. Keep right on any escalators or walkways, always let passengers alight before you board, and give you up your seat to anyone in need. Hong Kong is such an outdoors-y destination that it can seem like there's little to do on a rainy day but the city has two wonderful world-class museums—the Hong Kong Palace Museum (70 Hong Kong Dollars, or around $9), which contains hundreds of priceless treasures borrowed from the Palace Museum in Beijing, and the gigantic M+ Museum (190 Hong Kong Dollars, or around $24.25), where the focus is highly visual contemporary Asian arts. Served from morning until early afternoon, dim sum is the fuel that Hong Kong runs on. To beat the crowds, it's best to arrive before 12 p.m. or after 2 p.m. (try one of the world's most affordable Michelin star restaurants, Tim Ho Wan, or the 90-year old Luk Yu Teahouse). If you really want to pass for a local, pull on your spandex, grab an umbrella (everyone's favorite sunshade), and head for the hills to take advantage of Hong Kong's hundreds of miles of sensational hiking trails. Hong Kong consistently ranks among the world's safest cities. Violent crime and crimes against women are extremely rare; even pickpocketing is relatively uncommon. If you’re familiar with the hallmarks of Fielderian programming, you know that Fielder is prone to hyperfixating on seemingly mundane details that later return to completely upend viewers’ expectations. Evans feels comfortable taking control of something Holmes is doing, without so much as asking, and seems largely unaware of how his behavior might come across on camera. Even when an idea is big, it’s the little things that matter the most. The protein on your plate may do more than build muscle. Growing evidence suggests that where dietary protein comes from could influence inflammation in the gut, raising important questions about how food choices might affect people living with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).

IBD includes Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, two chronic conditions in which abnormal immune responses contribute to ongoing inflammation in the digestive tract. Symptoms can include persistent diarrhea, abdominal pain, fatigue, and intestinal bleeding, often alternating between periods of remission and unpredictable flare-ups. Although genetics, immune function, and gut microbes all play important roles, researchers are still working to understand how diet influences the disease.

IBD has also become increasingly common in the United States and worldwide over recent decades, alongside shifts in dietary habits that include greater consumption of animal protein. Despite these trends, scientists have yet to establish clear, evidence-based recommendations about which protein sources might be most beneficial for people with IBD.

As the authors explain, “Although diet is believed to be an important contributor to the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel diseases, there are few evidence-based dietary recommendations for patients to lessen disease severity.”

A new study in Cellular and Molecular Gastroenterology explored whether the source of dietary protein could change the severity of intestinal inflammation. Researchers compared diets containing beef, egg, dairy, soy, or pea protein across multiple mouse models of IBD.

Mice fed beef protein consistently developed the most severe intestinal inflammation, while those consuming pea protein experienced the least inflammation and only mild symptoms. The pattern remained consistent across the different models and in both male and female mice.

“Mice fed a beef protein diet exhibited the most severe colitis, whereas mice fed pea protein developed mild inflammation,” the authors say.

Importantly, beef protein alone did not cause overt intestinal inflammation in mice without a colitis trigger, suggesting that its effects depend on other factors involved in the disease.

The findings point to interactions between gut microbes, the intestinal barrier, and bile acids as a possible explanation for the differences. The intestinal barrier helps separate the gut’s contents from surrounding tissue, while bile acids help digest fats. How microbes interact with that barrier and those acids appears to influence whether these processes promote or reduce inflammation.

“The colitis-promoting effects of beef protein were microbially mediated,” the authors say.

Further experiments revealed that beef protein altered the balance of intestinal bacteria and was associated with a thinner protective mucus layer and higher levels of certain bile acids linked to inflammation. In contrast, pea protein supported a different microbial balance and bile acid profile.

The authors also found that adding psyllium, a soluble dietary fiber, to the beef-protein diet reduced inflammation in mice, suggesting that other dietary components may help counteract some of these effects.

Protein choices could become a focus of future dietary guidance aimed at helping people manage IBD and reducing disease risk and severity.

“These findings provide a rationale for determining whether modifying dietary protein source could lessen the severity of disease in patients with inflammatory bowel diseases,” the authors say.

#5658. A Stubborn Question Answered: New Research Reveals How the Milky Way's Disk Formed

The Milky Way is home to hundreds of billions of stars arranged in a glorious spiral pattern. But beyond the obvious spiral, our galaxy has other physical characteristics. It's divided into a galactic bulge, a galactic disk, and a galactic halo.

The disk is further divided into a thin disk and a thick disk. They're not only structurally different, but their stellar populations and other characteristics are different.

The thin disk contains young stars, and middle-aged stars like our Sun. It holds the spiral arms, where our Solar resides and also holds most of the Milky Way's gas and dust and is where young stars form. It also has higher metallicity, generated by previous generations of stars that have lived and died.

The thick disk is more puffy and diffuse than the thin disk. It holds ancient stars, and almost no new stars form there. It has much lower metallicity than the thin disk, and astronomers sometimes refer to it as a kind of stellar fossil record.

Astronomers wrestle with understanding the formation of these disks, and the Milky Way as a whole. Observations have led to a multiple explanations. In one, stars are born in the thin disk, and over time, encounters with gas clouds, the spiral arms, or even the central bar, perturb some of these stars. Dynamical scattering drives them away from the plane of the thin disk.

Another explanation says that as stars form in the inner disk, the gas they form from settles down into an even thinner disk. In this scenario, each successive generation of stars forms in an increasingly thin disk.

Astronomers have difficulty distinguishing between these competing explanations. It relies on accurate measurements of the ages of individual stars. That's difficult to achieve, especially for the oldest stars.

New research in The Astrophysical Journal Letters used Gaia data to tackle the issue. It's titled "A Two-stage Settling of the Milky Way Disk Revealed by Precise ChronoGal Ages," and the lead author is David Mirabal. Mirabal is a pre-doctoral researcher at the Instituto de Astrofisica de Canarias (IAC).

"The spatial distribution of Galactic disk stars of different ages and metallicities encodes its dynamical evolution history and formation pathways," the authors write in their research letter. "The lack of precise ages for large, representative samples of stars has traditionally been a drawback for securely differentiating among formation scenarios proposed to explain the vertical properties of the Milky Way disk."

This work is based on data in Gaia Data Release 3 (DR3), released in June, 2022. This research is one of multiple papers released by a collaboration of researchers, aimed at deciphering stellar ages in the populations of the thin and thick disks. In this one, the authors employed colour-magnitude diagram fitting of the Gaia data. From that, they derived age-metallicity distributions for the stellar populations. They also studied their vertical profile, their position in either disk.

Earlier in the Universe's history, the gas in the Milky Way was more turbulent and the galaxy was more puffed up vertically, as if it was one thick disk. Stars were born orbiting in the thick disk. As the gas calmed over time and became less turbulent, it cooled down and shrank down, creating a thinner disk inside the thick disk. There was a significant change about 10.8 billion years ago, a couple of billion years after the Big Bang, as the thin disk formed, with the older stars maintaining their orbits in the thick disk.

The existence of a clear break in the process about 10.8 billion years ago is the main argument in this research. The rate of vertical settling changed drastically at around this time.

"We find evidence that the scale height decrease rate changed drastically (~10.85) Gyr ago: at older ages, the evolution is very steep, with hZ (vertical scale height) dropping from over 1.26 to â¼0.5 kpc in merely 2 Gyr; subsequently, the thinning is very gradual, with hZ â¼ 0.1 kpc for the youngest populations," the authors write.

So before about 10.85 Gyr ago, the disk flattened rapidly. In only about 2 billion years, it thinned from about 4,100 light years thick down to only about 1,600 light years thick. After that, the thinning was much more gradual, reaching a thickness of only about 400 light years.

This figure from the research is packed with information that wrinkly astrophysicist brains can decipher. For the rest of us, the important part is the sharp drop in hZ, or vertical scale height, about 10.8 billion years ago. This is when the disk stopped thinning so drastically, leaving ancient stars on orbits in the thick disk, well above or below the remaining thin disk. Image Credit: David Mirabal et al 2026 ApJL 1010 L38

This means the vertical structure of the disk components grew from the thick layer inward, an upside-down scenario. Ancient stars in the thick disk weren't scattered there by interactions with gas clouds, spiral arms, or other things; they were born there when the disk was much thicker overall. As time passed, generations of stars were born in a successively thinner disk.

"The extremely rapid early vertical settling of the disk points to a fundamentally upside-down formation process at early times, when the thick disk component is basically formed," the researchers explain. "Enabled by precise ages and high completeness, our results exhibit two clear, time-dependent evolutionary phases for the Galactic disk, which allow piecing together a comprehensive picture of the physical processes responsible for a two-phase assembly history of the Galactic disk."

This created what we see today. The thick disk has older, metal-poor stars and almost no gas for new star formation. The thin disk has younger stars, is the site of most star formation, and holds most of the gas for new stars.

"We thus strongly support an evolutionary scenario defined by two distinct temporal phases: an initial, highly turbulent epoch that gave birth to the thick disk, and a subsequent more quiescent period of secular evolution and gradual gas settling, which formed the thin disk," the authors write.

#5651. These tiny snakes cartwheel to escape danger

New videos reveal, step by step, how the snakes pull off these rare acrobatics

This Pseudorabdion saravacense snake looks like it’s ready to slither, but new video captures its wildly acrobatic way of moving.

Cartwheeling seems like it should require both arms and legs. But some itty-bitty snakes in Southeast Asia manage it without either.

Scientists first shared snapshots of a dwarf reed snake (Pseudorabdion longiceps) cartwheeling away from danger in 2023. Now, videos of three Pseudorabdion species hightailing it down a racetrack in the lab have offered a closer look at this rare and strangely acrobatic behavior.

Each snake starts with its body curled into a C shape, head lifted off the ground and facing the way it’s going. It then whips its tail out behind it, briefly becoming airborne. The snake flips its tail forward and down past its head. When the snake’s tail hits the ground in front of it, another roll begins, researchers report October 19 in Current Biology.

We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday.

“What a cool snake behavior,” says Jessica Tingle, a biologist at Brown University in Providence, R.I., not involved in the work. “I find it especially amazing that an elongate animal like a snake can manage to roll, especially given that they do it in such an explosive way, which probably asks a lot of their muscles and bones.”

Yosuke Kojima was equally surprised when a colleague in Sarawak, Malaysia recounted witnessing this “very weird” behavior in 2019. So the researchers collected snakes from Malaysia’s Kubah National Park. They scooped up 11 P. saravacense, one P. collaris, one P. longiceps, plus six Calamaria reed snakes for comparison.

Finding the snakes was no easy task. They were only about 11 to 28 centimeters long. “They always hide under the leaves,” says Kojima, an ecologist at Toho University in Chiba, Japan. But were any of the snakes able to cartwheel away from the researchers? “No,” Kojima laughs. “We are quicker than them.”

The team set up a 2.2-meter-long racetrack and placed the snakes one at a time at one end. A gentle tap coaxed the snakes to move and cameras above and beside the track recorded the action. None of the Calamaria snakes cartwheeled, but all of the Pseudorabdion snakes did. At full tilt, they tumbled at 1.7 to 3.7 meters per second — about six to 13 kilometers per hour.

Through it all, the snakes held their heads fairly steady — reminiscent of how flying snakes hold their heads stable while launching into the air, the researchers say.

A cartwheeling snake first rolls its body along the ground to whip its tail out behind its head. Then, it swings its tail up and over its head. Finally, it brings its tail down on either the left or right side of its head. Once it the tail hits the ground, the snake is ready for another roll. In the cartwheel seen on top, the snake brings its tail down on the left side of its head, angling its whitish belly toward the viewer. In the cartwheel on the bottom, the snake’s tail lands on the right side of its head, showing its darker back to the viewer.

“I am very curious how such a strange and possibly demanding movement evolved … whether dwarf reed snakes have something special in their anatomy, physiology or neural control that lets them do it when their close relatives apparently can’t or don’t,” Tingle says. “Are their brains different in some way to let them coordinate a fast and unusual movement? Or do all snakes have the basic equipment, and they just don’t use it?”

Those may be open mysteries. But catching snakes cartwheeling on camera does tie up one loose end for Tingle.

“Some writers in the 1800s talked about ‘hoop snakes’ and described their motion as rolling, but that seemed pretty fantastical to me, and I chalked it up to overly inventive imaginations,” Tingle says. Those 19th century authors may or may not have been describing the exact behavior Kojima’s team recorded. “Either way, it’s neat to find out a real animal does something reminiscent of a fantastical old story.”
