#4650. Massive XRP Giveaway Announced by Binance

Popular cryptocurrency exchange Binance has revealed that it will be giving away a huge amount of XRP to its users. The crypto exchange will reward users who hold Ripple's RLUSD stablecoin on its platform. In a new announcement released on October 21, Binance said it will offer a grand total of 600,000 XRP in rewards.

The reward program is set to start on Oct. 23 and will end on Nov. 20.

As part of the XRP reward program, Binance will be airdropping the rewards on a weekly basis. The first airdrop is expected to take place on October 30. How can Binance users qualify for XRP rewards?

For users to qualify for the Binance XRP reward program, they will be required to hold a minimum balance of just 0.01 RLUSD in one of the qualifying accounts (spot, funding, earn, and margin accounts).

Binance has also confirmed that users won't need to hold XRP in their accounts in order to qualify for the giveaway. The exchange will calculate its XRP rewards based on the qualifying RLUSD balances.

Some of the factors to be considered include the users' average qualifying RLUSD balances and the effective annual percentage rate (APR) as determined by the exchange.

Binance has also revealed that there is no individual cap on the reward amount. However, it will end the reward program once the 600,000 XRP reward pool has been exhausted.

The crypto exchange has unveiled a few bonus multipliers that could significantly increase the amount of XRP that qualifying participants will receive.

Specifically, Binance users who increase their RLUSD holdings (compared to the last two weeks) will get a 1.2x reward multiplier on the qualifying incremental amount.

This means those who qualify for both will be able to claim a maximum 1.3x reward multiplier on their eligible holdings.

#4649. The FDE Bubble: Silicon Valley’s Most Expensive Game Of Pretend

Congratulations to the enterprise AI company that just raised another nine-figure round to solve a problem software is supposed to solve by itself: getting humans to use the product.

You know the one. Won’t name it. Don’t have to. You’ve read the press release — “autonomous,” “agentic,” “category-defining” in paragraph one, and then, a few paragraphs down, where the money is actually going: hiring an army of engineers to sit inside customer offices and manually make the “autonomous” thing behave.

Nothing says “we’ve built the future of work” quite like raising half a billion dollars to hire more people to do the work.

Here’s how it works, in case the deck was too artfully vague:

This is not a flywheel. This is a payroll with a pitch deck.

And the industry has stopped even pretending otherwise — it’s fundraising on it, loudly.

OpenAI pledged up to $1.5 billion into a deployment joint venture valued at $10 billion, with another $4 billion from TPG, Bain Capital, Advent, Brookfield and Goanna. The vehicle, DeployCo, has been reported with more than $4 billion in investment.

Meanwhile, job postings for “forward deployed engineer” are up somewhere between 800% and 1,000% year over year. Lightcast data reported by Fortune put the increase at more than 1,000% from January to August 2026. Financial Times data carried by PYMNTS put listings up more than 800% from January to September 2025, and Indeed data showed postings rising from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330 in April 2026. Total comp is pushing past $300,000 in the U.S. Employer postings list Anthropic at $280,000 to $320,000 base pay and OpenAI at $185,000 to $300,000 base, plus equity. Fortune reported some Anthropic roles reaching $400,000 in total compensation.

Somewhere, a VC is calling this “operating leverage” with a completely straight face.

It is not operating leverage. It’s a staffing agency that learned to say “agentic.”

Ask the one question the pitch deck conveniently never answers: “If you pulled the embedded engineer out tomorrow, does the customer still get what they paid for?”

If the honest answer is “well, no, but the ARR is real” — that’s not product-market fit. That’s people-market fit, and people-market fit has a brutally unglamorous ceiling: You can only hire humans so fast, and you can never hire them cheap enough to earn a software multiple. Every new logo doesn’t get more profitable to serve. It gets another headcount request.

Two companies, same $20 million ARR. One needs 10 engineers to keep the lights on. The other needs 100. Both slides say, “AI software company.” The difference shows up in gross margin, cost to onboard the next customer, and whether that cost falls as the company scales.

Here’s the thing about people-market fit: it feels great right up until someone asks the unfashionable questions: What does it actually cost, fully loaded, to onboard the next customer? Does that number go down as the company scales, or does it just grow the org chart? What happens to gross margin if the first year growth slows and the headcount can’t hide behind hypergrowth anymore?

Companies funding their FDE armies with fresh nine-figure rounds are betting nobody asks these questions until after the round closes and the confetti is swept up. That’s a fine bet in a hype cycle. It’s a terrible one in a market that’s about to start reading the fine print.

Because eventually, someone always reads the fine print. A CFO builds a cost-per-deployment model. A CIO benchmarks time-to-value against three vendors instead of trusting one demo. An investor finally does the unglamorous work of separating software ARR from human-powered ARR on the same balance sheet.

The distinction buyers and investors can test is on the numbers. Software ARR should get cheaper to deliver as it scales. Human-powered ARR does not. OpenAI guaranteed the private-equity backers of its deployment vehicle an annual 17.5% return over five years, which is a different structure from a growth thesis.¹

So no, your AI isn’t autonomous. But your business model might be — autonomously transferring the cost of a services company onto a software valuation, for as long as the music plays.

Enjoy the round. Hope the org chart was worth it.

#4647. A Post-Vitalik Ethereum Could Still Thrive, Says Co-Founder

Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin has pointed to the network’s growing independence as a clear marker of its maturity. In comments made during an October 9, 2026 interview with Balaji Srinivasan at the Network State Conference, Buterin said a healthy sign for Ethereum is that it can continue progressing even without his constant involvement.

The crypto Billionaire spoke about the trauma that came with holding a front-facing role in Ethereum. Envisioning a future where multiple leaders thrive, the Ethereum co-founder said decentralization is not about going from one leader to zero, but from one to many, and that Ethereum is beginning to move in that direction.

“The thing that I look forward to is, are other people in the foundation and community able to push it forward and defend the right values even without my involvement? And I feel like that has recently been happening more and more and very effectively,” he asserts.

Buterin noted that remaining in the spotlight for so long can be draining. He framed decentralization not as a shift from one leader to none, but as a move from one leader to many. He suggested Ethereum is beginning to demonstrate that transition.

He evaluates progress by whether people across the Ethereum Foundation and the broader community can keep driving the project forward and uphold core values such as security and privacy on their own. In his view, a particularly encouraging development is that more contributors are now taking initiative without waiting for him to speak first.

The remarks highlight Buterin’s long-standing emphasis on reducing reliance on any single individual as the ecosystem matures.

Vitalik Buterin proposed Ethereum in a 2013 whitepaper and co-founded the project, which launched in July 2015 as a smart-contract platform. He has led the network’s evolution into the leading decentralized computing network, including the shift to proof-of-stake. #4646. 10 Lessons From 10 Years — Here’s What It Really Takes to Build a Lasting Business

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

After 10 years, I’ve learned building a business that endures means creating an organization that can adapt when reality inevitably changes.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

It’s a strange moment to write about what makes companies last, with global uncertainty at historically high levels and leaders being asked to make huge bets on AI, talent and where the world is going. At the same time, employees are nine times more likely to be engaged when they believe their company has a secure future.

Consider those two findings for a second: The defining tension of building a company today is that the ground is shakier than ever, and the people you lead need more steadiness than ever. That tension is exactly what the last decade of building Ninety has been about.

I spent years as an investor and board member watching capable teams struggle with the same problems: lack of shared vision, cultures without purpose, priorities that didn’t translate into coordinated work and leaders who didn’t have a connected view of how their company was running. I founded Ninety to build the business operating system I kept wishing those companies had. In doing so, I started living these problems from the inside.

On our 10th anniversary, here are 10 lessons I learned the hard way.

That’s what attracts and retains great people. I keep coming back to the old Drucker line that culture eats strategy for breakfast. For me, that is how enduring organizations work. Ninety was never trying to be the cheapest tool or the one with the most features. It was built around service, stewardship, high trust and doing the right thing. That culture is what let us keep good people through hard years.

Every company has to choose a core value proposition, whether it’s lowest cost, constant innovation, exclusivity or service. We picked service, and we’ve been tested on it. When COVID hit and revenue dried up for entire client sectors overnight, the short-term move would’ve been to nickel-and-dime people to protect our own numbers. We chose the opposite. We gave customers grace when they were struggling, because in the long run, genuine care builds trust that no marketing budget can buy.

No plan survives the first real hit; what matters is whether the company can absorb it and keep functioning. That is resilience, and it comes from where you spend your energy. Energy is finite. Spend it fighting things you can’t control, like market swings, regulatory shifts or a competitor’s moves, and you’ll have nothing left for what you can control, like aligning your team, executing and managing cash.

A few years ago, EOS Worldwide dissolved its licensing program and launched its own operating system software. Ninety’s software had been built around that ecosystem, so this was a gut punch. We could have thrown a fit, taken our ball and gone home. Instead, we went back to the questions that keep our decisions aligned with who we are. We chose to stay agreeable, keep improving our own platform and remain true to our principles. When EOS later reached back out to reestablish the partnership, that grace paid off and we reengaged on far better terms.

You have to anticipate where the world is going and invest before customers even know they need it, whether it’s adopting technology in traditional spaces or integrating AI into core workflows. It’s important to temper your ambition with honesty, as real organizational evolution takes two to three years of grinding effort.

The market has a major impact on what kind of culture you need. A fast-moving, highly ambiguous space, like what we see around AI right now, requires people who can live in the gray area, move quickly, work hard and adapt. A culture that was perfectly right for one environment can start to break when the environment changes.

Every stage of development asks something different of the people leading it. Some leaders keep expanding their capacity to handle complexity, while others hit a ceiling they can’t even see. For example, the right team at 10 people might not be the team that carries you through to 100 or 300 employees. Someone can be a wonderful human and strong cultural fit and still not have what the next, more complex seat requires. Building something enduring means telling the truth about what the company needs.

Founders make plenty of bad calls. The point is to stay humble enough to see what happened, own it and get better. But some decisions can’t be undone, like letting people go. Those one-way-door moments can reveal a lot about the true nature of the organization and its leaders. You can delegate a lot of tasks and responsibilities as you scale, but you can’t fully outsource decisions that carry deep cultural consequences. Those you have to carry yourself and make sure they align with your values.

Great companies don’t have bystanders. The thing that goes unsaid is usually the thing that eventually hurts you. Enduring organizations create shared language and enough trust that people feel comfortable raising issues before they become permanent problems. At Ninety, we lean on phrases like “succeed or escalate,” “what’s working, what’s not,” and “what are the growth opportunities?” They may sound simple, but each one gives someone permission to surface a concern early.

It takes more than everyone simply doing their specific function well or optimizing their own department. Everyone has to understand what winning looks like and be able to step outside their lane when the company needs it. That is where a vision, a handful of clear quarterly rocks and an integrated business operating system earn their keep: They turn scattered priorities into coordinated work that everyone can see.

Ten years in, with more than 18,500 companies now running on Ninety, I’m more convinced than ever that building a great organization is a beautiful mess. It’s hard, humbling and full of lessons you would never have chosen. Love the game, be great at the work, stay humble, show grace and keep getting better. If the creek don’t rise and the sun still sets, you’ll find a way forward and be stronger for it in the end.

#4644. Europe wants more than promises from American tech

Margot Edelman – Global Tech Practice Lead, EdelmanCamilo Lascano Tribin – Senior Director, Tech Advisory, Edelman

European businesses are questioning how much control they have over the technology they depend on. For American tech companies, that creates a growing test of credibility, with consequences for how they compete and earn trust.

“We want to be sure nobody has a kill switch.” That was European Commission Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen’s warning when Brussels unveiled its latest technology sovereignty proposals in June. It captured a concern increasingly relevant to European businesses: what happens if access to the American technology supporting critical operations is disrupted by decisions made outside Europe?

Europe relies heavily on US cloud infrastructure, and the Commission is turning concerns about that dependence into measurable requirements. Its Cloud Sovereignty Framework assesses providers against 48 criteria covering legal control, data, operations and supply chains. Although developed for public procurement, the Commission encourages private organisation to use the framework too. Its proposed Cloud and AI development Act would extend sovereignty assessments across public-sector cloud services, with higher requirements for more sensitive uses. Virkkunen has acknowledged that some of the highest levels would be difficult for American providers to reach because of US legal obligations.

For American technology companies, this presents a growing commercial and reputational challenge. They have spent years demonstrating what their technology can do. European customers increasingly want to understand what providers can guarantee when political circumstances change. The answers depend on how services are operated and the commitments companies are prepared to make. Those business decisions are shaping the credibility of corporate promises long before they are communicated.

Three areas show how these expectations are changing.

The first is the control European customers retain over their technology.

In April this year, a European consortium led by Belgian telecommunications company Proximus won one of four contracts together worth up to €180 million under the Commission’s sovereign cloud procurement programme. The consortium uses services from S3NS, a French company controlled by Thales that operates Google Cloud technology. American technology therefore qualified for the Commission’s own sovereign cloud requirements through a European operating structure.

The three providers built around European technology received a higher sovereignty rating. That distinction shows both the opportunity for American technology and the limits imposed by remaining dependencies. How a company structures its service affects the control it can offer customers and the strength of the claims it can make about that control.

The second is what happens when a provider’s commitments are tested.

Microsoft offers one useful example. In April 2025, Brad Smith announced that Microsoft would challenge any government order to suspend its European cloud operations through available legal avenues, including the courts. The company subsequently made that commitment legally binding in contracts with European national governments and the Commission.

Smith also explained what would happen if a legal challenge ultimately failed, including continuity arrangements with European partners and backup copies of Microsoft’s code held in Switzerland.

For the technology leader responsible for keeping essential services running, these details provide a basis for assessing what protections exist and what happens in an extreme scenario. For executives, they make the risks attached to a critical supplier easier to understand. The commitment does not eliminate every possible disruption, but it gives customers a clearer account of what Microsoft has undertaken to do and where the limits remain.

The third is the substance of a company’s presence in Europe.

The Commission’s sovereignty framework considers the extent to which investment, jobs and value creation remain within the EU and European Economic Area. Public trust creates a separate pressure towards meaningful local commitment.

Edelman’s 2026 Trust Barometer found that people trusted companies headquartered in their own country more than foreign-headquartered companies in every market examined for that comparison. In Germany, 63 percent trusted German-headquartered companies, compared with 34 percent for companies headquartered abroad. When asked how a company from a country they distrust could earn their trust locally, respondents globally most frequently identified long-term community investment and local hiring.

These findings point to the importance of substantive local relationships for multinational companies. The Barometer describes a “polynational” approach centred on long-term commitments to the communities in which companies operate. For American technology providers, that raises questions about how their European presence is reflected in the business itself, including where they invest and how much authority sits within their local operations.

As sovereignty becomes measurable, claims about European control are becoming easier to challenge. Procurement teams can assess them against published criteria, while journalists and competitors can examine the dependencies behind them. Companies that describe their services as sovereign without being clear about those dependencies risk accusations of sovereignty-washing.

This is where reputation and communications become part of the business decisions. The credibility of a company’s European commitments depends on the operating arrangement and protections behind them. Communications leaders can help organisations anticipate the expectations those decisions will need to meet, bringing an understanding of customer concerns and public trust into the discussion before commitments are made.

American technology companies have established what their products can deliver. Europe’s changing expectations mean they now need to demonstrate what they can guarantee when circumstances change. Their reputation will increasingly depend on how clearly they explain those guarantees and how well the underlying business decisions support them.

Even Healthcare has raised $50 million in a funding round led by Khosla Ventures, as the Indian startup seeks to change how hospitals in the world’s most populous country make money and how doctors are rewarded.

The all-equity Series B round also saw participation from Alpha Wave and valued it at $300 million, triple its valuation 18 months ago, according to Even. The latest funding brings Even’s total capital raised to more than $120 million.

Hospitals in India typically earn more when patients undergo additional tests, procedures, or longer stays, even when they may not need them. Even wants to change that by focusing on patient care rather than pushing unnecessary treatments that drive up medical bills.

That approach has continued to appeal to Khosla Ventures, which has invested in Even since its seed round in 2021. The Silicon Valley venture firm is doubling down as Even moves to build and operate hospitals of its own, giving it greater control over patient care and costs.

Vinod Khosla, founder of Khosla Ventures, said Even’s approach goes beyond fixing individual parts of a fragmented healthcare system by bringing financing, technology, and care delivery together. The goal, he said in a prepared statement, is to reward better health outcomes rather than greater use of healthcare services.

“The clinical and economic results so far give us conviction that this can become an important new model for healthcare in India and beyond,” Khosla said.

Initially, Even relied on partner hospitals to treat its members, but Mayank Banerjee, who founded the startup in 2021 with Matilde Giglio and Alessandro Ialongo, said in an interview that the startup began questioning that model after finding that patients had undergone tests or been recommended surgeries it considered unnecessary.

“We think patients are getting not only a bad experience, but insurers are getting a bad economic deal. And as a result, we decided to fix it by building our own hospitals,” Banerjee told TechCrunch.

Even has since opened two hospitals in Bengaluru, both of which reached operational profitability, excluding depreciation, within a year of opening, Banerjee stated. The startup plans to open five more hospitals in the southern Indian city over the next six months, funded by its latest round.

Banerjee told TechCrunch that Even leases hospital buildings on 20-year agreements and spends on fitting them out and buying medical equipment.

Unlike hospitals where doctors may have financial incentives to recommend more procedures, Even pays its doctors fixed salaries — not tying their compensation to the number of tests or surgeries they perform, though Banerjee said doctors can earn bonuses based on patient-reported clinical outcomes.

The same principle extends to hospital management, whose compensation is not linked to the number of procedures performed, Banerjee told TechCrunch. #4638. Why So Many Small Businesses Stop Growing — and What It Takes to Break Through

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Most small businesses don’t fail because of bad products or weak demand. They stall because they’ve reached a predictable growth breakpoint.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

Do you remember what it felt like when the business started gaining momentum? New customers were coming in, revenue kept climbing, and every milestone felt like proof that your hard work was paying off.

Then, almost without warning, things became more complicated. Customers were still buying. The team was working hard. Revenue may have continued to grow. Yet every decision seemed to land on your desk, every new hire created more questions, and progress required far more effort than it did a year earlier.

If that sounds familiar, you’re not alone. I’ve seen this pattern play out with businesses across industries, and it usually has very little to do with the quality of the product or the demand in the market. More frequently, the business has reached what I call a breakpoint.

In The Small-Business Money Game, I describe breakpoints as the moments when a business outgrows the systems, leadership and habits that made it successful during the previous stage. One of the most common breakpoints happens around the $2 million mark. The exact revenue number isn’t the point.

Every business reaches moments where growth requires a different way of operating. The founders who recognize those moments continue building momentum. The ones who don’t find themselves working harder while the business stands still.

Think back to your first year in business. Speed mattered more than structure. You probably made decisions in minutes because everyone involved was sitting in the same room. Customer questions came directly to you. Hiring was informal. Communication happened naturally because the team was small.

Those same habits can become liabilities as the business grows.

Growth brings complexity. More customers create more demands. More employees create more communication. More opportunities require more decisions. The systems that felt perfectly adequate at $500,000 in revenue begin showing signs of strain as the business expands.

That doesn’t mean you’ve done something wrong. It means the business has grown beyond the operating model that helped you build it.

The habits that made you successful during the early years can become the very things that limit future growth. Think about your own business. Does every important decision still come through you? Does your team wait for approval before moving forward? Have you found yourself answering the same questions over and over because everyone assumes you’ll have the answer?

Imagine your sales manager has a proposal ready to send but waits two days for your approval. At the same time, your operations manager needs permission to hire another technician, and your finance team has questions about next month’s cash flow. None of those decisions feels especially significant on its own. Together, they create an organization that’s waiting for one person to move.

I saw this firsthand with a client. John had been a mason for more than 30 years. He knew concrete, brick and stone better than almost anyone I had met. He could look at a restoration project and understand exactly what the work would require. But as his company grew, he was still running the business the way he had once run a job site. Too much depended on what John knew, what he decided and what he could personally keep track of.

The numbers exposed the problem. His pricing covered labor and materials, but the business had expenses (rent, insurance, administrative costs, his own compensation or the expense of carrying the company through slower winter months) that never made it into his estimates. John had steady work and a full calendar, yet every new project could still leave money on the table.

We rebuilt his pricing from the ground up. We created five calculators, one for each major type of project his crews performed, so every estimate accounted for overhead and an appropriate profit margin. We also tied crew compensation to project deadlines, giving the people doing the work a financial stake in finishing jobs efficiently.

Those systems changed more than John’s pricing. They gave him a way to run the company without carrying every calculation and decision in his own head.

Over the next four years, his business grew from roughly $500,000 in annual revenue to nearly $3 million. The team tripled in size. John bought the house he had been working toward for years.

That growth required John to become something more than the company’s best mason. He had to build a business that could take what he knew and turn it into systems other people could follow. That’s what a breakpoint looks like in real life.

The founder hasn’t become the problem. The business has simply reached a size where one person’s capacity can no longer support the organization’s potential.

It’s easy to underestimate how far simple systems can take a business.

Many successful companies begin with conversations instead of documentation. Team members remember customer preferences because they’ve worked with them for years. Processes live inside people’s heads instead of written procedures. Everyone knows who to ask because the organization is small enough that communication feels effortless.

Have you ever noticed how those same systems become less reliable as the company grows?

New employees don’t have years of institutional knowledge. Customers receive different experiences depending on who answers the phone. Managers solve similar problems in completely different ways because there isn’t a consistent process to follow.

Those inconsistencies don’t appear overnight. They’ve always been there. Growth simply makes them impossible to ignore.

Breakpoints have a way of exposing weaknesses that smaller businesses can comfortably work around.

One of the biggest shifts founders make during this stage has nothing to do with finance, operations or sales. It has everything to do with leadership.

In the early years, your value comes from solving problems. As the business grows, your value comes from building a team that can solve problems without waiting for you.

That transition isn’t easy because it asks entrepreneurs to let go of responsibilities that once defined their role. Delegating decisions can feel uncomfortable. Creating structure can seem slower than jumping in yourself. Investing time in developing leaders doesn’t produce immediate results.

Over time, though, those changes create something every growing business needs: capacity.

When your team understands the mission, has clear expectations and has the authority to make good decisions, the business stops depending on one person’s availability. That’s the foundation for the next stage of growth.

It’s frustrating when a business feels harder to manage than it did a year or two ago. Many entrepreneurs assume that means growth has reached its limit. I’ve learned to see those moments differently.

Every growing business reaches breakpoints. They aren’t warning signs that you’ve failed. They’re evidence that your company has grown beyond the systems and leadership practices that brought you this far.

The businesses that continue growing don’t avoid breakpoints. They recognize them, adapt to them and build the leadership, processes and accountability the next stage requires. If your business feels heavier today than it did a year ago, pause before assuming you’ve reached your ceiling. Ask yourself a different question: Has my business stopped growing, or has it reached its next breakpoint?

The answer to that question may determine how far your company can grow from here.

Do you remember what it felt like when the business started gaining momentum? New customers were coming in, revenue kept climbing, and every milestone felt like proof that your hard work was paying off.

The infrastructure around an AI model can dramatically change its results. Here’s why it matters when evaluating claims — and choosing tools.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

AGI, artificial general intelligence, means a system that can match or beat human performance across essentially any cognitive task, not just the narrow ones it was trained for. That distinction matters because it’s exactly what’s being argued about right now.

Jensen Huang has a habit of declaring victory at the exact moment his company needs the market to believe it. So when OpenAI shipped GPT-6 Astra on Sept. 3, Huang posted on X that “AGI has arrived,” adding that “400K GPUs” are “coming online next.”

I’m an AI engineer, and I’m currently building ChatMakr, an AI-powered platform that creates multi-agent chatbots in seconds. I spent the last year watching every model release get relabeled as the one that finally changes everything. Here is the truth.

Astra isn’t a chatbot upgrade. OpenAI positions it as an operator — a system that browses, codes, runs multi-step workflows, models in Blender and handles cybersecurity tasks with minimal supervision. The benchmark numbers are genuinely striking: 97.6% on FrontierMath Tier 4, 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3, and a perfect 100% on ExploitBench, up from 78.5% for its predecessor, GPT-5.6 Sol. Those are not incremental gains.

But OpenAI itself, notably, stopped short of calling Astra AGI, choosing instead to emphasize “speed and versatility.” That restraint from the company that built the model is the tell.

The pushback isn’t really about whether Astra is impressive. It clearly is. It’s about the word AGI itself. Wharton professor Ethan Mollick has argued for months that what we’re seeing is “jagged AGI:” systems that are superhuman in some narrow domains and unreliable in others, which is a very different thing from general intelligence.

There’s still no agreed upon test for AGI, so declaring it “arrived” is closer to a subjective judgment call than a measurement. Augmented Mind and Manolo Remiddi put it plainly: “AGI is the point where you can trust the output without checking. We are not there.”

This is where it gets interesting for anyone actually building with these models. On paper, Astra and Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5.1 look neck and neck on several coding and reasoning benchmarks. In practice, engineers report meaningfully different output quality. Fable tends to produce more production-ready code with fewer output tokens, while Astra’s strength shows up more in computer use and multi-step automation. Snorkel AI’s testing revealed that Fable fails through a fundamentally different pattern than how it succeeds, losing valid work mid-trajectory rather than running out of budget, which matters far more to a shipping team than a leaderboard percentage.

The reason similar scores can hide such different outputs comes down to something most people outside AI engineering have never heard of: the harness.

A harness is the infrastructure wrapped around a model: the prompt assembly, tool access, memory management and error recovery that determine how the raw model is actually put to work. As MindStudio describes it: “A model is the engine. The harness is the car. Most benchmarks test the car.” Swap the harness and the same model produces wildly different results. Cursor’s own research found one model scoring 46% accuracy with one harness and 80% with another, with no model change at all.

Astra’s own benchmark history makes the point better than any explainer could. On ARC-AGI-3, Astra scored 62.7% using a standard harness, essentially tied with its predecessor, and 99.9% using a “Provider Adapter” harness that let it retain and reuse prior reasoning instead of restarting cold on every step, according to Augmented Mind and Manolo Remiddi. Same weights. Same model. A 37 point swing from infrastructure alone. That’s not a small footnote. It means a huge share of what gets reported as “model improvement” is actually harness improvement, and leaderboards that don’t control for it are telling a story, not reporting a fact.

It’s one of the best harness companies in the world, the layer that decides how coding models actually get used. Folding it into SpaceX’s GPU fleet is a bet that owning the layer is as valuable as owning the model, maybe more. OpenAI has already cut Cursor’s access to its models over trust concerns tied to Musk’s companies, which tells you the model providers see the same thing: Whoever controls the harness controls the product experience, regardless of which model sits underneath it.

Nobody serious disputes that GPT-6 Astra is a major model. What’s contested is the leap from “major model” to “AGI has arrived.” If you’re evaluating these systems for your own business, ignore the AGI framing entirely. #4624. How To Build A Billion-Dollar Company Without Venture Capital

Today’s venture capital ecosystem looks markedly different from even just five years ago.

Most significantly, a small number of AI companies are now attracting the vast majority of available venture capital. In Q1 2026, just four companies collectively raised $188 billion, which alone accounted for 65% of total global venture investment, per Crunchbase data.

Meanwhile, in that same quarter, just 20% of global VC funding went to non-AI firms. Deal count was also down 30%, meaning that checks are being made out to fewer companies all around.

So what happens to startups when the VC route is all but closed?

I built a company into a multibillion-dollar business without any venture funding. Here’s a few lessons I picked up along the way:

Many founders unknowingly treat fundraising as a measure of success. But while it’s useful, funding doesn’t result in success on its own.

Achieving strong levels of customer trust is also helpful when it comes to inspiring investor confidence. High retention rates, repeat purchases and sustained engagement drive the  financial fundamentals on which strong valuations are built. In B2B SaaS, for instance, companies with net revenue retention above 110% are valued at 2.4x the median. Additionally, customers provide a feedback loop that helps create the discipline every company needs, and each user is a vote of confidence that compounds over time.

Too many early-stage companies set prices too low because they fear that higher prices will repel customers. In my experience, however, underpricing more often signals uncertainty rather than good value. Customers want quality products and services, and they expect that the quality will be reflected in the price.

Incredibly, only 22% of B2B SaaS firms conduct rigorous pricing research, and firms more commonly regret underpricing than overpricing. So at the outset, it’s important for startups to inform themselves what price their product or service can actually sustain, and then raise quality if it’s too low.

Equally, in services markets where prices are more established around a norm, superior quality can be an important differentiator. At the same time, however, companies must continually resist the temptation to chase customers with ever-cheaper prices, which can start a race to the bottom.

Too many founders hire for speed rather than quality. Take the time needed to raise the bar with talent. Hire the right people, and they’ll help maximize every last drop of value.

Many years ago, I decided to focus on just a small fraction of our applicants and hire only the top 1% of talent. When we found the best developers, it meant we could compete on delivery quality rather than just price. Keeping costs low is important, but skimping on talent is not a winning strategy.

One of our best decisions was to replace manual hiring processes with proprietary machine-learning systems at an early stage. Not only do these systems screen developer applications, but they can also build, match and deploy top-quality teams for each client project.

This advance allowed us to cut costs associated with evaluating applications, accelerate lead times, and improve the quality of our teamwork. In parallel, we also invested in creating similar systems for our digital marketing so that growth wouldn’t require proportional increases in overhead.

Whether it’s in recruitment, sales or operations, process automation can create some of the biggest drivers of growth and help bring about conditions needed for significant expansion to occur.

Companies thrive in challenging funding environments by operating with discipline. That means obsessing over unit economics, prioritizing activities that directly drive revenue, questioning every cost, and eliminating anything that isn’t pulling its weight.

Not only does this aid growth, but it also makes a business more resilient in hard times. Financial constraints drive leaders to create efficiencies by automating workflows, streamlining processes and inventing innovative work models.

Years before COVID-19 forced others to follow our lead, our company streamlined its operations and boosted scalability by developing a remote-working model. When the pandemic made remote work necessary, the fact that we already had the systems and workflows in place gave us a competitive advantage over other firms, and this in turn led to significant growth.

The past decade convinced many founders that venture capital was the default path to building a meaningful company. But while funding cycles rise and fall, the fundamental principles of good business don’t. Infusions of capital can accelerate growth, but they cannot create a great company from scratch.

On the longest timescale, the most successful founders are the ones committed to solving problems rather than just selling a product and chasing funding. In a world short on funding, growth simply can’t happen without it.

#4617. ‘Weird And Maybe Uninvestable’: How Reach Capital Spots Its Venture Winners

Before James Kim evaluated education startups, he used their products in his classroom.

The Reach Capital partner began his career as a high school math teacher with Teach For America after studying astrophysics at Yale. He later worked in college access and Yale admissions, and earned graduate degrees in business and education from Stanford before joining Reach. As a teacher, he was an early user of ClassDojo, an experience he describes as “game-changing.”

His investment firm, Reach Capital, has also evolved. Founded in 2015 after spinning out of NewSchools Venture Fund, the San Francisco-based firm initially focused on technology for K-12 schools and districts. Its mandate has since expanded to learning, health and work, with investments in companies including ClassDojo, Cartwheel, Stepful and Replit.

In August, Reach announced its fifth fund, a $265 million vehicle that brought its assets under management to nearly $1 billion. The firm, which has backed more than 180 companies, plans to invest in approximately 50 additional teams through the new fund, targeting pre-seed, seed and Series A rounds.

Crunchbase News spoke with Kim about how Reach’s education roots inform its broader strategy, what it saw in AI detection company GPTZero, and why some of its best investments initially divided its own team. We also discussed how ownership stakes and secondary transactions have shaped the firm’s approach to returning capital.

This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

Crunchbase News: You started your career as a high school math teacher. How did that shape your view of education technology?

Kim: I taught ninth through 12th grade, mostly Algebra I and geometry. I was very early to ClassDojo. Back then, it was a very simple classroom management tool, and it was game-changing for me. It has since evolved into a sprawling suite, but I already knew technology could move the needle.

Reach began as an education investor. How did the firm’s focus evolve into learning, health and work?

Kim: Reach started in 2015, and our original focus was exclusively on learning and education technology — more specifically, technology sold into schools and districts.

Our founding partners were seeing the growth of devices in schools, one-to-one device ratios and broadband penetration. The belief was that you would start to see a proliferation of technology serving students, teachers and administrators and moving the needle on learning outcomes. That is what ended up playing out. Some of our early bets are now household names in edtech, such as Newsela, Nearpod and ClassDojo.

Reach spun out of NewSchools Venture Fund, where three of our four founding partners had started doing for-profit seed investing before 2015. Over time, we branched out from K-12 into higher education, and then into consumer learning.

We also started getting pulled toward healthcare and work. Health and wellbeing affect a learner’s readiness for the classroom, so we were being pulled toward mental health, behavioral health and developmental health. On the work side, formal learning typically ends in college, but you still need the bridge to a job. Once you’re on the job, you need to continue learning, especially in the current environment.

A few years ago, we officially put a stake in the ground and said we invest across learning, health and work. The common thread is that these are the most important systems underlying human development and flourishing.

So far, the breakdown has been roughly 50% learning, 25% health and 25% work.

These sectors have different buyers, regulations and business models. What did investing in education teach you that translates to healthcare and work?

Kim: Education is highly regulated, and institutional buying is complex. Many stakeholders must provide input, and decisions often require board-level approval. We were familiar with and comfortable with regulated products and complex go-to-market motions.

Selling to a university or a K-12 district, in terms of the complexity and the need to intersect with regulation and policy, is quite comparable to selling to a health system, hospital or insurance carrier.

Cartwheel is a good example of where our experience gave us an advantage. It provides mental health teletherapy, but the customer is the school district and the care setting is in schools.

While we were relatively new to behavioral health when we made that investment, we were very familiar with selling to school districts and implementing an intervention within a school setting. That gave us an advantage both in winning the investment opportunity and supporting the company afterward.

Reach talks about “elevating human potential.” What makes an investment fit that mandate?

Kim: We look for products and services that increase access to quality education, quality care or quality earnings, or some combination of those. We really have to see the mechanism for that happening.

It’s not good enough to be pitched a SaaS or AI product and have the founders say, “This will make people more productive at work, and therefore maybe they will earn more or enjoy their jobs more.” That’s plausible, but it isn’t a good enough theory of how the product actually increases access to quality earnings.

Several teammates, including myself, are former educators. We care about meaningful, needle-moving impact.

We address access from both the demand side and the supply side. On the demand side, we build products that create delightful, impactful experiences for consumers: Brilliant, Stepful’s allied healthcare training, and Replit for what its founder calls “citizen developers.”

On the supply side, it’s helping schools, health systems and companies function more efficiently and effectively. WorkWhile, for example, helps logistics and hospitality companies hire hourly workers better and treat them better. We think about the problem from both sides of the market.

Reach backed GPTZero, which agreed to join Superhuman. Was that an investment in solving a problem created by AI?

Kim: That wasn’t actually our investment thesis. In my opinion, they have the best AI detector on the market. But their thesis was that AI use in education should not be banned. It shouldn’t be punitive or become a cat-and-mouse game between the educator and the learner.

Since all of us as professionals use AI in our jobs, students should learn how to use it responsibly and thoughtfully. It was the first AI detector company I had seen with that positioning, as opposed to helping schools, districts and universities clamp down on AI usage.

That’s why I liked it. I don’t see it as a problem for students to use AI for certain purposes, such as brainstorming, organizing thoughts and editing. GPTZero helps educators work with students and teach them how to use AI responsibly.

Kim: Yes, most notably in AI governance and evaluations.

Going back to regulated categories, you need to make sure AI is not irresponsibly using personally identifiable information in education or healthcare. Those categories have very strict regulations, and infrastructure companies are emerging to help ensure AI products interact with that data responsibly.

I do think there are opportunities for large, successful, profitable companies to be created in response to the problems AI creates.

#4608. CertiK Verified 40.68M TPS for Sui, Here Is What It Means

AI-assisted summary, reviewed by NullTX editors.

The biggest throughput figure a blockchain security firm has ever signed off on is sitting on CertiK's homepage right now: 40.68 million transactions per second.

It isn't CertiK's own number, and it isn't an on-chain number either. If you read the 2,000-word report behind it, you'll come to become more interested in what CertiK chose not to verify than in what it did.

Scroll past the client logos and media mentions on CertiK and you'll find it in the "Latest Research And Market Insights" strip. One card says CertiK independently verified Sui's Mainnet Tunnel performance campaign, which recorded more than 15.2 billion off-chain transactions across 7,680 tunnels at a peak of 40.68 million TPS.

Most readers will see "CertiK" next to "40.68 million TPS" and stop there. Crypto social media mostly did the same. Sui's own post described the figure as set live on stage at Sui Basecamp 2026 and certified by CertiK, and added that the network now clears in under 20 seconds what Visa processes in a day.Walking through CertiK's verification method when the report first came out. Now that the number has been promoted to CertiK's front page, it's worth looking at exactly what it represents.

According to CertiK's announcement, Sui ran a live Mainnet performance campaign on October 7, 2026, during Sui Basecamp Day. It recorded 15,241,348,297 tunnel transactions across 7,680 tunnels, and every tunnel was opened and settled on chain.

A Sui Tunnel works roughly like a private room. A small group of participants carries out many application actions without sending each one to the blockchain. Sui records the starting terms when the tunnel opens and the final result when it closes. Everything in between happens off-chain as signed receipts. CertiK compares the design to Bitcoin's Lightning Network but notes that tunnels can handle more than payments.

The test was more varied than a single payment loop. The campaign ran 10 runs across five workload types: simple payments, flash, blackjack, tic-tac-toe and battleship. Payments still made up most of the activity. Its 6,144 tunnels committed more than 12 billion transactions, or 78.98% of the total.

This is the part of the report I respect most. The peak came from the busiest complete second, between 08:46:17 and 08:46:18 UTC, when 40,682,328 qualifying tunnel transactions were committed. The highest 60-second rolling average was 40,339,143.65 TPS.

Doing the math on the gap between those two figures. It's about 343,000 transactions, or roughly 0.84%. In other words, the network held near-peak throughput for a full minute rather than spiking for one second.

CertiK also avoided the easiest way to inflate a number like this. For concurrent tunnels sharing the same time window, it added up their transaction counts once and divided by a single shared duration. It did not add individual per-tunnel rates together or multiply one tunnel's rate by the number of tunnels.

The checking itself was thorough. All 7,680 executions and all 960 slots reached VERIFIED status, and the 15.24 billion committed transactions matched the full-history admitted receipts exactly. The work ran on dedicated AWS infrastructure with 128 vCPUs and 247 GiB of RAM.

There's also a small correction in the story. Sui's first announcement reported 40,614,180 TPS. CertiK's audited peak came in about 68,000 higher, a 0.17% difference. A verifier landing slightly above the project's own claim is unusual, and I think it reflects well on the method.

The homepage card doesn't mention any of this, but the full report is clear about its limits.

First, this isn't on-chain throughput. CertiK states that Tunnel TPS measures off-chain tunnel transactions and is not a measurement of Sui's on-chain capacity.

Second, the data came from the project being tested. The Sui team supplied the campaign data and executed all test scenarios, and CertiK examined the retained evidence and recalculated the totals.

Third, the cryptographic checks were not exhaustive. CertiK reconciled the arithmetic, receipt counts and timestamps, but it did not re-run individual signatures or do a fresh cryptographic replay of every receipt.

Fourth, the report can't speak to how the system would behave in normal use. The records don't establish clock accuracy, failure rates, latency percentiles or results across repeated runs. The sustained figure is the best 60-second window observed, not a guaranteed minimum.

Put simply, CertiK verified that the accounting is correct. It did not verify that Sui can handle 40 million real users' actions every second.

The number that's most telling isn't the TPS figure. More than 15.2 billion off-chain transactions were anchored to Sui through just 1,920 on-chain lifecycle transactions: 960 openings and 960 settlements.

By simple calculation, that's about 7.9 million off-chain actions for every on-chain transaction. That ratio is what the tunnel design is built to achieve. It's an impressive engineering result, but it measures how well Sui compresses activity, not how fast its base layer runs.

This isn't the first speed record CertiK has signed off on, and the earlier ones show why comparisons are tricky. In its earlier TON verification, CertiK worked from per-minute transaction count data and arrived at 104,715 TPS for TON's testnet. In April 2025, Qubic announced 15.52 million TPS verified by CertiK, measured over 10 peak ticks on its Layer 1 mainnet.

On paper, Sui's tunnel figure is about 2.6 times Qubic's and nearly 390 times TON's. But these were three different kinds of tests: a sharded testnet, a base-layer burst and an off-chain channel network. They don't belong on the same leaderboard.

My view is that the 40.68 million figure is real, well documented and carefully bounded. It's also a measure of what an off-chain system can process, not what Sui's blockchain itself handles. CertiK's report is honest about that, even if the one-line homepage card isn't.

#4606. Your Next Customer Might Be An Agent. Here’s How To Build Your Brand For Both Humans And AI.

You’ve just moved into a new house. It’s unfurnished; the walls are bare. Ten years ago, you might have loaded up Google and started searching for furniture that you like, consulting interior design magazines and looking for offers on retailers’ websites. In 2026, however, there’s an entirely different option at your fingertips. You could take a photo of the empty room, upload the image to an AI agent, share your budget and style, and get a list of furniture that suits your preferences. You could even have the agent purchase on your behalf.

Instead of just selling to one customer base — humans — brands are now selling to two.

This second audience of AI agents makes decisions on different criteria than humans. Often, in fact, AI agents will make purchasing decisions before a human ever sees the product page. There’s a real chance that all the emotive copy marketers have so carefully crafted will get broken down and reconstituted by a machine. Just relying on the human touch isn’t enough anymore.

A word of caution, though: Marketers shouldn’t forget the human entirely in the rush to prepare for AI agents. It’s easy to get carried away with visions of an entirely agentic future. But at the end of the day, each agent is still catering to a human with their own preferences, and personalization will only get more granular over time. The trick is to optimize for both audiences simultaneously, not just one.

Newer startup founders have the advantage here. Established brands have to retrofit decades of copy, guidelines and approval processes to make themselves agent-ready. New brands, on the other hand, can build in copy and a brand voice that’s legible to humans and agents from the very start.

But what does this look like in practice?

To start, brands should work out a distinct voice — and write it down. In most early-stage companies, the brand voice solely exists in the founder’s head. This might work if the copy’s written by a handful of people who work with the founder, but it doesn’t work well when the writer — or reader — is a machine.

AI tools follow explicit rules and drift without them. I’ve spent years at my startup, Jacquard, working out how to get AI agents to write better content, but it’s only now that we’re seeing agents on the other side of the process too — and that makes the rules matter even more.

What a brand sounds like matters, but what it doesn’t sound like matters just as much. Brands should note words never to use and the claims never to make. Often these instincts don’t get written down; they feel obvious to the team who already know them. But if you take the time to spell them out before you have a team — or a machine — producing your copy, you’ll save yourself from running into issues further down the line.

Marketers also need to realize that product descriptions aren’t just administrative: they’re now core brand assets. In an agent-mediated world, they may be the most-read brand copy you own, since agents read them when looking for a product to recommend or purchase. If two personalized AI agents look for furniture for two different people, they may surface the same chair for both, but with the description tweaked for each user. The source description needs to carry the brand intact.

Where possible, brands need to test channels for both readers. Email inboxes are the clearest current example of the “second reader.” Both Gmail and Apple now summarize messages before the recipient opens them and offer summaries above the body of the email — which may be read before the email itself.

So before sending, check what the AI summary says your email is about. Does the offer survive? Does it maintain what makes you unique? It’s not time-intensive, and it’s a small habit any marketing team can adopt today with no tooling.

Brands also need to ensure they’re measuring the outcome of their marketing — not just the volume. Marketers who measure the success of their materials only by the amount produced will find their data meaningless as AI makes content production easier and easier. Focus instead on business outcomes, conversions and revenue. These are the metrics that will show if your marketing team is really doing their job.

And finally, don’t lose sight of the human. No one knows how fast AI purchasing will scale. Humans are still predominantly the ones reading copy and making purchasing decisions. Even AI agents, the second audience, cater to their users’ preferences.

New founders and brands are best positioned to take advantage of the transition to a dual-audience economy. Though it’s hard to say exactly how, the retail landscape will look very different in five or 10 years. Building these decisions into your brand now is easy.

#4601. Startup Spotlight: Ex-Amazon duo finds the right fit for indie artists with apparel marketplace

Chronicling the Seattle and Pacific Northwest startup scene. SEE MORE

by Kurt Schlosser on Oct 22, 2026 at 10:00 amOctober 22, 2026 at 8:04 am

Loomerang made its first sale less than 12 hours after it went live in June. A customer in Houston bought a shirt designed by Joe, an independent artist in Japan.

Four months later, the Seattle-based marketplace for artist-designed T-shirts has 40 artists from 15 countries and recently expanded to India.

Vijay Roy, Matt Koyama, Arka Majumdar, CEO, President, CDO

Founded 2024 · Seattle and Kolkata, India

Loomerang works by handling everything but the art. Artists open storefronts for free, set their own prices, and get 60% of what’s left after manufacturing costs. The company prints, inspects and ships every order, for now out of co-founder Matt Koyama’s garage.

Koyama described Loomerang as an attempt to run a more responsible business in a capitalist system, one built around people who spend their lives working on a craft.

“How can we reward them for that rather than making them feel like they’re being pushed to the margins constantly?” he said.

Loomerang was started in early 2024 by CEO Vijay Roy, a nearly 11-year Amazon veteran. He and Koyama had worked together on Amazon’s print-on-demand book business. Koyama joined as a co-founder a few months later and went full time this spring after leaving Xbox. A third co-founder, Arka Majumdar, leads design.

The company is bootstrapped. Its 14-person team is spread across Seattle, Minnesota and Kolkata, India, and the founders aren’t taking salaries.

Koyama, who also spent time in gaming, sees the same pattern across creative industries.

“The best stuff comes out of the indie space because these are the people that are willing to take risks, to invent, to revolutionize,” he said.

We caught up with Loomerang for this edition of GeekWire’s Startup Spotlight.

In 50 words or less, give us your startup’s elevator pitch?

Talent is everywhere. Opportunity isn’t. Loomerang connects people with the artists and stories behind their clothes: a tattoo artist in Seattle, a painter in Pune, India, an illustrator in Ibaraki, Japan. Artists launch apparel brands for free and keep most of the profit. We print, inspect and ship every shirt.

What problem are you obsessed with solving?

Starting an apparel brand is expensive. You need inventory, a manufacturer, a storefront and a way to reach customers. Most talented artists can’t take that risk. The ones who license their work to big brands usually get a small cut. We want to flip that by removing the upfront cost, handling production with real care, and making sure the artist keeps most of the profit.

What surprised you after talking to customers?

Almost nobody knew who designed their clothes. The exceptions were luxury pieces from designers who were already famous, or a shirt someone bought directly from an artist at a market or art fair. Those people could tell us the artist’s name and why the piece mattered to them. We’re trying to give every Loomerang purchase that same feeling.

How has AI changed the way you build your company?

We’re very intentional when using AI, because it’s a threatening presence for many of the artists we work with. We don’t use it to make art. We use it behind the scenes in our code and operations, and in tools that help artists with practical work like upscaling files. Keeping our overhead low means more of each sale goes to the artist.

What’s one thing people misunderstand about your startup?

People think it’s too good to be true. Artists pay nothing to join and keep 60% of the net profit on every sale. The first question is usually, what’s the catch? There isn’t one. The fashion industry has trained artists to expect a small cut of what their work earns. We think that’s backwards.

What’s the toughest decision you’ve made in the past year?

Holding off on new products. We planned to add hoodies and crewnecks before the holidays, but we decided to prioritize our artist roster and our quality promise. We’d rather ship fewer things we can stand behind than have a wide product range we aren’t fully ramped up on.

What’s the one piece of advice you give to other entrepreneurs?

Build something that creates value for people instead of taking it from them. It’s slower and harder, but those are the kinds of companies we need in today’s world. Technology keeps making it easier to cut people out. The best businesses will find ways to bring more of them in and lift them up.

An independent artist’s first thought for launching a brand is Loomerang, not a big platform that pays them a fraction of what their work is worth. When artists in Seattle, Pune and everywhere else make a real living through us. And when “who made this?” becomes a normal thing to ask about your clothes.
