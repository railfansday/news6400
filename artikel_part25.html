#5199. Tesla (TSLA) releases Q3 2026 financial results: margins rebound, profit still falls

Tesla (TSLA) released its financial results and shareholders’ letter for the third quarter (Q3) 2026 after market close today. We’re updating this post with all the details from the financial results, shareholders’ letter, and the conference call later tonight.

The earnings call with management is scheduled for 5:30 p.m. ET.

Wall Street came into the report expecting a flat quarter. The FactSet consensus, as displayed on Estimize, called for $28.06 billion in revenue, essentially unchanged from the $28.1 billion Tesla posted in Q3 2025 and a bit below the $28.24 billion it reported last quarter.

Profit expectations were lower. Analysts were looking for non-GAAP earnings of $0.45 per share, down 10% from $0.50 a year ago. The Estimize crowd was slightly more bullish at $28.36 billion and $0.48.

We already had the volume numbers. Tesla delivered 486,532 vehicles in Q3, down 2.1% year-over-year but well ahead of the 461,974 consensus, while producing only 464,391. It also deployed 13.7 GWh of energy storage, up from 12.5 GWh a year ago but short of the roughly 15.9 GWh analysts expected. For the full setup, see our Q3 2026 earnings preview.

So the question was never volume. It was whether Tesla could stop the profit slide after Q2, when operating margin fell to 1.4% and EPS missed the consensus by 20 cents.

After the market closed today, Tesla released its financial results for the third quarter and reported $28.05 billion in revenue, flat from a year ago and in line with the $28.06 billion Wall Street expected. Non-GAAP earnings came in at $0.48 per share, beating the $0.45 consensus but down 4% from $0.50 a year ago.

It’s a better quarter than Q2, but the trend hasn’t changed. Margins recovered, with auto gross margin excluding credits back above 18%, yet operating income still fell 56% year-over-year as spending on AI and Optimus grew faster than gross profit. On a GAAP basis, net income dropped 43% to $787 million, or $0.22 per share.

Total gross margin was 19.6%, up 160 basis points from 18.0% a year ago and well above the 16.8% Tesla posted in Q2. Operating income fell 56% to $718 million, for an operating margin of 2.6%, compared with 5.8% a year ago and 1.4% last quarter.

Operating expenses came in at $4.78 billion, up 39% year-over-year. Research and development alone jumped 69% to $2.76 billion as Tesla spends on what it now calls “Super Intelligence” (SI), Optimus, and the Cybercab. Stock-based compensation, which includes the 2025 CEO pay package, hit $1.17 billion, up from $663 million a year ago.

Capital spending was $4.73 billion, more than double the $2.25 billion of a year ago, and free cash flow came in at $1.53 billion, compared with $3.99 billion a year ago and negative $1.09 billion in Q2. Tesla ended the quarter with $45.4 billion in cash and investments. GAAP net income included a $288 million unrealized gain on Tesla’s bitcoin holdings, partly offset by a $142 million unrealized loss on its SpaceX equity investment.

That puts Tesla’s capex at $13.01 billion through the first nine months of 2026, only about half of the “more than $25 billion” management guided for the year. To hit that number, Tesla would need to spend roughly $12 billion in Q4 alone, more than double its biggest quarter so far. The Q3 letter doesn’t update the guidance, and capex actually fell sequentially from $5.79 billion in Q2. So either Q4 spending explodes, or the 2026 plan is quietly slipping into 2027. The lower spending also flatters this quarter’s positive free cash flow.

The number we watch first is automotive gross margin excluding regulatory credits, now that the credit market has mostly dried up. It came in at 18.1% this quarter, up from 16.3% in Q2 and 15.4% a year ago, though still below the 19.2% of Q1, when Tesla got help from warranty and tariff one-time benefits. Tesla credits a better regional and product mix and more FSD subscription revenue, partly offset by lower average selling prices.

Regulatory credits brought in just $113 million, down 73% from $417 million a year ago and below the $146 million in Q2. That line isn’t coming back now that automakers no longer pay federal fuel-economy penalties.

Energy was the weak spot on revenue. Energy generation and storage revenue fell 16% to $2.88 billion, which Tesla partly blames on tariff-related customer refunds, even though deployments rose 10% to 13.7 GWh. The segment’s gross margin jumped to 33% from 20% in Q2, but energy gross profit still slipped to $954 million from $1.07 billion a year ago.

Services and other was the bright spot. Revenue grew 33% to $4.61 billion, and Tesla says gross profit hit a record $734 million on charging, insurance and parts sales.

Tesla says “battery availability continued to be the limiting factor in increasing auto volumes in the quarter,” and that it ended Q3 with “the largest backlog for our passenger vehicles in four years.”

That’s a new explanation for flat sales. Deliveries were still down 2% year-over-year, “other models” (Cybertruck, Semi, Model S and X) fell 48%, and Tesla delivered 22,141 more cars than it built, bringing inventory down to 11 days of supply from 15 in Q2. But average selling prices fell again, which isn’t usually what a supply-constrained automaker does.

On autonomy, Tesla says active FSD subscriptions reached 1.74 million, up 67%, though that count includes upfront purchases, as defined in Musk’s 2025 pay package. Its Robotaxi service reached nearly 2 million cumulative unsupervised miles by the end of the quarter, a month after Cybercab joined the Austin fleet.

#5196. 7-MW solar system completed for Arkansas water district

Madison Energy Infrastructure and Verogy have completed a 7.2-MW solar system for Beaver Water District (BWD) in Northwest Arkansas. The project is expected to generate approximately 12,300 MWh annually, meeting 15 to 20% of BWD’s electricity needs.

“This solar installation strengthens our ability to deliver safe, affordable, and reliable water to the communities we serve,” said Beaver Water District CEO M. Lane Crider, P.E. “It lowers long-term operating costs, supports regional growth, and reinforces our commitment to sustainability. Our customers will benefit from this investment for decades to come.”

Based in Lowell, Arkansas, Beaver Water District serves over 425,000 customers in Northwest Arkansas. The district has earned national recognition for sustainability, including the 2023 Sustainable Water Utility Management Award from the Association of Metropolitan Water Agencies. BWD selected Madison Energy Infrastructure through a competitive RFP process to deliver the solar project, with Madison leading development, contracting, and financing, while partnering with Verogy to oversee design, procurement, permitting, and construction. BWD estimates the solar installation will generate $7 million to $11 million in lifetime savings, helping to manage long-term operating costs as demand for water service continues to rise.

#5195. Tested: 2027 Honda Passport TrailSport Is Legit Off-Road

Honda revamps the almost-new Passport with a suspension lift. Hey, who wants to throw on some 32s?

Gather ’round, everyone, and let me tell you about the new 2027 Honda Passport TrailSport. Wait, you say, didn’t we just drive the 2026 Honda Passport TrailSport, and wasn’t that new too? Yes. Both things are true, because Honda gazed upon its most off-road-capable production SUV and decided it could use a suspension lift. I get it—I thought the same thing when I was in college and bought a 1987 Jeep Cherokee that I immediately lifted three inches so it could fit 31-inch-tall tires. Honda’s 2027 suspension mods don’t yield that much lift (the 2027 model gains 1.4 inches over the 2026 Passport), but I’ll concede that Honda’s system is more thoroughly engineered. Unlike my old Cherokee, the Passport is unlikely to puke a driveshaft on the highway because somebody neglected to consider the relationship between spring height and drivetrain angles.

Honda debuted the TrailSport’s nearly 70 percent new suspension at Overland Experts, an off-road training school on 200 acres adjacent to Uwharrie National Forest in central North Carolina. Uwharrie is famous for its challenging terrain—some of the trails have guardrails to prevent errant 4x4s from tumbling to oblivion—and the OEX campus features some similar terrain. Once at Uwharrie, I was testing a Can-Am ATV and blasted up a terrifying climb to find, at the summit, a crowd of onlookers with phones out, filming. It’s that kind of place, a viral video waiting to happen.

I assumed that Honda’s chosen trails at OEX would be tamer than the worst of Uwharrie on the other side of Route 109, and I was right, but not to the degree I expected. I’ve attended a lot of off-road launches where a manufacturer creates a photo-op off-road course that’s carefully designed to occasionally kick a rear tire in the air without actually challenging vehicle or driver. Hey, you guys know any UB40?

All we had on hand was the stock scissor jack, which worked but was a struggle to get started because it was, in scientific jargon, too flatty-flatty. But the virtue of an easily accessible full-size spare was affirmed.

#5194. The Fog of Tech

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

Why the Future of War Can’t Escape the Past

At a time when debates about national security tend to be polarized along partisan and ideological lines, there is one thing analysts and policymakers across the political spectrum seem to agree on: the future of warfare will be determined by technology. Hypersonic missiles, quantum computing, drone swarms, and artificial intelligence have all been hailed as transformative technologies that will shape battlefields, remake strategies, and upend established military doctrines.

Without question, these technologies are already having a tremendous impact. In Gaza, Iran, Lebanon, Ukraine, and elsewhere, every party with the capacity to employ artificial intelligence and autonomous systems has done so—in the air, on land, and at sea, and at times in massive numbers. And there is also no question that in order to maintain its status as the world’s preeminent fighting force, the U.S. military must ensure that it has the most sophisticated and cutting-edge capabilities available. To continue developing and operationalizing these technologies, defense planners should prioritize scaling and integrating them and developing innovative operational concepts to employ them. The risk of being overtaken by rivals such as China is real.

But an equally significant risk exists: call it “the technology trap,” in which policymakers develop a misplaced sense of certainty about how innovations will change war and what warfare will look like in the future, while not paying enough attention to the unchanging fundamentals of war. An excessive focus on the role of technology would be shortsighted and lead militaries in dangerous directions, especially if planners are highly confident about how new capabilities will develop and make decisions in line with that confidence—which will almost certainly be misplaced.

For war planners, faith in technological answers can obscure the most fundamental questions: Who kills, who dies, and who decides? In the post–Cold War era, this myopia has contributed to dreadful outcomes in Afghanistan, Iraq, Ukraine, and Gaza. In the decade to come, as U.S.-Chinese tensions persist, the fog of tech could help pave the way for a disastrous great-power war. Policymakers should worry less about getting the right technology at the right time and more about how fixating on technology might make them lose sight of the essential elements of war and find comfort in false certainty.

More than 50 years ago, the military historian Michael Howard famously cautioned that future wars are unknowable. “I am tempted indeed to declare dogmatically that whatever doctrine the Armed Forces are working on now, they have got it wrong,” he wrote. “I am also tempted to declare that it does not matter that they have got it wrong. What does matter is their capacity to get it right quickly when the moment arrives.”

But Howard may have been a bit too sanguine about the costs of getting it wrong. For Americans, the paradigmatic example from recent history is how the U.S. military’s technological prowess in the 1990–91 Gulf War seemed to herald a new, high-tech American way of war that would guarantee victory in the post–Cold War era. Instead, Washington slowly and painfully learned that the technological advantages that beat Saddam Hussein’s regime could not defeat insurgencies in Afghanistan and Iraq during the post-9/11 wars.

Predicting the role of technology in future wars has become even harder today thanks to the speed, scale, and complexity of contemporary technological progress. In the past, transformative innovations tended to be expensive at their outset—in resources or personnel—or required specialized knowledge and skills accessible to only a few. Think of the combined power of the rifle, the railroad, and the telegraph in the nineteenth century, or fighter jets and nuclear weapons in the twentieth century. By contrast, many of today’s technologies have low barriers to entry: they are cheap to produce, and access to the knowledge necessary to make them has been democratized and globalized. Enemies today often face each other on more equal battlefields, making it trickier for war planners to understand when and for how long new tools will confer an outsize advantage.

Focusing too much on procuring the shiniest equipment also obscures the ways that high-end technology works in tandem with more basic tools. In the 2010s, defense planners failed to account for this dynamic when predicting future battlefields, which they believed would be dominated by sophisticated platforms. In 2017, a senior Pentagon official declared that the F-35 stealth fighter would be “a war winner.” The 2018 National Defense Strategy, drafted under the direction of Elbridge Colby, who now serves as undersecretary of defense, anticipated that a few primarily high-end capabilities—including advanced computing, artificial intelligence, robotics, hypersonics, and biotechnology—would “ensure we will be able to fight and win the wars of the future.”

Instead, what emerged were conflicts such as the war in Ukraine, which can be described as World War I meets World War III. Sophisticated Russian ballistic missiles wreak havoc on Ukrainian cities, as do precision-guided glide bombs delivered by fighter aircraft out of the range of air defenses. At the same time, however, highly lethal bombs are dropped by drones carried aloft by simple balloons, and one of the most effective antidrone defenses consists of fishermen’s nets (or similar netting) draped over security checkpoints, critical infrastructure, and, in the case of Izium, an entire small Ukrainian city. A sense of certainty that sophisticated tech would dominate battlefields represented a failure of imagination that has produced real-world consequences: a dozen years ago, few would have predicted that during a U.S.-Israeli war on Iran, cheap Iranian drones would assault soft targets in Gulf Arab states, as they have since February.

An obsession with technology can obscure the precept that war remains a human endeavor. As the military theorist Carl von Clausewitz said almost two centuries ago, “War is not the action of a living force upon a lifeless mass . . . but always the collision of two living forces.” Technology can inadvertently make war appear mechanical and sterile. But the nature of war has not changed. Its language is violence, and technology simply offers new and different ways to communicate. Therefore, the fundamental questions of war remain salient regardless of technological change: What are the ends (particularly with respect to politics), the ways, and the means?

Perhaps the most perilous time for leaders to be distracted by the allure of technology is when considering whether to go to war in the first place. They often mistakenly believe that technology will make doing so less costly in political terms, particularly if they assume innovations will help reduce casualties. A fixation on the tangible and quantifiable advantages technology confers can detract from vital debate and discussion of the broader consequences of resorting to violence. This dynamic likely influenced Russia’s decision to invade Ukraine. Putin and his advisers believed that the Russian military was sufficiently capable thanks to a massive technological reform effort that began in 2009. In the run-up to the 2022 full-scale invasion, Russian Defense Minister Sergei Shoigu proudly claimed that more than 70 percent of Russia’s military had advanced weapons. Within weeks of the invasion, it had become clear this had conferred little advantage on Moscow. The same goes for the U.S.-Israeli attempt to topple the Iranian regime in 2026, a decision shaped in part by the belief that the sophistication of the American military, particularly when compared with its opponent, made success almost inevitable. In the wake of U.S. strikes on Iran’s nuclear sites in 2025, Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth declared that the attack showed Tehran that Washington’s military capabilities are “nearly unlimited.” In fact, the limits of those capabilities became glaringly obvious when the Iranian regime withstood the 2026 onslaught and even managed to close the Strait of Hormuz.

There are ways to avoid the technology trap. The first is to determine a clear goal before going to war. Planners must develop a theory of victory before a single shot is fired and then steadily put that theory into practice. These critical processes cannot be negated by or outsourced to technology. Before launching the 2026 war on Iran, the Trump administration failed to clearly articulate the operation’s goal, instead offering a shifting set of justifications and rationales and various descriptions of the desired end state. The hope seems to have been that technological wizardry would make it possible to achieve everything on a long wish list without having to prioritize or navigate tradeoffs.

To clarify goals, planners must identify what Clausewitz termed “the center of gravity” of a military operation: “the hub of all power and movement, on which everything depends.” This is where an opponent’s power originates, so it needs to be the focus of a military’s attention and resources. During the 1990–91 Gulf War, the commander of U.S. forces in the Middle East, General Norman Schwarzkopf, identified the center of gravity as Saddam Hussein’s command and control of his military. Accordingly, U.S. strategy focused on impeding Saddam’s ability to communicate with his troops and weakening the Iraqi military’s ability to execute orders—a state of affairs that helped convince Saddam to accept a cease-fire. Without this clarity about where to direct efforts and how to measure their impact, a military will struggle to achieve its ends. Early in the 2003 Iraq war, the U.S. military’s inability to identify the Iraqi population as the center of gravity—that is, failing to understand that the political and military leadership no longer wielded control—resulted in years of devastating losses by encouraging tactics that fomented anti-American sentiment, fueled an insurgency, and made it more difficult to establish a credible and functioning government.

Another way to avoid the technology trap is to conduct deliberate, impartial, and regular reviews of lessons learned during and between wars. Iran closely studied the attacks that Israel and the United States carried out on its nuclear sites in 2025. Recognizing that another war would likely pose an existential risk to the Islamic Republic regime, Iranian security officials engaged in a serious and intense effort to prepare. By devolving command and control at both the political and the military levels during the 2026 war, the regime has survived a lengthy onslaught. U.S. allies are, of course, equally capable of such adaptation. In recent years, NATO’s Joint Analysis and Lessons Learned Centre has maintained a team of researchers dedicated to studying the war in Ukraine, a smart way to institutionalize this task and a potential model to apply to other theaters.

The combination of a focus on strategic aims, preparation, and learning remains crucial for effective decision-making in war and cannot be replaced by technology. Similarly, the ways and means of warfare are influenced by technology but have not been fundamentally changed by it. Consider, first, the importance of coalitions and the difficulties of managing them. Going to war exposes the distinct and sometimes even opposing interests, objectives, and perspectives of allies. Planners can devise workarounds when they face disagreements in the heat of conflict, but those may be short-term fixes that mask strategic cleavages.

In early 2026, the United States and Israel launched the war on Iran relying solely on each other. Although the U.S. and Israeli militaries have never operated more closely together in the history of their relationship, their limited success calls into question the partnership’s strategic acumen, despite its remarkable technological capabilities. The two countries notably failed to bring in other partners in the region or in Europe. Early on, several U.S. allies and partners denied the American military access to their territory, bases, and airspace. Although U.S. defense planners have always understood that they might not receive such permissions—Turkey, for example, was unwilling to let the U.S. military invade Iraq from Turkish territory in 2003—the resistance this time was of a different quality and scale. Although most countries ultimately acquiesced, the ones that initially declined threw a wrench into U.S. defense planning, since the American way of war is predicated on an unparalleled global basing network. According to several media reports, Israel found ways around this challenge by setting up a military staging area in Iran during the 2025 war and one in Iraq in 2026. But the ad hoc nature of such creative approaches causes problems for military planning.

As the war has dragged on, the Arab Gulf states have not formally joined the U.S.-Israeli coalition, although at various times during the conflict, Bahrain, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates have used military force against Iran. Their willingness to use force was significant in and of itself, but it did not influence the battlefield, despite some of the sophisticated capabilities those countries possess. On the other side, Iran masterfully managed its proxy, the Lebanese militia Hezbollah, pulling it into the conflict and enabling Tehran to project power through relatively unsophisticated technologies such as rockets, mortars, and most notably, cheap drones that Hezbollah repeatedly deployed against Israeli soldiers in Lebanon and Israeli civilians inside Israel. Meanwhile, Russia also helped Iran target U.S. military facilities in the Gulf, resulting in devastating (though not determinative) consequences.

The complexities of coalition maintenance are on display in Europe, as well, where defense planners are wrestling with a security trilemma. First, they must figure out how to incorporate a highly capable and modernized Ukrainian military into continental defense planning. As Germany turbocharges investments in its military, Europe must also contend with a shift in its internal balance of military power—one with uneasy historical echoes. And, incredibly, during the past year, Europeans have had to consider the risk of aggression on the part of the United States, as President Donald Trump mused about securing U.S. control of Greenland. These three realities introduce a host of tradeoffs into European strategy, since prioritizing any one of them would likely make it harder to deal with the other two—and no technology offers a way out.

Technological determinism can also lead to faulty assumptions about casualties—both civilian and military. Today, target selection is increasingly shaped by artificial intelligence, and strikes are increasingly carried out by autonomous systems. Many have argued that these technological advances will improve targeting and better protect civilians. To date, however, it is unclear whether autonomous systems have delivered on this promise. During the war in Gaza, for example, Israeli military personnel have been able to rapidly approve targets generated by an artificial intelligence system called Lavender. But such systems reflect the rules of engagement set by decision-makers, and after Hamas’s October 7 attack on Israel in 2023, the Israeli military raised the level of civilian casualties it would accept when striking Hamas militants. Perhaps for that reason, researchers who conducted an experiment testing the decision-making of around 2,000 Israeli military personnel found that in hypothetical scenarios involving high levels of collateral damage, they demonstrated “algorithmic aversion” and a desire to rely more on human decision-making.

Ironically, civilians may become more vulnerable, not less, as new technologies make it harder to attack military personnel and targets, increasing the risk of collateral damage and even providing some parties with an incentive to deliberately attack civilians and soft targets. Many militaries, including the American one, increasingly employ technology to minimize the risks borne by their own forces. This desire for immaculate war is not new. Nearly 700,000 U.S. service members participated in the Gulf War in 1990–91, and contrary to expectations of a bloodbath, only 143 of them died, an outcome that bolstered arguments that technology had made it possible to carry out major military operations with minimal risk to American troops. Two years later, the Battle of Mogadishu undercut that view, as the U.S. military withdrew from Somalia after 18 service members died in a protracted fight with Somali militants armed with cheap and relatively low-tech weapons such as rifles, machine guns, and rocket-propelled grenades. Early in the present century, the rapid American overthrow of the Taliban in Afghanistan rekindled the dream of relatively bloodless warfare made possible by innovations. But the grinding fights that followed there and in Iraq once again poured cold water on the idea.

Technological hopes spring eternal, however, and the U.S. military once again seems to be planning operations that use high-tech tools to reduce risks to its personnel. In an operation carried out in Venezuela this past January, that meant employing special operations forces complemented by space-based and cyber platforms and more than 150 aircraft and drones—all to capture a single person, Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro. In Iran, it has meant waging a war to transform conditions on the ground, but with attacks carried out primarily from the air and by sea.

To be sure, this approach is linked to the fact that the United States is not engaged in existential wars. And when the U.S. military has fought an adversary that perceives the conflict as existential, as in the Iranian case, American officials are often surprised that their adversaries refuse to back down and are willing to absorb staggering losses. Even if technology allows for fewer American casualties, that does not mean it should short-circuit robust political debate about whether to go to war. Casualties, after all, are hardly the only cost that contemporary warfare can impose. Even wars that produce relatively low casualties can destroy economies, bring down governments, and rip apart societies.

Countries that haven’t waged war in many decades, such as China, may be even more susceptible than the United States to the technology trap. Such countries might focus on acquiring innovative tools rather than preparing for adversaries that know how to integrate new technologies at scale. This may present some opportunities for Washington given its substantial recent experience in formulating concepts of operation tied to technological advancements. But countries such as China might be no less confident about their capabilities, even though they are significantly less experienced, which means the risks of miscalculation and escalation could easily increase.

Deep faith in new technologies could result in much smaller militaries, as machines replace human personnel. In the United States, this shift could widen the gap that already exists between ordinary Americans and those who serve, as even fewer Americans will have connections to—and therefore care about—how, when, where, and why Washington resorts to military force. This detachment of the military from the society it serves is corrosive to the institution itself and to democracy more broadly since it undermines the principle that war-making should reflect the will of the people. Countries that rely on conscription won’t face quite the same problem, but technological determinism will affect their relationships with their publics, as well: even if such countries need fewer citizens to serve, the technology trap could still reduce social cohesion by further marginalizing the sectors of society from which conscripts tend to be drawn.

Perhaps the most pernicious effect of the technology trap might be the way it could hollow out deterrence, as policymakers are tempted to adopt a myopic approach to the use of force shaped by deterministic views of technology’s impact on the battlefield. Deterrence requires an adversary to cogently read signals regarding capability and will. If both parties in a conflict have confidence in their own capabilities and in those of the other side, they may face increasing pressure to outdo each other in demonstrating a greater will to fight. Such a dynamic could result in a dangerous escalation spiral, particularly if it is paired with assumptions that technology will minimize the role and responsibilities of humans.

It is possible to escape the fog of tech. The most important thing for strategists and officials to grasp is that even though the means of warfighting will keep changing, the fundamental nature of war will not. There is not, and will never be, a shortcut—technological or otherwise—to answering the hard questions one must confront before sending troops to fight, kill, and die.

The Kremlin’s New Mental Map Is Changing the War in Ukraine—and the Confrontation With the West

In 2022, when Russia launched its full-scale war in Ukraine, the Kremlin’s strategic thinking was shaped around several core ideas. It viewed the United States as its principal geopolitical rival, an adversary that had encouraged Kyiv to impose strategic defeat on Moscow. It considered Europe and NATO largely as an extension of American power. And it saw nuclear muscle flexing as efficient means to exert coercive pressure.

Today, after nearly five years of war, and 22 months of the second Trump administration, the mental map guiding Russian strategy has changed. First, Russia now sees Europe, instead of the United States, as its principal geopolitical adversary, a shift that has driven Russia’s defense establishment to call for an approach known as “castling” to deal with the main threats. Related to this, Moscow now attributes new strategic agency to Europe itself because of the dramatic military modernization taking place in many European countries and the growing interest among some European leaders in developing or extending their own nuclear deterrent. A third shift concerns Russia’s theory of deterrence: Moscow’s traditional understanding of nuclear coercion is giving way to new thinking about nonnuclear strategic deterrence using advanced conventional weapons systems aimed at Europe.

These new lines of thought, which have gained prominence among influential defense intellectuals and policy experts close to the Kremlin over the past year do not need to be endorsed in formal security doctrine to matter. As the past decade has shown, ideas circulating within Russia’s national security establishment have shaped how officials frame problems, define options, and ultimately make major strategic choices. The three recent shifts in Russian strategic thinking will likely play an important part in how Moscow approaches any negotiation to end the war, as well as how it manages future escalation and competition with Europe and the United States.

As the Trump administration steps up its efforts to engage Moscow, it is now negotiating with a Russian leadership whose strategic mindset has evolved considerably since 2022. For the United States, understanding Russia’s new mental map will not only be crucial to any attempt to find an off-ramp with Moscow. It will also be essential to reducing the risk of inadvertent escalation between Russia and Europe and to ensuring strategic stability on the continent in the years to come.

One of the most notable trends in Russian strategic thought over the past year has been the emergence of the idea of “castling.” A term borrowed from chess, castling in geopolitics describes the repositioning of forces and resources to optimize a state’s strategic posture. Moscow’s defense experts have used the concept to describe a strategy for dealing with a new reality: for the first time since World War II, they have argued, Europe, rather than the United States, has become Russia’s principal geopolitical adversary. According to this view, European countries are now the principal source of Ukraine’s military capacity, political resolve, and strategic resilience; it is Europe and not the United States that has made Ukraine its proxy and is encouraging Kyiv to pursue Moscow’s strategic defeat.

As a consequence, Moscow must direct coercive pressure at Europe—its elites, its decision-making centers, and its critical infrastructure. By doing so, Russia can instill into Europe’s calculus what theorists in Moscow call a “deterring fear”: a European reluctance to antagonize Russia through further support to Ukraine. Such action, they argue, can also rebuild Russian credible deterrence for long-term competition with the West, which is now centered on Europe.

Castling does not imply a change in Russia’s overall objectives, which remain the same as those invoked by Russian President Vladimir Putin at the time of the 2022 invasion. These include banning NATO enlargement to Ukraine and other former Soviet republics; rolling back NATO’s eastern flank; and restricting NATO military exercises, as well as the conventional and nuclear deployments of NATO members, near Russian borders. The difference is that Russian coercion must now be aimed at Europe rather than the United States to achieve these objectives.

Along with this revised assessment of Europe, Russian strategists have interpreted the Trump administration’s own strategic stance as evidence that Asia has replaced Europe as the principal theater of U.S. strategic competition. They point to the administration’s reluctance to back Ukraine, its desire to terminate the war, and its continued overtures to the Kremlin. The administration’s decision in mid-October to provide sanctions relief to Russia and apply new pressure on Ukraine amplify this view in Moscow. Russian strategists also assume Washington seeks to solidify its control of the Western Hemisphere and views China, not Russia, as its primary long-term adversary.

Moscow believes that Washington is thus seeking to reduce its commitments in secondary theaters—particularly Europe and the Middle East—in order to concentrate political, military, and organizational resources on Beijing. As Russia sees it, the new American assertiveness in Venezuela, Cuba, and the Caribbean is unsurprising. By contrast, the current war in Iran—the biggest U.S. military engagement in recent years—has been read in Moscow as an unintended deviation from the new direction of U.S. policy, one that Washington seeks to correct as soon as possible. Whether this reading reflects actual American intentions matters less than that it has become a prevailing understanding in Moscow.

A related trend in Russian discourse is a growing fixation on what defense experts refer to as Europe’s “nuclear curiosity” and new military assertiveness near Russian borders. Specifically, Russian strategists have been concerned by the French and British governments’ modernization of their nuclear programs and apparent readiness to wield them more assertively. Moscow has also noted the prospect of French or British nuclear guarantees extending to European states and nonnuclear European capitals considering the development of nuclear options of their own.

Russian sources identify Germany, Poland, and the Nordic-Baltic states as “nuclear curious,” whether in pursuing nuclear-threshold status or seeking protection under French and British nuclear umbrellas. In this regard, comments by Polish officials in early 2026 expressing support for a Polish nuclear deterrent have been particularly noted. Russian strategists have also closely monitored discussions by Paris and London about offering extended deterrence to the continent as an alternative to the U.S. umbrella.

From Moscow’s perspective, any new attempt by a European state to become a nuclear power would cross a redline and could potentially create a casus belli. Moreover, Europe’s growing assertiveness on nuclear issues is happening in parallel with the deployment of conventional deep-strike precision weapons near Russian borders. For NATO, this more active European posture mitigates the risk of Washington shifting away from its European allies, but to Moscow it signals that Europe could potentially undertake strikes aimed at disarming Russia’s nuclear arsenal or decapitating its leadership.

To Russian analysts, these various developments also suggest Europe’s growing strategic autonomy from the United States. Traditionally, Moscow has viewed Europe largely as a “parking lot” for U.S. forces: a region of the world that is relevant but strategically subordinate to Washington. That assumption is changing. Along with the perception that Europe is now Russia’s principal geopolitical adversary, the continent’s increasing strategic autonomy, including on both nuclear and conventional issues, has become a crucial variable in Russia’s calculus. According to Moscow’s defense intellectuals, Russia may need to change its posture to contain this trend, including by seeking new tools to keep Europe at bay and to coerce it below the nuclear threshold.

To align with Russia’s new mental map, Russian defense intellectuals and military officers have been examining how to make Moscow’s coercive threats more effective. During the first years of the war, Russia’s nuclear muscle flexing and hybrid warfare failed to prevent Ukraine’s allies from funneling financial support, military aid, and targeting intelligence to Kyiv. This approach also failed to deter Ukraine’s land incursions into Russia and strikes on symbolic Russian targets and Russian strategic infrastructure, let alone compel Kyiv to surrender.

Today, NATO is preoccupied with Russia’s escalating cross-domain coercion, or what U.S. and European analysts call hybrid warfare. But that is only one part of the Russian approach. As Moscow concentrates on Europe as the new primary threat, it is also drawing on new tools of coercion that Russian experts call nonnuclear strategic deterrence. For Russian military authors, NNSD involves using advanced conventional capabilities, including precision-guided weapons, drones, ballistic, cruise, and hypersonic missile systems, as well as “weapons based on new physical principles”—a Russian catchall term for electromagnetic-pulse and high-power microwave weapons to deter and compel adversaries.

Conceptually, NNSD belongs to what Russian experts refer to as the “art of strategic gestures,” a euphemism for using, or threatening to use, nuclear and nonnuclear capabilities to deter and coerce an adversary. The concept is not new. Since the early years of this century, Russian strategists have tried to develop conventional alternatives to nuclear deterrence. But until the full-scale war in Ukraine began, in 2022, these ideas remained underdeveloped. NNSD has emerged from the lessons learned during the conflict.

From the outset of the war, Russia used tactical ballistics, cruise missiles, and hypersonic capabilities, which it has paired with drones and maneuver warfare. But even when coupled with the threat of nuclear coercion, this approach underdelivered, in the view of many Russian analysts. As a result, as the war has become entrenched, in particular since 2024, Moscow has shifted its focus to more advanced ballistic salvo waves, including strikes using Russia’s intermediate-range Oreshnik ballistic missile.

Drawing on this experience, Russian military theoreticians center NNSD on using massive, deep, precision strikes against the adversary’s critical military, political, and economic infrastructure. The strategy, they suggest, should be aimed not only at Ukraine but also at its European supporters. Although the exact contours of NNSD are still emerging, the concept appears to be more escalatory than the hybrid warfare and deniable asymmetric campaigns that Russia is currently pursuing in Europe.

This is more than a peripheral theoretical discussion. There are already indications that NNSD has gained traction among Russia’s senior military officers: for example, since 2025, the flagship journal of Russia’s general staff, Military Thought, has published several articles on the subject. All the branches of the Russian military are also developing their own versions of NNSD and are seeking to claim a comparative advantage in its execution. This interservice competition may increasingly normalize what constitutes acceptable coercion below the nuclear threshold and shape targeting priorities, as well as escalation management.

As the Kremlin sees it, the war in Ukraine began as an attempt to impel Europe to reshape its security architecture. Yet the strategic landscape confronting Moscow today may look even more threatening than the one it sought to change in 2022. Washington needs to recognize that the Kremlin regards Ukraine not as a discrete conflict but as part of a broader struggle over Europe’s strategic order. Any U.S.-led effort to broker an off-ramp in Ukraine will need to reckon with Moscow’s emerging castling logic and its mounting anxieties about Europe. A settlement that addresses the immediate battlefield while leaving Moscow’s wider frictions with Europe untouched is likely to provide only a temporary pause in this larger conflict. Washington needs to understand that Russia’s nonnuclear coercion has become a part of its ongoing deterrence strategy against Europe rather than simply a feature of the war itself.

Even if Moscow engages in negotiations to end the war, it is likely to continue using military coercion against Ukraine, as well as Europe. It will do so for several reasons: to align the terms of the settlement with Russian interests, to refine Russia’s emerging theory of nonnuclear strategic deterrence, and to force Europe to adopt a different security architecture. Russia may therefore undertake precision strikes on critical infrastructure, and even possible decapitation attempts, as well as calibrated attacks of varying intensity in Ukraine, alongside diplomatic engagement. By using such tactics, Moscow will be seeking not only to gain leverage in negotiations with Kyiv but also to restore its strategic deterrence with Europe. In response, Europe, doubting the credibility of the Trump administration’s commitments and seeking to demonstrate its own resolve, may decide to escalate.

Washington should thus be prepared for the possibility that stepped-up efforts to terminate the war may coincide with heightened security risks in Europe. Given that the dynamic between Europe and Russia is now characterized by pervasive mutual suspicion, conspiracy theories, and expectations of war, there is a growing danger of inadvertent escalation. Actions by either side that are intended as limited coercion, signaling, or leverage might be misread, thus triggering a self-reinforcing action-reaction cycle in which each side’s effort to restore deterrence instead erodes it. Paradoxically, this dynamic could emerge precisely at the moment when all sides—including the Kremlin—are seeking an off-ramp. Both Europe and Russia risk crossing a “culmination point of coercion,” beyond which any additional threats to use force, or its actual use, could expand the war further.

For Russia and Europe, then, the main challenge will be to bolster deterrence without crossing this tipping point. For Washington, the primary task is not only to mediate the substance of a deal but also to learn how to choreograph negotiations amid ongoing military action. #5174. A Less-Is-More Strategy for America

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

BARRY R. POSEN is Ford International Professor of Political Science at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

The Folly of Expanding the Exhausted U.S. Military

On October 8, the USS Abraham Lincoln and its accompanying strike group returned to the United States after 320 days at sea. The deployment—a near record for the modern American military—had become controversial. As the group’s ships went month after month without a port call, conditions deteriorated precipitously. Sailors reported supply shortages, plumbing issues, and water contamination. Morale dropped markedly; eight sailors even tried to commit suicide. By the time the ships finally left the Middle East, their struggles had made headlines across the country.

The travails of the Lincoln are hardly the only story about the U.S. military to generate alarm since the war in Iran began last February. Military analysts have expressed concern about other carrier groups’ lengthy deployments. They have fretted about the Trump administration’s decision to pull soldiers and weapons stationed in the Indo-Pacific out of the region and position them in the Middle East. Perhaps most of all, they have worried as the United States drains its stockpile of sophisticated munitions.

These problems all share the same root cause: the U.S. military is not built for the near-constant use it has experienced for decades. Ever since the Cold War, American defense planners have structured the U.S. armed forces to be ready to fight two major conflicts simultaneously. In practice, that means Washington has constructed a force that is designed to win short, sharp wars. For that reason, the military does not have enough units, weapons, or personnel to handle never-ending conflicts and simultaneously use its forces around the world to coerce enemies and reassure partners.

American officials are now trying to modestly increase the size of the military. More important, they are hoping that huge infusions of money will increase the capability of the existing armed forces. But making these changes will be exceptionally challenging—and they are unnecessary. The United States is a highly secure country with many capable and wealthy partners. It does not need a defense apparatus capable of fighting everywhere all at once. Instead, it would be much better off focusing its attention on a smaller set of issues and scaling down accordingly.

The U.S. nonnuclear forces, which consume more than 80 percent of the defense budget, are structured and sized for particular contingencies—currently, a clash with China, Russia, or both nearly simultaneously. The military’s weapons are therefore designed for maximum lethality and survivability in what military planners expect to be an unbelievably dangerous environment. To that end, the Pentagon has emphasized purchasing precise, long-range, and stealthy weapons. These are supposed to kick open the doors of enemy defenses and allow other expensive advanced platforms, such as F-35 fighter jets or B-2 or B-21 bombers, to deliver large quantities of slightly less sophisticated but still quite lethal precision weapons.

Washington has never expected these wars to last long. Instead, it imagines that under the fierce pressure such conflicts would generate, the adversary will cry uncle or the war will escalate to nuclear threats or—worse yet—nuclear use. During the Cold War, for example, the United States and its allies planned for an extremely intense, roughly 30-day conflict against the Soviet Union. Today’s military planners have probably made similar assumptions about a war with Beijing or Moscow. Highly capable conventional forces and the risk of nuclear escalation, American thinking goes, should deter an attack. If deterrence fails, U.S. planners hope to stop an enemy in its tracks and create escalation risks so great that the adversary will realize its errors and try to reverse course.

But such thinking means that Washington does not have enough resources to sustain long, great-power conflicts. At $100 million each, the F-35 fighter aircraft, a mainstay of the air force, navy, and Marine Corps, is simply not designed for mass production. Neither are the long-range stand-off missiles that would be the preferred munitions in the early days of such a war. The U.S. military has developed sophisticated theater ballistic missile defenses, but their main purpose is to defend U.S. forces and essential military infrastructure—not towns, cities, and civilian industries. They can be used for this purpose under certain conditions, but doing so quickly exhausts the supply of missiles. In a war with China or Russia, civilians in local, allied countries will need to look after themselves.

These problems have become readily apparent in Washington’s war against Iran. The United States likely thought it could rapidly overpower the Islamic Republic—at best a middle power, albeit one with great ambitions. After all, under the right circumstances, the U.S. military handily overmatches these kinds of enemies, as it did Iraq in the 1990–91 Gulf War. But Washington’s enemies have smartened up since then. They no longer simply produce smaller and less capable versions of the great-power militaries that American forces are designed to deter and combat. Instead, they have prepared themselves to take a beating and keep on fighting, even if at modest levels of effectiveness.

Overly ambitious war plans and faulty policies are not the only reason the U.S. military is struggling. Politics is also creating difficulties. Because the American people hesitate to support wars abroad, U.S. politicians will do anything to avoid casualties. That means they take all the high-end weaponry that the military stockpiles to break down great-power defenses and instead spend it profligately on regional conflicts. Similarly, they deploy scarce air defense weapons to protect allies’ towns and cities, lest those allies consider finding new partners or decide to settle a war by compromising with their enemies.

U.S. policymakers also generally overuse the military. Politicians like to accompany diplomatic messaging with action, in part because they do not believe in diplomacy anymore, which pushes them to parade or use the armed forces far more often than is warranted. When asked to act, commanders usually salute and say yes. Others simply cannot sit still. Commanders, after all, took on their roles because they wanted to command something.

The result is a worn-down military. The defense press is full of stories about ships and aircraft that are not up to standard because they have been out too long, resulting in deferred maintenance. This, in turn, creates repair problems for other units. When a ship must stay in dry dock longer than usual to receive adequate repairs, other ships must wait longer for servicing—which means their needs also grow. Ultimately, the navy has fewer fully ready combat ships available in the event of a great-power challenge. The army has similar problems, with combat and support vehicles arriving at maintenance depots in unexpectedly poor condition. In the air force, the F-35’s “full mission capable rate,” which was never great, has deteriorated from 38 percent to 25 percent since 2021. And the overall readiness of air force aircraft has declined in recent years, in part simply because of overwork.

All the while, U.S. soldiers, sailors, pilots, and marines are constantly on guard, coercing and deterring U.S. enemies and reassuring allies. This, too, creates high levels of activity that can negatively affect retention. Although the military has generally been successful at retaining senior enlisted personnel in recent years, it may soon run into difficulty. Since 2024, navy warships have repeatedly gone to sea undercrewed. The ultimate result of this overuse is that the sharp edge of the military is dulled, and the steel blade is weakened.

U.S. defense officials are aware that they have a problem. Currently, they are trying to solve it by building a somewhat bigger and far more technologically capable military. Congress, for example, appears poised to increase the Pentagon’s so-called base budget—which encompasses what the Congressional Budget Office defines as the “enduring costs of manning, training, and equipping the military”—by over 25 percent, or to $1.1 trillion for the 2027 fiscal year. The Pentagon hopes to secure an additional $350 billion over the base budget in a subsequent bill. Defense officials also want to add over 40,000 new service members. But this is, at best, a personnel increase of three percent, which is much too small to fill the additional units needed to solve extant readiness problems. And the fancier weapons Congress hopes defense officials will buy cannot overcome the strain of constant deployments and repeated wars. The defense industrial base is barely keeping up with current demands, and recruiting large numbers of new service members would likely require offering much higher wages, which would then need to be extended to everyone else. Personnel costs would start consuming an ever-greater share of the defense budget. U.S. officials could instead opt for conscription, but that would produce a backlash.

There is, however, an alternative: scaling down. Instead of pretending that it must be able to fight two great-power wars at once, Washington should cut its policy coat to fit the available cloth. It should continue to build forces for major contingencies, such as deterring adventurism by China. But policymakers should agree to slow down other projects. Congress should task the Pentagon with reviewing the armed forces and come up with an activity budget, which would be based on an assessment of how much real military activity—flying hours, ship days at sea, training days in the field—can be undertaken without harming the readiness of the total force. It would then allocate that activity appropriately. Based on what they ought to know about the virtual attrition the military has suffered thanks to 30 years of hyperactivity, commanders should determine what level of exercises, forward deployments, and regional wars the military can actually support without doing damage to the capabilities it wishes to have ready every day to deter a great-power war and, if need be, wage one effectively. The U.S. Navy already knows the activity level that its fleet can support, which it has detailed in its Optimized Fleet Response Plan. But American presidents and defense secretaries have simply ignored this plan whenever they wanted to deploy more forces.

The activity budget should not be classified but instead should be public information, listed along with the dollar budget that the Pentagon publishes annually, voluminous summaries of which can be found at the Defense Department’s comptroller’s website. When politicians want to exceed the activity levels on which they have previously agreed, they should have to explain why and how they plan to repair the force after intense use. Commanders should think of this as an ethical issue. Every now and then, one should resign when overuse causes serious problems.

To help lighten its load, Washington will need to hand off some responsibilities to others. This should be feasible despite the need to manage Moscow’s aggressive impulses, which are the presumed cause of one of the contingencies—a war with Russia—that drives U.S. defense planning. The principal European states have more than enough economic and technological resources to deal with Russia on their own, if they so choose. The transatlantic bargain should thus be more forthright about the limited liability that the United States is prepared to assume for the continent’s defense.

The National Defense Strategy of the Trump administration is honest about this fact, but U.S. military activity levels and deployments in Europe suggest that the United States has not truly reduced its role in Europe’s defense. The supreme allied commander of NATO is still an American, when the position should be filled by a European. Tens of thousands of U.S. troops are still based all over Europe; many should come home. Pentagon websites and those of American commands in Europe feature laundry lists of exercises and maneuvers across the continent that involve not only forward-based U.S. forces but also rotating deployments of units based in the United States. These numbers, too, should diminish.

American officials may want to be able to solve most of their global security challenges by simply buying more forces. But a much better solution is for the United States to change its grand strategy and act with greater restraint. Washington simply does not have the will or the resources to provide for both Asia’s and Europe’s defense while fighting protracted conflicts elsewhere, most recently in the Middle East. Nor, for that matter, does it need to. The United States is inherently a very secure country. It is rich, endowed with bountiful natural resources, insulated by vast oceans, and blessed with relatively weak and pliant neighbors. Only huge changes in the political constellation of power in Asia, Europe, or the greater Middle East could seriously affect U.S. security.

Washington should buy sufficient military power and stock adequate reserves of raw materials to ensure against such changes. But in general, it can afford to be less proactive. A change in grand strategy might even allow the United States to cut the defense budget. If concerns about the ballooning U.S. deficits are correct, then the Pentagon will need to help pay the bills to set things right.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is likely to be in the hot seat after the midterm elections in November, if Democrats gain control of the House and possibly even the Senate, as many polls suggest.

That legislative majority would give Democrats the power to convene oversight hearings and subpoena witnesses to testify about Hegseth’s prosecution of the war in Iran and handling of deadly strikes on alleged drug smugglers in the Caribbean.

Some lawmakers even hope to impeach Hegseth for high crimes and misdemeanors. They accuse the onetime Fox News host of violating two major laws: the War Powers Resolution, which limits how long an unauthorized military conflict can last, and the War Crimes Act, which makes it a federal crime to violate certain international humanitarian laws.

Republican Rep. Thomas Massie filed an impeachment resolution against Hegseth in September and is expected to force a vote on the matter when the House returns from its long midterms recess the week after the elections.

In the case of the Iran war, the resolution accuses Hegseth of illegally continuing military strikes against Iran beyond the 60-day clock allowed under the law. He is further accused in the resolution of ordering the extrajudicial killing of over 220 alleged drug traffickers in the Caribbean Sea and Eastern Pacific, which even if the narcotics-smuggling charges were true is not grounds for summary execution under U.S. law. The secretary has also been criticized for overseeing secondary strikes on multiple survivors of the boat attacks who were still clinging to the wreckage of their destroyed vessels when they were killed.

Additional matters that Democrats want to investigate include Hegseth’s role in the deadly Feb. 28 missile strike on the Minab elementary school in Iran, which killed over 150 people, including 120 children; his sharing in early 2025 of classified military operational details in a Signal group chat; and his general noncompliance and stonewalling of congressional demands for military information that Congress is entitled access to.

Democratic Rep. Mike Levin is supportive of the drive to impeach Hegseth. The four-term lawmaker and environmental lawyer, who represents a California district with a strong military footprint, spoke with Foreign Policy about the legal and political considerations.

This interview has been edited for clarity and length.

Foreign Policy: If Democrats win the House and potentially even the Senate in the November midterms, you’ve called for Democrats to pursue the impeachment of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Why impeach Hegseth?

Mike Levin: Hegseth took us into this war without asking and kept it going with no vote or no consideration of the votes to end it. I think he’s consistently betrayed his oath.

It’s not just about the war in Iran, although there are some very specific things that I have deep concern with, such as the Minab elementary school in the early stages of the war. But it’s also about the boat strikes [in South America]. It’s about Signalgate. It’s about obstruction as well.

We do know from the two Trump impeachments that the appetite of the Senate to convict is virtually nonexistent. But it’s important that we understand what the founders intended for the impeachment power to be used for. In this instance, you have an out-of-control secretary who is both incredibly arrogant and incompetent, and those are very dangerous things to combine.

FP: What is your response to the argument that Democrats are overreacting to what amounts to strong disagreements over policy and that if Democrats pursue impeachment against Hegseth, how are they any different from House Republicans who in 2024 impeached then-Homeland Security Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas?

ML: With Mayorkas, they were claiming that he was violating federal law, but those claims were dicey at best, as opposed to here, where I think it’s fairly clear whether it’s the War Powers Act, whether it’s the War Crimes Act. It’s tough to prove corrupt intent with obstruction, but I think he has committed obstruction as a constitutional matter. He ginned up this cease-fire pause argument, but there is no provision in the War Powers Act that says that. They just made it up. Ultimately, if that were litigated, even the Supreme Court would acknowledge that there’s no part of the War Powers Act that includes the ability to pause for cease-fire in an ongoing conflict where you have major combat operations in the same theater both before and after the cease-fire. It directly contradicts the law.

On the boat strikes, you have the [U.S. Defense Department] Law of War manual. There are those in Congress who have studied this and know this far more than I, including our veterans [such as] Jason Crow and Chris DeLuzio. What they have said is: If people are shipwrecked and they want out of the fight, you’re not allowed to go kill everybody. I think that’s fairly cut and dried. So, I think it’s really apples and oranges from Mayorkas, where you had federal immigration law.

FP: Should the other matters—including the elementary school attack, Signalgate, and the obstruction of information—Congress is lawfully entitled to be pursued via committee hearings and the subpoena process?

ML: For purposes of an impeachment resolution today, my own opinion is that to the extent high crimes and misdemeanors have been committed sufficient to justify an impeachment vote, it’s on the basis of those two laws that I think fairly clearly have been broken. These are not just any two laws. These are two laws bedrock to our values as a nation, to the Constitution, to our norms.

There are far more Republicans, particularly more traditional Reagan-Bush Republicans, in the Congress who know this. My hope is that more of them will be willing to break from what is so clearly unprecedented, unconstitutional, and illegal.

FP: Democrats impeached President Trump twice—in 2019 over Ukraine, and then again in 2021 over the Jan. 6 insurrection. Considering that the president was able to persuade a plurality of American voters [in the 2024 presidential election] to send him back to the White House, are there political lessons that Democrats should draw from how they pursue an impeachment of Hegseth?

ML: There are things that we learned from the two Trump impeachments. One is you have to build the record. The Jan. 6 committee took its time; they put Republican witnesses under oath. I think they moved public sentiment and did so effectively. The first impeachment followed an inquiry, but there were fights over lots of the witnesses and things like that and perhaps didn’t move public opinion as much.

The key is a very simple story is very important as it regards public sentiment.

People understand killing survivors on the water. They understand a war Congress voted to end. People can understand that pretty simply. You have to have crossover votes as well, you have to have bipartisan support, or you’re not going to get very far. Ultimately, Republicans have to be courageous enough to help carry the message, otherwise it just reads to independents as being another partisan fight.

Impeachment is the tool the founders gave us to hold the president and other executive officials to account. There are still some reasonable Democrats that disagree on whether it was all worth it to impeach Donald Trump. I personally think that, as a matter of principle, putting the record down for history, had I to do it over again, I think that’s more important than the alternative.

#5167. Brazil’s Economic Impasse

MONICA DE BOLLE is Senior Fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics, an Adjunct Professor at Georgetown University, and a co-author of The New Economic Nationalism.

Neither Lula nor Bolsonaro Can Solve the Country’s Problems

On October 5, the day after the first round of Brazil’s general elections, the country’s financial markets were euphoric. Brazil is struggling with high debt and low growth, and investors are eager for leaders to reduce budget deficits, deregulate, cut taxes, and perhaps even privatize state assets. After nearly four years under the left-wing President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, known as Lula, who has been slow to implement such measures, investors hope a right-wing government will create opportunities for bold reform.

Rule by the right might be exactly what Brazil gets. The far-right Liberal Party made substantial gains in the legislature, becoming the single largest party in both the Senate and the Chamber of Deputies. Its presidential candidate, Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of the notorious former President Jair Bolsonaro, led the aging incumbent, Lula, by nearly two percentage points in the first round of voting. Lula has shown himself capable of impressive political comebacks, but Flávio Bolsonaro is still the favorite in the runoff vote scheduled for October 25.

Even if Bolsonaro wins, however, the prospects for the Brazilian economy are dim. The policies he has put forward are nothing close to the substantial fiscal consolidation program that will be necessary to bring down debt and stimulate private investment, and his determination to weaken Brazil’s political institutions creates the kind of uncertainty that scares investors away. Economic growth has been sluggish under Lula, whose administration has struggled to relieve the high debt burdens afflicting the public sector and households. What would truly doom the country to economic crisis, however, is the kind of authoritarian-inspired politics that a Bolsonaro government could usher in.

Brazil is in serious economic trouble. The country’s sovereign debt ratio has ballooned to 83 percent of GDP, fiscal deficits leave no room for additional government spending, and high interest rates are hurting consumers and investors. Meanwhile, the Iran war has raised the price of fuel and other goods, weighing on household balance sheets already strained by high debt and leaving many Brazilians desperate for leaders who can alleviate their daily hardships.

This predicament is years in the making. Before Jair Bolsonaro was elected in 2018, his predecessor, President Michel Temer, enacted a cap on public spending to contain Brazil’s runaway fiscal problems. Temer introduced the cap as a constitutional amendment to prevent future governments from changing the spending ceiling to suit their needs. Yet Jair Bolsonaro raised the cap several times anyway, which required convincing Congress to amend the constitution. In return, the executive branch effectively ceded control of the budgetary process to the legislature.

Lula therefore inherited a system in which he had little room for fiscal adjustment, and he made few attempts to fix the problem during his term. The result was high budget deficits and persistent inflation, which forced the central bank to raise interest rates substantially. In turn, high interest rates damaged household balance sheets and lowered overall investment and growth.

If Lula were reelected, he would find it incredibly difficult to introduce new fiscal reforms and other measures to support growth. He promised to reduce spending and has proposed a comprehensive overhaul of Brazil’s tax system, which would help increase the budget surplus and reduce public debt. Yet Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party and its allies now hold a majority of seats in the Senate and the Chamber of Deputies, allowing them to block any legislation Lula might pursue.

Yet it is overly optimistic, if not completely unrealistic, to expect that Bolsonaro would fare better. Neither candidate has provided much detail during the campaign about what he would do to fix the economy. Bolsonaro has instead spoken often about “institutional reforms,” which he has said would include amending Brazil’s 38-year-old constitution to make existing labor and environmental regulations more flexible, among other changes; meddling with the Supreme Court; and granting amnesty to his father, who is serving a prison sentence for leading a 2023 coup attempt. Those steps are likely to drive away investors because stable institutions are necessary for an economy to function well. A government that undermines the judicial system and the constitution will compromise the integrity of its economic policies, no matter what those policies are or how good the reputations of the officials making them may be.

Gathering support for such an overhaul of the country’s political institutions, moreover, would require compromises that could worsen Brazil’s economic troubles. To secure enough votes in Congress to advance Bolsonaro’s priorities—for example, two-thirds of the Senate would need to approve the removal of a Supreme Court justice, and three-fifths of both houses must approve a constitutional amendment—the government would have to bring legislators outside its coalition on board. Those politicians would almost certainly demand substantial funds for their constituencies in exchange for their support, adding to the budget deficit.

Bolsonaro’s far-right movement will also be focused on holding together its recently formed coalition of governors, mayors, and voters. Much of its constituency is lower-middle-class Brazilians who rose out of poverty during the boom years two decades ago, while Lula was serving his first two terms in office. After seeing their income growth stagnate in the years since, these voters have turned to the Liberal Party, but their loyalty is new. Most are self-employed and face economic precarity. Both Bolsonaro and Lula are now trying to appeal to these voters by promising initiatives that boost income and offer household debt relief, but delivering such programs—if it is even possible—would only exacerbate Brazil’s economic woes.

What Bolsonaro has shared about his economic policies does not inspire much confidence, either. His party has said it would reduce government spending by $40 billion, or 1.5 percent of GDP, and deliver primary budget surpluses—a measure of the budget balance that does not include interest payments. But it has not explained how it would accomplish this, nor has Bolsonaro elaborated on his vague plans for controlling the rise in public debt. Ultimately, it is unlikely that Bolsonaro can deliver on any of these promises, especially if he keeps his pledge not to cut pensions and key social programs.

Part of the problem is that the Liberal Party’s economic program lacks a viable plan for reducing costs. It proposes eliminating at least ten government ministries but has no estimate of the resulting cost savings. When Jair Bolsonaro took similar steps after he assumed office in 2019, removing cabinet positions and laying off public servants, those moves had little effect on fiscal outcomes. Other promised measures include reducing Brazil’s value-added tax, phasing out a tax on financial transactions, lowering taxes on electricity and fuel, spending $180 billion on infrastructure over four years, and building 2.5 million new homes. None of them would fix Brazil’s fiscal problems; they would only make those problems worse.

The same goes for the rest of Flávio Bolsonaro’s tax plan. His economic advisers have said they intend to reverse every tax created or raised under Lula’s finance minister, Fernando Haddad. Haddad’s tax increases were intended to shift the tax burden to high-income brackets, raise government revenue, and reduce inequalities in the tax system. These taxes have contributed to a modest increase in revenue but fell short of delivering budgetary surpluses. Bolsonaro has argued that the measures are impeding investment because they tax higher income brackets more heavily, but there is no evidence to support this idea. Instead, repealing all these tax increases would worsen Brazil’s fiscal situation. A simple calculation shows that Brazil needs to increase its primary budget balance by about six percent of GDP, either through increased revenue or decreased spending (or both), to keep its debt from rising further. That adjustment would have to be spread over at least three years, giving indebted households and firms time to change their consumption and investment patterns, to avoid a recession. Slashing taxes, however, would reduce government revenues, raising the debt.

Brazil’s high interest rates are another constraint. The central bank’s current rate is 13.75 percent, astronomical by Latin American standards, dictating interest rates everywhere in the economy. Public debt service payments alone consume some 8.7 percent of GDP. Bolsonaro would not be able to lower interest rates significantly without sparking a recession, so he would face the same problem that has plagued Lula during his latest term—few good options for containing a rapidly growing debt.

Neither Lula nor Bolsonaro is well positioned to solve Brazil’s economic problems. A decisive turn toward illiberalism under Bolsonaro, however, would all but assure economic disaster.

Flávio Bolsonaro has never disavowed his father’s championing of Brazil’s dictatorship, which lasted from 1964 to 1985, and his Liberal Party traces its lineage to the party that sustained the military regime. Moreover, he has pledged to release his father and his father’s associates who are in prison for staging an insurrection and a coup attempt in 2023. Flávio’s brother Eduardo Bolsonaro, a former member of Brazil’s Congress who would likely play a significant role in his brother’s government, has openly expressed his admiration for Viktor Orban’s illiberal regime in Hungary, claiming a desire to learn the “best practices” of a movement that rewrote Hungary’s constitution, reshaped its courts, silenced its press, and deployed other overtly authoritarian tactics. And the substantial far-right bloc in Brazil’s Congress supports policies to undermine institutions, including by impeaching members of the Supreme Court—a step these parties advocated even before corruption allegations emerged against several of the court’s justices. (Flávio Bolsonaro is also under investigation for his involvement in the same corruption scandal.)

Although Brazil is not at risk of returning to military rule, the former authoritarian regime serves as a warning of what could happen if a Bolsonaro administration follows its illiberal inclinations to their logical conclusion. During those two decades of military dictatorship, Brazil spent at unsustainable levels, mostly funding state enterprises, to preserve the regime’s hold on power. The country defaulted on its public debt, endured corrosive hyperinflation, and saw income inequality skyrocket. Six economic stabilization plans failed and growth stagnated. Brazil is not alone in this experience. No Latin American country that swung from democracy to authoritarian rule in the past century has benefited economically from the shift. Countries elsewhere in the world that prospered after such a transition, including some in East Asia, became less authoritarian during their economic ascent.

Brazil’s rightward shift fits a regional pattern. The common thread that unites Brazil with Argentina, Chile, and Colombia is the crude libertarian worldview that each of their far-right movements espouse. All of them talk about citizens becoming entrepreneurs free of the state. They tell voters who have unstable employment and little economic security that their policies will bring prosperity. But their populism rarely wins the confidence of investors. Like its peers, when the Brazilian far right says it can overcome the country’s substantial problems with institutional demolition and a threadbare economic agenda, what it really offers is empty promises.

#5165. Tesla pushes unsupervised FSD to Q2 2027, and only inside Robotaxi zones

Tesla now says unsupervised “Full Self-Driving” in customer-owned cars will arrive in Q2 2027. It’s the latest date on a promise Elon Musk has been making to FSD buyers for the better part of a decade.

But there’s a catch. On Tesla’s Q3 2026 earnings call today, AI chief Ashok Elluswamy described it as owners adding their own vehicles to Tesla’s managed Robotaxi fleet, not owners getting a car that drives them anywhere they want to go.

“When can we anticipate FSD unsupervised in customer cars?” was one of the top retail shareholder questions submitted for the call, backed by about 1.1 million shares, as we noted in our Tesla Q3 2026 earnings preview.

Elluswamy’s answer was Q2 2027. And the way he framed it, step one isn’t a software update that lets you climb into the back of your Model Y and sleep while it drives you to your destination – what Elon Musk sold to customers since 2016. It’s Tesla letting owners put their cars into its own Robotaxi network, where Tesla runs the operation.

So an owner’s car would only drive itself unsupervised where Tesla’s Robotaxi already operates.

Tesla’s Robotaxi service runs in six US metros right now, and most of the service areas cover small slices of those cities. When Tesla mapped its Miami Robotaxi zone, it covered mostly West Miami and a strip toward Doral and Sweetwater. Orlando and Tampa launched in July with similarly small geofences.

Inside those zones, the service sticks to low-speed routes, largely stays off highways, and avoids the harder parts of town. In the Bay Area, it isn’t unsupervised at all. California rules still require a safety driver in every car because Tesla doesn’t want to release data to the California DMV, which it has to do beyond a level 2 autonomous system.

The fleet is small too. Tesla says Robotaxi reached nearly 2 million cumulative unsupervised miles by the end of Q3, a month after Cybercab joined the Austin fleet, according to Tesla’s Q3 2026 results. Third-party tracking this spring showed the number of fully driverless Teslas on the road in the dozens, nowhere near the hundreds of thousands Musk had talked about. Waymo, meanwhile, was already in both Orlando and Tampa before Tesla showed up, and it has over 3 million miles.

That’s not what Tesla sold. For years, the pitch to FSD buyers was a car that would eventually drive itself anywhere through a software update.

Musk told investors in July 2025 that unsupervised FSD would be available “for unsupervised personal use by the end of this year in certain geographies.” A year earlier, he said he’d be “surprised” if Tesla didn’t have unsupervised self-driving the following year. And HW3 owners who bought FSD have already been told their computers can’t run unsupervised FSD at all, with no clear upgrade plan.

Now the target is Q2 2027, and the product is narrower: your car joins Tesla’s fleet, in Tesla’s zones, on Tesla’s terms.

I'll take a Tesla FSD promise seriously when they start making progress in the California DMV process towards being approved for autonomous driving. Right now, they along with 23 other companies have the DMV permit to do testing with a driver. 5 companies, but not including Tesla, have the permit to do driverless testing. 3 companies have the permit for driverless deployment. Waymo for all speeds and conditions. Mercedes for daytime highways with speed under 40 mph (ie can handle being autonomous in a traffic jam). Nuro with speeds under 35 mph.

It took Waymo 10 months to progress from driverless testing to driverless deployment. Thus, it would be extremely optimistic to hope that Tesla will have the California DMV permit for driverless deployment by Q2 2027. I note that providing a California taxi service is important enough for Tesla for them to be operating that service with human drivers.

If you can believe it. Believing a Tesla FSD timeline is a fool’s errand at this point, and I don’t see any reason to treat Q2 2027 differently from every other date Tesla has given since 2019.

But even if it happens on time, look at what’s actually being promised. This moves the goalpost from “your car drives you anywhere while you sleep” to “your car can work as a Robotaxi inside a small geofence that Tesla controls.” No highway road trips. No driving you home from the airport across town. No unsupervised commute unless you happen to live and work inside one of the zones.

It’s the goalpost moving of all goalpost moving.If one thing was clear from the call, Tesla has no idea when it will actually happen. They are just guessing. Ashok again reiterated that safety is the bottleneck, which is why Tesla limits itself to low-speed roads and harder environments. He guesses that those safety issues will be fixed by next year, but he doesn’t know.

Health care costs are rising fast in the U.S., and even workers with solid health care benefits through work are feeling it, according to an annual survey of nearly 2,000 employers about their health benefits. The report was published Thursday by KFF, a nonpartisan health research organization.
