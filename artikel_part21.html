#5390. Just in: Delta confirms Medallion status requirements for 2027

Delta Air Lines will not raise its elite status requirements in 2027, TPG has learned.

For the third straight year, the carrier plans to leave its Medallion qualification thresholds unchanged, a spokesperson for the airline confirmed Thursday.

That's certainly welcome news to Delta flyers.

Last week, two of America's four largest airlines announced significant shakeups to their paths to status.

But reaching Delta status in 2027 will look a lot like it did in 2026: SkyMiles members will need to hit the same number of "Medallion Qualifying Dollars" to earn or maintain their status.

Read more: Last-minute strategies to earn Delta elite status

As a reminder, SkyMiles members earn 1 MQD for every $1 spent on Delta-marketed flights or on Delta Vacation packages. For flights marketed and operated by one of the carrier's eligible partners, MQDs are calculated as a percentage of the distance you fly and the fare class you purchase.

Plus, members with a certain Delta-American Express credit cards can earn MQDs via card spending.

Some cards also offer an annual 2,500 MQD head start.

As a reminder, all members' MQDs will reset on Jan. 1, 2027, and any MQDs exceeding a given threshold will not roll over. Your activity in the next calendar year will earn Medallion status for the rest of 2027 and all of 2028, and will ultimately expire on Jan. 31, 2029.

Delta hasn't significantly altered its Medallion thresholds since late 2023, when it implemented a major overhaul to the SkyMiles program — changes that, at the time, sparked major backlash among members. Delta ultimately lowered the thresholds by roughly 15% across the board.

At the time, Delta executives said they hoped those changes would prevent the airline from having to repeatedly raise and tinker with status requirements, year after year.

Now, SkyMiles members have seen three years pass without a major status hike — and the required MQDs still haven't reached those from 2023's original announcement.

It's been an eventful month for major airline loyalty shakeups.

Last week, United Airlines eliminated "flight segments" as a status metric and raised the spending required to reach top-tier Premier 1K status.

Southwest Airlines changed some of its status thresholds as part of a larger Rapid Rewards shakeup, one that also had implications for its popular Companion Pass.

American Airlines remains the largest U.S. carrier not to detail its status requirements for the new year. But American's new "status year" doesn't begin until March, so we likely won't learn about any AAdvantage program changes for a couple more months.

Delta's decision to keep status requirements intact comes as top rival United has pushed to woo SkyMiles members to switch loyalties. United recently extended a pointed status match offer to Medallion elites, pointing to its installation of Starlink Wi-Fi on planes.

#5384. Volkswagen tells ID.4 owners to limit charging to 80% as part of a recall, or it will do it for them

Volkswagen told certain ID.4 drivers to limit charging to 80% as part of a recall by October 21. Unless drivers opt out, VW will automatically “apply a temporary vehicle setting” remotely.

Earlier this month, Volkswagen issued a recall impacting 22,524 ID.4 electric SUVs model years 2023 to 2026.

In a letter sent to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) on October 6, Volkswagen urged owners to only charge their vehicles to a maximum of 80% battery capacity until a fix is ready.

Although the root cause is still unknown, Volkswagen said that “certain charging profiles combined with a state of charge over 80% may trigger thermal propagation,” or a fire within the battery.

In an email sent to owners with vehicles included in the recall, Volkswagen said that unless they opt out by October 21, it “intends to apply a temporary vehicle setting that limits charging to 80%.” The update will be done remotely and reflected in the mobile app on or about October 27, 2026.

You can accept or decline the change in the myVW app by selecting Accept Setting or Decline Setting by October 21. It will be shown as a 93EX High-Voltage Battery Settings notification.

Several ID.4 owners are already receiving the update. The recall follows a field study as part of an ongoing review of Volkswagen vehicles not included in previous battery recalls.

What exactly is strategic stability supposed to stabilize?

When U.S. President Donald Trump visited Beijing in May, he and Chinese President Xi Jinping agreed that the U.S. and China should build a “constructive relationship of strategic stability.” At their September meeting in Washington, they reaffirmed that framework and specified that it should rest on respect, fairness, and reciprocity. The leaders were not declaring an end to competition but acknowledging something more realistic: Competition needs a political framework to prevent it from drifting toward confrontation.

What exactly is strategic stability supposed to stabilize?

A more useful definition starts with the boundaries of competition: how it is conducted, what ends it serves, and how far it is allowed to spread. The two sides can compete intensely without their chief objective being to undermine each other. They can take security measures without turning every area of economic and technological interaction into a national-security battlefield. And they can disagree sharply over regional issues while keeping channels of communication, and even cooperation, open elsewhere.

A focus on stability is not the opposite of competition but is instead the factor that keeps competition from becoming a self-reinforcing escalation.

This is why the Washington summit mattered. The Beijing meeting in May established a political framework for the relationship, and the September meeting and its aftermath offer a chance to give that framework greater continuity.

The significance of agreeing on a shared goal of stability goes beyond any single agreement. For much of the past decade, China-U.S. diplomacy has too often followed a reactive pattern: Tensions rise, leaders intervene, relations improve, and another dispute interrupts the progress.

Xi and Trump’s December 2018 meeting in Buenos Aires produced a trade truce, but tariff escalation resumed in May 2019. Xi and then-U.S. President Joe Biden’s November 2022 meeting in Bali opened the way for renewed diplomatic engagement, only for the balloon incident in February 2023 to derail then-Secretary of State Antony Blinken’s planned visit to Beijing. These episodes show not that leader-level meetings are ineffective, but that momentum from one-off summits is fragile unless it is sustained by regular leader-level engagement.

Therefore, a more durable pattern would look different. Regular leader-level engagement could help turn crisis management into something closer to strategic management, giving officials on both sides political space to address problems before they become emergencies.

That would represent a meaningful change. The United States and China are unlikely to resolve their deepest differences anytime soon, but they can become better at living with them. A stable equilibrium does not require accommodation across the board but enough predictability that disagreement in one area does not constantly threaten the entire relationship.

The larger value of regular summits lies in meeting the expectations of the international community that the two nations could establish a more regular political rhythm—one in which competition continues, diplomacy remains active, and neither side needs a crisis before it sees a reason to engage.

If both sides strengthen themselves while also building clearer expectations, more reliable communication, and better crisis-management channels, stability can lower the risk that competition ends in conflict. Conversely, if competition accelerates while mechanisms for managing it remain weak, the present calm may prove little more than an intermission.

The practical task, then, is to build shock absorbers into the relationship. Every major-power relationship encounters accidents and unexpected disputes. The danger in China-U.S. relations is that a problem in one area can still contaminate the whole relationship. For example, an export-control dispute can spill into security dialogue, a military encounter can freeze economic communication, and a cyber or AI-related security incident can quickly be interpreted through the lens of strategic hostility.

A more resilient relationship would make such spillovers less automatic. Strategic stability should provide a floor under competition, not a ceiling on cooperation.

That requires moving beyond summit-generated stability. The next phase should focus on turning presidential consensus into habits.

Economic mechanisms such as the Board of Trade and the Board of Investment should keep functioning even when negotiations become difficult. Military channels should remain open when political relations worsen, because that is when they are most immediately needed. On AI, the two countries do not need a grand bargain before they can discuss shared terminology, major safety incidents, cyber-risks, and the role of human decision-making in high-consequence systems. Recent U.S.-China economic talks have already begun to include AI risks alongside more traditional trade issues, suggesting that such conversations are becoming more feasible.

Academic, business, local, and student exchanges also deserve protection. At the Washington summit, Xi invited 100,000 young Americans to visit China for study and exchanges over the next five years. That initiative addresses a longer-term vulnerability in the relationship: the loss of firsthand knowledge about each other. Turning the invitation into sustained contact will require accessible programs, reliable travel arrangements, and institutional support in both countries. Without such contact, future policymakers will have fewer opportunities to test their assumptions against experience.

Future summits may tackle individual or immediate problems, but the most meaningful outcome would be a China-U.S. relationship that can absorb the next shock without having to be rebuilt from scratch.

#5365. How Much Do F-35 Pilots Earn Compared To F-22 Pilots In 2026?

All United States Air Force fighter jet pilots are required to be commissioned officers. This is also true of the US Navy, the Marine Corps, and many US allies (such as the Royal Air Force). The main exception is the Army, where helicopter pilots may be warrant officers. Fighter pilots operate with a high level of responsibility and decision-making and often have to make independent, split-second decisions in combat that can have major strategic or political consequences.

In rare instances, a pilot's decision (such as in a tense border standoff or violation) could spark a war. Pilots also bear leadership roles and need to be educated (including a bachelor's degree) to understand their complex aircraft and much more. This is true for all F-35 and F-22 pilots.

In the United States, as well as other professional Western air forces, pilots are not paid based on the aircraft they fly. Instead, they are paid based on their rank and their years of service. This is at least somewhat similar to the commercial sector, with important differences. At major airlines (such as Delta and United), pilots are primarily paid based on their seniority and rank (Captain vs First Officer), not on the aircraft that they fly.

However, in practice, widebody aircraft captains flying thick routes tend to get paid the most. This is not because of flying the widebody aircraft per se, but rather ​​​​​​​because flying widebodies is a position typically reserved for the most senior captains. There is no equivalence with fighter jet pilots in the Air Force. Fighter jet Lieutenants, Captains, Majors, and Colonels all fly the same aircraft that they are certified to fly.

There is no Air Force counterpart to career progression between aircraft types. For example, it's common for commercial pilots to fly regional aircraft, then progress to narrowbodies, then to widebodies. Air Force pilots do not start with A-10s, then move to F-16s, then F-35s, and then F-22s.

As such, an F-35 fighter pilot with the same rank and years of service as an F-22 pilot should earn the same, all else being equal (such as deployments). This said, the Air Force is currently rapidly developing the F-47 to complement/replace the F-22 starting in the early 2030s. There may be opportunities for some Raptor pilots to start transition training on the upcoming F-47 in the years ahead.

As stated, pilots are assigned to a particular aircraft type, and they generally stick with it throughout their military careers, although crossing to other platforms is possible and does happen. However, what differs is that while base pay may remain the same, the quality of life, deployment tempo, and the aircraft prestige differ with the internal cultures flying specific fighter jets.

Some aircraft may tend to be combat deployed more than others, and so pilots of some aircraft types may net more deployment and combat pay than others. Test pilots, demo teams, and some high-visibility roles may have some minor additional pay incentives. Again, ​​​​​​​this is not strictly speaking tied to whether a pilot is flying an F-22 or F-35, but whether one aircraft tends to be used more for roles that attract higher incentives or allowances.

Perhaps the most notable distortion in the baseline that pilots are paid based on rank and years of service is the retention bonuses. The main program here is the US Air Force Aviation Bonus, which offers up to $50,000 per year in lump-sum payments for those who extend their contracts.

This is mostly for Lieutenant Colonels and below. The Air Force prioritizes fighter and bomber pilots, but doesn't distinguish between fighter jet types (such as F-22, F-35A, and F-15EX pilots). They are all treated as 11F career field pilots.

The typical compensation for entry-level fighter pilots (O-1 Second Lieutenant to O-2 First Lieutenant) is around $50,000 to $65,000 in annual base pay. This rises to $80,000 to $100,000 for mid-career O-3 Captains and then to $110,000 to over $150,000 for O-5 Majors and O-6 Colonels.

This is just base pay, and pilots will typically receive additional flight pay and allowances. Flight pay and allowances boost Lieutenants' compensation to around $75,000 to $95,000, Captains to $100,000 to $140,000, and Majors and above to $150,000 to over $200,000.

Retention bonuses alone can add $25,000 up to $50,000 in extra compensation, with the average retention bonus being around $33,781 per year for fighter jet pilots. There are other benefits that don't neatly show up in total compensation figures, like pensions, insurance, and sometimes grants for university studies.

None of these hinges on whether the pilot is flying an F35 or an F-22. Overall, US fighter jet pilots are among the best paid in the world, although they still typically lag behind their commercial pilot counterparts. One of the major issues the USAF faces is pilots not renewing their contracts and instead opting to fly commercial, where senior widebody pilots at Delta, United, and American can earn up to $450,000 annually.

The Air Force initially conceived of purchasing 750 F-22 Raptors. However, the cost of the program and de-prioritization due to the collapse of the principal adversary (the USSR) saw the procurement eventually slashed to just 187 serial production examples. Of these, around 32 are Block 20 training variants, and only around 145 Raptors are combat-coded.

Whereas the F-22 was built as a highly specialized air dominance fighter, the F-35 was designed as a true multirole fighter with an emphasis on stealth penetration and striking high-value targets (like enemy radars). This makes the F-35 overall more flexible and operationally useful.

The US Air Force has a program of record to eventually purchase 1,763 F-35As, the Marines' program of record is for 420 F-35B/Cs, and the US Navy's is for 273 F-35Cs. By mid-2026, the USAF had taken delivery of around 550 F-35As and had around 183 F-22s remaining in inventory.

The USAF has not disclosed how many qualified pilots it has for each fighter jet type. However, extrapolating from the number of fighter jets in service and assuming it has 1.25 to 1.5 pilots per aircraft, it should have a total of 220-280 qualified Raptor pilots. The number of F-35 pilots is now likely to be somewhere in the ballpark of 650 to 850.

While the pay for Raptor and Lightning II pilots may be the same, that doesn't mean the cost of those pilots to the Air Force is the same. One of the best reports estimating the costs of training US fighter jet pilots is from a 2019 Rand report using 2018 numbers.

Unfortunately, this is a little dated and reflects the F-35 early into its entry into active service, but still, it is useful. According to the report, the cost of training an A-10 pilot was $6.0 million, an F-15E pilot $5.6 million, an F-16pilot $5.6 million, and an F-22 Raptor pilot $10.9 million.

The report provided two numbers for F-35As, with the F-35A pilot transition at $9.5 million and the F-35A basic at $10.2 million. If these numbers are adjusted for generic US-wide inflation, then the cost of training an F-22 pilot is $14.1 million in 2026, with the costs of F-35A being $12.2 million (transition) or $13.1 million (basic).

Military expenditures tend to inflate faster than overall national inflation. Using a defense inflation adjustment, the cost of training a Raptor pilot would be $16.9 million in 2026 and $14.7 to $15.7 million for F-35A pilots. This said, it is unclear if real costs have gone up or down as the F-35 program has matured. These numbers are better understood as a reasonable best guess based on reported 2018 costs.

With all else being equal, F-22 and F-35 pilots earn the same, with even certain bonuses and incentives not distinguishing between the types of fighter jets flown. Notably, both of these aircraft only exist in single-seat variants. China and Russia have recently developed two-seat variants of their respective J-20 and Su-57 fighter jets, but this is not something the US has done.

The dominant rank flying the Raptor and Lightning II fighter jets on combat missions is that of Captain. Captains form the bulk of operational pilots. These earn $80,000 to $100,000 in base pay (depending on the years of service) and $100,000 to $140,000 after incentives and allowances are accounted for. With that being said, higher ranks can sometimes fly combat missions, mostly majors but also sometimes Colonels.

“You have to understand that many people are sharing that bathroom,” she said. “And research suggests that there are significant germs on sinks, flush handles and toilet seats on airplane bathrooms. While a comfortable indoor humidity level is anywhere from 30% to 65%, the humidity inside an airplane cabin is only around 10% to 20%, according to Northwestern Medicine. This can dry out the mucous membranes of your nose and airways, making them much more susceptible to viruses and bacteria. To combat this, stay hydrated.

“Bring your own bottle of water and aim to drink eight ounces of water per hour you’re in the air to maintain your hydration,” said Dr. Nate Favini, the chief medical officer at Pair Team, a virtual care company. “Alcohol and caffeine will dehydrate you even more, so save the cocktails and coffee for your destination.”

In addition to washing your hands, be cognizant of those areas on a plane that are particularly high-touch surfaces. The flu virus, for example, can live on any hard surface for up to 24 hours.

“Don’t use the tray table and, if you really have to, make sure you carry sanitary wipes with you and clean it before you use it,” said Gafanovich. “Also, don’t store items in the seatback pocket. We know many people use it, so it’s likely to be full of germs.”

Testa also recommends avoiding or wiping down overhead air vents, lavatory flush buttons and seat belt buckles. So, be sure to stock up on hand sanitizing wipes before your next flight.

Stick with classics like Purell, or try these wipes designed specifically for travelers. And if you just want to spray your hands (or everything), try Dr. Bronner’s lavender-scented organic hand sanitizer spray in TSA-friendly bottles.

No matter how clean your hands are, it’s best to keep them away from your face, too.

“Even if your hands have touched the dirtiest, germiest surfaces, you won’t get sick from that unless you bring those germs to a point of entry (nose, mouth, eyes),” said Florida-based internist Dr. Frank Contacessa. “Get in the habit of never rubbing your eyes, touching your mouth or your nose unless you just washed your hands. This is one of the greatest ways to avoid getting sick.”

Just can’t kick the habit of touching your face? Try gloves.

Dr. Julie Fischer, an associate research professor in the department of microbiology and immunology at Georgetown University, told TPG, “Gloves are useful in that they remind people not to touch their own noses and mouth ... when you’re wearing gloves, you become hyper-conscious of that. But the best protection for individuals is to be very careful about handwashing.”

Studies have shown that the aisle seats on an airplane experience significantly more traffic and run a greater risk of exposure. Why? In part, sick patients tend to brace themselves on those seats when walking to the bathroom.

“The window seat is a better selection if you want to minimize the risk of respiratory transmission,” said Testa. These seats generally don’t have as much contact with passengers.

While you might think that sitting in recycled air for hours could increase your risk of illness, keeping your air vent on may actually help.

“There’s been some research that showed that using the overhead air vent, directed straight downward, can create a cone of protection,” said Contacessa. “It can actually prevent airborne germs from getting close to you. The airflow from the vent can help to ward off another passenger’s sneeze germs.”

In fact, high-efficiency particulate air, or HEPA, filters on planes remove at least 99.97% of airborne viruses and bacteria. That air is also refreshed about 20 times per hour, whereas air in a typical office only gets refreshed about 10 times.

Before you even set foot on a plane, you can start preventive measures to avoid getting sick. The CDC recommends staying updated on vaccinations, including annual flu shots, COVID-19 boosters and RSV vaccines for those eligible, to minimize the risk of severe illness.

In addition, "Prioritize sleep, a balanced diet, and supplements like vitamin D and zinc," said Bhatia.

It's also worth packing vitamin C. “Studies have shown that people under stress who take 1,000 milligrams of vitamin C per day are less likely to fall ill with viral infections,” said Favini. “And travel is often stressful.”

On Oct. 1, police in Laos raided a private jet that was headed for the Philippines with more than half a ton of crystal meth aboard.

The smuggling was a polyphonically international affair.

The jet was Malaysia-based, owned by a company run by two Malaysian and two Singaporeans. The man who allegedly chartered it is thought to be a politician in south Philippine city of Mindanao. Those arrested in the bust included a Singaporean, a Filipino, a Malaysian, and 10 Lao airport staff.

While scam centers now dominate headlines about crime in Southeast Asia, the region’s traditional illicit business—drugs—may well still be bigger.

For comparison, in 2023, criminal syndicates operating in the Mekong defrauded victims of some $43.8 billion worldwide, according to a conservative estimate by the U.S. Institute of Peace. Meanwhile, within Southeast Asia alone, sales of meth were worth between $48.1 billion and $74.8 billion annually, according to a report published this year by the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC).

The UNODC found that compared with 2019, when it published its previous report on the topic, drug markets have grown larger—as have the criminal syndicates, which operate in ever more sophisticated ways, protected by state weakness and systemic corruption. For drugs, as with many licit commodities, Southeast Asia is now a major hub for production, transit, and consumption.

Drug manufacturing is now concentrated in just one corner of the Golden Triangle: Shan state, in northern Myanmar. Control of the province is split between the Myanmar military and a number of armed rebel groups. The production and sale of drugs has long been a major source of revenue for many of these groups, including the state military.

Meth—also known as yaba or shabu, depending on its form—is the key product. The drug is often popular with those working long hours in physically demanding jobs because of its energizing qualities.

Meth sales outside the region may also be substantial. Research by Jason Eligh at the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime (GI-TOC) suggests that Myanmar meth has now gone global. He describes a sophisticated system where manufacturers have worked hard to ramp up production in industrial-scale labs and diversified their raw materials to use precursor chemicals not subject to legal controls.

Production of other synthetic drugs in Shan state is also growing, including ketamine, MDMA (aka ecstasy), etomidate (an anesthetic that is smoked recreationally), and “happy water” (cocktails of various synthetic drugs).

The drugs are then smuggled out not just into Thailand and Laos, the two other corners of the Golden Triangle, but also west into India and out of the port of Yangon—and from these places to the wider world.

However, northern routes directly into China seem to have been squeezed in recent years.

Researchers, including Eligh, accuse Beijing of tacitly tolerating foreign criminal syndicate activity so long as its own citizens are not affected. Many of the precursor chemicals for synthetic drugs seem to come from China.

China also exercises substantial influence over Shan state and key groups implicated in its booming meth industry, such as the United Wa State Army, National Democratic Alliance Army, Shan State Progress Party/Shan State Army-North, and the Myanmar military. However, groups such as the Arakan Army, the Shan State Army-South, and unaffiliated criminal syndicates all also play a role.

The drugs trade of course also entangles with other criminal enterprises. Kristina Amerhauser and Ioannis Vlassis of GI-TOC report that groups involved in the meth trade are also often connected to the scam centers. And smuggling routes, corrupt officials, and money laundering outfits can facilitate multiple different illicit activities.

A diplomatic march on Tokyo. The United States held further meetings with the Myanmar junta in Tokyo in early October, Reuters reported last Friday.

Few details were confirmed other than that talks were led on the U.S. side by Assistant Secretary of State for Intelligence and Research Michael Vance, who also led September talks in Myanmar.

Japan’s facilitating of talks reflects its ambiguous position on Myanmar and its own moves to normalize relations. Japan did condemn the 2021 coup and has yet to officially recognize the military government. However, it maintained a diplomatic presence and never joined sanctions. Japanese companies also maintained a notable presence there.

Japan is now likely moving toward full recognition of the junta. Yohei Sasakawa, Japan’s special envoy for national reconciliation in Myanmar, announced on his blog last week that he was meeting junta President Min Aung Hlaing on Monday. While I have yet to see this confirmed, it would follow a prior visit to Myanmar in August.

This also comes alongside the first public meeting, on Oct. 1, between Japan’s interim charge d’affaires in Myanmar and junta Foreign Minister Tin Maung Swe.

Analysts Hirokazu Omatsu and Kyi Sin have suggested that Japan’s past experience as a mediator to intractable conflicts in Southeast Asia, including Cambodia in the 1990s and Mindanao in the Philippines; its deep ties to the region; and its trusted status are all assets here.

However, they also point out that from the junta’s point of view, its experience with Japanese mediation is less positive. The informal cease-fire that Sasakawa helped mediate between the Myanmar junta and the Arakan Army in 2022 was used by the latter to regroup ahead of its successful renewed assault in 2023.

Crash. On Friday, Indonesia’s stock market dropped 11.57 percent. This was its worst stock market crash since 1998, when the Asian financial crisis shrunk GDP by more than 13 percent and forced the resignation of the dictator Suharto.

Things aren’t quite so dramatic this time around. The economy looks wobbly, and money has been flooding out, but the situation is much less serious.

What triggered this huge drop was not a total collapse of economic confidence but a bruising announcement by the index provider MSCI that it was now considering downgrading Indonesia to a “standalone market.”

MSCI’s ratings are used by major global investors to decide how to allocate their investments. This January, it announced that it was considering downgrading Indonesia from an “emerging” to a “frontier” market. This was due to rampant fraud on the Indonesian stock exchange. Money started leaving sharpish. Indonesia responded by announcing reforms.

Many investors expected that these would avert a downgrade. Therefore the announcement last week that MSCI was in fact considering an even sharper downgrade for Indonesia, to its lowest level, prompted panic.

Explaining its decision, MSCI said: “Despite the announced reforms, significant concerns remain around market dysfunction, including continued suspected coordinated trading behavior and lack of evidence of meaningful enforcement.”

Indonesia has promised more reforms. And President Prabowo Subianto is, according to his brother, “very angry” about this development. In January, the head of the stock exchange and the top financial regulators resigned. Who gets the ax this time?

Trade deal and EU visit. On Monday, To Lam started a six-day visit to Europe. He is accompanied on this trip by numerous top officials, including the defense minister, minister of public security, and foreign minister.

The visit starts in Denmark and will then move to Belgium, where he will meet with European Council President António Costa and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen.

The European Union and Vietnam have been steadily deepening ties. In 2020, they inked a trade agreement, and the diplomatic relationship was upgraded to a “comprehensive strategic partnership” this January.

What’s next? Interestingly, in a recent interview with the Vietnamese press, EU Ambassador to Vietnam Julien Guerrier noted Vietnam’s role as a security partner. He suggested that there was potential for further cooperation on maritime security, protection of undersea cables, and counterterrorism.

The visit also follows the signing of a trade agreement between Vietnam and the European Free Trade Association (EFTA) on Sunday. Made up of Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, and Switzerland, EFTA lets those countries participate in the EU’s single market without joining the bloc. In 2025, bilateral trade between EFTA and Vietnam stood at about $5.4 billion.

In Focus: International Anger Over Indonesian Haze

Malaysia and Singapore are reacting with increasing anger to the thick transboundary haze from Indonesian forest fires, which briefly made Singapore and Kuala Lumpur the world’s two most polluted cities.

In Singapore, Senior Minister K. Shanmugam vowed to use “every legal and regulatory lever” to go after companies linked to the haze.

At least 10 companies have been notified under the Transboundary Haze Pollution Act, which lets Singapore fine companies that are deemed to contribute toward haze in the city-state.

How effective this will be is unclear. The scale and complexity of the haze crisis means that legal action against individual companies might have limited effect.

The law also looks pretty toothless. Fines are capped at 2 million Singaporean dollars (about $1.5 million). The law was first used during the 2015 haze crisis, but of the six companies then notified, two cases were closed, and four remain open. And three companies notified this year were also notified in 2015.

In Malaysia, Foreign Minister Mohamad Hasan has said he will raise the issue at the next summit of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), scheduled for November. He said ASEAN already had mechanisms to jointly address natural disasters but haze was not included in this. However, the government is not planning to take legal action.

ASEAN does have a treaty that addresses transboundary haze, but it hinges on national governments explicitly requesting assistance. As last year’s floods in Sumatra demonstrated, Indonesia under Prabowo is often reluctant to ask for foreign help.

Indonesia’s reaction to all this has been mixed. Ministers have confirmed that the cabinet is now discussing accepting foreign assistance. But the national police chief has pointedly claimed the involvement of foreign parties in the fires.

Meanwhile, the head of Indonesia’s government communications agency also proclaimed that Indonesia sends oxygen, not just smoke, to neighboring countries.
