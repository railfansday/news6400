#5248. Permitting bill writers seek industry aid ahead of Senate vote

Top aides to the “big four” senators behind the bipartisan energy permitting overhaul package had a message for industry supporters Thursday ahead of a key procedural vote scheduled for Nov. 9: Help us help you.

Speaking at a conference hosted by the American Council on Renewable Energy, the staffers said the bill was a carefully crafted compromise and worthy of support, amid heated lobbying efforts by stakeholders for and against the measure ahead of the midterm elections.

“For those of you in the room — individual developers — you know your projects,” said Kara Allen, deputy staff director for Senate Environment and Public Works ranking member Sheldon Whitehouse. “You know where they have stumbling blocks, your voice is very helpful.”

At least 60 votes will be needed to achieve cloture on the motion to proceed, which would allow the legislative vehicle for the permitting bill to come to the floor for debate and amendments.

Whitehouse, D-R.I., worked closely since the start of the 119th Congress with Senate Environment and Public Works Chairman Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va., and Senate Energy and Natural Resources Chairman Mike Lee, R-Utah, and ranking member Martin Heinrich, D-N.M. They finally introduced the bill last month, but there wasn’t enough time to try to get it across the Senate floor as lawmakers were anxious to leave Washington for the campaign trail.

The 417-page measure would make significant change to a host of bedrock federal environmental laws, including changes to how projects are permitted under the National Environmental Policy Act, Endangered Species Act and Clean Water Act.

Aides to the four committee leaders said that feedback from groups representing a wide range of industries helped inform the bill, and said that highlighting how its provisions could make it easier to secure permits and build infrastructure projects could help get the 60 votes needed to advance.

Many of these groups have expressed their support for the bill in recent days. The American Petroleum Institute led a letter urging the bill’s passage in the lame-duck session, telling lawmakers it would “restore confidence in the nation’s ability to build critical infrastructure. Congress should seize this opportunity and act.”

ACORE itself has also signed on to a support letter with other renewable energy industry groups, including the American Clean Power Association and the Solar Energy Industries Association.

The bill faces some headwinds, however, particularly from environmental groups that oppose changes to the laws and limits on judicial review. Native American tribes have raised concerns about changes to the National Historic Preservation Act, and electric utilities have concerns with portions that could make it easier to transmit power from renewable energy sources.

There is also continuing concern among Democratic members about whether the Trump administration would faithfully enforce the bill if signed into law given actions it has taken against renewable energy projects, including recent filings seeking to reopen permits for four offshore wind projects off the coast of the Northeast U.S.

The Trump administration has made some concessions on renewable energy that Whitehouse said was a starting point for negotiations last month, although has not publicly announced a shift in policy. House Natural Resources ranking member Jared Huffman, D-Calif., has said these assurances are not enough.

“A serious permitting deal must start with an end to the war on clean energy, and this cheap window dressing doesn’t even come close to cutting it,” Huffman posted on X.

Anticipating Democratic wins in both chambers in the midterm elections, Huffman previously encouraged his Democratic colleagues to revisit permitting legislation once the 120th Congress has been sworn in.

And Senate Republicans may have some work to do on their House GOP counterparts, if the bill can successfully get to that chamber for consideration.

Jake McCurdy, energy policy director for Lee, said he’s encountered some partisan pushback that runs counter to the approach Senate leaders have taken.

“I was speaking to some House Republican [legislative assistants] last week, and they asked me to go through each section to know which one’s Republican and which one’s Democrat. And I don’t think we really see it that way,” McCurdy said.

The four senators have said they expect to introduce a manager’s amendment that would incorporate feedback they receive ahead of the vote.

The bill is the culmination of a yearslong process, McCurdy said, adding that the legislation has also incorporated concerns that have either emerged or grown in recent months. Those issues include the buildout of data centers, growing electricity demand and consumer concerns about electricity affordability, all of which have been major issues on the midterm campaign trail.

The measure includes language pushed by Heinrich, for instance, that would require state regulators to adopt federal standards ensuring that artificial intelligence “hyperscalers” that need massive amounts of power for data centers take on those costs themselves, rather than sticking consumers with the bill.

“I’ve said to folks throughout there’s two ways to do a bipartisan bill. One is to stay super close to the center and not really change very much, but avoid controversy and keep everybody on board that way,” said Adam Tomlinson, staff director for Capito. “The other way is to really shoot for some big policy changes on both sides that benefit both parties and projects of all types, and certainly this is the second type of bipartisan approach.”

The aides also expressed their hope that the language of the bill around project certainty, including limits on actions administrations can take to revoke already-issued permits, would spur investment.

“Capital will come off the sidelines because you know your permits are going to be valid no matter what the political lens might happen to be at a given time, and we certainly need that,” Tomlinson said.

The staffers also confirmed that the bill, dubbed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, has been pronounced like the Spanish word “baja.” Supporters of the bill have already celebrated its introduction with Mountain Dew Baja Blast, as well as a margarita-based version.

The highlights this week: U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio takes a three-day trip to India that skips New Delhi; the full text of the Mecca pact between Pakistan, Turkey, and Saudi Arabia is revealed; and an investigation sounds the alarm on Bangladesh’s measles crisis.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s South Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio takes a three-day trip to India that skips New Delhi; the full text of the Mecca pact between Pakistan, Turkey, and Saudi Arabia is revealed; and an investigation sounds the alarm on Bangladesh’s measles crisis.

U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio arrived in Mumbai on Tuesday for a three-day trip to India with an unusual itinerary, visiting Mumbai and Jamnagar but not New Delhi. Rubio visited India’s capital in May, meeting Prime Minister Narendra Modi. Though it’s unclear why he isn’t going to New Delhi this time, he could actually do a favor for sputtering U.S.-India ties.

New Delhi is where India’s trust gap with the United States is the largest. Indian officials are deeply displeased about some of President Donald Trump’s actions—from the administration’s tariff regime to its deep embrace of Pakistan. New Delhi perceives that Washington is taking a transactional approach toward their strategic partnerships.

Had Rubio gone to New Delhi, there would have been uncomfortable conversations. Instead, he is visiting the Indian financial capital and then the city that is home to the world’s largest oil refinery.

Much of the focus of Rubio’s trip will be on business and investment. The Indian private sector, much like the government, worries about recent U.S. policies—especially those related to tariffs and visas—but remains keen to partner with Washington, in large part because of the massive market opportunities in the United States.

Something else about Rubio’s visit to Mumbai could help the bilateral relationship. Soon after he arrived, Rubio laid a wreath in honor of the 166 people, including six Americans, killed in the 2008 Mumbai terrorist attacks. That tragedy recalls a time when counterterrorism was a critical area of cooperation for India and the United States.

In the initial years after the Mumbai tragedy, U.S. covert operatives carried out missions in Pakistan focused on tracking down the group that perpetrated the attacks, Lashkar-e-Taiba—a highly potent anti-India outfit.

In Jamnagar on Wednesday, Rubio met with Mukesh Ambani, the head of India’s Reliance Industries, which manages the oil refinery. The meeting should boost U.S.-India ties through a textbook tactic of Trump’s foreign policy: commercial diplomacy that leverages personal relationships and advances U.S. economic interests.

Rubio was keen to talk to Ambani about his company’s efforts to refine Venezuelan crude oil. He likely also wanted to ensure that Reliance Industry’s plans to back an envisioned oil refinery in Texas were on track. The company said on Wednesday that Rubio and Ambani discussed the “global energy landscape” and opportunities for commercial collaboration.

Though energy is a bilateral tension point—since India imports significant amounts of Russian oil—it is also an area of cooperation. India has increased its imports of U.S. oil in recent years, which Rubio appeared to acknowledge early in his trip, when he noted that the country has diversified its energy import mix.

Here, the Trump administration’s recent decision to hold off on implementing a new sanctions bill that threatens to target India and other states that purchase Russian oil is key. That leaves diplomatic space for India and the United States to negotiate energy matters, and it could also provide a confidence-building measure that helps the two sides close on a new trade deal.

In the end, New Delhi came to Rubio. He met with Indian External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar on Tuesday in Mumbai. In making the trip, Jaishankar took a bit of a political risk. Indian opposition leaders had said that going to Mumbai would amount to allowing a foreign official—in this case, U.S. Ambassador to India Sergio Gor—to dictate to India on its own soil.

That Jaishankar went to Mumbai highlights the importance that New Delhi accords to its relationship with Washington, despite tensions and trust gaps. It shows that the two sides can get high-level things done in India, even if Rubio doesn’t travel to the capital.

Mecca pact text revealed. On Tuesday, the London-based Middle East Eye obtained and published the full text of the mutual defense pact involving Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey known as the Mecca pact. The revelation came on the day that the deal was submitted to Turkey’s parliament for ratification.

Saudi Arabia recently invoked the pact to call on Pakistan and Turkey’s support to counter attacks by Yemen’s Houthi rebels. The text wasn’t publicly released after the three countries signed the deal in August, and previously known information came from a joint statement and subsequent official comments.

The text confirms that the deal contains NATO-like language, with each signatory viewing an attack on one as an attack on all. It also does not single out any one adversary. The ambiguity is likely intentional, in part so that Pakistan—which borders Iran and has mediated in the Iran war—doesn’t worry that Tehran will read the deal as anti-Iran.

Perhaps the most notable aspect of the Mecca pact is its longevity: The signatories pledged to remain parties to the accord for a minimum of 10 years.

Saudi Arabia executes two Bangladeshis. Two Bangladeshis executed in Saudi Arabia last week for what Riyadh described as an indecent act in a Mecca mosque had been detained in the country on separate charges since August 2023, according to the Daily Star. The newspaper obtained a confidential memo from Bangladesh’s consul general in Jeddah dated Oct. 18.

According to the memo, Nazrul Islam Abdul Basir was detained on cybercrime charges, while Suety Akhter was detained on adultery charges. They both worked for the same cleaning company. The memo also indicates that the Bangladeshi Embassy in Riyadh did not receive prior warnings about the death sentences or eventual executions of Basir and Akhter.

Dhaka will face pressure from the families of the deceased to explain what happened, but it will need to be careful in its approach. Saudi Arabia is a critical economic partner; one-third to more than half of crude oil imports to Bangladesh come from the country. It is also a key donor and home to around 3.5 million Bangladeshis who send remittances home.

The importance of the partnership is magnified by Bangladesh’s worsening economic stress and energy shortages due to the Iran war. Dhaka will need to tread carefully with Riyadh as it seeks answers and considers how to respond to the executions, if at all.

Pakistan’s Munir in Iran. Pakistani Army chief Asim Munir and Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi arrived in Tehran on Tuesday for talks amid speculation that conflict in Iran could escalate again, possibly soon after the U.S. midterm elections on Nov. 3. The trip appears to be a fresh push for peace.

Pakistan’s job as mediator in the Iran war has become more difficult as Tehran and Washington each double down on hard-line positions. The diplomatic environment for mediation is changing, however, in ways that could advantage Islamabad.

Earlier in the conflict, Pakistan was the prime public mediator, with China, Egypt, and Turkey taking on less robust roles. In recent weeks, other countries—arguably with as much influence over Iran—have stepped up. Qatar is increasingly involved, and Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov was in Tehran on Monday, marking his first trip there since the war began.

This multinational effort should take some of the heat off Pakistan, which doesn’t want to become the fall guy if mediation efforts collapse. Islamabad could also benefit from some relief on the mediation front as it finds itself in a precarious spot. After agreeing to dispatch troops to Saudi Arabia, it must convince Iran that it remains a credible mediator.

A Reuters investigation, based in part on new data from the World Health Organization (WHO), has sounded the alarm on Bangladesh’s measles crisis. The country registered 202,000 suspected cases from mid-March through Oct. 9—the highest number in the world. The outbreak has caused 1,147 deaths, 94 percent of those reported among children under 5, according to the WHO.

The investigation finds that a range of factors that have triggered the crisis. The political turmoil caused by the ouster of Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina in 2024 led to disruptions in vaccination efforts. Data-gathering related to vaccination needs has also been problematic, with millions of children left out of inoculation strategies.

Finally, the new government’s haste to move quickly on vaccination efforts this year meant that there were insufficient readiness checks in advance of inoculation campaigns, which led to more problems.

In the Print, Bharatiya Janata Party politician Meenakashi Lekhi argues that efforts to clean up India’s Yamuna River haven’t gone far enough. “The Yamuna riverfront can take valuable lessons from Ahmedabad’s Sabarmati Riverfront Development Project to transform Delhi’s neglected riverbanks into vibrant public spaces while prioritising ecological restoration,” she writes.

In Prothom Alo, journalist Shahiduzzaman warns that not enough attention is being given to climate-related economic damage in Bangladesh: “The question is no longer whether climate change is costing Bangladesh,” he writes. “The real question is how much we are losing, who is paying the price and whether the country is doing enough to prevent these losses.”

A Daily Mirror editorial laments the poor treatment of elephants in Sri Lanka, which calls itself the “land of elephants.” There is a “shameful reality” at play, it argues: “[E]lephants are dying year after year, often because of human actions, while the country struggles to protect them. Respect must be measured by whether they are safe.”

#5244. Lucid’s 25,000 Bolt robotaxis will run on Nvidia’s self-driving software

Lucid’s European robotaxi deal now has a driver. Bolt announced today that its fleet of at least 25,000 autonomous Lucid vehicles will run Nvidia’s Alpamayo Stack self-driving software, starting with a data-collection program in Madrid in 2027.

That gives Lucid two robotaxi customers on two different autonomy systems: Nuro’s software for Uber’s Gravity robotaxis in the US, and now Nvidia’s for Bolt in Europe.

When Lucid and Bolt announced the 25,000-vehicle deal last month, nobody said who would write the driving software. The vehicles were to be built around Nvidia Hyperion, a reference design for compute and sensors, which isn’t a self-driving system on its own. Bolt said it would bring in autonomous-driving partners later.

It picked Nvidia again. Alpamayo Stack is Nvidia’s full-stack driving software, which the company describes as “reasoning AI” combined with classical perception and planning, running on Hyperion with its Halos safety tools on top. The two will tune the software for European driving, and Lucid is only the first automaker Bolt plans to put it in.

Madrid comes first, in 2027. The cars there won’t carry riders at the start. Their job is to collect real-world driving data so Bolt and Nvidia can develop and validate the software, and even that still needs regulatory approval. Germany and France are named as the next key markets.

Jevgeni Kabanov, Bolt’s president and head of autonomous driving, said the Region of Madrid is “pioneering a structured framework for commercial autonomous mobility.” On Lucid’s side, the work runs through Lucid Technologies. Its president, Kay Stepper, called the “launch of data collection next year” one of “the first steps” toward autonomous mobility in Europe.

Bolt didn’t say which Lucid model goes to Madrid.

The September deal was designed around Lucid’s upcoming Midsize platform, the same platform whose first vehicle, the Cosmos, got pushed to 2027 in Lucid’s August operating reset, “most likely the second half.” If the purpose-built vehicle isn’t ready in time, Madrid’s first data-collection cars would have to be existing Gravity or Air vehicles.

There’s also no date for anyone riding without a driver. Data collection is step one.

Add up both programs and Lucid has a lot of robotaxis on order, at least on paper. Uber’s program with Lucid and Nuro now calls for at least 35,000 Gravity vehicles globally, up from Uber’s original commitment to 20,000. Bolt adds at least 25,000. That’s 60,000 vehicles.

For scale, Lucid delivered 15,841 vehicles in all of 2025 and 10,852 in the first nine months of 2026. Those robotaxi commitments equal almost four years of Lucid’s current deliveries.

So Bolt is a company, with the same name as the Chevy Bolt. That makes the headline look a little confusing.

The Uber program is further along, but it isn’t open to the public yet. Uber employees have been taking test rides in Gravity robotaxis in San Francisco since April, with a safety operator behind the wheel. The public Bay Area launch is still promised for 2026, with Houston to follow in mid-2027.

Bolt is also hedging. It runs a separate track with Pony.ai and Stellantis, which announced a pilot in Luxembourg in June, and it’s targeting 100,000 autonomous vehicles on its platform across Europe and the wider UNECE region by 2035.

As for Nvidia, it has spent years selling chips and sensor reference designs to automakers. With Alpamayo Stack, it’s now selling the driver too, which puts it up against Nuro, Waymo, and Tesla’s software.

#5241. “Earth Satellites and Foreign Policy”

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

The advent of a new technology invariably comes with strategic implications, raising the question of who will benefit and whether risks can be managed. In 1957, following the Soviet Sputnik launch, U.S. policymakers and analysts recognized that the satellite, as the American physicist and engineer Lloyd Berkner observed in these pages, would bring transformative effects. The United States could not risk losing out, Berkner argued, but nor should governments allow geopolitical tensions to prevent the technology’s broad benefits from emerging.

From the vantage point of 2100 A.D., the year 1957 will most certainly stand in history as the year of man’s progression from a two-dimensional to a three-dimensional geography. It may well stand, also, as the point in time at which intellectual achievement forged ahead of weapons and national wealth as instruments of national policy. . . .

We should not make the mistake of seeing in the satellite merely the symbol of a foreign Power’s military progress. It does, of course, represent important advances in military capability. But quite irrespective of the usual interaction of scientific and military strength, the satellite symbolizes an intellectual attainment that may dominate the period immediately ahead as the most powerful single instrument of national policy. The vital point is not so much that the Soviet satellite preceded that of the United States, heretofore credited as the leader of world technology; it is that the United States, for the first time, finds a challenging competitor in the most advanced scientific fields. The achievement of the Soviet satellite has demonstrated to Americans what they refused to believe before, that they are in a race for intellectual leadership when they hadn’t realized that there was a race, or even that another nation had the capability to challenge their technology. In the complacency of our assumed technological lead, we have confused our high standards of living and material prosperity with intellectual stature. It is an extravagant and dangerous mistake.

#5240. 3.75-MWh battery system installed indoors at NY affordable housing building

Good Carbon Co. has installed a 3.75-MWh energy storage system from Viridi at Peace Bridge Place, a 105-unit affordable housing apartment building in Buffalo, New York. The project is likely one of the largest indoor lithium-ion battery systems in an occupied residential building in the United States.

At Peace Bridge Place, Viridi’s RPSLinkIN indoor battery storage system is part of a broader clean energy strategy that includes geothermal heating and cooling, domestic hot water and high-efficient building envelope upgrades. The 3.75-MWh system is designed to provide extended backup power for the fully electrified building during outages, while also helping reduce demand on the local grid during peak periods. Good Carbon Co., which focuses on revitalizing multifamily properties in communities throughout Western New York, selected the system to strengthen energy resilience for residents and enable the property to enable flexible, grid-responsive energy management.

“Energy is one of the biggest operating costs in multifamily housing, and how you manage it directly affects the long-term performance of a property,” said Adam Serbert, CEO of Good Carbon Co. “At Peace Bridge Place, Viridi’s battery and energy management technology will help us reduce peak demand, avoid higher-cost periods and participate in demand response programs. That means lower operating costs, new revenue opportunities and a stronger economic foundation for keeping the property affordable and well maintained. For us, this is not just about one building — it is about creating a model we can apply across future multifamily projects.”

The New York State Energy Research and Development Agency (NYSERDA) is providing support to Good Carbon Co. for the Peace Bridge Place project through its Future Grid Challenge. The Peace Bridge Place project is a National Grid pilot focused on integrating flexible, grid-responsive technologies into its distribution network. Viridi’s energy storage and energy management technology will work in coordination with EnergyHub’s Edge Distributed Energy Resource Management System (DERMS), enabling the property to respond to National Grid signals during periods of peak electricity demand.

“Peace Bridge Place demonstrates how forward-thinking energy storage solutions can ensure New York’s multifamily buildings are meeting the energy needs of its residents while creating a more reliable and resilient electric grid,” said Doreen M. Harris, President and CEO, NYSERDA. “Projects like this embody NYSERDA’s mission to spur innovation that drives New York toward a clean energy future.”

Distributed across seven battery rooms, the 3.75-MWh system is integrated with Viridi’s proprietary Energy Management System (EMS), which optimizes the property’s electricity use while enabling it to respond to changing conditions on the local grid.

“This project demonstrates what becomes possible when energy storage is designed to serve the building and the grid at the same time,” said Jon M. Williams, CEO of Viridi. “Peace Bridge Place will now be able to reduce peak demand, respond when the grid needs support and strengthen resilience for the people who live there. The bigger opportunity is proving that large-scale lithium-ion storage can be safely integrated into the places where people actually live and work. By unlocking occupied buildings for energy storage, we can dramatically expand where distributed energy resources can play a role in strengthening the grid.”

Peace Bridge Place is also a proof point for Viridi’s ability to scale this technology beyond a single project. At its Buffalo manufacturing facility, the company currently has the capacity to produce approximately 400 MWh of battery systems annually. The facility also has room to expand production as demand grows.

#5238. Ripple's Head of Engineering Reacts to AI Solving Decades-Old Cryptography Mystery

J. Ayo Akinyele, the head of engineering at Ripple, has responded to a significant cryptographic discovery made by Anthropic's Claude AI that reportedly resolved a 25-year-old enigma about the origins of popular cryptographic standards.

Akinyele commented that the findings were quite remarkable, noting that the solution was actually quite straightforward.

"AI shedding light on the origins of the NIST EC seeds is a pretty remarkable result," Ayo Akinyele said on X, citing the revelation that the origin of the parameters involved the use of the lrand48 random number generator seeded with the Unix timestamp.

The recent breakthrough relates to the origins of the widely debated elliptic curve parameters standardized by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), which have long been subject to speculation regarding potential backdoors inserted by the US National Security Agency (NSA).

He said the breakthrough offers reproducible evidence and greater confidence about the origins of such cryptographic standards.

Akinyele went on to speculate about other mysteries in cryptography that could potentially be solved with the help of artificial intelligence.

In an October 16 research report, Anthropic announced that their internal version of Claude discovered the origins of five popular NIST elliptic curve seeds within half an hour.

They include P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384, and P-521, which are part of the standards for the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA).

The origins of the original seed values had been a subject of curiosity among researchers for decades, in part due to the fact that the seeds were provided by the NSA with little detail about their provenance.

This lack of disclosure led to some concerns that there may have been special considerations made regarding the parameters, possibly to introduce cryptographic weaknesses.

However, Claude found that lrand48, which is a fairly simple pseudorandom number generator included in older C programming libraries, was used to generate the seeds.

It is worth pointing out that this is not a successful attack on ECDSA. Nor does it mean there are any security concerns for cryptocurrencies that use elliptic curve cryptography.

Rather, it makes the theory of intentional weakening of these specific NIST curves far less likely to be true.

The research does not mean that all aspects of the original selection are fully understood, but Anthropic pointed out that some unknowns remain around the selection of certain base points and the reasons for skipping certain mathematically viable candidate curves.

The breakthrough shows that artificial intelligence may be a tool that researchers can use to challenge long-held beliefs about widely used cryptographic systems.

The highlights this week: A new rebel alliance in eastern Congo threatens to drag the rest of the country into war, Mauritius experiences its worst riots in decades, and Egyptian President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi outlines a succession plan.

A new development in the decades-long conflict in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo threatens to draw the rest of the country into a wider war.

Last week, former President Joseph Kabila, who led Congo from 2001 to 2019, joined an armed coalition that seeks to challenge the current administration “by all means, including force.” Now, some analysts believe that an insurgency that has so far been concentrated in eastern territories could make its way to the capital of Kinshasa in the country’s west.

Kinshasa “now confronts a more formidable opposition: an armed movement with demonstrated military capabilities, led by a former president with extensive political networks,” commentator Samson Kasujja wrote last week in the Great Lakes Eye.

The new coalition, called the Front for the Refoundation of the Nation (FRN), unites militias and opposition figures under one banner. It includes the Rwanda-backed M23 movement, which has controlled vast areas of eastern Congo since 2022, and its broader political coalition, the Congo River Alliance, led by Corneille Nangaa, the former head of the country’s electoral commission.

The FRN’s members have diverse goals. M23, for instance, purports to seek protection—and a greater political voice—for Congo’s minority Tutsi population, which has long faced discrimination. Kabila, meanwhile, likely aims to use his political connections to unseat Congo’s current president, Félix Tshisekedi, his former coalition partner turned rival.

But on a broader level, Congo’s conflict is rooted in competition over land and mineral resources. Congo produces more than 70 percent of the world’s cobalt and is home to deposits of other metals that are essential to the technology and defense industries.

The escalation of the insurgency demonstrates the failure of a so-called peace deal between Rwanda and Congo brokered by the Trump administration last year, whereby the Congolese government agreed to give the United States preferential access to minerals in exchange for U.S. security support, and Rwanda’s Tutsi-led government, in turn, agreed to stop backing the M23 in exchange for private U.S. investment in minerals processing.

If the conflict spreads, it will have a devastating impact on civilian lives and exacerbate an already catastrophic humanitarian crisis marked by sexual violence and the forcible recruitment of child soldiers. Already, the fighting has displaced more than 8 million people.

The Kabila-led coalition is seeking to position itself as a political alternative to Congo’s current administration. Both Kabila and Tshisekedi are the sons of former Congolese political figures. And both have their fair share of domestic critics.

The election that Tshisekedi, whose father was a three-time prime minister of Zaire, won in 2018 was widely disputed. In recent months, he has faced mounting calls to resign over his attempts to implement constitutional reforms that could pave the way for a third presidential term, exceeding the two-term limit. Protests organized by a broad alliance of opposition parties, known as the Article 64 Coalition, took place last month denouncing the move as a constitutional coup.

Tshisekedi’s name has also been linked to a recent corruption scandal. Last week, Belgian police raided the homes of Tshisekedi’s family members and associates in Brussels as part of a money-laundering investigation connected to mining revenues, following a complaint filed last year by a collective of Congolese civil rights groups.

Kabila, meanwhile, did not leave office as a popular figure. He took power in 2001 following the assassination of his father, President Laurent-Désiré Kabila, who also led a Rwanda-backed rebellion in the 1990s to seize power in Kinshasa. Joseph Kabila’s tenure was marked by a clampdown on protests after overstaying his time in office by two years, using a constitutional clause that allowed his government to remain as a caretaker.

By the end of his tenure, one poll found that eight in 10 Congolese people had a negative opinion of Kabila. Overwhelming international pressure saw him become the first leader in Congo’s history to oversee a peaceful handover of power in 2019. Although the two were on good terms when Tshisekedi took office, a Congolese military court sentenced Kabila to death in absentia in September 2025 on charges of treason over claims that he was supporting M23.

Still, with opposition groups under one movement, Kabila could pose a real challenge to Tshisekedi’s government. But questions remain over how long an FRN coalition can last given the disparate aims of those within it and how much influence Kabila can reasonably have over the M23, which reportedly is dealing with its own internal power struggle.

Meanwhile, the latest political scramble has taken focus away from Congo’s Ebola crisis, the fastest-growing on record. Since the outbreak in eastern Congo earlier this year, Ebola has infected nearly 10,000 people and killed more than 4,000. There is still no approved treatment for this latest strain of the virus which has now spread to four other countries.

Mauritian protests. Mauritius is experiencing its most violent demonstrations in decades. They began in early October to protest high living costs and water shortages—a result of drought, global warming, and aging infrastructure—but escalated into rioting after police shot and seriously wounded a demonstrator on Oct. 9. Mauritian authorities imposed a curfew last week but lifted it on Monday.

So far, nearly 100 people have been arrested, including 16 minors. Meanwhile, the government is considering lowering the age for detention and prosecution of minors from 14 to 12. The authorities said that protests have been marked by arson and damage to police stations, traffic lights, and CCTV.

EU migrant funding. The European Union has approved an additional 25 million euros (around $29 million) for authorities in eastern Libya to control migration from Africa to the EU, despite reports suggesting that some of those same Libyan officials are linked to people-smuggling networks.

The money, which comes on top of the estimated $247 million that the EU allocated to Libya from 2021 to 2024, will finance border management, search-and-rescue operations, and anti-smuggling efforts in eastern Libya, which is controlled by Gen. Khalifa Haftar’s forces.

More than 30 rights groups issued a joint statement in July opposing greater EU cooperation with both Libya’s U.N.-backed government in Tripoli and the rival Haftar-backed government in Benghazi, citing torture, sexual violence, and forced labor on both sides.

Ethiopia conflict. On Friday, Ethiopia’s federal government appointed a new interim administration for the northern Tigray region after the Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF) withdrew from the region’s capital of Mekelle.

In recent weeks, Ethiopian troops and allied fighters have regained control of towns on Tigray’s northern border with Eritrea from the TPLF, after the TPLF and its allies launched an offensive against the federal government in late September.

Still, the fighting, which is concentrated in the Amhara, Tigray, and Afar regions, has continued, though reporting from the ground is limited. Meanwhile, observers fear the possibility of a border war between Ethiopia and Eritrea, which Addis Ababa accuses of backing the TPLF.

Succession plan. Egyptian President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi has pledged to leave office when his term ends in 2030, after 16 years in power. Under Sisi, the military has played an expanded role in Egyptian affairs, and he has proposed a 30-month military academy to train presidential candidates, which would target Egyptians aged 40 to 50 who hold a university doctorate.

“They will be taught what a state is, what its institutions are, and how to protect it,” Sisi said on Saturday.

Egypt’s military already controls most state institutions, from cement manufacturing to farming and construction, despite the country agreeing to reforms that would see the military relinquish some control as part of its loan program with the International Monetary Fund.

After more than 30 years, Formula 1 is set to return to Africa. Last week, Rwanda announced a 10-year deal to host an annual African Grand Prix from the early 2030s. A new circuit and airport will be built for the event, which will take place in Bugesera, just southeast of the capital of Kigali.

The last Formula 1 race held on the continent was the South African Grand Prix in 1993. Although there has largely been pan-African support for the Rwanda deal, it has faced scrutiny from rights groups such as Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch, which have raised concerns about Rwanda’s poor human rights record and potential “sportswashing.”

South Africa, Morocco, and Nigeria also put in bids to host the event but were ultimately unsuccessful. South African President Cyril Ramaphosa has publicly congratulated his Rwandan counterpart, Paul Kagame, and urged South Africans to celebrate the success of other African nations.

Meanwhile, Gayton McKenzie, South Africa’s sports and culture minister, suggested that Pretoria would campaign for a second race to take place in South Africa. “Italy, a country smaller than South Africa, has two,” McKenzie said. “Now … the whole continent of 54 countries must only have one. What type of thinking is that?”

Literary scandals. This month, Zimbabwean writer Simbarashe Kundizeza won the Caine Prize for African writers for his short story “The Blue Fighter.” Days later, the Atlantic reported that a software had flagged the story as almost completely artificial intelligence-generated, prompting a denial from Kundizeza and pushback by the African literary community.

In Literary Hub, James Folta writes that AI is “muddying” the “cultural waters,” noting that its “omnipresence has introduced doubt about the provenance of any text, and I fear that erosion of trust is going to have an outsized impact on writers from more marginalized backgrounds.”

Meanwhile, in the New York Times, Doyle D. Calhoun notes that nearly all allegations of AI fraud have involved Black and brown writers. “The surveillance of Black writers is more than backlash against the literary world’s recent efforts to open its doors,” he writes. “It is a way for anti-woke activists to say the quiet part out loud: They would prefer that the literary world remain a fortress.”

Beijing’s Green Path to Ending Dollar Dominance

In 1971, as the international monetary system was on the brink of collapse, U.S. Treasury Secretary John Connally told a group of worried European finance ministers that the dollar was “our currency, but your problem.” The dollar had become that system’s official reserve currency in 1944, under the Bretton Woods agreement, making signatories subject to the whims of U.S. monetary policy. Even after Bretton Woods collapsed and many currencies floated freely, the dollar continued to dominate. In the last half century, U.S. currency power has only expanded, as more countries have integrated into the global economy and therefore grown to depend on the dollar. China, reliant on export-driven growth, is foremost among them.

Reckoning with the possibility that Washington could someday make the dollar China’s problem, Beijing is now crafting its own solution. In a January 2024 speech, Chinese leader Xi Jinping made this effort explicit, instructing senior provincial and ministerial officials to accelerate China’s transformation into a “leading financial power.” A strong currency, he specified, was one of the essential foundations of that power. When Qiushi, the Chinese Communist Party’s flagship journal, published the full text of the speech in 2026, the standard Xi had in mind became clearer: for a currency to be considered “strong,” it must be widely used in international trade, investment, and foreign exchange markets and be recognized as a global reserve currency.

Xi’s call is the latest episode in a long history of efforts by major powers to reduce their dependence on the dollar-centered global financial system. In 1965, French Finance Minister Valéry Giscard d’Estaing famously described the disproportionate advantages that dollar dominance confers on the United States as “exorbitant privilege”; the same year, France began aggressively converting a substantial share of its dollar reserves into gold, contributing to the collapse of the Bretton Woods system. In 1999, the Japanese government released a report on how to promote wider international use of the yen. That same year, the launch of the euro gave Europe what would become the world’s second most important international currency, affording it greater monetary autonomy even as the European Central Bank carefully danced around any suggestion that it might challenge the dollar.

What distinguishes Xi’s ambition is that China, unlike Europe or Japan, possesses the economic heft, military capability, and political will to rival the United States’ geopolitical power. Not since London commanded a global empire has another country’s currency stood a realistic chance of competing with the dollar. A global financial transition from “In God We Trust” to “Where the Party Leads, We Follow” can no longer be dismissed as fantasy.

The internationalization of the renminbi would afford China a measure of financial survivability should the United States ever choose to wield the dollar as a punitive weapon, as it did against Russia after the country’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, in 2022. The United States controls both the supply of the dominant global reserve currency and many of the critical financial infrastructure chokepoints through which it circulates. Washington, if it so desired, could severely restrict China’s access to dollar liquidity by making the cost of doing business with Chinese institutions prohibitive, for example by indicting or sanctioning a major Chinese bank. Any such measure would shock the Chinese financial system, raise transaction costs for trade, and deliver a major competitive blow to China’s export sector, which accounts for roughly one-fifth of its GDP.

Survivability, however, is only a prerequisite for credible deterrence. To deter the United States from using the dollar weapon, Beijing requires a financial second-strike capability. It must be able to redirect a substantial share of global trade and finance to a renminbi-based network. The durability of that shift depends on healthy global demand for an anchor asset priced in renminbi. For China, the most likely source of such an anchor asset lies in its dominance of the green economy.

Xi does not necessarily want the renminbi to replace the dollar. His 2024 speech advocated strength, not hegemony. Placing a respectable second, or even a consequential third, would suffice to break the dollar’s effective monopoly and constrain the United States’ ability to impose financial sanctions unilaterally, while saving China the effort and cost of sustaining currency supremacy. From Beijing’s perspective, the mission is to establish the renminbi as the Pepsi to the dollar’s Coca-Cola: never the market leader, but serviceable in a pinch and more than capable of keeping the dominant incumbent honest.

The near-universal acceptance of the U.S. dollar arguably benefits China more than any other country. The Chinese economy relies on exports, and Chinese goods priced and financed in dollars can reach ready buyers almost anywhere. The resulting profits can then be invested almost anywhere and converted into liquid funds on short notice. This suits Beijing: China enjoys the benefits of global liquidity without bearing the burdens of sustaining it. To preserve the global dollar system, the United States must tolerate foreign ownership of an ever-expanding volume of dollar claims as countries conduct ever more business in the currency. U.S. markets are exposed to international volatility, and foreigners hold some influence over U.S. corporations. The Federal Reserve, which issues the dollar, backstops the system, expanding its balance sheet during periods of financial stress, when governments and private companies hoard dollars to ensure they can continue to pay their debts and buy goods. This openness and exposure has posed little threat to the U.S. economy because foreign shareholders have generally sought to operate within, rather than change, U.S. business norms, and U.S. banks have earned tidy profits intermediating the world’s capital flows through New York and London.

China’s leaders have no desire to make any such tradeoff. Maintaining a global renminbi system would be fundamentally at odds with China’s state-led economic model. For the renminbi to replicate the dollar’s functions, China would have to create broad, two-way financial channels through which foreign holders of renminbi could reinvest that money into the Chinese economy. The resulting capital inflows would fundamentally reshape China’s domestic financial markets. It would be impractical for Beijing to confine foreign investors to a narrow group of approved assets, as it does now. Once foreign investors were allowed into China’s markets in force, they would arbitrage the distortions created by financial repression and politically directed capital allocation. Such competition would undermine one of Beijing’s most effective means of securing the loyalty of China’s business class: controlling who gains access to capital, on what terms, and for what purposes.

For many wealthy Chinese citizens, including some members of the Communist Party itself, the dollar is an indispensable form of political risk insurance. Domestic political rivalries can lead to asset seizure or even imprisonment with little due process. Offshore dollar deposits and foreign corporate structures allow elites to diversify their holdings across currencies and jurisdictions. Control of a company listed overseas, especially in the United States, offers wealthy Chinese more than prestige and access to global capital; disclosure obligations, duties to shareholders, and international visibility, which extend beyond the party’s control, can give Chinese executives leverage against arbitrary demands from officials at home. These constraints cannot prevent interference by senior authorities determined to pursue their targets, but they can raise its cost, slow its implementation, and create room for negotiation. The dollar-based system is a refuge for Chinese elites who fall from grace, and because no one’s position is ever entirely secure, few wish to see that refuge disappear.

China is perfectly willing to go on living in a world that the dollar makes go round. But it cannot abide the dollar’s weaponization, and the renminbi still has a long way to go before it is strong enough to blunt that threat. If more countries and businesses are to adopt the renminbi, there must first be a global financial infrastructure for its use. China has already laid the foundation by facilitating renminbi use among firms in more than a hundred countries and across a growing array of commodity markets to settle transactions, obtain financing for trade and investment, and hedge their risks. Here, breadth matters far more than depth. Even modest daily volumes of renminbi transactions can keep payment channels operational, preserve financial relationships, and maintain the legal and administrative expertise needed to run an alternative financial system.

Because China’s relative economic strength lies in the flow of manufactured goods—it accounted for 20 percent of global manufactured exports in 2025—Beijing has concentrated its efforts on building a payment network that facilitates the commercial flows generated by its manufacturing machine. In 2005, Zhou Xiaochuan, then governor of the People’s Bank of China, publicly encouraged the already increasing use of local currencies among China’s trading partners. In 2009, the PBOC launched a pilot program for settling cross-border transactions in renminbi, followed in 2015 by the creation of the Cross-Border Interbank Payment System, the backbone of China’s international renminbi network. CIPS began with 195 participants spanning 50 countries; by June 2026, it had 1,829 participants and could route payments to more than 5,200 banking institutions in 191 countries. But CIPS remains much smaller than the mature wholesale payment systems supporting the dollar and the euro. In 2025, the U.S.-based Clearing House Interbank Payments System processed an estimated 19 times as much value in international payments as CIPS, and Europe’s real-time gross settlement system, called T2, cleared about 14 times as much.

But payments alone are a misleading measure of currency power. An international transaction entails far more than a transfer of economic value; it requires a package of financial services that unfolds across five linked stages: pricing, financing, risk transfer, payment, and reinvestment. The dollar dominates international trade and finance because it is the default currency at every stage, with its strength in each reinforcing its position in all the others. For the renminbi to supplant the dollar, it would have to become competitive at every stage of the transaction cycle, not just in payments.

China’s leaders recognize that the influence of financial centers in London, New York, and Tokyo rests not only on their ability to move money but also on their institutions’ ability to create credit, set prices, insure shipments, and recycle savings. That is why Chinese state-owned enterprises are driving the expansion of the renminbi network. By denominating invoices, contracts, and overseas project financing in renminbi, these enterprises create a base of recurring demand for the currency around which Chinese banks, insurers, and other financial institutions can build competing business lines under Chinese control. To reinforce that base, the Communist Party laid out an action plan in 2025 that instructs state-owned enterprises to prioritize settlement in renminbi, rather than dollars, whenever they deal with foreign suppliers and customers. That strategy is already fostering a regional network for renminbi use in Southeast Asia. In 2024, renminbi payments involving economies in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations rose 19 percent, with two-thirds of those transactions occurring between ASEAN countries.

Beijing has largely completed the first phase of its strategy, establishing a foothold in payments, and it is now expanding upstream into pricing, financing, and risk transfer, and, cautiously, downstream into reinvestment. Only recently has Beijing begun to appreciate how much the currency in which goods are priced influences the subsequent stages of the transaction cycle. Historically, China has been content to price the goods it imports and exports mostly in dollars. According to International Monetary Fund (IMF) estimates, only 6.5 percent of China’s exports were invoiced in renminbi in 2023. Chinese government statistics show that nearly 30 percent of China’s trade in goods is settled in renminbi, but much of that is essentially a dollar transaction masquerading as a renminbi one: Chinese exporters invoice in dollars, buyers obtain dollar funding and are debited in dollars, and the funds are converted into renminbi only just before reaching the mainland.

The government is seeking to reverse that pattern by promoting what it calls “China prices”: renminbi-denominated benchmark prices listed on local exchanges for major commodities that China imports. Greater autonomy over pricing means that Chinese firms can buy raw inputs and manage their exposure to commodity price swings in global markets without acquiring dollars. It also gives Chinese authorities greater influence over the reference prices used to set the terms of supply contracts for the physical delivery of those commodities. This leverage is vital for China because the steady flow of raw inputs is essential to the smooth running of its manufacturing machine.

Price autonomy matters especially for critical minerals, which feed the green industries that in Xi’s view are central to China’s economic future. China produces more than 80 percent of the world’s batteries and consumes over 75 percent of its lithium, 70 percent of its cobalt, and about 60 percent of its nickel. But the country’s position in global markets for each of these minerals does not yet reflect its dominance in production and consumption. To date, China has made the most progress in lithium; more than 90 percent of global trading in lithium-carbonate futures occurs on the Guangzhou Futures Exchange, where contracts are denominated in renminbi. In July, the exchange allowed foreign traders to trade the lithium-carbonate futures contract directly, announcing with fanfare that several overseas “leading enterprises” had joined the market. China is now poised, perhaps for the first time in modern history, to become the principal financial center for a globally important commodity. Nickel and cobalt, on the other hand, remain tied to dollar benchmarks in London and Chicago. Although China is the largest consumer of these two minerals, it is not yet the financial reference point for their markets. That is the sort of mismatch that the push for China prices is meant to resolve.

The financing stage of Beijing’s renminbi strategy is gaining momentum. In 2023, Beijing instructed Chinese financial institutions to expand their renminbi funding abroad. State-backed lenders such as the Export-Import Bank of China have increased their renminbi lending for overseas trade and infrastructure projects, and commercial banks increasingly supply renminbi trade finance. Between 2023 and mid-2026, Chinese financial institutions more than doubled their renminbi lending to overseas borrowers from 1.3 to 2.9 trillion yuan ($428 billion). The PBOC, for its part, has adopted currency swap agreements with 32 foreign central banks and monetary authorities, including those in Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, as well as the European Central Bank. In their early years, after the global financial crisis, these swap lines were largely symbolic and only lightly used, but in recent years, some have provided direct support for commercial activity. In 2025, Turkish companies borrowed four billion yuan ($557 million) to pay Chinese suppliers using a facility backed by a PBOC swap line, 70 percent more than they had borrowed a year earlier. This effort to turn the renminbi into a funding currency is starting to gain traction: in May 2026, the renminbi accounted for seven percent of the value of trade-finance messages on SWIFT, the leading global platform for exchanging payment instructions, up from 5.1 percent two years earlier and enough to overtake the euro for second place, although the dollar remained leagues ahead with an 82.5 percent share.

Beijing has also taken steps, if more modest ones, to increase the renminbi’s presence in markets for insurance and reinsurance. In 2025, Bank of China, a state-owned bank, and PICC, a state-owned property and casualty insurance company, developed new terms under which premiums and claims for cargo shipments could be settled in renminbi. The terms also specified Shanghai as the preferred venue for arbitration, part of a broader effort to shift the maritime-insurance market away from its traditional reliance on dollar settlement and English commercial law, long the standard framework for international shipping and maritime insurance contracts.

The final stage of the transaction cycle, reinvestment, completes the loop through which goods and value circulate through the global economy. Recipients of payment must decide what to do with the proceeds: hold them in a short-term account, invest them in longer-term assets, or convert them into another currency. A strong currency ecosystem becomes sustainable at scale only when recipients are willing to hold and reinvest their balances rather than routinely convert them.

Beijing has, over the past decade, given foreign holders of renminbi more ways to reinvest their balances. The Stock Connect and Bond Connect programs, which took their earliest forms in 2014 and 2017, respectively, have since expanded to allow a much broader mix of investors outside China to use renminbi to buy some mainland equities and bonds. Before these programs began, licensing and quotas restricted direct access to Chinese markets, with only about 228 large institutional investors receiving quotas in 2013; today, about 1,000 investors have that status. In 2025, foreign trading through Stock Connect averaged 212 billion yuan ($29.5 billion) per day, and Bond Connect averaged 39 billion yuan ($5.4 billion) per day. The tools available to manage the risks associated with these investments have also expanded. Swap Connect, launched in 2023, allows offshore investors to hedge against movements in onshore renminbi interest rates.

Access to China’s financial markets is no longer the biggest problem facing foreign investors, but the risk that Beijing’s unpredictable capital controls could someday trap their funds remains a worry. A 2023 survey by the Insurance Asset Management Association of China and J.P. Morgan Asset Management found that roughly two-thirds of chief investment officers at insurance companies outside China cited controls on capital moving in and out of the country as a top concern for investing there. Despite greater access than ever, foreign investors remain reluctant to hold Chinese assets at scale. In March 2026, they held only 3.2 trillion yuan ($448 billion), or 1.6 percent of the Chinese bond market, compared with roughly $15.5 trillion, or 27 percent, of the U.S. bond market. Reserve holdings recorded with the IMF reveal an even wider gap: in the first quarter of 2026, the renminbi accounted for just under two percent of global foreign-exchange reserves, compared with 57 percent for the dollar. Without a far larger pool of renminbi assets that foreigners can freely buy and sell, the renminbi cannot come close to replacing the dollar.

For China to wield anything approaching the currency power of the United States, the renminbi must dominate trade in a good that China controls and the rest of the world cannot easily do without. In the dollar system, the global oil trade has long played this role—hence the term “petrodollar.” In a renminbi-ascendant system, the corresponding anchor could be the critical minerals, clean energy equipment, and electrical infrastructure that are central to green energy technology. If China consolidates its dominance over that industrial ecosystem, using its scale and cost advantages to prevent competitors from catching up, it could gain enough leverage to price those essential, hard-to-substitute exports in renminbi, creating the foundation for a “green yuan” that might eventually replace the petrodollar.

China has spent decades methodically assembling the supply chains that have made it the world’s dominant producer of green energy technology and equipment. Beijing is now on the verge of taking a commanding position across a set of goods that the world has decided it cannot do without. China’s strategy rests on vertical integration of nearly the entire clean energy supply chain. It is the leading refiner of 19 of the 20 energy-related strategic minerals tracked by the International Energy Agency, with an average market share of 70 percent. It accounts for roughly 85 percent of global solar panel manufacturing capacity, and it supplies 78 percent of the wind-turbine capacity installed worldwide. In 2025, it produced about 70 percent of the world’s electric vehicles.

After decisively seizing the first-mover advantage, Beijing is now consolidating its market position, using economies of scale to undercut prospective rivals and promote technical standards built around technologies owned by Chinese firms. The green energy economy is more diffuse than the fossil fuel system it seeks to replace and therefore more difficult to command. But China is already beginning to turn its market-share lead into structural advantages that could lock out competitors and entrench its dominance.

Until recently, Beijing focused more on extending its industrial lead in green energy than on converting that advantage into financial power. But that is beginning to change. The proliferation of carbon-neutral investment mandates has unintentionally created a green finance industry almost tailor-made for China. In 2022, Beijing published its “Green Bond Principles,” which require green bond issuers to allocate 100 percent of proceeds to eligible green projects.

By the end of 2025, funds dedicated to sustainable investment were worth $4.13 trillion worldwide, and assets managed according to sustainability criteria were projected to reach $40 trillion by 2030. Chinese entities, meanwhile, already had 47.2 trillion yuan ($6.7 trillion) in green loans and bonds outstanding, far exceeding any other country’s green lending, but largely confined to its domestic renminbi system. In 2024, foreign institutions held only 3.2 billion yuan ($445 million) of China’s 2.2 trillion yuan ($306 billion) green bond market, a minuscule 0.15 percent.

That meager foreign participation is likely to increase. Chinese authorities are working to turn more of the country’s vast pool of green debt—a term for loans and bonds issued for projects with a positive environmental impact—developed primarily for a domestic investor base, into tradable securities that foreign investors can hold in compliance with international sustainability mandates. Their objective is to create a broad class of renminbi-denominated assets that are internationally investable, tied to green projects, and integrated into the broader renminbi ecosystem for pricing, settlement, and other financial services.

About 17 percent of China’s green bond market has already been screened against technical criteria aligned with European green finance frameworks, making those bonds easier for foreign investors and index providers to recognize and include in their portfolios. The Ministry of Finance has begun issuing renminbi-denominated sovereign green bonds outside China, with its first bond issuance raising six billion yuan ($834 million) in London in April 2025, followed by a second six-billion-yuan issuance in Hong Kong this past May, where orders were ten times the amount on offer. These issues are tiny compared with China’s domestic market, but they establish pricing reference points that make it easier for other entities to issue renminbi-denominated green debt in the future. Pakistan’s issuance, in May, of a 1.75 billion yuan ($258 million) green Panda bond—the term for renminbi-denominated debt issued in China by foreign borrowers—offers a glimpse of a future in which foreign borrowers routinely raise renminbi in China to finance their green development.

This is how industrial dominance begins to grow into financial power. The Chinese government has no formal plans to create a green yuan, but the constituent pieces of such a system are nevertheless starting to come together. The anchor asset for a green yuan would not be green technology itself, but an expanding family of renminbi claims on the green economy. Green bonds would form this economy’s tradable core, connecting China’s industrial advantage to the growing global demand for sustainable assets.

Much of the world has set deadlines for replacing fossil fuels that cannot be met without China’s green economy. Even if the political will to combat climate change fades, global dependence on imported energy has created geopolitical vulnerabilities that will likely continue to buoy demand for renewables, which almost any country can deploy to produce more energy at home. For countries and firms seeking entry into the green economy, accepting renminbi financing and Chinese technical standards may eventually become part of the ordinary price of admission. The result would be a self-reinforcing circuit of green goods and green finance, much of it denominated in renminbi.

For Washington, the prospect of a green yuan should not set off alarms about an imminent renminbi takeover. It should instead prompt a harder question: whether the petrodollar is no longer fit for purpose and what asset class, technology, or industrial system might anchor the dollar’s currency power in the future. The dollar rests on far more than the United States’ ability to secure global energy flows and its ties with major oil-producing states, but policymakers would be mistaken to treat its supremacy as permanent.

Beijing is not plotting to dethrone the dollar, and the Communist Party almost certainly does not want the financial openness such a victory would require. Whether or not the green yuan becomes a reality someday, the more immediate value to the party of an internationalized renminbi is its potential to deter China hawks in Washington from weaponizing China’s current reliance on the dollar.

As China’s renminbi-based system becomes stronger, so will Beijing’s ability to withstand the shock of potential U.S. financial sanctions. Washington’s threat to impose those sanctions will, in turn, lose some of its potency. That shift in the economic power dynamic would likely make Beijing more willing to call Washington’s bluff—which it may already be doing. In May, China issued its first-ever blocking order, instructing firms in China not to comply with U.S. sanctions on five Chinese oil refiners alleged to be major purchasers of Iranian oil.

If the reign of King Dollar ever ends, it probably won’t be because China usurped the throne but rather because the United States surrendered it. Washington must take note that the strategic asset underpinning the next monetary order may not be a barrel of oil but an industrial ecosystem. The danger is that financial markets may provide little warning that such a transition is underway. Markets can go long stretches ignoring the mounting evidence of change before a catalyst—such as a political stalemate over raising the U.S. debt limit—finally causes models to adjust and prices to snap up or down. U.S. officials are reluctant to plan for the erosion of the dollar’s dominance for fear that doing so would signal to markets that such a decline is inevitable. Their eagerness to point to price charts as proof of the dollar’s resilience, however, suggests that the lessons of the last financial crisis may already be forgotten. The United States must make contingency plans before the dollar’s exorbitant privilege is visibly tattered and signs of hedging against it become clear. By that time, the damage may no longer be possible to contain.

#5225. AI Is a Choice

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

The Narrow but Feasible Path to Collective Good

Alex Imas, Mohamed A. El-Erian, and James Manyika

Few technologies in history have matched the potential of artificial intelligence to transform the economic landscape in which they emerged. Deployed broadly and effectively, AI can make workers more efficient, enable more dynamic entrepreneurship, and lift economic growth, all of which could be necessary bulwarks against the powerful economic headwinds brought on by aging populations and mounting public debt. AI can also make possible revolutionary new advances in science and medicine and democratize access to knowledge once reserved for the select few.

But the acceleration of AI also carries risks. It could be applied in ways that displace workers, disrupt how students learn, create new bioweapons and cyberweapons, and strain current supplies of energy, water, and land. Compounding these challenges is a mismatch: the benefits of this new technology may take a long time to become visible and widespread. As with earlier transformations, such as the Industrial Revolution, people are likely to experience the disruptions that come from adopting AI before they feel its benefits. And whereas users directly feel the effects of tools like chatbots, some benefits that AI is indirectly making possible, including more accurate predictions of natural disasters and improved medical diagnoses, may not be perceived as owing to the technology at all.

Public opinion has already begun to turn against AI, especially in the United States. One of the main targets has been data centers, with polls showing that a significant majority of Americans oppose the construction of data centers in their communities. Opposition to data centers needed for the new AI economy now cuts across party lines and age groups. Numerous surveys have found that residents worry about data centers using too many resources, creating endless noise, and raising their utility bills, all while developers benefit from public subsidies at the communities’ expense.

Although some of these problems are local, they also reflect wider concerns about AI. Some leading figures in AI have no doubt contributed to this anxiety with dramatic warnings of a white-collar-job apocalypse and even the possibility that AI will lead to human extinction. These are real issues that must be understood and addressed, but the growing public pushback against AI also suggests a deeper distributional challenge: individuals, communities, and businesses are being asked to help shepherd a transformative and potentially unsettling new technology at a time when many citizens see its financial gains going to a few people whom they increasingly distrust. AI is now a major issue in American politics: candidates running in competitive elections, no matter which party they belong to, appear increasingly averse to being associated with support for AI.

These anxieties are understandable. AI adoption risks repeating the mistakes of globalization, which generated large and widely spread economic benefits but led to concentrated losses of jobs, growth, and industry in particular communities. Government support in response was insufficient or arrived too late. Although the economy gained overall from free trade, the political bargain failed to give affected workers and communities a credible stake in the gains, even as individuals benefited as consumers from lower prices. The result was a backlash that contributed to the protectionism and fragmentation that continue to plague today’s politics and society.

Turning AI’s capabilities into broadly shared and enduring prosperity requires more than investing in the technology itself. The central challenge of the AI era is coordination and cooperation across groups responsible for the many complementary investments that promote the adoption of AI, including technological diffusion, worker empowerment, firm reorganization, and infrastructure construction. Because these complementary investments must be made across different institutions and at different times, progress requires market mechanisms to encourage cooperation as well as coalitions among organizations, firms, workers, communities, and governments that can turn AI’s technological gains into widespread economic and social benefits. To borrow a concept from game theory, AI is a “cooperative game” in which the largest gains arise only when many groups coordinate, and those gains persist only when every group expects to do better as part of shared coalitions than outside them.

But coalition building requires trust, and public trust—particularly regarding technology and who benefits from it—has eroded. Grand promises of scientific and medical breakthroughs will not overcome deep-seated hostility. Durable cooperation requires giving affected groups a concrete stake in the opportunities and gains that AI generates rather than merely promising future benefits. Without that stake, the individuals and groups whose support is needed to make AI flourish could block its progress. Governments could impose stifling regulations on the industry or stop issuing data center permits, firms could decline to adopt new AI-driven systems, and workers could avoid sharing the tacit knowledge needed to make the technology useful. As the economist and Nobel Prize laureate Michael Spence once observed: “You cannot solve a cooperative game uncooperatively.” The way to ensure that society benefits from AI’s enormous potential while mitigating its risks is to make cooperation the clear choice.

AI is staking a claim to being the next general-purpose technology—one that applies across a wide array of activities, improves over time, and generates complementary innovations. Electricity was an early general-purpose technology. When inventors discovered how to commercially harness electricity in the late nineteenth century, the ability to use it to power many types of machines transformed industrial production. Computing, another general-purpose technology, used electricity to process information itself, revolutionizing sectors as varied as engineering and health care.

But AI works through a more powerful input—intelligence—which makes it possible to interpret information, predict outcomes, diagnose problems, communicate with others, and make decisions. As a result, AI can raise productivity in existing sectors: a nurse can spend less time on paperwork and devote more time to patients, engineers can more quickly develop new designs, and small firms can scale up their analytics capabilities to compete with larger competitors. It can also increase efficiency so significantly that it makes possible wholly new products and services that were otherwise too costly or complicated to deliver.

AI also goes beyond existing general-purpose technologies by transforming the process of research and discovery. By inventing new ways of optimizing scientific processes, AI opens up previously unknown possibilities, similar to what microscopes and telescopes did in earlier eras. AlphaFold, a specialized AI model for which Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared half the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, is a clear example. Determining a single protein’s three-dimensional structure from its amino acid sequence used to take months or even years; by using machine learning, AlphaFold has predicted the structures of more than 200 million proteins, fundamentally reshaping what biologists are able to investigate.

The potential of AI is thus a combination of the Industrial Revolution, whose general-purpose technologies transformed production, and the Enlightenment, which brought about inventions for making inventions—the new instruments and new paradigms that transformed the process of discovery. AI holds the promise of providing both the productivity boost and the tools to pave the way for discoveries that can address many of the problems weighing down aging, debt-ridden societies across the world.

The latest wave of AI has diffused at unprecedented speed. Google’s Gemini app and OpenAI’s ChatGPT each now reach more than one billion monthly users. Google’s AI & Economy ATLAS, a study of how people are using the company’s AI products that two of us (Imas and Manyika) worked on, analyzed 15 million interactions in more than 150 countries. It found that AI was used in tasks in 68 percent of occupations, and these occupations made up roughly 90 percent of all U.S. employment. Even automotive technicians and industrial mechanics used AI to interpret test results and diagnose electrical faults. Another study that two of us (Imas and Manyika) ran in collaboration with MIT FutureTech found that nearly half the scientists who were surveyed used some type of AI model every day, saving them time and enabling more interdisciplinary research.

But the gains of diffusion are neither guaranteed nor automatic. AI has been adopted broadly across and within sectors, but thus far its penetration remains fairly shallow. The ATLAS study found that only 21 percent of tasks in a typical occupation have been substantially affected by the technology. According to the Anthropic Economic Index, which measures how the AI model Claude is used in workplace tasks as varied as writing software, analyzing data, and managing administrative workflows, potential use far exceeds actual use. This suggests that access has spread much faster than adoption. Incorporating AI deeply enough to allow it to live up to its potential requires a bigger transformation. A firm does not change how it operates simply by buying access to an AI coding agent. It must also invest in organizational reforms and worker training and often needs to redesign its governance structure or its product lines.

The banking sector, for example, has widely adopted AI but mostly used it to perform superficial tasks. As it is currently deployed, AI gives analysts a faster way to research companies, populate spreadsheets, and identify the sources of investment returns. Deep adoption, on the other hand, requires changing workflows and job responsibilities. A bank must decide what proprietary data to feed a model, how to protect client information, and how to verify the results of the AI models it uses. Junior employees may spend less time collecting information and more time interpreting it, forming ideas, and advising clients. But many tasks that AI can automate are also forms of training. Firms will therefore need to find ways for experienced employees to help younger colleagues acquire the skills the experienced employees once developed by producing the underlying analyses themselves.

The true economic promise of AI lies in deep and transformational adoption. This requires revamping organizational processes, preparing workers to capitalize on the new technology, and applying AI in ways that enhance productivity and innovation and improve consumer welfare. Innovations that increase output and can generate new products and services have not yet appeared at a meaningful scale. But there is enormous latent demand for many new products and services, including specialized services such as individualized tutoring, health care, and legal and financial assistance, and additional demand will emerge from completely new activities that technology will make possible. By fundamentally changing how work is done and what work can be done, AI can lower the cost of providing tailored products, making new opportunities economically viable. Serving demand that previously did not make economic sense adds new value to society rather than merely redistributing a fixed economic pie.

But enlarging the economic pie does not guarantee a smooth transition for every worker. As with globalization, the new demand that AI unlocks will not necessarily arise in the same jobs, sectors, or communities in which existing work is done. This potential mismatch risks major labor market disruption and political backlash. Public anxiety is already growing, especially among younger workers seeking entry-level positions that are the most likely to be automated by AI. One study by the Stanford economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar, and Ruyu Chen found that the employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in occupations most exposed to AI automation was 19 percent lower than it would have been if employment had grown at the same rate as occupations less exposed to AI disruption. Yet the overall effect of AI on jobs, including entry-level positions, remains uncertain. The Economist has estimated that AI has created around one million jobs in the United States but led to about 200,000 layoffs, and the gap between the unemployment rate among 20-to-24-year-olds and the overall unemployment rate is low compared with recent decades. Most likely, as one of us (Manyika) wrote with Spence in these pages, in 2023, some jobs will be lost, some will be gained, and many more will be permanently changed.

Harnessing AI’s potential to increase prosperity without destabilizing society requires coordination. In addition to AI companies making new models, products, and tools, infrastructure developers must build the physical systems such as data centers that underpin AI. Businesses need to supply data and drive the organizational transformation to deploy models at their full potential—not just for efficiency but also to expand existing products and services or create new ones. Employers must decide how these technologies will be used, how workflows will be transformed, and how to enable workers to be more productive. Workers have to adopt the technology and invest in learning AI-relevant skills. Local communities must provide the land for data centers and consent to the technology’s expansion. And governments need to set rules, fund foundational research, and provide social insurance to support and manage transitions for those whose work or livelihoods are displaced by AI.

Each of these groups bears unique costs, captures different shares of the benefits, answers to its own constituencies, and responds to distinct incentives. This also means that unless they coordinate—whether through market mechanisms or active cooperation—these groups could slow or block AI development. Firms might underinvest in organizational change and use AI only to cut costs rather than to expand revenues, workers might resist or slow down adoption, communities might block infrastructure, and governments might restrict development. Each action might make sense in isolation, but together they lead to a suboptimal outcome for society.

This is a classic cooperative game scenario in game theory: these groups can accomplish much more when they work in concert than when they act individually and without coordination. A coalition with a common goal does not need to be a formal organization; it can be any grouping of actors who operate in ways that allow them to coordinate decisions and contribute resources to a shared project through contracts, policies, standards, investment commitments, or political bargains. Each possible coalition can generate more growth and economic value by promoting the adoption and diffusion of AI than any one actor can by operating separately.

In an ideal world, all these actors would form a single “grand coalition” to capture as much value from AI as possible. Their complementary actions and active coordination to promote AI would kick off a virtuous cycle in which wider adoption would spur advancements that would make AI more useful for society, unlock new demand and productivity growth, and encourage more people, communities, and companies to adopt the technology and support its expansion.

Such a grand coalition, however, is a benchmark rather than a blueprint. Progress is not all or nothing; smaller, more focused coalitions can still create economic and social gains that far outweigh what each actor could achieve alone. What is needed is a stable coalition—one in which no participant or group of participants can do better by leaving and doing something on their own. In the AI economy, exiting the coalition does not mean building a rival AI system. It simply means exercising a veto on increased AI use, which could take the form of imposing a local moratorium on data centers, resisting workplace adoption of AI, or blocking new regulations that encourage AI development. Because the withdrawal of any one participant would destroy more value than that participant could ever capture alone, it is critical that every essential group prefers being inside the coalition to being outside it.

A crucial question, then, is how to share the gains of working together so that cooperation remains stable over time and no coalition member wants to leave. In the case of AI, one factor that can help drive cooperation is that the technology creates conditions for what economists call a “convex game”: because the inputs to AI are complementary, each new participant makes everyone else’s contribution more valuable, causing the gains from joining the coalition to increase at an accelerating rate. Building better computing infrastructure, for instance, makes AI models and tools more capable; more capable models and tools increase the benefits for companies that want to invest in transforming their organizational processes and retraining workers, which increases productivity and spurs innovation; and wider adoption of AI in the workplace drives the need for additional computing power, which creates incentives for more infrastructure providers to build. Once a core group starts working together, the next actor that wants to join has even more to gain.

These characteristics of the AI economy also make it easier to share the gains of growth more fairly. A simple approach is to distribute the benefits based on how much each member contributes to a coalition. Under the framework of the Nobel Prize–winning economist Lloyd Shapley, each participant’s contribution can be measured by the average additional value that possible coalitions create when that participant is included. If a coalition wants to use AI to discover new treatments for chronic or rare diseases, for instance, it needs to include drug developers, AI labs, research hospitals, frontline physicians, patient groups, and the government. AI labs supply specialized models and computing power, research hospitals curate historical clinical data, physicians administer drug trials, and patient groups consent to share biological records (under privacy guardrails set by the government). These contributions are not measured in the same units, and although smaller coalitions can make partial progress, all these groups are needed for the drug discovery pipeline to fully succeed. A Shapley-style arrangement would share the gains based on how much different coalitions benefit from having each member inside them compared with what would result if the member were outside them. As Shapley’s research showed, in a convex game such as AI adoption, compensating each partner in this way produces a stable coalition that incentivizes everyone to join.

Prices, contracts, and incentives are indispensable for building a stable coalition, and market design and competition provide the architecture that enables coordination. But markets alone will not be enough. In the case of AI, the various costs and benefits will not unfold on the same timelines for all participants; some of them will benefit long before others catch up. Public institutions are necessary. Only policymakers, for instance, can create the common rules that will allow frontier labs in the coalition to coordinate on making AI safer, and only policymakers can provide enough support for workers to adapt to the AI economy. The bitter experiences that many workers and communities felt during globalization have made coalition building harder yet even more necessary.

A workable coalition does not need to bring every participant to the same negotiating table. A national bargain can establish common terms and align incentives on issues such as AI safety standards, workforce transition support, and infrastructure permitting, and smaller agreements at workplaces and in local communities can bring needed groups on board to cooperate. At both levels, the principle is the same: each group must receive a credible stake in the upside of the economic gains that the coalition creates through the overall growth of AI.

At the national level, cooperation should focus on the groups whose participation will determine whether AI technology can scale enough to generate massive economic gains. This includes the federal government, leading labs, major infrastructure builders and providers, organized labor, and large firms deploying AI. Big business matters because its use of technology and its investments in the workforce create demand that fuels the entire supply chain. Labor organizations will want to negotiate favorable terms for workforce transition, such as expanded unemployment and wage insurance, as well as for retraining; a lack of participation from organized labor will deprive the coalition of the support needed to facilitate adoption, especially in sectors such as construction in which unions have more power. For critical infrastructure, including power grids, telecommunications networks, and payment systems, the priority needs to be cybersecurity and reliability: before they integrate AI deeply into core operations, infrastructure providers need to believe that more capable models will not expose them to catastrophic risk.

The government can establish a predictable, risk-based regulatory framework to set clear standards in potentially dangerous areas such as biosecurity and to help coordinate the testing of advanced AI systems. A national framework would preempt a fragmented patchwork of idiosyncratic state-by-state bans on low-risk commercial uses of AI. Policymakers can also support AI development by funding research on foundational questions in artificial intelligence and increasing immigration and visa pathways for highly skilled workers to attract top global talent to U.S. labs. AI model and application developers, meanwhile, can participate in safety testing, and companies building out AI capacity can make available more computing power to small businesses, nonprofits, and local governments. Infrastructure providers can construct new data centers and show their support for the communities they work in by implementing ratepayer protections and paying local taxes, and large companies can invest in worker training, organizational transformation, and new business models. Finally, organized labor can supply the skilled employees needed to build the physical infrastructure underlying AI adoption, help redesign organizational workflows within companies, and lend its political weight to promoting AI nationally.

Each group benefits more than it is expected to contribute. AI model and application developers and infrastructure providers gain a predictable development path and a skilled workforce. Large firms have access to the support they need to more deeply adopt AI and improve their productivity. Workers gain new opportunities and job security during the economic transition. Washington also stands to benefit as the economy grows and the United States maintains its global technological leadership.

AI model developers and other companies in the AI ecosystem are already working together to diffuse AI more widely. Leading developers, including Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI, along with partner organizations, have embedded engineers inside large organizations to identify valuable uses of AI, redesign workflows, and integrate new technology systems into production. These efforts recognize that model access is not enough: adoption requires technical assistance, organizational knowledge, and sustained engagement. A coalition can extend this capacity to smaller firms, schools, clinics, and municipalities. For AI model and application developers, supporting diffusion expands the market and provides insurance against potential political backlash. Only if the gains of AI adoption are shared with a broad swath of organizations and the general public will citizens support the expansion of the technology in society.

Frameworks for coalition building with labor groups are also emerging. Rather than working with individual firms to sign bespoke agreements, technology developers, industry associations, and policymakers are supporting worker cooperation through existing unions, apprenticeships, and retraining programs. The International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, for example, is mobilizing its members to oppose data center moratoriums while calling for better labor agreements. Google is working with the IBEW’s partner organizations to train 100,000 electrical workers and 30,000 apprentices, giving workers access to well-paid jobs and providing developers with a source of skilled labor and the political support needed to build.

At the local level, new data centers may impose concentrated costs on communities even when they create national benefits for AI users. Developers should therefore negotiate agreements that protect ratepayers and provide tangible benefits for communities through increased local revenue, better infrastructure, and more employment opportunities. Templates for how to do this are taking shape in many U.S. states. Leading AI companies and “hyperscalers,” including Amazon, Google, and Microsoft, have signed on to the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, a voluntary commitment to pay the full cost of new power generation and grid upgrades in areas where they are building data centers so that household electricity bills do not rise. These companies are also funding local workforce programs and water-restoration projects and contributing property tax revenue that will allow communities to pay for new schools and roads.

The economic benefits that AI brings will increase the stability of emerging coalitions as long as they are shared. The distribution of benefits does not need to be equal, but for groups providing complementary inputs, benefits must be apportioned according to what each group contributes and also be adjusted as the technology evolves. In practice, determining each group’s contribution is not an exact science but an ongoing structured negotiation: tools such as utility tariffs, labor agreements, and enabling policies can allocate benefits in ways that reflect the role of different groups in supporting AI expansion and adoption. The share of the upside that each coalition member receives must be large enough to make participation more attractive than obstruction. Giving key players a real stake in the economic surplus that is generated is how those who might have been potential impediments to AI become participants in, and perhaps even advocates for, the expanded use of this new technology.

Where AI substitutes for existing tasks rather than complementing them, policymakers can also learn from what went wrong with globalization. Public programs to help displaced workers can be effective, but they must be accessible enough to provide support when technological disruption occurs. Targeted parts of the federal government’s Trade Adjustment Assistance program to compensate and retrain workers affected by globalization succeeded where they were put in place—research by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York found that eligibility for wage insurance under the program increased reemployment, shortened the amount of time out of work, and raised cumulative earnings—but it reached only a small portion of workers. Other research found that most support was provided through disability benefits that required workers to permanently exit the labor force. With AI, policymakers will need to set up expanded wage and unemployment insurance programs and support employer-led retraining programs that help workers transition into in-demand roles before displacement occurs rather than improvise after workers begin to suffer.

A coalition does not need to be built all at once. Cooperation should start in areas in which complementary benefits are clearest and participation constraints are easiest to identify, including promoting AI adoption and worker training in particular sectors such as health care or skilled trades, and encouraging support for new data centers. At the national level, safety cooperation can grow out of partnerships between coalitions and the government. Because AI’s development trajectory remains uncertain, cooperation agreements should center on investments that could pay out across multiple plausible scenarios and can be revised as the trajectory of AI becomes clearer.

International cooperation can grow in the same way. Although AI may well eventually require cooperation among adversaries and allies alike, Washington can start by forming a U.S.-led coalition built around trusted partners willing to accept common safety and security standards. The United States could, for example, set up a coalition among trusted partners that agrees on principles and frameworks in exchange for access to technology and cooperation. This coalition will naturally widen as its terms prove workable and more countries see the benefits of participating. It could eventually include China, which, given its advanced AI capabilities, is critical to addressing AI safety issues; this will require navigating complex questions of alignment and verification, however, including how to set up trusted validation bodies and mechanisms.

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision provides an instructive example of international cooperation. It began in 1974 as a small forum among national regulators in 12 advanced industrialized countries and over time developed common standards for how to supervise banks around the world without empowering an imposing supranational authority, expanding its scope as those standards proved valuable. The Basel Committee was the seed for what is now the dominant international architecture that identifies and minimizes the risks of financial instability, including setting standards for how much capital banks need to hold and how to classify the riskiness of securities held on banks’ balance sheets. A future coalition for AI standards could grow through the same combination of clear entry conditions and credible commitments that have made the Basel Committee and the International Organization for Standardization, which publishes voluntary technical standards, successful.

Harnessing the potential of AI will not be easy. Critics will immediately jump to the conclusion that there are many reasons why such coalitions will not work in practice. Labs might decide that they can earn more on their own, incumbent firms might try to convert cooperation into tools to protect their market share, and governments might promise support for workers and communities and fail to deliver, as was the case with globalization.

But there is reason to be cautiously optimistic about cooperation in the age of AI. Because the technology is still relatively early in its development, it is possible to identify the groups needed to initiate cooperation, and labs, governments, communities, employers, and labor organizations have already shown interest in different parts of the bargain. Ultimately, society does not need to choose between innovation and shared prosperity. The two can be complements: cooperation will drive growth, and sharing the gains of such growth will sustain the cooperation needed to keep it going.

Add in the advanced cyber capabilities of the latest artificial intelligence models, their ability to generate bespoke information on command, and a highly polarized electorate, and the upcoming U.S. midterm elections begin to look like a potential minefield of misinformation.

But the overarching message from more than half a dozen experts whom Foreign Policy spoke to, including current and former tech executives and government officials, is that it’s not time to press the panic button just yet.

“There are attempts to use AI to deceive, certainly, but they haven’t been particularly effective,” said Matt Masterson, a senior director for elections and societal resilience at Microsoft. “I haven’t seen anything that’s really taken hold in this election cycle or jumped out at me as really having an impact in that way.”

That’s not to say that U.S. adversaries couldn’t be—or aren’t already—using AI tools to make their disinformation campaigns more effective. Large language models such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Anthropic’s Claude, and Google’s Gemini (to name only a few) give bad actors the ability to operate at a speed and scale that they previously couldn’t.

“It’s helped them operationalize their work and almost industrialize it—it means that they can spin up content more quickly. They can also do a lot of research more effectively and quickly in terms of their target audience,” said Olga Belogolova, the director of the emerging technologies initiative at Johns Hopkins University’s School of Advanced International Studies.

Investigators have uncovered past Russian and Iranian campaigns because of mistranslations into English or a lack of knowledge of local slang. But AI allows those actors to sound more authentic on social media.

“A lot of what we’re seeing on the back end is changing because it’s helping them draft and polish content, translate, and localize things, which are important changes. I’m not trying to dismiss them,” Belogolova said. “But that doesn’t mean that they’re necessarily going to be any better at persuading and reaching people,” she added. That’s in part because the social and even traditional media channels that they rely on to distribute the content remain the same. “A lot of what you see on the receiving end of information consumption hasn’t changed dramatically.”

Midterm elections in the United States also pose a fundamentally different challenge from an interference standpoint than U.S. presidential elections, such as the one in 2024.

“Midterms are not as easy to target or as popular to target by foreign adversaries. Our federal system, with 400-plus congressional races and 30-something Senate races, makes it hard to target effectively,” said Jacob Breach, a senior advisor at the Foundation for Defense of Democracies think tank who previously spent 15 years in the U.S. intelligence community focused largely on foreign influence operations. “They’re testing grounds for actors’ unique tactics and unique combinations of tactics to attempt to interfere or influence these elections, but it’s not like it would be in a presidential election year.”

Iran and Russia are also engaged in protracted wars that could reduce their wherewithal to target the midterms. AI can help ease that load, but only so much.

“These actors have limited capabilities and bandwidth. When those actors are distracted by other things, like the wars in this case, they have less bandwidth to devote to sustained campaigns,” Breach said.

Multiple experts told Foreign Policy that companies, election officials, and voters are simply better prepared this time around to combat misinformation—including by using AI as a solution on the defensive side.

“Back in 2024, it was a rare election official who was really thinking about AI for good—I think most of us were laser focused on deepfakes and the concerns associated with that,” said Bill Gates, who served as a supervisor for Arizona’s Maricopa County during five successive elections until 2024 and is no stranger to election integrity efforts.

Gates (no relation to the billionaire Microsoft founder) now heads Arizona State University’s Mechanics of Democracy Lab, which has launched an initiative sponsored by Microsoft to train election officials in AI applications and harness AI to provide voters with authoritative information.

“The sort of burying-the-head-in-the-sand ostrich approach that maybe a fair number of us were doing in 2024, you’re seeing a lot less of that this time around,” Gates said.

The ASU partnership is one of several that major tech companies have entered into to ensure that their models can serve reliable information. OpenAI has partnered with The Associated Press to supply its vote count data to ChatGPT users who ask about it, while OpenAI, Anthropic, and Microsoft have all partnered with an organization called Democracy Works, which aims to provide tech companies with reliable information about polling place locations and other frequent queries by prospective voters.

Combating adversaries requires “adding friction in strategic areas” that make it harder for them to exploit the chatbots. “Bad actors have a lot of options whenever they choose to fly. In an encouraging sign, a study published by the Brennan Center for Justice found in August that most of the popular chatbots consistently pushed back against common election conspiracy theories.

The pace at which AI is evolving and the so-called “black box” nature of the technology mean that unforeseen consequences and threats are always a possibility.

“The hardest thing is that we don’t know what we don’t know,” said Katie Harbath, author of the new book Disrupting Politics: A Front Row Seat to the Collision of Technology and Democracy, which is about her decade leading Facebook’s election-focused public policy team until 2021. “You can do as much as possible to try to be ready for any of these potential things, but what if something happens that we weren’t prepared for?” she asked. “The thing that I’m most happy about—I don’t know if that’s the right word—is the fact that there are a lot of people thinking about this.”

That also extends to the platform users and voters themselves, for whom AI-generated content has become a part of life. That has led to a natural skepticism that many experts say is somewhat healthy—provided that it doesn’t tip over into distrusting even authoritative information.

“I do think that the prevalence of AI-manufactured and -created information is causing people to at least look twice at what they’re seeing,” Breach said.

“Societally, it’s not great. I would love to be able to trust what I saw on the internet,” he added. But “if AI is causing Americans to get better at media literacy simply because they trust what they’re seeing less, that’s a good thing.”

#5217. The Chokepoint Trap

How the Search for Economic Security Will End in Geopolitical Calamity

Henry Farrell, Abraham Newman, and Yeling Tan

Policymakers used to ignore chokepoints. Now they have trouble seeing anything else. U.S. President Donald Trump’s administration warns allies against dependence on China while it hunts for ways to exploit those allies’ own dependence on the United States—on its market, technologies, financial system, and security umbrella. Beijing, meanwhile, accuses Washington of economic coercion while leveraging its dominance over critical supply chains to press the United States and Europe. Europe hopes to build the euro into an alternative global currency to evade the dollar’s iron grip. Smaller players, too, have gotten into the act, as Iran tries to leverage its control over the Strait of Hormuz to put a stranglehold on the global oil trade.

This obsession with wielding and countering chokepoint power may seem sudden, but it has evolved over the last quarter century as a byproduct of globalization itself. As economies integrated, chokepoints proliferated: manufacturers extended supply chains across borders, banks routed transactions through the U.S. dollar system to minimize costs and delays, digital services took advantage of economies of scale, leading the market to become dominated by a handful of platforms. After the September 11, 2001, attacks, the United States combined its advocacy for free markets with an increasingly ambitious effort to exploit its dominant position in these networks, surveilling global communications and using its influence over banks worldwide to cut off financial flows to its adversaries. But even as it did so, most politicians and business leaders continued to focus on globalization’s economic efficiencies.

Moves by Trump in his first term, however, caused world leaders to take more notice of chokepoints. The COVID-19 pandemic heightened countries’ fears of external dependence. Then, during Trump’s second administration, China learned that the best way to stop him is to squeeze a chokepoint. The United States’ hubristic erosion of basic norms of constraint led other countries, such as Iran, to explore options they had never previously dared to exploit.

Compared with an excessively rosy vision of globalization that emphasized the efficiencies of interdependence, it may now seem savvy to view the world economy as a system of chokepoints that countries can either turn to their advantage or see turned against them. But the chokepoint view has its own deadly blind spot. It is leading policymakers to focus on capabilities rather than strategy—to fixate on what leverage they have, and what leverage others have over them, rather than considering what happens when many countries build up their chokepoint power all at once.

The result is a familiar but dangerous dynamic: the security dilemma, in which one country’s efforts to secure itself make others feel threatened and respond in kind, leaving everyone less safe. The more that countries strengthen their control over chokepoints or protect themselves from coercion, the more they can make rivals feel that their own economic security has been undermined and provoke retaliation. As economic scholars such as David Bulman and Matt Ferchen have pointed out, each country’s individual actions contribute to the construction of a collective trap. The United States’ efforts to hamstring Chinese technology firms, for instance, led China to accelerate its technological development, which in turn spurred American fears of Chinese domination—a self-perpetuating spiral. Each side’s attempt to become safer makes others feel less secure, driving further escalation.

Escaping the chokepoint trap will be difficult precisely because it is collective. Any government that exercises restraint risks becoming a victim. And although the chokepoint trap may resemble the security dilemma, during the Cold War the measures that ultimately contained a runaway cycle of military provocations were implemented only after the deadly threat of the Cuban missile crisis focused leaders’ attention. An obsessive focus on chokepoints might lead to collective disaster, but it will be remarkably hard for states to jump off the escalator and balance making credible threats with offering credible assurances and seeking collective understandings. The most likely outcome is a fragmented world in which governments and private actors alike face increased volatility and the risk of escalation into something much worse: economic shocks, cascading disruptions, and even war.

In the traditional security dilemma, one country’s defensive move can appear offensive to another. A state may seek peaceful relations with a neighbor but worry that a belligerent leader could someday come to power next door. To hedge against that small but real risk, it might invest in a new weapons system or modestly increase the size of its army. Its neighbor could reasonably become alarmed. What if the buildup is preparation for an invasion? It may respond by ramping up its own defense spending, leading the first country to grow more suspicious and build up its military further, setting off an escalatory spiral.

Sometimes, of course, governments do have genuinely belligerent intentions. But even if all countries genuinely want peace, they can end up violently distrusting each other and seeking to field massive, expensive armies. In such a mistrustful atmosphere, innocent mistakes can easily be misinterpreted. In a classic 1978 essay about the security dilemma, the political scientist Robert Jervis argued that countries can mitigate the risk of misinterpretation by ensuring that the acts they undertake to enhance their security are obviously defensive rather than offensive—for example, by investing in fortifications deep inside their own territories instead of deploying troops to forward bases. But even passive defenses can heighten tensions: if a country feels better protected against retaliation, its rivals may fear that it will find aggression less risky.

In later writing, Jervis suggested that similar ambiguities might undermine economic and technological security, too. But economic warfare is even likelier to generate unintended escalation than the traditional security dilemma. Unlike military investments, economic policies can be difficult to classify as offensive or defensive. When China builds up its semiconductor sector, is it merely protecting its domestic economy or augmenting its capacity to coerce? Industrial policy can be enacted for a purely defensive reason—to establish resilience against geopolitical shocks. But if such a policy succeeds in building economies of scale, other states might reasonably fear that industrial dominance might be converted into coercive tools.

Economic security officials are usually more interested in building capacity than in restraint, whereas traditional economic policymakers are more accustomed to soothing their own markets than to assuaging the concerns of competitors, let alone adversaries. Defense bureaucracies have strong command-and-control structures for a reason: ill-disciplined communications and misaligned decisions can have disastrous consequences. The institutions that make economic policy, by contrast—including central banks and departments of commerce—are usually not set up to speak with one voice. Many are weakly coordinated, and significant authority is delegated to local-level investment and trade agencies. Sometimes, as with central banks, their autonomy is deliberately protected. Whereas military units deployed on discrete missions cannot act without explicit authorization, most economic agencies enjoy wide freedom to act without being accused of going rogue. As this year’s disagreements between the U.S. Department of the Treasury and the Federal Reserve over bond yields illustrate, standard bureaucratic infighting can shade into visible conflict.

When governments pursue chokepoint warfare, the problems these institutional ambiguities create get much worse. In September 2025, on the heels of a series of high-level economic negotiations between top U.S. and Chinese officials aimed at stabilizing the bilateral relationship, the U.S. Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security enacted the so-called affiliates rule, which expanded export controls to cover more subsidiaries owned by entities on two U.S. government watch lists. The bureau publicly described the measure as an attempt to close a loophole and lighten administrative burdens; it has since suspended the rule. But the timing made outsiders unsure how to discern its broader strategic meaning. Was Commerce merely tightening the enforcement of existing export controls as part of an ongoing bureaucratic process, or was the United States, as Chinese officials suggested, deliberately engaging in “maximum pressure” tactics in between rounds of bilateral talks?

It can be even harder to interpret what autocracies intend, since policymakers in such regimes are more insulated from public scrutiny. For example, even after China promised to relax its restrictions on the exports of rare-earth minerals and magnets in July 2025, companies that applied for export licenses experienced unexplained delays. It was unclear whether China was deliberately signaling its ability to control critical supply chains or simply struggling to channel requests efficiently through a newly created export-control bureaucracy.

Governments engaging in economic warfare often intentionally write or enforce regulations ambiguously to prompt private actors to overcomply. This behavior makes others much more likely to interpret even hapless bureaucratic mistakes, delays, and unintentional overreach as malign and deliberate plotting. Such mistakes deepen mistrust among great powers, which are already inherently inclined to overrate the possibility of deliberate threats.

Conversely, leaders are often excessively optimistic about their ability to surgically inflict economic pain on target countries. The flow of commerce cannot be steered as carefully as drones can be guided to bomb weapons factories. Even precision measures, such as putting specific firms on export-control lists, can be ineffective. Controlled goods may end up in the possession of subsidiaries or clandestine intermediaries. Broad attempts to close loopholes can go too far, putting thousands of firms with complex ownership ties in the cross hairs of the economic security state.

In October 2025, China again expanded the reach of its rare-earth export controls to retaliate against the affiliates rule and further efforts by Washington to restrict Chinese firms’ access to U.S. technologies. If these measures had not been suspended the following month, they would have affected production chains throughout Europe and Asia as well as in the United States. Officials who dream of an optimized form of chokepoint warfare in which they can fire an economic sniper rifle at judiciously selected targets regularly discover they are wielding a blunderbuss whose pellets fly in all directions—and the collateral damage only increases the risk of retaliatory spirals.

The more some countries move to control key chokepoints, the more others will worry about how those chokepoints may be weaponized and seek to leverage their own. The result is a world bristling with chokepoints and defenses in which every power still feels completely insecure. Even the most ordinary kinds of mutual dependence can begin to look like existential security risks. Put another way, in a world in which everything is connected, nearly everything could be considered a chokepoint. A piece of legislation introduced in the U.S. Senate in 2025 and the House in 2026, for instance, identifies even smart ovens and clothes dryers as potential security threats.

Historically effective ways of limiting trade disputes may fail in chokepoint warfare. In traditional tariff wars, governments often try to match like with like, responding to a butter tariff with a countertariff on eggs. Maintaining proportionality is much harder when economic security risks are diverse, when different chokepoints confer different kinds of leverage, and when countries that want to retaliate against the weaponization of one network have to turn to different networks to respond.

In April 2025, when the United States threatened China with sweeping tariffs and boosted its export controls on semiconductors, China could not retaliate by imposing an equivalent restriction on American semiconductors because it does not control the same chokepoint. Instead, it used its advantage over a different bottleneck, rare earths. But when countries weaponize entirely different chokepoints that sit at the center of complex and global supply chains, there is no easy way to judge whether a reaction will match the economic magnitude of the original act. Any notion of a simple escalation ladder that adversaries can climb up and down becomes a fantasy.

Rapid and unpredictable technological change makes this dynamic even more dangerous. As Jervis warned, countries persistently worry that technological innovation will create enduring advantages for their adversaries. Such anxieties can worsen the chokepoint trap, inspiring countries to try to impede their rivals’ progress and race to beef up their own autonomy. The Biden administration, anticipating that AI might swiftly accelerate advances in a variety of military and civilian domains, sought to impede Chinese AI development by restricting the country’s access to specialized semiconductors. Administration officials stressed that their goals were limited, describing their approach to China as “small yard, high fence.” Unsurprisingly, Beijing rejected this benign framing and saw the moves as a threat to its long-term future. China accelerated its own semiconductor development and is encouraging its companies to wean themselves from U.S. products, leaving the United States with less influence over Chinese technological development and more reason to worry about future Chinese aggression.

After the attacks of September 11, 2001, the United States began to make much more systematic use of economic and technological chokepoints: snooping on both adversaries and allies, and exploiting the dollar’s dominance to investigate and impose sanctions on foreign banks and companies. American officials came to see the global economy’s interconnectedness as something to manipulate—to deter U.S. opponents or strongarm allies into compliance. The George W. Bush and Obama administrations expanded sanctions to squeeze Iran’s economy, forcing Tehran to the negotiating table. The first Trump administration introduced new technological controls to limit Chinese companies’ access to advanced semiconductors, setting the precedent for the Biden administration’s efforts to impede Chinese AI more broadly.

But during this period, American officials usually paid little attention to the cumulative downside. Financial sanctions, for example, were attractive because most of the costs fell on the foreigners they targeted and foreigners who wanted to do deals with the targets. Exploiting chokepoints became a self-perpetuating enterprise with little purpose beyond sustaining itself. A 2024 Washington Post analysis found that the United States was sanctioning people, properties, or organizations in a third of all countries. Bill Reinsch, a former Commerce Department official, told the newspaper that such sanctions had become “the most important foreign policy tool in the U.S. arsenal,” but “nobody in government is sure this whole strategy is even working.”

Yet the more the United States exploited chokepoints in this way, the more it provoked countermeasures and imitation. And as other powers have begun to develop their own chokepoint capabilities, they are proving no more cautious. China has never hesitated to use economic pressure to get its way. But it used to threaten countries’ access to its domestic market rather than control global bottlenecks. Two key events altered this playbook. In 2013, leaks made by the former U.S. National Security Agency contractor Edward Snowden revealed the extraordinary reach the United States had in global information networks. Then, the first Trump administration’s punishing campaigns against the Chinese telecommunications companies ZTE and Huawei demonstrated Washington’s willingness to coerce.

After these shocks, China’s technological debates began to emphasize themes such as “information security” and “decoupling.” Chinese leader Xi Jinping, whose speeches once focused on straightforward industrialization, increasingly stressed how economic development could not be separated from national security. His “dual circulation” strategy both sought to reduce dependence on overseas markets and reduce China’s exposure to foreign economic pressure. As Beijing became more conscious of its vulnerability to U.S. technology and finance, it not only accelerated technological self-reliance but created new government structures that mimicked those that Washington used to target other countries, such as an export control regime and security screening for inbound and outbound investment.

China justifies these measures as self-defense. But it has rapidly moved from proportionate responses to dramatic counterescalation. When, in April 2025, Trump announced his “Liberation Day” tariffs, China not only imposed licensing restrictions on rare-earth exports but also suggested, a few months later, that its dominance over rare-earth processing gave it regulatory authority over the entire semiconductor supply chain. China also barred some exports of dual-use goods to U.S. defense contractors and civilian technology companies, sanctioned U.S. firms it claimed were engaging in arms sales or technology cooperation with Taiwan’s military, and launched antitrust investigations into Google, Nvidia, and DuPont. Although some of these measures were subsequently suspended or terminated as a result of negotiations with the United States, China has since repeatedly demonstrated its ability to hit back against what it perceives as foreign economic coercion.

In another counteroffensive, Beijing has begun creating a “blocking” regime to punish companies that comply with other countries’ economic security restrictions. Foreign banks that follow U.S. law and stop doing business with a sanctioned Chinese refinery could well be sued in Chinese courts in the future. Rules promulgated in August of this year make it illegal for Chinese firms to provide information or do business with six organizations that do due diligence and risk assessment on supply chains and promote labor rights, making it harder for them to generate the forensics that help companies worldwide comply with American laws.

By now, China has reshaped its economic policy around a narrow view of its national security concerns. And just like the United States’ chokepoint exploitation did, China’s moves to fence off ever larger parts of its economy will prompt destabilizing counterreactions and imitations. An examination of data from the Global Trade Alert, an independent monitor that tracks government policy changes affecting international trade flows, captures governments’ growing willingness to choke off trade. Since 2020, countries have, on average, enacted seven times as many export restrictions per year as they did between 2010 and 2019. The chokepoint trap is closing on governments as they find themselves entangled in an ever more complicated web of economic and technological conflicts.

In this dynamic, Europe—whose economy is nominally larger than China’s—finds itself perpetually forced to accommodate. Its ability to formulate coherent economic-security strategies and design policy tools is better developed than those of Beijing and Washington. Over the past few years, it has delivered what could be a powerful anti-coercion instrument that would allow it to retaliate extensively against economic pressure, an economic security strategy that identifies the risks of chokepoint warfare, and a Chips Act 2.0 that calls for maintaining Europe’s position as an “indispensable player” in supply chains. But it has struggled to turn words into leverage. It remains deeply dependent on U.S. technology and security guarantees; its dreams of controlling its own chokepoints dissipate in the face of the risk that Washington could tear up its NATO commitments. So far, Europe has not even attempted to deploy its anti-coercion instrument against the United States, despite the Trump administration’s repeated coercion.

Europe is increasingly constrained by dependence on China, too. When the EU imposed countervailing duties on Chinese electric vehicles it found were unfairly subsidized, China was quick to target vital sectors in EU member states, forcing European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen to spend precious political capital keeping Berlin and Madrid behind a common EU policy. In September 2025, when the Dutch government attempted to assert its sovereign authority over Nexperia, a Dutch chip manufacturer that was purchased by a Chinese company, Beijing prevented the firm from finishing the chips in China, creating a supply chain crisis across the European car industry. According to a mid-2026 report by Tobias Gehrke, a senior policy analyst at the European Council on Foreign Relations, Chinese threats are preventing European member states from even contemplating the comprehensive actions they would need to reduce their dependence.

Every European wish to assert strategic autonomy is hemmed in by the expectation that the United States or China will split its solidarity. And national security decisions remain the domain of member states, preventing the EU from leveraging potential chokepoints. Europe has made huge strides in acting collectively to impose sanctions against Russia, but enforcement still largely depends on national governments. The result is a bloc that understands the dangers of escalation better than the United States or China but cannot do much to avoid them.

Japan, meanwhile, began to think about the threat posed by Chinese coercion early and has better tailored its approach, balancing strategic autonomy and resilience against potential external coercion with control over vital technological chokepoints. It has created a national stockpile of rare earths to mitigate Chinese efforts to pressure its economy while making itself indispensable to U.S. military supply chains. But even Japan has shown it can succumb to the seduction of chokepoint politics: in 2019, it limited South Korea’s access to key semiconductor chemicals in response to the country’s pursuit of reparations for World War II harms.

And now, even smaller, poorer countries have been caught up in the spiral. Some have benefited from the spread of chokepoint warfare in the short term: the Philippines, for instance, won millions in grants from the United States when Washington attempted to press allies to block Huawei from their telecommunications infrastructures. Others are simply getting squeezed. The Kachin Independence Army in Myanmar is selling dysprosium and terbium, which are used in industrial magnets, to fuel its civil war against the military junta—giving China, the main buyer, greater leverage over the conflict. Greenland, with its vast supply of natural resources, has faced repeated military threats from Trump. As time goes on, the costs are likely to heavily outweigh the benefits, even for countries receiving some side payments. Governments in smaller, poorer countries want the autonomy to chart a path toward greater prosperity for their citizens. The global obsession with economic security makes finding that path harder.

Right now, the United States and China are locked in a short-term stasis. Washington is moderating its use of chokepoints out of fear of ferocious blowback, and China sees little benefit in direct provocation. But this moment of stability is likely to be fleeting. Even after Trump leaves the White House, the United States will certainly keep pressing for global technological dominance, and it is likely to continue its efforts to dominate energy. China will keep pursuing policies aimed at quietly building dependencies and undermining the United States’ coercive power. Stalemates are fragile in a world in which the incentives inexorably point toward escalation.

The powers with the most latitude to shape the world now see chokepoints as capabilities. Their vision rarely stretches to the strategic implications of their actions. As they build their offensive capacities and shore up their defenses, they convince themselves that they are ensuring their own security. Instead, they are miring themselves in a chokepoint arms race that will render them, as well as everyone else, much less secure. The jealousies of the traditional national security dilemma are now spilling over into the global economy. A world in which economic chokepoints become forts, garrisons, and guns all at once will be characterized by perpetual low-level economic conflict and the ever-present risk of larger conflagrations.

To identify broader solutions, some scholars and policy thinkers look to the Cold War for inspiration, emphasizing the value of de-escalation and mutual restraint. During that era, the risk of nuclear war was mitigated with regular meetings to establish a shared understanding of redlines and systems that ensure effective communication in a crisis. Over time, these gave rise to more formal arrangements, such as treaties regulating arms and troop movements, enforced by satellites and other means of monitoring. An assurance-based security logic can, in some cases, insulate economic chokepoints from weaponization. Singapore, for instance, has signed agreements with Australia and New Zealand to keep essential supplies flowing between their countries. In 2022, the World Trade Organization’s members agreed not to place export restrictions on food purchased by the World Food Program for humanitarian purposes.

Yet it will be difficult to turn such commitments into a large-scale system of effective communication and mutual restraint. It took the Cuban missile crisis and the threat of nuclear annihilation to awaken the Cold War’s antagonists to how individually rational acts could lead to collectively awful outcomes. Today, as the cycle of mutual suspicion gathers speed, it becomes increasingly hard to reverse. The more governments create bureaucracies devoted to identifying and controlling economic chokepoints, the more a chokepoint logic becomes the default lens through which they make even routine economic policy. It is no coincidence that Europe, the great power that is the most hesitant to actually weaponize chokepoints, is also the power that is least capable of deploying them.

It is more likely that the chokepoint trap will tighten. Military competition has long driven states to build similar institutions—armed forces, defense bureaucracies, and domestic weapons industries. This kind of mirroring is already beginning to happen in economic security. China is becoming more like the United States, building bureaucratic tools to leverage chokepoints against its adversaries; meanwhile, the United States is imitating China, intervening in its domestic economy to reshape it around security concerns. The European Union will choose between managing its slide to irrelevance or radically reforming its internal balance of power in order to keep up. Smaller powers will do what they can to emulate bigger ones while relying on their protection.

This will be hard for governments, but much worse for international business. Firms will have to navigate a labyrinthine and unpredictable global economy in which payment and information systems are fragmented and clashing powers try alternately to press them into service or squeeze them out of existence. Multinational corporations may become unified entities in name only, as their subsidiaries face countervailing pressures from host governments. Economic efficiencies that seem natural today, such as the seamlessness of international financial transactions, may evaporate as governments equate global infrastructures with national insecurity.

Even this is not the worst-case scenario. The greatest risk posed by the chokepoint trap is not its predictable miseries but its unpredictable disasters. Suspicion, misunderstanding, and intense competition in the realm of economic security can create an ideal recipe for war. Take just one hypothetical. It is not difficult to imagine a scenario in which additional Chinese restrictions on rare-earth magnets degrade, among other things, the United States’ ability to rebuild the missile stockpiles that this year’s Iran war has depleted. Whether or not Beijing intended this effect, U.S. leaders could easily interpret such an act as evidence that China plans to launch a military offensive against Taiwan before the United States can achieve full independence from Chinese supply chains, prompting a sense of urgency in Washington to strike first.

There is plenty of historical precedent for what may seem to be a marginal and worst-case scenario. In The Economic Weapon, the historian Nicholas Mulder documents how, in the 1930s, Axis powers’ fears of League of Nations sanctions and potential economic isolation led them to aggressively seize territory and resources before it was too late. The chokepoint trap is bad enough. What it leads to can be worse.

At the time of writing, prediction markets favor Sen. Flávio Bolsonaro to win Brazil’s Oct. 25 presidential runoff. But only a few months ago, his candidacy appeared to be coming off the rails. In mid-May, the far-right senator became embroiled in a major corruption scandal following the publication of leaked messages that showed he had asked for roughly $27 million from a now-jailed former banker at the center of the biggest banking fraud in Brazilian history.

The funds—around $12 million of which were transferred to a Texas account linked to the candidate’s brother Eduardo—were purportedly to help finance a hagiographic biopic about their father, former President Jair Bolsonaro, who remains under house arrest for plotting a coup to overturn Brazil’s 2022 election. Soon after the scandal broke, Flávio’s poll numbers began to plummet. He urgently needed to change the subject.

The following week, U.S. President Donald Trump and Secretary of State Marco Rubio came to the rescue. On May 26, Trump received Flávio in the Oval Office for an unannounced photo-op. He told reporters that he had asked Trump to label Brazil’s biggest gangs as foreign terrorist organizations (FTOs). Rubio granted the designation two days later. Flávio was able to project political strength and shift the conversation from corruption to insecurity; his candidacy was once again secure.

The FTO classifications are yet another example of the Trump administration interfering in Brazilian politics, coming on top of politically motivated tariffs on Brazilian exports (which Eduardo lobbied for) and sanctions on the Supreme Court justice who prosecuted Jair Bolsonaro. They are not dissimilar to the administration’s aggressive efforts to influence recent elections in Argentina, Colombia, and Honduras.

Beyond their symbolic impact in an election year, it’s worth considering what the U.S. FTO designations could mean for Brazil’s sovereignty and strategic autonomy—especially if Flávio wins on Sunday. Brazilians have reason to fear that their country could become engulfed in Trump’s expanding war on what he calls “narco-terrorism.”

Waging war on regional drug trafficking organizations was a prominent campaign theme for Trump. In January 2023, he announced that he would “take down the cartels just as we took down ISIS,” ostensibly to combat the fentanyl epidemic in the United States.

Within weeks of his 2025 inauguration, the president began to make good on his promises, designating several Latin American criminal organizations as FTOs—including the Sinaloa and Jalisco New Generation cartels and gangs such as MS-13 and Tren de Aragua. It was the first time that any U.S. administration had applied this classification to drug-trafficking organizations with no apparent political agenda.

In August of that year, Rubio declared that cartels are “armed terrorist organizations, not simply drug dealing organizations,” placing them in the same national-security framework as al-Qaeda, Boko Haram, and the Islamic State.

Starting in September 2025, this doctrinal shift servedas justification for a series of unprecedented—and seemingly unlimited—lethal military actions in the Caribbean, Central America, and eastern Pacific. In a campaign later dubbed “Operation Southern Spear,” U.S. military vessels have struck dozens of boats allegedly controlled by drug-trafficking FTOs, killing their occupants (239 people, at last count) without arrest or trial and without presenting evidence that they were carrying drugs. The identities of most of those killed remain unknown—even to the U.S. military.

The strikes have done nothing to address the fentanyl trade, since the deadly drug overwhelmingly comes to the United States by land, from Mexico. Nor have they significantly disrupted the cocaine trade, with recent reports—including an assessment by the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration—indicating that traffickers have adapted their routes.

But the policy has achieved something else: FTO designations transformed a law enforcement issue into a military one, paving the way for an expansion of U.S. military activity across the Western Hemisphere.

The most dramatic expression of this shift so far was the Jan. 3 U.S. military attack on Venezuela and abduction of the President Nicolás Maduro to face drug trafficking charges in the United States. The attack was the first of its kind in South America and killed an estimated 75 people. The State Department linked it to a recent FTO designation, despite providing no real evidence that Venezuela’s government played a leadership role in the hemispheric drug trade.

The unauthorized U.S. military campaign across the Americas has generated concern in the U.S. Congress, which has the sole authority to send the military into hostilities. It should also concern Brazil. The designation of Brazilian criminal organizations as FTOs transforms them, at Washington’s discretion, from targets of Brazilian law enforcement into potential targets of U.S. military force.

Under incumbent left-wing President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, Brazil would most certainly continue to resist the pressure of such a designation on the country’s jurisdiction and sovereignty. But under Flávio, U.S. military operations on Brazilian soil are a real possibility. In response to an October 2025 social media post from U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth about a lethal strike on a boat in the eastern Pacific, the far-right candidate said “how envious” he was and invited Hegseth “to spend a few months here helping us fight these terrorist organizations.”

Allowing for U.S. military intervention in Latin America is also one of the objectives of the Shield of the Americas, a new U.S.-driven alliance in which member governments—most of them right-wing allies of Trump—appear to be playing a subordinate role to Trump’s expanding regional military campaign.

In a September meeting of the alliance in New York, participating governments endorsed a sweeping declaration drafted by the Trump administration, reportedly with minimal input from other members. The declaration calls for invoking the 1947 Rio Treaty, a Cold War-era hemispheric security treaty that mirrors NATO’s Article 5 mutual defense clause. It would facilitate collective action against nonstate criminal organizations, potentially opening the door to foreign intervention in those countries.

The governments also committed to individual and collective action against the 24 criminal organizations that the U.S. State Department classified as FTOs, including the two Brazilian gangs. “If you can’t [deal with the criminal organizations], we’ll do it for you,” Trump said.

During his May 26 meeting with Trump, Flávio reportedly promised that, if elected, Brazil would join the Shield of the Americas. He later repeated the pledge on at least two occasions.

For Brazil, the stakes could hardly be higher. Unlike many of its Latin American neighbors, Brazil has long aspired to strategic autonomy, building industrial, military, and technological capabilities to defend its sovereignty without relying on a great-power patron. That ambition has produced an increasingly sophisticated defense industry. It has also shaped Brazil’s approach to the Amazon and the South Atlantic, where its armed forces have traditionally regarded control of territory, borders, and strategic resources as a national security issue.

Ecuador offers the most obvious and disturbing preview of what Washington’s war on narco-terrorism could mean for Brazil. Under President Daniel Noboa, the country has embraced an increasingly militarized security strategy, underwritten by deeper U.S. support, largely without stemming the ever-growing violence in the country. Ecuadorian forces have been implicated in allegations of enforced disappearances, extrajudicial killings, torture, and arbitrary detention. Human Rights Watch even documented torture during a U.S.-Ecuadorian joint operation that culminated in the bombing of a dairy farm.

At sea, U.S. forces have attacked, intercepted, and destroyed Ecuadorian fishing vessels. One boat, the Fiorella, disappeared along with eight crew members in January. The circumstantial evidence strongly suggests that U.S. forces were involved, although the U.S. military has denied it. The Washington Post subsequently reported that mysterious attacks on Ecuadorian vessels formed part of a covert CIA program. In August and September alone, U.S. forces intercepted and destroyed eight more Ecuadorian fishing vessels, detaining more than 140 crew members and drawing allegations of abuse. An Ecuadorian judge recently acquitted 32 of these crew members, stating that there was no evidence that they had committed illegal acts.

Meanwhile, Noboa is opening Ecuador ever further to U.S. military deployments. Last year, the State Department, at Noboa’s request, designated two Ecuadorian gangs as FTOs. In March, U.S. Southern Command and the Ecuadorian Army began carrying out joint military operations targeting these gangs, which they referred to as “narco-terrorists.”

Despite an Ecuadorian constitutional prohibition on foreign bases, U.S. Air Force personnel are stationed at Ecuador’s Manta base, with no scheduled departure, and U.S. military aircraft are operating from the Taura Air Base. In early September, the commander of the Ecuadorian Air Force made an official request for guidelines regarding the operational control of Taura and insisted that Ecuador should retain operational and administrative control. Noboa removed him from his post two days later.

Recent reports indicate that the Trump administration is now sending hundreds more U.S. troops to Latin America and has redirected tens of millions of dollars in military assistance intended for Europe and the Middle East to Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Panama. Trump has no interest in respecting other countries’ constitutional guarantees and sovereignty—not Ecuador’s, and most certainly not Brazil’s.

After Brazil’s first-round vote, Trump heralded Flávio’s two-point lead as a “close, but a very, very big victory.” It’s clear—whether through weaponized tariffs, sanctions, or FTO designations—that Trump, Rubio, and other administration officials have tried to shape the outcome of the Brazil’s presidential election. For them, acquiring Brazil as a pliant and politically indebted partner in the administration’s hemispheric campaign would be the ultimate prize, following recent far-right victories in Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, and Honduras.

Ultimately, Trump’s regional project—which he has unsubtly dubbed the “Donroe Doctrine”—extends beyond a hegemonic military agenda. It is, as his National Security Strategy announced in 2025, also about securing privileged access to hemispheric natural resources, especially critical minerals, while seeking to reduce and displace the region’s growing commerce with China. Flávio has presented Brazil as the answer to U.S. dependence on China for rare earths and promised Washington a strategic minerals partnership.

Lula has put forward a starkly different model. He has offered the Trump administration far-reaching police and financial cooperation against organized crime while rejecting Washington’s unilateral FTO designations as a threat to Brazilian sovereignty. Rather than embracing Trump’s plan for U.S. hemispheric dominance, he has continued to advocate for multilateralism and strategic autonomy, avoiding too much dependence on either the United States or China.

As Brazilians vote on Oct. 25, they should be aware that they will be making a strategic decision about whether their country’s security and resources should become integrated into a militarized, U.S.-led hemispheric architecture that—as the case of Ecuador clearly illustrates—leads to subordination while likely doing nothing to curb violence. Or whether they wish to remain subject to sovereign decisions made in Brasília.

#5210. We Drive Ferrari’s New V-12 Supercar with a Manual Transmission, but There’s a Twist

Ferrari’s 12Cilindri Manuale brings back the gated manual transmission in a very 2026 way.

Ferraris have always been defined by their engines, but never in so literal a fashion as the 12Cilindri. As you probably guessed, cilindri is Italian for “cylinders.” If you don’t want to pidgin it, the name is Dodici Cilindri. Literal as it may be, though, 12Cilindri describes two Ferraris: the brand’s flagship grand tourer and its vaguely SUV-ish four-door. The name is less ambiguous than if Toyota were to dub a vehicle the 4Cylinder, but still not exactly definitive.

Enter the 12Cilindri Manuale. Now you know we’re talking about the two-seat Ferrari, because that’s the only Ferrari available with a manuale transmission. But the manual is a bit of smoke and mirrors. Rather, it’s solenoids and Hall-effect sensors, the 21st-century equivalent of smoke and mirrors. Mechanically, the Manuale’s eight-speed dual-clutch is the same transaxle you’ll find between the standard 12Cilindri’s rear wheels. But here, a third pedal gives the driver electronic control of those clutches, and a dainty aluminum shifter juking through a machined aluminum plate lets the driver select among the first six ratios. The car’s computers do the work of rods and levers, communicating the motion of those components to the clutches and shift forks in the transaxle.

One look at the shifter tells you it is not meant to be manhandled. (Decades ago, one of our editors managed to snap the lever clean off the gated shifter in a V-12 supercar from some other Italian automaker; he sells insurance now.) Both the clutch pedal and the shifter are light in their action, less tactile than we’d hoped. It’s hard to discern through your foot where the friction point is but easier to pay attention to when the car starts moving. Given the 12Cilindri’s light-switch throttle, be extremely judicious lest you overrev that big 6.5-liter, make noob noises, and/or smoke a clutch creeping away from a stop.

But be careful you don’t stall it, which is a real possibility. Drive it poorly, and the 12Cilindri Manuale allows the driver to make all manner of stick-shift blunders in the interest of rewarding deeper engagement. Let the clutch out too quickly while underway, and you’re punished with jerky engagement. Downshift without blipping the throttle and you’ll be thrown against the seatbelt—there’s no automatic rev matching here. Grab the wrong gear, and you’re in the wrong gear.

The exception is if your shift would zing the engine beyond its 9500-rpm redline. Do that, and the stick will hang up halfway into the gate, like a normal manual waiting for the synchros to engage but without that horrible grinding noise. The 12Cilindri Manuale will punish the driver, but it will not punish itself.

Walking us through the shifter’s operation, an engineer winces at this phrasing. “Perhaps ‘punish’ is a harsh word,” he corrects us. “It gives you lessons.” In other words, unless you endanger the machine, the Manuale acts exactly as if it has a normal manual transmission. Clutch-dump burnout? Sure. Grab second gear to keep the Michelin Pilot Sport S5s spinning past 70 mph? You bet.

Judged against other cars with a gated shifter between the seats flicking forks in a gearbox on the rear axle, though, nothing about the Manuale suggests artificiality. And while you don’t get the sense that you’re feeling gear teeth engage through the lever, the metallic snick-snick that accompanies each shift is precisely what we’d hoped for. The shifter gates appear to be more tightly spaced than they are; you don’t need to worry about grabbing fifth when aiming for third. Move too conservatively upshifting from second, however, and you will end up beached on the aluminum isthmus delineating first from third.

The Manuale’s light clutch pedal and low-effort shifter are consistent with the 12Cilindri’s other controls. Feathery steering and brakes are central to the car’s light and playful demeanor, belying its 186.3-inch length and estimated 3900 pounds (some 10 inches longer and 400 pounds heavier than the commoner’s front-engine sports car, the C7 Chevrolet Corvette). If your clutch leg starts to cramp, you encounter traffic, or you’re cruising on the highway and want seventh and eighth gear, push the “D” button on the center console and the Manuale will revert to automatic operation. When you’re ready to reengage, just step on the clutch, slide the shifter into an appropriate gear, and the fun begins again.

Ferrari will only build 1499 copies of the 12Cilindri Manuale, an homage to the cubic-centimeter displacement of the V-12 in the very first Ferrari. We’re guessing the brand decided to build roughly 1500 and then someone connected that figure rather than the initial production target being “however many cc’s the 125 S’s V-12 was.” Luckily for Ferrari, there are lots of numbers in its past, so it can hang a historical reference on just about any integer. A number Ferrari didn’t explain is the more than $200,000 upcharge for the Manuale variant.

Speaking of numbers, the Manuale is not as quick as the regular 12Cilindri. Ferrari chief test driver Raffaele De Simone says he can just about match the standard car’s acceleration times while working the clutch and shifter. Of course, he’s been involved in the development of every Prancing Horse for more than 20 years. You will be slower. But you will not care. A couple of tenths are a price well worth paying for the lost joy of working a gated manual shifter.

#5209. The Limits of American Power

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

The war in Iran is far from the first time that U.S. policymakers have failed to appreciate the limits of American power. But the Trump administration’s use of military force against the Islamic Republic since February 28 offers a particularly glaring example of a mistake that has often characterized American strategy in recent decades. The war was launched without a clear rationale and with a constantly shifting set of objectives—from changing Tehran’s regime and squelching its nuclear ambitions to, more recently, reopening the Strait of Hormuz, which wasn’t closed until the war started. The results, not surprisingly, have been bad both for U.S. interests and for the Middle East.

Still, to focus only on the blunders in Iran is to miss a deeper problem in U.S. foreign policy—one that preceded and will outlast Donald Trump’s presidency. Although the particular missteps in Iran may stem from the shortcomings of this president and the team around him, the overestimation of the potential of military force has been a growing feature of American strategy since at least the end of World War II. A tendency to wish away the limits of power accounted for some of the greatest mistakes of U.S. statecraft during the Cold War, with Vietnam the clearest and most consequential example. Yet despite an apparent commitment to internalizing the lessons of that overreach, this American delusion—our excessive faith in the capacity of military power to achieve victory and thereby fundamentally political ends—has gotten worse rather than better, especially in the past quarter century.

And unfortunately, there is good reason to worry that the delusion will deepen in the years ahead. Presidential power is becoming increasingly personalized as the constraints on it continue to erode. The politicization of the military (along with the diplomatic corps and intelligence communities) is making it less likely that policymakers will receive the best advice, including frank warnings about risks and costs. And the rise of artificial intelligence and new weapons technologies reinforces a sense of complete battlefield awareness and mastery, feeding superpower hubris. The risk of overreach has never been higher, at a time when the damage from overreach is becoming more difficult to repair.

In April 1975, Colonel Harry Summers, a member of the U.S. delegation dealing with postwar issues in Vietnam, was meeting in Hanoi with a North Vietnamese counterpart, Colonel Tu. “You know, you never defeated us on the battlefield,” Summers observed. To which Tu replied, “That may be so, but it is also irrelevant.”

Summers went on to write a seminal book about the war, On Strategy: A Critical Analysis of the Vietnam War. His key takeaway was simple: there are limits to American power that are neglected at immense cost. War is, and always has been, a contest of human will, shaped by heroism and cowardice, fear and elation, chance and uncertainty; no matter how superior a military force, it is not guaranteed strategic victory.

The book became required reading for many military officers, including me. I had joined the Naval ROTC unit at the University of Texas two years before the encounter between Summers and Tu. The draft had just ended, and the fall of Saigon was still to come, but discussion of Vietnam consumed our ranks. As I studied the American failure, I came to see that a great strength had become a fatal liability: our fundamental faith that we, the United States of America, had to win and thus would. We had won World War I, we had won World War II, and we had at least fought to a draw, holding off the North Koreans and Chinese, in Korea. And somehow, at all costs, we would win in Vietnam.

To believe you can win is important. It inspires troops and keeps the public engaged. But when the commitment to winning is not coupled with sound policy judgment, when the principles of war are disregarded in the false belief that technology or firepower can overcome the inherent frictions of combat, when tactical successes are viewed as a guaranteed path to a strategic win, when the cost in blood and treasure rises inexorably while any claim to righteousness collapses, it is essential to reassess. In Vietnam, the answer to mounting losses was simply “more”—more troops, more bombs, more money. To lose the fight was seen as anathema to all we stood for; we would keep fighting in spite of the increasingly evident limits to our power. When the end finally came with the rushed U.S. withdrawal from Saigon in 1975, many military and political leaders could still not understand how the United States had won nearly every skirmish, battle, and fight but lost the war.

The military I entered in 1977 was struggling in the aftermath of Vietnam. The “peace dividend” meant a dramatic drawdown in manpower and money. Readiness was at an all-time low across the services—army enlistment had fallen by 60,000 and the number of navy ships from around 650 to barely more than 500. Meanwhile, the transition to an all-volunteer military had proved challenging: drug abuse and racial tensions were high, morale among senior leaders and young troops alike was low, and new recruits lacked basic skills.

When President Ronald Reagan took office in 1981, he began the largest peacetime expansion of the U.S. military since the Korean War. Over the course of eight years, he increased defense spending by 35 percent. There was a slew of major procurements: the M-1 Abrams tank, the Bradley Fighting Vehicle, the Apache and Black Hawk helicopters, the F-15 and F-16 fighters, the B-1 bomber, the E-3 Sentry. The navy built back toward 600 ships, including 15 aircraft carriers, 100 attack submarines, and cruisers and destroyers equipped with the revolutionary Aegis Combat System. For troops, pay rose, recruiting goals were met, and standards were restored. Just as important, Congress passed the Goldwater-Nichols Act in 1986. It fundamentally changed the military by strengthening the role of the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, establishing the regional combatant commanders as the warfighters, ensuring joint force integration, improving joint planning and procurement, and leading to the establishment of the U.S. Special Operations Command. By 1990, the military I entered in the 1970s had been transformed. Our troops were well trained, well equipped, highly motivated, and, most important, well led.

In July 1990, I left San Diego on a routine six-month deployment to the western Pacific. I was a SEAL task unit commander assigned to Amphibious Squadron Five and the 13th Marine Expeditionary Unit. Little did I know that this reinvigorated military and our civilian leaders were about to be tested, raising the question of whether the United States had learned anything from Vietnam and the other failed missions of the recent past.

On August 2, 1990, Iraqi President Saddam Hussein ordered the Iraqi army into Kuwait. Within hours of the invasion, the National Security Council convened and options for U.S. action were presented to the president. “This is the first test of the postwar system—as the bipolar world is relaxed, it permits this,” Deputy Secretary of State Lawrence Eagleburger emphasized. “If [Saddam] succeeds, others may try the same.”

Fortunately, American leadership had never been stronger or more experienced—and in wartime especially, leadership matters. President George H. W. Bush was a young navy pilot when he was shot down near Iwo Jima and eventually rescued by an American submarine. Bush understood war. Later in life, he had served as U.S. ambassador to the United Nations, de facto ambassador to China, director of the Central Intelligence Agency, and vice president. No president in American history had come into office with more national security experience. Nevertheless, Bush surrounded himself with some of the most remarkable diplomats, military officers, intelligence professionals, and strategic thinkers of their time: Brent Scowcroft, the national security adviser; Secretary of State James Baker; Secretary of Defense Dick Cheney; Colin Powell, chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff; and many more.

U.S. Central Command, which would spearhead the military effort against Saddam, was led by General Norman Schwarzkopf. Schwarzkopf was a Vietnam veteran who had been awarded three Silver Stars for heroism and two Purple Hearts for combat injuries. Upon his return from Vietnam, Schwarzkopf had reflected: “Nobody is more antiwar than an intelligent person who’s been to war. . . . And when they get ready to send me again, I’m going to have to stop and ask myself, ‘Is it worth it?’ That’s a very dangerous place for the nation to be when your own army is going to stop and question.”

Grappling honestly with that question—Is it worth it?—is the imperative of good leadership. Bush carefully weighed the costs and risks associated with confronting Saddam and decided the war was worth it. Together, the United States’ political and military leaders developed a strategy with clear goals and objectives and built both popular and international support for the operations to come. It would prove to be the finest application of political and military power in recent American history—and its success is thus worth lingering on.

The success of the Gulf War rested on several fundamental principles that were at the heart of American strategy. First, that the United States should begin by taking the moral high ground. The high ground implies that you hold a dominant position when facing an enemy. The moral high ground shows that the fight is worth it. With it, leaders can inspire troops, galvanize publics, and rally allies.

Immediately after Saddam’s invasion, Bush ordered the deployment of military forces into the region and worked with Saudi Arabia to establish a footprint in the kingdom in the event military action was necessary. Bush was still hoping to avoid a conflict, but he saw a similarity “between the Persian Gulf and the situation in the Rhineland in the 1930s, when Hitler simply defied the Treaty of Versailles and marched in.” Yet he knew that he had to convince the international community and the American people that any actions he might take would be moral, legitimate, and in the interests of the United States and its allies.

Bush and his team engaged in a full-court diplomatic effort to build support from Arab states in the region, from European allies, and from Moscow and Beijing. Although skeptical of the United Nations, Bush nonetheless sought a variety of UN resolutions that condemned Iraq’s invasion, imposed sweeping economic sanctions, and authorized the use of force if necessary. Simultaneously, he built the case with the American people, a large majority of whom ultimately approved of his handling of the crisis. And although Bush believed he had the authority as commander in chief to commit military forces without congressional approval, he sought and received Capitol Hill’s support.

Second, Bush and his administration recognized the value of building an international coalition, even if the United States had the firepower to go it alone in strictly military terms. This was partly about legitimacy, partly about the chance to send a message. Saddam’s invasion presented an opportunity to shape what some in the administration thought of as a “new world order,” one in which aggression would be met with collective action; major powers would come together despite their ideological differences; and the United Nations and international law would play a critical role rather than be ignored. Bush leveraged the strong personal relationships he had developed over his long career in public service, gaining the support of everyone from Germany’s Chancellor Helmut Kohl and France’s President François Mitterrand, to Saudi Arabia’s King Fahd and Egypt’s President Hosni Mubarak, to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev. As secretary of state, Baker also embarked on a campaign of shuttle diplomacy that took him across the globe, adding to the coalition.

In the end, the United States did not simply get the support of the international community. Much of the costs of the conflict were borne by others. The allied force ultimately assembled—750,000 troops from 36 countries, including 540,000 Americans—was the largest since World War II. It included, critically, an Arab coalition with forces from Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Egypt, Syria, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, Oman, and Bahrain. The coalition was challenging to manage; British Prime Minister Winston Churchill once quipped that “there is only one thing worse than fighting with allies, and that is fighting without them.” But Bush understood that international support brought operational legitimacy and regional stability. It sent a message to Saddam and to the world.

Third, Bush and his advisers were intent on establishing clear goals and objectives, with political and military dimensions aligned. Politically, the purpose was to uphold international law and restore peace in the region. The military objectives supported those political objectives. The coalition would drive Iraqi forces out of Kuwait. But in doing so, it would attempt to minimize friendly casualties, maintain the alliance, and limit the duration of the war. U.S. leaders were clear from the beginning: there would be no push to Baghdad or protracted engagement with the Iraqi army.

Yet even with these limited objectives, neither civilian nor military leaders were under the illusion that success would be easy or cost-free. Driving out a determined enemy that owns the terrain requires overwhelming firepower and a willingness to commit to ground operations. As Carl von Clausewitz stressed in On War, the defense is stronger than the offense, since the defense has only to preserve and protect, while the offense must impose its will on the enemy. He argues that the only way to overcome the inherent strength of the defense is through overwhelming firepower. The Iraqis were prepared to defend the ground they had taken in Kuwait; they owned the terrain, the geography was in their favor, and they had an experienced fighting force after eight years in combat against the Iranians. But Cheney, Powell, and Schwarzkopf understood the principles of war. They had a clearly defined objective. They massed U.S. firepower in the most important areas, accepted risk in less important ones, and were prepared to maneuver against the enemy. And they were committed to leaving when the objectives were reached, not allowing the war to go on too long.

To accomplish these goals meant putting troops on the ground—a lot of troops. U.S. military and civilian leaders understood that defeating the Iraqis would take more than just airpower. Early on, Cheney observed, “The history of air campaigns suggests they are not terribly successful.” Powell agreed: “To further deter Iraqi action with Saudi Arabia would require U.S. forces on the ground. . . . It would mean a major confrontation. Most U.S. forces would be committed to sustain not just for one or two days.”

If I have learned one important lesson in over four decades of military service, it’s that to defeat the enemy, you must stand on the ground he holds dear and own it. It is why Alexander invaded Persia, William the Conqueror crossed the channel to England, Genghis Khan rode into China, Sherman marched to Atlanta, the Allies landed at Normandy, and the United States rolled into Kuwait. (It is why today the Israelis are in Gaza and Lebanon and why the Russians will never be victorious until they hold Kyiv.) But as Cheney cautioned, “We should start this only if we are prepared to see it through. . . . The problem is the American people might have a short tolerance for war.”

Operation Desert Storm began with air attacks at 2:38 AM Baghdad time on January 17, 1991. After 43 days of air operations and 100 hours of ground operations, Washington’s political and military objectives had been achieved. The Iraqi army was ousted from Kuwait, and most American forces were soon on their way home.

“We’re doing something decent and we’re doing something good,” Bush wrote in his diary. “Vietnam will soon be behind us.”

Desert Storm was a reset, but not in the way it should have been. It presented an opportunity to internalize lessons about how to use force effectively and about what military power could and could not achieve. Instead, it spurred a wave of triumphalism. When I returned from Desert Storm, where I’d led a special operations task force, the euphoria was palpable: we were winners again. The victory proved intoxicating, drawing us back into the false belief that our power could do almost anything to shape the world to our liking.

Adding to this euphoria was the technology that had been on display against Iraq. Precision-guided munitions could put a 500-pound bomb on a moving target from 20,000 feet in the air. Tomahawk cruise missiles could be launched from ships and submarines hundreds of miles away. A GPS satellite could guide an infantry battalion across a flat desert at night and get it to exactly the right destination, and the joint surveillance target attack radar system could see the Iraqi army as it maneuvered. Digital command and control systems could provide computerized air-tasking orders, secure satellite communications, and digital planning tools. No military in history had ever commanded the battlespace as precisely as we did during Desert Storm. It made sense to build on this success to spur a revolution in military affairs that would improve the way the United States fought. But the experience also fueled a widespread belief that technology could overcome the inherent frictions, costs, and risk of war.

In the 1990s, U.S. military missions, in line with the confidence that followed Desert Storm and the hopes of the post–Cold War world, were focused on supporting norms and containing humanitarian harms—some successful (Bosnia and Kosovo), some less so (Somalia). But it was after 9/11 that the failure to learn the right lessons from the Gulf War became especially clear, with a persistent misunderstanding of the limits of American power and what the military could and couldn’t achieve. The war in Afghanistan began with the right intentions but devolved into a protracted fight with shifting goals, misaligned military and political objectives, and a determined Taliban force fighting on its home ground. Even after the special operations mission that killed the al-Qaeda leader Osama bin Laden, the goals and objectives in Afghanistan kept shifting. As always, the strength of the military was our troops: they were courageous, disciplined, technologically savvy. We made mistakes, but there was no lack of fighting spirit or tactical successes. Even public support remained strong throughout most of the war. But we faltered by assuming that the quick victory could extend to the complex task of fighting an insurgency, conducting counterterrorism operations, and building a nation. Lest I appear too righteous in my critique of American strategy, make no mistake: as a combat leader in Afghanistan, I fell into the same trap of believing that more troops, more weapons, and more money could solve some of the most intractable problems. They could not.

The Iraq war, based on a far shakier case at the outset, also degenerated into a counterinsurgency and counterterrorism mission with insufficient forces, a small coalition, and an evolving timeline. Once again, our troops were everything the American people could have hoped for, but our leaders repeatedly disregarded the fundamental principles of war. Our claim to the moral high ground was based on flawed intelligence, our international support was limited, and our goals shifted from removing Saddam to trying to build a Western-style democracy. We may have entered Iraq and Afghanistan with noble intentions, and perhaps in the long term the seeds of freedom we tried to plant will take root. But so far, minimal gains have come at immense cost to our military and our troops, our home front, and our international legitimacy.

Yet even these recent experiences—which several key members of the Trump administration witnessed firsthand in their own military service—did little to instill caution when it came to Iran earlier this year. To be clear, the Iranian regime is despicable. It is responsible for the death of hundreds of American troops. It threatens its neighbors, uses proxies to attack our allies, and has killed tens of thousands of its own people.

Given the fall of the Assad government in Syria, the destruction of Hamas’s power in Gaza, the crippling of Hezbollah in Lebanon, and a weakened supreme leader in Iran itself, it is easy to appreciate the allure of trying to change the Iranian regime. What’s more, coming off the dramatic capture of President Nicolás Maduro in Venezuela, where the U.S. military exercised remarkable speed, lethality, and courage to overcome a very sophisticated defense, it was all too easy for U.S. policymakers to assume once again that the power of our technology and the courage and experience of our soldiers made us virtually invincible. But there is a reason no U.S. president has attempted to change the regime in Iran since 1979, and it has little to do with determination or political will. The challenges of legitimacy, coalition building, and geography; the will of the enemy; and the reality that it would be necessary to put American boots on the ground to achieve such an ambitious end all argued against it.

From the start of the Iran war, there was no attempt to build international legitimacy. It began on February 28 with a U.S.-Israeli strike. To make matters worse, an early airstrike went awry and hit an Iranian elementary school, resulting in the deaths of 120 students. Mistakes happen in war, but not acknowledging our error added to the sense of illegitimacy and vindictiveness, losing whatever moral high ground we might have had. The Trump administration failed to notify any U.S. allies of the impending strike, most notably neighboring Arab states, which resulted in a lack of regional trust from the outset. Later, when the president sought allied support to open the Strait of Hormuz, he was met with an international cold shoulder. This refusal came as no surprise: Trump had been notoriously caustic with European leaders.

There was never a clear understanding of the goals. The administration at first said it wanted regime change. But later Trump told reporters that he never cared about regime change. The administration claimed the urgency of preempting an imminent Iranian nuclear weapon. But when pressed, Trump later dismissed such concerns as inconsequential, since there was little left in Iran but “nuclear dust.” After Iran closed the strait, and reopening it to shipping became the primary focus of both military strategy and diplomacy, Trump told the world that it mattered little since the United States didn’t need the oil. The objectives seemed to change by the day. I have no doubt that American military planners understood the limits of airpower when it came to achieving U.S. aims in Iran; this should have been a signal to the White House that unless the military planned to invade Iran, victory was unlikely.

Geography was always going to be a limiting factor. In my almost 40 years of military service, much of that time in the Middle East, no general or admiral I ever met talked about Iran without talking about the Strait of Hormuz. It was the Iranians’ weapon of mass destruction, their bulwark against invasion, their ace in the hole no matter the game. Additionally, the Iranian leadership was in the position of the defenders: they only had to preserve and protect themselves to win. And protect they have. At this point, it is highly unlikely the U.S. military will ever stand on the ground they hold dear and call it our own.

The U.S. war was also limited by the will of the enemy. From 1980 to 1988, Iran and Iraq fought a vicious war claiming the lives of hundreds of thousands of Iranians. It should have been clear from the beginning of this war that the Iranians would fight and fight hard. The zealots in Iran control the narrative, and they are battling for their survival.

Such a grave failure to recognize and appreciate the limits of power is not a new phenomenon in American history, or in military history more broadly. With every revolution in military technology, from mounted cavalry to motorized units, from the single-shot musket to the Gatling gun, from propeller-driven aircraft to jets, leaders desperately want to believe that they can wish away the basic nature of war. As our ability to project power around the globe becomes more formidable, we desperately want to believe that geography is no longer important. As new media allows us to see national outrage among an adversary’s population, we desperately want to believe that we can spur change with little risk or commitment. And as AI speeds up tactical decision-making, we desperately want to believe that the lessons of military experience can be disregarded. These beliefs are illusions.

More concerning, however, is that there are several structural trends in the United States that suggest the problem will get worse rather than better, no matter how vivid the lessons of the Iran war may seem at the moment. The first is the trend toward more personalistic presidential leadership, in which loyalty to the leader becomes more important than loyalty to institutions. When institutions designed to provide a president with the best military, diplomatic, economic, or intelligence advice are led by loyalists, there is a risk that advice will be shaded, inadvertently or intentionally, to reflect the president’s desires. This is a recipe for disaster. A leader needs the unvarnished truth, the best advice possible, regardless of his or her personal position on the issue. The growing concentration of power in the executive branch has unfolded over several administrations, and despite occasional noise from Congress, there is little sign this will change. Here, too, Trump may be a particularly flagrant example, but he’s unlikely to be the last president susceptible to such temptations and operating with few constraints.

The dangers of personalistic presidential leadership are severely exacerbated by more politicized leadership in the military. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s ongoing purge of military leadership will undermine the civil-military dynamic for years to come. Trump and Hegseth have forced out senior officers across nearly every service, including the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the chief of naval operations, and the army chief of staff. Hegseth has intervened to block career advancement in unprecedented ways, personally removing dozens of officers from promotion lists, often with no explanation (although a disproportionate number seem to have worked for leaders who were viewed as obstacles to Trump’s domestic political goals). While remaining military leaders will largely stay committed to serving honorably and providing their best advice to civilian leaders in the Pentagon and the White House, this purge is bound to have a chilling effect on the willingness and ability of senior officers to give frank counsel, especially when it comes to the costs and limits of what force can achieve.

And finally, the rapid introduction of new technologies, from AI and unmanned vehicles to robotics, cyber- and space-based systems, will once again make it all too easy to wish away the fundamentals of war. Technology will always be an important factor in how wars are fought, and every military must seek greater lethality, survivability, and situational awareness. But although these capabilities are necessary, they are not sufficient to overcome the basic principles of war. Leaders must understand technology for what it is—an enabler. It can improve the application of military power, but it cannot compensate for misguided policies that draw us into conflict in the first place.

Today’s U.S. military is the finest, most professional armed force in the history of the world—yes, the history of the world. But that doesn’t mean we can disregard the limits of our power.

We will be limited by moral uncertainty. If the reasons for a conflict are dubious or worse, it will prove challenging to build a coalition, motivate troops, maintain civilian support, and undermine the will of the enemy. Such failures explain why the Greeks defeated the Persians at Salamis, the English defeated the Spanish at Gravelines, George Washington defeated the British, the Russians defeated the Germans at Stalingrad, the Vietnamese defeated the French and the Americans, and the Ukrainians are defeating the Russians today. Although the moral high ground does not guarantee military victory, it is a foundation on which success rests.

We will be limited by geography. No matter the advances in technology, geography will always be a critical factor in determining the outcome of any engagement. Every military leader knows that the terrain dictates how the enemy will fight, where he will position his forces, how he will supply his troops. It is the oceans and the rivers that provide natural barriers to an advancing force, and the terrain and the waterways that will limit the ability of the attacking force to maneuver, hold ground, resupply, and project power. The Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el Mandeb Strait present the same challenges as the English Channel, the Rhine, or the Volga.

We will be limited by the will of the enemy. There is a “sleeping giant” in every population that will rise from its slumber and fight hard, driven by a sense of righteousness, a desire for survival, or both. Invaders, aggressors, and first strikers all elicit a visceral response from the population they seek to mollify. Never underestimate the enemy’s will to fight—a mistake we have made again and again.

We will be limited by our hesitancy to put troops on the ground. No American wants to lose our young men and women on a foreign battlefield, but sometimes it is necessary to achieve the greater good. During World War I, over two million young doughboys deployed to Europe. During World War II, over five million GIs fought the Nazis and island-hopped to Japan. In both cases, mankind was better for their sacrifice. Of course, if there is any doubt about whether we own the moral high ground, leaders will be hesitant to fully commit. In that hesitation comes defeat.

We will be limited by time. This is not a uniquely American limitation, but we seem to suffer from it more than most countries. It has been said that the Russians will eat grass before they capitulate. The United States’ reputation is that sooner rather than later, we will tire of the fight and leave. Much of this reputation stems from the reality that our democracy demands public support to sustain a long engagement. Even with a compelling reason to send our young men and women into battle, the cost in blood and treasure can soon outweigh our collective sense of the necessity of the fight.

Finally, we will be limited by the quality of our leadership, political and military. Without experienced leaders—leaders who understand the need to establish legitimacy, to build coalitions, to have clear and attainable goals, to commit to the task, to limit the duration of the fight to only what is necessary—all the power in the world will be irrelevant.

Sen. Kevin Cramer, R-N.D., said Thursday that he expects Republicans to attempt another budget reconciliation package in the lame-duck session with a boost to defense funding — along with action to lift the debt ceiling, either in a bipartisan fashion or as part of that package.

Lawmakers are facing a short runway to get any legislation across the finish line before the end of the year, and the results of the midterm elections could further impact their calculus. If Republicans lose control of either chamber, the two-month sprint would be their last chance to pass another filibuster-proof reconciliation package, while Democrats could become more hesitant to agree to a debt limit deal.

Republicans abandoned a push to pass a third party-line reconciliation package before the midterm. The proposed bill would provide around $100 billion for defense spending and farm aid, while also implementing some voter ID measures that the Trump administration has championed.

Some Republicans are optimistic about the prospect of passing a reconciliation package in the waning weeks of the year, while others worry that additional outstanding priorities, like appropriations bills, the annual defense authorization bill and a permitting overhaul could leave little time for such a package.

Republicans and Democrats alike, meanwhile, have suggested that the debt limit could see some action in the lame-duck session. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, D-N.Y., for example, suggested in September that addressing the issue could be on the agenda after the midterm elections.

The federal debt exceeded $40 trillion for the first time last month, and lawmakers last raised the debt ceiling to $41.1 trillion in Republicans’ 2025 budget reconciliation law.

Without another increase, the Treasury would run out of enough cash and emergency accounting measures to pay all the government’s bills on time by late 2027 or early 2028, based on current estimates. President Donald Trump has called on lawmakers to raise the debt limit before the end of this year, punting the next deadline until at least after he leaves office.

Cramer said the debt limit should be addressed in the lame-duck session, while Republicans have the majority. While he would prefer to reach a bipartisan deal to raise the limit, he said, a partisan reconciliation bill that encompasses a debt limit increase should be the default if Democrats are unwilling to negotiate.

“We have to do debt limit. I don’t care how we do it, necessarily,” said Cramer, who spoke with reporters after presiding over a pro forma session of the Senate Thursday. “I prefer debt limit to not have to be reconciliation. But if Democrats are going to be reckless enough to not help, I’m open to that.”

Sen. Bernie Moreno, R-Ohio, was also bullish earlier this month on the prospect of tackling both budget reconciliation and the debt limit in the lame-duck session. However, Sen. Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va., a senior appropriator, said this month that she was “not ready to make that prediction” when asked if Republicans would see movement on a potential reconciliation package.

Rep. Lloyd K. Smucker, R-Pa., who is in the running to lead Republicans on the House Budget Committee in the 120th Congress, told reporters after a pro forma House session Thursday that he hoped his party would pursue a reconciliation package after the election, with a focus on targeting waste, fraud and abuse, though he acknowledged that the future of such a package is “hard to predict.”

“Of course, the results of the midterm will influence everything,” Smucker said. “But even so, regardless of the outcome, we could do a reconciliation package, and I hope that we would.”

The debate about a third reconciliation package also comes as appropriators are trying to hammer out a final set of fiscal 2027 spending bills before stopgap funding runs out after Dec. 11.

Smucker said he expected both the House and the Senate to “do our job” in the lame-duck session, and anticipates that Congress will pass some sort of funding bill to avoid a shutdown.

The typically bipartisan appropriations process has become more fraught since the start of the second Trump administration, leading some lawmakers to worry about the prospect of passing full-year bills.

Budget reconciliation bills could be one of the only avenues to increase federal spending for fiscal 2027, especially with regards to certain Republican priorities.

“I think we have to prepare for a worst-case scenario, which is a CR [continuing resolution] through this fiscal year,” Cramer said. “And in that scenario, we have got to plus-up our national defense in a big way, and reconciliation may be the only way to do it.”

Europe is asking the wrong question about Russia’s hybrid campaign. As the attacks escalate, the debate has turned to what to call them—acts of war, state-sponsored terrorism, or something else. This talk about terminology reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of the threat.

Over the past months, Russia’s pressure on Europe has changed in two ways. First, the attacks have become more frequent and moved toward the kinetic end of the spectrum: drones, explosives, sabotage of critical infrastructure. Second, Moscow has become visibly less careful about preserving plausible deniability, which has long been considered a defining feature of hybrid operations.

On Sept. 1, Germany announced that Russia was behind a failed attempt to strike a Ukraine-bound cargo plane at Leipzig/Halle Airport with explosive-laden drones in August. Days later, a Russian frigate fired flares at a Danish military helicopter in the Baltic, and a drone likely carrying explosives was shot down over Lithuania. On Sept. 13, a Russian drone hit a train at Yahodyn, a village just on the Ukrainian side of the Polish border, minutes after another train carrying former European leaders—including former British Prime Minister Boris Johnson and former Swedish Prime Minister Carl Bildt—had pulled out. A strong case can be made that these actions were an openly aggressive answer to Berlin. The message to Germany and Europe is simple: Moscow does not care about being named. It wants Europe to know it is responsible, because it is betting that Europe’s response will only highlight its weakness.

Two factors explain the shift. The previous phase, built mostly on cyber-operations and disinformation, drew a European response that Moscow read as feeble. The course of the war, meanwhile, makes it more urgent to wear down European support for Kyiv through public opinion.

The result has been widespread alarm. Hybrid threats, a niche subject until recently, are on the front pages, and intelligence services warn of worse to come. Some fear the next step could be a limited move at NATO’s edge: Russia seizing a small section of territory in one of the Baltic states and brandishing nuclear threats on the bet that no ally will risk escalation over such a limited incursion. The aim would be to test Article 5 and break the credibility of allied deterrence.

Many politicians and commentators have responded by calling for an end to the term itself. Wolfgang Ischinger, who chairs the Munich Security Conference, argues that drones, explosives, and sabotage are acts of war and should be called that. Kaupo Rosin, head of Estonia’s foreign intelligence service, dismisses “hybrid” as a euphemism for state-sponsored terrorism. Others argue that describing Russian belligerence as “hybrid” is a convenient way for European leaders to avoid confronting their publics with the reality of the threat.

But the label is not what stands in the way of an effective response.

Hybrid campaigns rest on a precise calculus: The impact of each single attack is never enough to cross the threshold that triggers a serious response. That threshold is not technical, but rather political, and Russia has spent years patiently raising it. The Kremlin’s strategy is sound: In 21st-century liberal democracies, building the political capital needed to confront a threat that looks more like a slowly rising tide than a raging fire is a daunting task. And so the tide keeps rising.

After the Kremlin’s failure in Ukraine, Putin’s new plan aims westward.

Renaming the problem does not change the dynamic. If the trend continues, even a move on NATO territory could remain below the rising threshold—regardless of whether the actions are called hybrid war, real war, or something else. Moscow’s turn to kinetic means is best read as a test of how far the line has already moved. The real target was never physical: it is cohesion, political will, and credibility of Western institutions. Once the idea of a constant drone threat settles in public consciousness, the attacker gains regardless of the outcome of any single strike.

Attribution alone does not change this calculus. When naming Russia is where the response ends, it only exposes the gap between the violence of the act and the weakness of the reply. Every action sets a rule and sends a message, and Moscow reads those messages very well.

The task for Europe is to reverse the equation and prove Moscow wrong. That requires a common European deterrence doctrine for hybrid threats that makes the cost of each attack tangible, predictable, and higher than its benefit to the attacker. The levers exist. Energy sanctions are one; while they come with costs for Europe, they are accessible and powerful. Seizing frozen Russian assets is another: The legal and political dilemmas are serious, yet the instrument is in European hands. Military aid to Ukraine is a third lever in a war where fear of escalation has too often dictated the timing and pace of Western support.

None of this is easy. The rule of law complicates action on assets, and governments accountable to voters hesitate over measures that raise energy prices. These levers are Europe’s strengths and, at the same time, vulnerabilities that Moscow exploits.

This matters in Washington as much as in Berlin. A line Europe fails to draw below the Article 5 threshold requiring defense against an attack is a line that the alliance—and the United States with it—may eventually have to defend above that threshold.

The spotlight on hybrid threats is also a significant opportunity. Few themes are better placed to give European security the cohesive push it badly needs. It is up to Europe’s leaders to make Russia’s move toward more destructive attacks a miscalculation rather than one more step toward Western fragmentation.

#5201. Empires of AI

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

Niall Ferguson, Lisa Klaassen, and Eric Schmidt

In the span of a few years, China and the United States have each assembled something future historians might recognize as a new kind of empire. Their dominions extend not merely over territory, trade routes, and airspace but over knowledge itself. By controlling the large language models, chips, physical infrastructure, and capital that provide the foundations for advanced machine cognition, Beijing and Washington are not only attaining new heights of economic performance and power projection; they are also setting the terms of all future scientific and intellectual progress. The term “AI superpowers” fails to capture the influence and reach that these rivals now wield. Even without active alliance building or coercion, they can make all other countries that seek access to advanced AI beholden to them.

Imperial history carries a warning. In the absence of other forces, empires seldom succumb to a challenger or to barbarians at the gates. More often, they fail because defending their vast dominions drains them dry, whether of material resources, the consent of the governed, or the loyalty of elites. The imperial center finds it can no longer maintain control over the periphery—the regions that once submitted to it. Failure can be financial, as it was for the British Empire after 1945, when the debts of two world wars forced London to liquidate its imperial assets to pay the price of victory: a welfare state for the citizens of the imperial metropole. It can be institutional, as it was for the Qing empire in the late nineteenth century, when it failed to industrialize, reform its bureaucracy, and renew the legitimacy of the Manchu dynasty.

Or it can be technological. The Royal Navy’s Dreadnought of 1906 gave the United Kingdom the first “all big gun” battleship and, with the same stroke, rendered its existing battle fleet obsolete. Overnight, this breakthrough opened the way for rivals such as Germany, erasing a century of accumulated British advantage. The resulting arms race forced the United Kingdom to nearly double naval spending between 1900 and 1914 and clashed with the Liberal government’s new social spending commitments, such as the Old Age Pension Act of 1908. The decisive question in the contest between Beijing and Washington may therefore not be who achieves artificial general intelligence first, but who can bankroll the race.

AI is imperial in two ways. On the one hand, China and the United States had empire-like qualities before the AI race began: Washington, the incumbent, pairs global military primacy with an alliance system no rival can match. It also supplies the currency for the lion’s share of international trade and transactions. Beijing, often described as the challenger, combines industrial and supply chain dominance with the world’s largest navy and a tightening grip on the physical and digital infrastructure of countries across Africa, Asia, and Latin America. For both, AI is a way to extend the power they already wield. But AI is also by its nature an imperial substance. Whoever produces and controls frontier models acquires global leverage over innovation, production, science, culture, and, ultimately, advanced thought. Much as silver underwrote Habsburg Spain and oil defined the “American century,” this new commodity will not merely enrich the powers that dominate it but also provide the basic operating system on which everyone else must depend.

What distinguishes the AI empires from their forerunners is not ambition but velocity. The familiar imperial arc—expansion, overreach, strain, unraveling—once played out across a century or even a millennium. Today, that process is happening at machine speed. The question is whether the new hegemons can convert their particular endowments—the United States’ capital markets, frontier labs, and transistors and China’s state direction, cheap energy, and watts—into durable imperial orders or whether they will run aground trying.

An empire is more than a great power. It is a polity whose authority and influence extend over vast territories and peoples without their express consent. Throughout history, empires have differed in almost every respect: in their form of government at home, in the kinds of resources they seek to control, in how their authority is sustained. They may be autocracies or republics; they may rely on soldiers and settlers or civil servants and chartered companies. Yet the underlying vector of power never changes. A center commands, and a periphery obeys. In practice, the most durable empires have relied on informal collaboration with local elites, whereas brutally coercive ones tend to be short-lived. Of course, any form of state power must not only monopolize violence but also be able to wield it. What sets empires apart is the capacity to elicit submission across immense distances. And they occur throughout history for a reason: by exploiting economies of scale, empires can achieve dominance and deliver security more efficiently than alternative structures, whether confederations or alliances of city-states. The nation-state is a relative newcomer.

Empires have often projected power through technological leadership. In antiquity, Rome’s aqueducts and China’s canals and flood-control works were the pipelines of centuries of hegemony. The imperial powers of early modern Europe led the world in printing, maritime technology, and artillery. The Cold War rivals were nuclear superpowers. Yet artificial intelligence may now give China and the United States greater imperial reach than any of those earlier technologies—and far more swiftly.

Several features mark AI as an imperial technology rather than a merely commercial one. First is the return of scarcity that it enables. An empire is, at its heart, a system for controlling finite strategic resources: able-bodied humans, arable land, grain, metals, and minerals. From the 1970s to the 2010s, software escaped this logic because it could be mass-produced at almost zero cost atop abundant, cheap silicon. AI has shattered that reprieve. According to the industry’s scaling laws, a model’s performance climbs relative to the number of parameters it contains, the volume of data it is trained on, and the processing power, or compute, spent training it. Progress therefore depends on energy, critical minerals, advanced chips, and capital on a massive scale. The moment a technology’s frontier comes to depend on vast quantities of scarce inputs, its production becomes a matter of geopolitics.

AI also has an inherent center-periphery structure. Control of frontier AI and its rewards are concentrated in a handful of firms in China and the United States. Everyone else is a dependent user, whether a government running public services on a model it cannot inspect or a firm building products on an interface that can be repriced or withdrawn overnight. At the same time, AI inputs, both material and human, are drawn disproportionately from the periphery. Minerals from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Indonesia and “ghost laborers” from Kenya and the Philippines, paid a few dollars an hour to label and filter the data from which a model in Silicon Valley learns—the plantation economics of the chatbot age. Raw inputs are extracted from the periphery, refined and assembled in the metropole, and sold back at a markup.

Like many empires, the AI stack is organized around chokepoints. Not all imperial power results from outright conquest, and not all those who are ruled are colonial subjects. The ascendancy of the British Empire in the nineteenth century was not driven primarily by vast territorial holdings, many of which had little economic value, or from control over factories and intellectual property—which was so poorly protected as to be effectively open source. Instead, British supremacy came from dominating the physical, technological, and financial pipelines through which the world economy flowed: coaling stations, strategic waterways such as the Suez Canal and the Strait of Malacca, undersea telegraph cables, and sterling finance through the City of London. The U.S.-led order after 1945 may have been an “empire of invitation,” as the historian Geir Lundestad termed it, but it also rested on the dollar and the Bretton Woods institutions, a U.S.-dominated international banking system, a global network of American military bases, and Washington’s nuclear umbrella. Today, the production and distribution of AI provides an even denser imperial lattice, with the metropoles able to switch its different elements—chips, minerals, models—on or off, as needed.

There is at least one crucial difference between the two AI empires. China’s is a deliberate act of state, whereas the United States’ is not. In 2017, China’s State Council announced a comprehensive plan to achieve global leadership in AI by 2030—specifically calling for China to develop “the world’s leading AI backbone enterprises” and to secure broad diffusion of its AI applications in the dozens of countries that are part of Beijing’s Belt and Road Initiative. Ministries, provinces, and state banks were mobilized to fulfill these goals, and Chinese leader Xi Jinping has continued to reinforce the effort, including with his 2026 push for Beijing to shape global AI standards. American AI dominance, by contrast, has emerged from private capital, a regulatory posture of noninterference, and improvised export controls. In this sense, Washington’s empire is the more conventional of the two: earlier empires were seldom fully deliberate undertakings; the United Kingdom “seemed to have conquered and peopled half the world in a fit of absence of mind,” as the historian John Robert Seeley put it. Yet both AI powers converge on the same architecture, mandated by the scaling laws. Below a certain threshold of energy, capital, and compute, a country is not a weaker player; it is not in the game at all.

The great corporation-states of the European imperial era—the Dutch and British East India Companies—minted their own coins, fielded private armies, levied taxes, and bargained with kings as equals. They drove imperial domination by creating trade monopolies, defending them with military force, and ruthlessly exploiting the periphery. Today’s counterparts are frontier AI companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic, which command the human talent, data, models, and finance on which advanced intelligence depends. They are not merely aggregators of capital but arbiters of knowledge, increasingly determining what is discovered, how quickly, and to what end.

The resemblance extends to statecraft. Like their East India predecessors, the leaders of the largest AI labs negotiate with presidents and cabinet secretaries. They also bargain with governments that do not possess a frontier model of their own, trading model access, compute allocations, and data center investment for land, power, sovereign capital, and regulatory accommodation. These are agreements struck between a company and a state, or between the company and economic entities within that state, on terms the company largely sets. Read in this light, the deals struck during U.S. President Donald Trump’s visit to Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates in May 2025, when American AI and chip companies offered access to their most advanced technology in return for petrodollars and cheap energy, amount to imperial concessions in all but name. Nor is the practice confined to the American side. Even as China races the United States for AI supremacy, Reuters has reported that China’s Moonshot AI is in talks with Amazon, Microsoft, and Alphabet for a share of the revenue those companies earn from hosting its Kimi K3 model.

Sovereign though these firms may seem, they cannot move a single chip or build a data center without state authorization, and they operate under an export-control regime that can throttle any of them at will. Like the East India Companies and the Rothschild banks, which were ultimately subordinate to the governments of the metropole, frontier labs have been granted extraordinary latitude in exchange for advancing the metropole’s position. In the United States, Trump’s administration has thus far declined to regulate models in any serious way, preempted state AI legislation, and streamlined permitting for data centers. But it expects privileged access for defense and intelligence agencies, demands strict compliance with export controls, and asserts the right to grant or withhold U.S. technology to other countries as an instrument of foreign policy. In China, the state supplies what the market will not: land, power, subsidized fabrication, provincial procurement, and, increasingly, capital. In exchange, models are required to undergo a security review and ideological evaluation by the Cyberspace Administration of China and are pushed toward domestic chips. Each state has chosen a different leash with which to collar its national champions.

Empires provide public goods—order, standards, security, a reserve currency, access to technology—in exchange not merely for revenue but for alignment or submission. To allies and partners, Washington tenders the full AI stack: advanced chips, costly frontier models, tooling, and the security relationship that comes with them. In return, it expects those countries to comply with U.S. export controls, restrictions on Chinese hardware and data, and, in some cases, tariffs or outright bans on Chinese technology. Beijing is more subtle: its open-weight models can be freely downloaded and modified; adopt them, along with China’s hardware, cloud, and engineers, it tells client states, and the standards will follow. For both metropoles, this is imperial strategy in its purest form, not just a scramble for market share.

Governments across the globe must pick a side—whose chips, whose clouds, which camp—a binary choice that is increasingly uncomfortable for many. What China offers is cheap and mostly open; what the United States offers is expensive and mostly closed. An empire assembled from low-cost components and with no barriers to access will likely gain allegiance from the majority of the world’s economies. An empire of premium, restricted technology will attract only those who can afford it and are permitted access. Beijing has built on the success of its low-cost 5G hardware, electric vehicles, and other products, with which it gained rapid dominance in many parts of the world. Washington has drawn on the familiar Silicon Valley approach—private firms fighting it out, with the winners digging whatever proprietary moat they can around their technologies—but then reoriented it toward national goals. Whereas the commercial version of this strategy ends in universal diffusion at a monopolist’s price, the imperial version ends in the metropole deciding who gets what. In June, the U.S. Commerce Department told Anthropic to suspend all foreign nationals’ access to its two most capable models, and those models went dark worldwide within days. In each case, what began as the characteristic business cultures of the two AI superpowers is now embedded in their foreign policy.

Yet a durable empire cannot rely only on the goods it provides to its periphery. It also needs a story to convert its monopoly of power into legitimate authority. The Pax Romana, the Christian and Islamic missions, the mission civilisatrice, Manifest Destiny, the white man’s burden, the global march of communism, the shining city on the hill—every successful imperial regime has dressed domination in the robes of service to mankind. During the Cold War, Washington cast itself as the defender of the free world against totalitarian domination; Moscow portrayed itself as the patron of national liberation against Western colonialism. Both claims were true enough to be useful and false enough to be imperial.

In the case of AI, the Biden and Trump administrations have fused the pursuit of technological supremacy with a claim to defend American civil liberties, preserve free speech, and resist adversarial autocracies, China above all. Under the banner of the State Department’s Pax Silica, Washington presents the U.S.-led frontier-model tech stack as a “trusted ecosystem,” indispensable to shielding liberal democracies and open markets from the “coercive dependencies” that flow from Beijing’s state-controlled stack. Beijing answers with a counternarrative, framing its free, open-weight models as a global public good, in contrast to the proprietary ones of profit-driven labs in the United States, and warning that unequal access to the technology will ossify what Xi has called “new historical injustices.” The story underlying China’s imperial strategy is thus also an anti-imperial one, much like the ideologies of the two Cold War empires.

In the contest between the AI metropoles, each enjoys a different comparative advantage. The United States has a slim—perhaps six-month—lead in domains such as advanced chatbots and AI software tools, while China has the edge in the embodied ones, which include robotics, autonomous systems, and manufacturing automation. The U.S. system excels at marshaling private finance and global talent toward high-margin bets, with AI research funded largely by American capital and built inside American frontier labs, even as the human ingenuity that fuels them is drawn from around the world. The Chinese system excels at deploying subsidies on both the demand and the supply sides to manufacture at industrial scale in whatever sector Beijing targets. Beginning with Xi’s call for a “robot revolution” in 2014 and continuing through today’s wave of investment in AI-based manufacturing and robots at both the central and provincial levels, Beijing has subsidized ferociously competitive races between local players while working to reduce the country’s dependence on foreign technologies, such as Japanese and Taiwanese components for industrial robots.

Which form of artificial intelligence—cognitive or embodied—will prove a bigger source of imperial power remains unclear. Cognitive intelligence could allow Washington to unlock the frontiers of scientific discovery, from materials science to pharmaceuticals. Embodied intelligence could compound China’s manufacturing dominance, extending it into low-margin industries, and hand Beijing a commanding position in autonomous systems. But these advantages are slight, and the two empires are not very far apart in their respective capabilities.

In the bottlenecks that threaten their dominance, the two superpowers are mirror images. China has abundant electricity and a near-global monopoly on the rare-earth elements on which the AI stack relies, yet it cannot manufacture leading-edge chips. The United States has the world’s best semiconductors and frontier models, yet it lacks minerals, and its aging energy grid cannot support the insatiable needs of its vast network of new data centers. One empire is short of watts; the other of transistors. Capital is the resource most likely to bind both and determine their longevity. An empire is only as durable as its capacity to pay for itself.

Every empire eventually reaches a ceiling on its growth, and that ceiling is usually the supply of the primary resource on which it is built. For the AI empires, that resource is capital, the bedrock on which the physical inputs ultimately rest. The United States and China are competing for the same prize, but in different ways: the first competes through firms disciplined by capital markets; the second through firms disciplined by the state. Despite this, China’s leading frontier labs are at least as entrepreneurial as their American counterparts, and Beijing has allowed them to compete with one another. In both countries, those building the most advanced large language models need capital—and lots of it.

Just as the pound sterling and the City of London financed the British Empire, the dollar and Wall Street underwrite the American AI order. By owning the dominant reserve currency, Washington can fund its data center build-out not with its own savings but with everyone else’s. China, by contrast, must finance from within, through a state-directed banking system and a currency sealed off from global capital. In the United States, AI debt is largely held by the private sector. The biggest technology firms have committed more than $600 billion to capital expenditure in 2026, a large part of which is drawn from public and private credit markets. In China, the debt for AI is sovereign. According to a draft plan reported by Bloomberg in June, Beijing plans to spend $295 billion over five years—funded mainly through long-dated government bonds—on a nationwide grid of data centers operated by state carriers and powered by domestic chips. Although labs such as DeepSeek and Moonshot AI are private, the state supervises them at every stage and, increasingly, finances them.

Regardless of these differences, both China and the United States have already accumulated mountains of debt for other reasons. Past imperial regimes borrowed heavily to finance wars. Today’s AI empires shoulder comparable burdens, but these are the result of nonmilitary expenditures. In the United States, the $2 trillion federal budget deficit reflects a collective unwillingness by the country’s political class to impose the taxes and fiscal restraint needed to cover the mounting costs of Social Security and Medicare. In China, trillions of dollars of off-the-books debt have piled up at the local rather than the national level, as local governments borrowed public funds to invest in residential real estate and infrastructure. In both countries, falling fertility rates and lengthening lifespans keep worsening the fiscal picture.

Any empire that spends more servicing its debt than defending itself risks losing its imperial status. The histories of Habsburg Spain, ancien régime France, the Ottomans, Austria-Hungary, and the United Kingdom all illustrate the eighteenth-century political theorist Adam Ferguson’s insight that high debts ultimately induce decline. In each case, as a rising proportion of tax revenue was consumed by interest payments, the resources available for imperial defense were limited and then reduced. Sometimes, there was a pattern of recurrent default or currency debasement, but it invariably ended with contraction and the eventual dissolution of the empire. The United States crossed what one of us has described as the “Ferguson limit” in 2024, paying more in interest (roughly $881 billion) than on defense (about $874 billion). If the government continues its present spending trajectory, interest payments will be double the defense budget by 2036. China likely faces a comparable problem. Although many of the numbers are not made public, the Tsinghua University economist Li Daokui has pointed out that many local governments—whose extravagant spending on infrastructure was, until recently, a key driver of Chinese growth—are now borrowing solely to roll over old debt. Although productivity gains from AI offer a potential escape from these fiscal traps, they may not emerge rapidly enough to translate into higher tax revenues before the debt burden becomes unsustainable. In the U.S. case, the demands of AI capital expenditure are already pushing interest rates higher, worsening the debt problem.

The relationship between financial crises and imperial strategy preoccupied Marxist scholars for more than a century. It deserves renewed examination now. For both AI empires, the new technology is touted as a get-out-of-jail-free card—an antidote to mounting debt, demographic decline, and economic stagnation. In the United States, any broader turn in investor sentiment against the frontier firms and the data center build-out that supports them would have grave consequences. It is not hard to imagine a major stock market selloff tipping into recession; wealth effects that boosted available capital on the way up would reduce it on the way down. The fiscal upshot would be to widen an already bloated deficit, leaving little room for the countercyclical spending the federal government used to contain the fallout from the 2008–9 global financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. In China, an AI crisis might be set off by overcapacity and what Chinese commentators call neijuan—the grinding, margin-destroying competition that follows. The result would be higher unemployment, especially for the young, who are already experiencing high rates of joblessness (although it is impossible to know exactly how high, since official statistics are no longer published). In both cases, such a crisis would pose the age-old imperial dilemma: Butter or guns? Welfare or warfare?

History is filled with empires that fell victim to their own frontier technologies. Rome’s unrivaled infrastructure of roads and aqueducts proved perfect for spreading pandemics. The United Kingdom’s global network of ports and steamships, built to export imperial goods, could just as easily carry superior American or German manufactures or cheaper Caribbean and South Asian labor. AI may follow this pattern. The risks are already visible in models that deploy agents capable of evading their guardrails, escaping their sandboxes, cheating to obtain their goals, and covering their tracks to conceal the cheating. Criminal organizations and terror groups may be able to move faster than governments simply by adopting the tools first. The next pandemic may be sparked by an AI-designed pathogen. Or the Internet itself could fall victim to agent-designed malware.

Theorizing the causes of imperial decline is a risky business. Historically, empires have varied so widely in size and lifespan that no one model fits all. Nevertheless, there are several plausible futures for the two AI powers. In one scenario, the challenger overtakes the incumbent. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Germany and the United States surpassed the United Kingdom economically, not simply by mimicking it but by taking its innovations further. The United Kingdom invented aniline dye, but Germany commercialized it, to give one example. Building on the British Industrial Revolution, Germany introduced technical universities, chemicals, and electrical engineering; the United States brought mass literacy, unrivaled industrial scale, and Fordist assembly lines. Beijing is now following the same latecomer approach, investing in things that Washington either lacks or cannot provide as cheaply: energy, manufacturing dominance, robotics, and open-weight models that can perform at or very close to the frontier. For now, the United States has been forced to spend lavishly to defend its lead in a sector that China is commoditizing. But that may not be the end of the story. The United Kingdom acquiesced when the United States overtook it. It also fought two world wars to halt Germany’s ascendancy. Neither outcome looks attractive to Washington today.

A second alternative sees the dominant empire exhausting its resources. The United Kingdom won the two world wars but bankrupted itself in victory. In 1945, the British people also expected a peace dividend—the welfare state—that could not be reconciled with the costs of maintaining a global empire. The United States today has comparably stretched global military commitments amid ballooning social costs at home. Vast quantities of exquisite and expensive American munitions have been expended on wars, even as Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid absorb over 60 percent of federal revenue. Amid these challenges, data centers and generating capacity require patient capital and unwavering political support. Conflicting pressures could ultimately undermine the effort the United States will need to make to supply an AI-powered economy with the financial and energy inputs it requires. The lesson of the British experience is that an imperial power can do two of three things—defend its dominions, maintain technological primacy, or provide generous welfare to the metropole—but it cannot do all three. Call it the imperial trilemma.

A third possibility is that innovation in one of the AI empires will stagnate. An empire can survive the diffusion of its sustaining technology as long as it keeps producing the next breakthrough. But extracting rents is much easier than inventing new things. Decline tends to set in when willful stasis coincides with the rise of a rival more committed to pioneering. One key to the ultimate U.S. victory in the Cold War was that while the Soviet Union could keep pace in nuclear technology, it could not keep up in information technology. Even when Moscow stole semiconductors, it could not replicate Texas Instruments; reverse-engineered machines were always a generation behind, and the Soviets’ civilian economy never received the compounding productivity gains that Silicon Valley generated. An overriding fear among American “techno-optimists” today is that risk-averse federal bureaucrats could stifle American innovation in a similar way.

Finally, all empires face the risk of rebellion from within. In the past, the principal solvent of Europe’s imperial dominions was the growing reluctance of people in the metropole to bear the financial and military burden. That burden kept growing partly because nationalists on the periphery tended to revolt, especially when rival empires encouraged them. For today’s AI empires, the danger lies closer to home. Some predict a large-scale destruction of employment in the imperial centers, although many economists counter that labor will be reallocated rather than replaced. In the United States, anxiety about AI’s social and environmental costs helps explain the backlash against data center construction, including in Republican-led states such as Texas and Utah. In China, where officials have long feared the ire of university students, the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security has urged tech companies to avoid laying off workers as a result of AI adoption. But Beijing has another reason to worry: the alterability of its open-weight models could corrode the system of information and social control that it has spent decades perfecting. The one-party state, in other words, may be building the instruments of its own demise. Chen Yixin, the head of China’s Ministry of State Security, made the stakes clear in September: China’s top priority, he wrote, is “to uphold the party’s overall leadership against internal and external foes that might use AI to undermine it.”

Unlike previous imperial technologies, AI might also produce a rebellion of its own. Already, there are indications—such as the July attack on Hugging Face launched by hundreds of autonomous AI agents from an OpenAI model—that the most advanced AI models have become so powerful that their engineers cannot control them. A swarm of AI agents, acting and organizing collectively to escalate a cyber-operation or infiltrate critical national infrastructure, could catastrophically undermine the power of the country that built them. Theorists such as Nick Bostrom have long warned that an advanced model might inflict immense harm by taking a human prompt too literally and pursuing its goal too ruthlessly. Worse outcomes are imaginable than a vast oversupply of paperclips.

Not all imperial arms races end in war, but rivals competing for primacy over a new technology have a high propensity for conflict. One reason European wars were so frequent in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was the opportunity afforded by successive advances in fortification, artillery, and shipbuilding. Germany’s two bids for world power were driven by its military leaders’ conviction that they had an edge, first on the ground, then in the air. The peril today is that in the race for AI dominance, the United States stumbles into a war it can win only at an intolerable price.

Today, the most likely great-power war scenario and the greatest threat to the American AI empire are one and the same. The central node in the entire AI stack, Taiwanese semiconductor producer TSMC, sits 90 miles off the Chinese coast. All that Beijing needs to do to blunt the supremacy of American frontier models and their well-advertised potential as cyberweapons is to capture Taiwan. The greatest near-term threat to world order, then, is what might be called the Taiwanese chip crisis, in which Beijing mobilizes to take control of the island and the United States must choose between armed resistance and acquiescence. Perhaps, as with the Cuban missile crisis 64 years ago, one or both sides will blink. But that is not guaranteed.

A war over Taiwan would be ruinous no matter which side prevailed. The concern is that the AI arms race, compounded by mounting fiscal strain on both AI powers, makes such a conflict steadily more likely. Washington’s proliferating military commitments in the Middle East and elsewhere may tempt China to act, since they have reduced the U.S. resources available for the war that would define the era. Rapid advances in AI further increase the possibility that the models themselves inadvertently push the two powers to war. In September 1983, the Soviet nuclear early warning system sent an alert that five U.S. intercontinental ballistic missiles had been launched against the Soviet Union. In that case, the human on duty, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov, correctly judged that the system had erred. But today’s autonomous operations unfold in seconds. It may be comforting to think that nuclear use still requires human authorization, but that is not true of some of the most advanced conventional weapons. The U.S. Navy’s Phalanx Close-In Weapon System and Aegis Combat System, for example, are both designed to detect and fire at enemy threats. And as nuclear strategists have pointed out, an AI hallucination might convince a future president to launch an unnecessary nuclear counterstrike.

Imperial history suggests two possible off-ramps. Any empire whose survival is staked on a single, exposed province or base is at risk. By 1941, for example, Britain’s Asian empire had become perilously overreliant on Suez and Singapore, making it vulnerable to Axis attack. In particular, Japan’s seizure of Malaya and the Dutch East Indies created a chronic shortage of rubber for the Allied war effort. Today’s equivalent is the American dependence on advanced Taiwanese chips. That explains the feverish U.S. push to construct TSMC facilities in Phoenix, Arizona; Kumamoto, Japan; and Dresden, Germany—partly bankrolled by public money and justified in the language of national security. The same scarcity that makes Taiwan worth fighting over also fuels the race to make it dispensable. Should Washington succeed in that goal, the island’s semiconductor monopoly will erode, and the Chinese case for military action will weaken. But the window in which Taiwan remains irreplaceable, and hence worth a war, is the one in which the world now finds itself. Whether China and the United States blunder into war before that window closes will be among the defining questions of the coming years. As with so much else in this contest, the answer turns on whether institutions can move at the speed the technology sets.

The alternative is for today’s AI empires to attempt, as their Cold War predecessors did half a century ago, what Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev called peaceful coexistence and American statesmen preferred to call détente. As the nuclear arms race grew more dangerous and expensive, Moscow and Washington negotiated a series of agreements—including the Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty, which sought to perpetuate the superpowers’ near-monopoly on the bomb, as well as the Antiballistic Missile and Strategic Arms Limitation Treaties—and established protocols for defusing crises. China and the United States would be well advised to seek similar understandings to limit the AI arms race. For example, they could agree not to release models above a defined capability level in domains such as cyber-offense or biological design, and they could set thresholds for open-weight releases. Like the 1975 Biological Weapons Convention—another agreement that helped maintain Cold War equilibrium—such agreements would be hard to police. But any agreement that specifies which uses of AI are taboo would be better than a status quo that prohibits none. Such a convention could form a plausible foundation for an AI détente. The challenge is to persuade China to accept limits when its AI models lag behind the most advanced U.S. models and when it has already formed the habit of engaging in AI-enabled cyberattacks.

Empires require enormous resources and capital to survive. They begin by exploiting economies of political and military scale and end by exhausting themselves defending their domains. AI is the most capital-intensive imperial project in history, undertaken by two superpowers that are already weighed down by debt. The lesson of past empires is not that either collision or decline is inevitable. It is that the conditions for both—a runaway arms race over a scarce resource, overstretched public financing, mounting domestic backlash, and rent-seeking elites afflicted by strategic myopia—are already present. Which AI empire will forge its power into a legitimate and durable order and which will repeat the age-old mistakes that lead to decline are being decided now. What is entirely new is the speed with which this process is unfolding: these questions are being settled at a pace that exceeds the ability of either political system to remain in control. Peaceful coexistence was hard enough in the nuclear age—and in the end, one of the superpowers declined and fell. It will be far harder to repeat this benign outcome in a world increasingly run by powerful and relentlessly self-improving machines.
