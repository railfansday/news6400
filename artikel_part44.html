#4250. Ethereum’s four-minute settlement plan could lower the cost of moving crypto

Ethereum researchers want to cut the wait for a transaction to become final from about 16 minutes to roughly four, a change that could make it cheaper to move money between blockchains.

Ben Edgington, who coordinates Ethereum’s fast-finality research, filed the draft Wednesday with fellow researchers Francesco D’Amato, Roberto Saltini, Yann Vonlanthen and Mikhail Kalinin.

Its initial target is a four-fold improvement, though the design is unfinished and has no activation date.

In the current mechanism, a transaction can land in an Ethereum block well before the network treats it as final, the point where validators, the computers that secure the network, commit to never rewriting it. Anyone moving funds off Ethereum has to decide when it’s safe to release money on the other side.

But sixteen minutes is a long time to have money in limbo.

Say someone sends $10,000 from Ethereum to another network. A transfer service can pay them right away from money it already holds there, then wait to get repaid once the original transaction settles. The customer gets their funds in seconds, and the service has $10,000 tied up until Ethereum is done.

Ethereum Foundation research counts that idle money as a real cost for these firms, and a shorter wait lets them reuse it sooner. In practice, a service fronting a steady $1 million an hour would have about $267,000 waiting on repayment with a 16-minute delay. At four minutes, however, that drops to roughly $67,000.

Stablecoin providers such as Circle CRCL, which issues the dollar-pegged USDC, already charges for speed. Its fast route from Ethereum currently costs 0.01%, or $1 per $10,000, before network and application charges. Its standard route carries no Circle transfer fee.

Circle also caps the total value of fast transfers waiting on final settlement, and each completed settlement frees up room for new ones.

The 16-minute wait comes down to how Ethereum’s validators vote. Each one casts a single vote that does two jobs at once, helping pick the next block and helping lock older blocks in for good. Validators take turns casting those votes, and a block only becomes final after two full rounds of everyone voting.

Both jobs share one vote so finality can only move as fast as that rotation. The proposal gives each job its own vote, so the locking-in can run on a faster clock that developers can keep tightening over time.

The researchers say splitting the votes would also make it easier to eventually swap in quantum-resistant signatures. Ethereum would need that upgrade if quantum computers ever get strong enough to break today’s cryptography.

However, four minutes would still be slow next to Solana, which switched on its Alpenglow upgrade Wednesday with a target of finality in about 150 milliseconds.

The draft leaves voting timings, penalties and parts of its security analysis unresolved as of Thursday. Edgington wrote that “many aspects remain TBD” and that the immediate aim was getting a proposal number — which puts the idea into Ethereum’s formal upgrade pipeline.

#4247. The Pleasure of Almost

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

My alarm goes off. I stumble out of bed to check my email. Before coffee!

I check my email because I am waiting for people to get back to me. I am waiting to hear back from publishers. I am waiting to hear back from television production companies. I am waiting to hear back from poetry contests. I am waiting to hear back from people who said they would get back to me, some of whom seemed quite excited about getting back to me as soon as humanly possible.

What I want is an answer. And I want that answer to be a "yes." I have been very clear with the universe about this. But lately I’ve begun to suspect I don’t want all those yeses quite the way I had thought I did.

I recently spoke with Irish philosopher Peter Rollins on my podcast Fifty Words for Snow, where my cohost and I discuss words without English equivalents. Peter suggested something that sounded insane: that there may actually be pleasure in not getting what I want.

The word Peter brought us was jouissance. In psychoanalysis, jouissance refers to enjoyment that involves frustration, discomfort, or even suffering—the sort of thing that makes you continue doing something you claim you would very much like to stop doing. We imagine satisfaction waits at the end of our pursuit. But wanting itself gives us something: anticipation, purpose, a reason to get up and see whether anyone has emailed.

Peter used an example from philosopher Blaise Pascal: Imagine you are going hunting. You rise before dawn, lace your boots, pack a lunch. You know where the rabbits cross. Today, you might catch one. Then someone pulls up and hands you a rabbit. Here, they say. Saved you the trouble.

If the rabbit were all you wanted, this would be excellent service. You could go home. But something has been taken from you along with the trouble: the morning ahead, the listening, the possibility of movement in the grass.

Of course, if someone told you there were no rabbits anywhere in the woods, that would ruin the morning too. You need a rabbit. And you need it some distance away.

For years, I have hunted the Great Yes: A publisher saying yes. A producer saying yes. Someone with the authority to confirm that all this effort is leading somewhere.

Occasionally, I have caught it. I have published books and written for television shows. I know the pleasure of getting the call. But then the job ends. The book gets published. And then I need another “yes,” another rabbit.

I mean, I do want the book deal. If a publisher is reading this, please don’t interpret my growing self-awareness as a withdrawal of my submission. But I’m beginning to suspect that part of what I want is the pursuit: finding tracks, losing the tracks, finding them again. I like the hope, the anticipation. I like believing something could happen overnight. The trouble comes when I spend the entire morning staring at an empty clearing. When I check my email twenty times. Before breakfast.

But lately I’ve begun to notice that, alongside the frustration, it’s true: There’s something I enjoy about the whole thing. The calculating. The maneuvering. The anticipation and avoidance of obstacles. The possibility that today I’ll spot the ears of that elusive “yes” just above the grass.

Recognizing that part of me enjoys the pursuit seems to make the desire less onerous. I still want to catch the rabbit. But I can recognize that I have the privilege of being on a hunt, of having something I want to follow.

At any moment, something might move in the brush.

On Oct. 19, 2016, the European Space Agency's ExoMars mission arrived at Mars!

ExoMars 2016 consists of the Trace Gas Orbiter and the Schiaparelli lander. The orbiter and lander traveled there together and separated on Oct. 16, three days before the orbiter entered orbit around Mars, and the lander touched down on the planet's surface. But something went wrong during the lander's descent, and it crashed into the ground.

Schiaparelli did not survive the accident, but the Trace Gas Orbiter is still functional. It is currently circling the planet, looking for methane and other gases in the Martian atmosphere that could be evidence for possible biological activity.

ExoMars 2016 was a landmark mission for the European Space Agency and designed to be the vanguard of a two-flight ExoMars program to send an orbiter, lander and rover to Mars.

Launched in March 2016, ExoMars 2016 aimed to achieve two goals for ESA and Russia. First, the mission aimed to use the Schiaparelli lander to test landing techniques that would be vital on a planned ExoMars 2018 rover. Second, the mission would deliver a critical orbiter around Mars to study the planet's atmosphere and serve as a communications link for that future Mars rover.

In the end, ExoMars 2016 was just a partial success, with ESA officials calling it "96% successful." due to the loss of the lander.

While Schiaparelli separated smoothly from the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter spacecraft, it stopped communicating with Earth just 1 minute before its planned landing on Oct. 19. Photos from NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter at Mars later revealed what appeared to be its crash site and, nearby, its jettisoned parachute. Officials suspect Schiaparelli did not fire its descent thrusters as designed in order to slow itself enough for a safe landing. A data glitch was cited as its cause.

The Trace Gas Orbiter, meanwhile, continues to study Mars to this day and has spent 10 years and counting studying the Martian atmosphere.

The launch of ExoMars 2018, which includes the Mars rover Rosalind Franklin, was delayed by the Schiaparelli failure, as well as the loss of its Russian Proton rocket when ESA severed its partnership with Russia over that country's war on Ukraine.

The ExoMars rover is now expected to launch no earlier than 2028, with a landing on Mars expected in 2030.

In 2026, another country took aim at Mars on this day.

The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency attempted to launch the ambitious Martian Moons eXploration mission (called MMX) to land a probe on the Mars moon Phobos and return samples to Earth.

#4244. An AI-racing drone beat the best humans and set a world record

Published Oct 14, 2026 2:00 PM EDT

Artificial intelligence is undeniably good at playing games, regardless of its larger societal and environmental impact. In 1997, IBM’s supercomputer Deep Blue famously beat chess grandmaster Garry Kasparov, and nearly two decades later, Google’s AlphaGo managed to defeat a human champion at the much more complex game Go. Since then, another AI seems to beat humans at incredibly complex video games like StarCraft or Dota 2 every few months. Impressive as these feats are, AI has mostly outperformed humans by making us play on its home turf, as in, simulated and structured environments. But now, a team of engineers at the Micro Air Vehicle Lab at the Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands has built a lightweight smart drone that’s fundamentally altering that dynamic.

The team entered its AI-powered quadcopter in a high-profile drone race in Abu Dhabi that pitted AI-piloted drones against human-piloted ones. The niche sport, known as first-person-view (FPV) drone racing, has pilots steer remote-controlled drones through a series of small gates at blistering speeds of 60 to 100 miles per hour. They control the drone by using goggles that give a direct feed into a camera attached to the drone’s front. From a human’s perspective, it’s as if they’re flying through the sky.

The team didn’t just fish the race. They took first place, besting several human FPV champions along the way. It marked the first time an AI-controlled drone beat a human pilot in an international racing competition. Another team from the University of Zurich built an AI-controlled drone that won a race against humans three years earlier, but only in a controlled lab setting. This more recent example showed that a simply trained machine, with minimal onboard sensors and hardware, could be inserted onto a non-controlled test track meant for human operators and still come out on top.

The team won this race in November 2025 but new details about the science behind this achievement are published today in the journal Science Robotics.

“Instead of giving the robot increasingly powerful sensors and computers, we wanted to find out how much intelligence we could extract from very limited resources,” Delft University aerospace engineer and study co-author Christophe De Wagter said in a statement. “Drone racing provided an extreme environment in which to test these ideas outside the laboratory. It pushed us to develop AI that was not only fast but also robust, and able to adapt when external reference measurements were unavailable.”

FPV drone racing isn’t for the faint of heart—or, more accurately, for those with a weak stomach.  Footage of drones flipping and somersaulting at extreme speeds can make even spectators feel sick. Pilots fly using a pair of joysticks that control the drone’s throttle, roll, pitch, and yaw. That human-and-machine team has to fly through several small race gates that make up a lap. The drone finishing with the fastest lap times wins. It’s an extreme sport where even a microsecond of a mistake can lead to a violent crash.

Though it’s not quite as popular as it was at its peak in the late 2010s, the sport still draws a considerable online audience. That’s pretty impressive, for a sport that only became possible thanks to simultaneous advances in several technologies. Namely lightweight batteries, miniaturized cameras, and the goggles and wireless video links that let pilots see what their drones see all had to come together for this extreme form of racing.

The fact that drone racing requires interacting with machines already equipped with sensors makes it a particularly interesting test bed for robotics and AI researchers. In 2022, the University of Zurich outfitted a drone with an AI model appropriately called Swift. That AI system collected real-time data pulled from the drone camera to “see” what it was seeing and to locate gates and avoid obstacles. An onboard neural network then parsed through all that in real time, making split-second decisions on the best routes to fly. Swift beat champion human pilots, a major feat at the time, but it did so on an indoor track measuring roughly 82-by-82-feet that the team had designed and trained the system for. When a condition even as subtle as the lighting changed, Swift’s performance suffered as a result.

Inspired by those results, the Delft team set out to build an AI racing drone that could better adapt to its environment. Like Swift, their machine called MonoRace uses a powerful AI model that interprets a video stream captured by the drone’s camera in real time. MonoRace also draws on an onboard inertial measurement unit (IMU), a sensor that tracks the drone’s acceleration and rotation. This IMU pulls data from onboard sensors as well as the camera, and helps the AI system better understand where the drone is position-wise on the track.

That understanding of place makes it more equipped to fly without crashing even in less familiar environments. It also improves the drone’s perception over a solely camera-based approach, which can struggle when camera data is less than stellar. Remember, all of this is happening very, very fast.

“Flying at very high speed with only one camera is challenging because the images are blurry, deformed, and the AI system has very little time to precisely determine where the drone is,” Delft University robotics researcher and study co-author Stavrow Bahnam said in a statement.  “We developed a robust way of combining what the camera sees with the drone’s own motion information, allowing it to navigate the track at high speed without GPS.”

What sets MonoRace apart from complex AI systems running on larger vehicles or humanoid robots is how stripped down it is. The entire system has to run on a tiny computer on the drone, light enough not to slow the drone down and efficient enough not to introduce lag. And yet, it’s still quite powerful, with the AI issuing an estimated 500 commands every second. Rather than train the model on one specific track, they trained it to learn how to adapt using a reinforcement learning approach. After many attempts of trial and error in a simulated environment, MonoRace figured out the best ways to quickly solve navigation problems and then apply that in the real world.

“Instead of first planning a desired flight path and conventional commands, our neural network learns to directly control the motors,” study co-author and Delft University Aerospace Engineering PhD candidate Robin Ferede added in a statement.

The team put MonoRace to the real test last November at the Abu Dhabi Autonomous Racing League, a major competition where some of the best human pilots battle to out. During its flights, MonoRace topped speeds of roughly 62 miles per hour as it looped, dove, and zipped through gates. It managed to beat out every other AI racing team in the competition, and also boasted a better time than three former human world champion pilots.

According to the team, these results make MonoRace the first AI-piloted drone to beat human pilots in an international racing competition, and  could have an impact on  drone racing and  physical AI more broadly.  With self-driving vehicles and even some humanoid robots entering larger-scale production, there’s an increasingly high demand to build AI systems that better grasp how to move about the physical world without barreling into everything around it. A drone that can do that while performing acrobatics at more than 60 miles per hour suggests that engineers are, at minimum, heading in the right direction.

So far, that hasn’t resulted in larger robots suddenly superseding humans at sports. The fastest robot sprinters, though admittedly quick in short bursts, also tend to crash into walls and burst into flames.

#4240. Resistant Starch Supplements Could Help Your Gut Health. But So Can This Low-Cost Fix.

Hot potato, cold potato: The hottest trend in blood sugar regulation—explained.

ADMITTEDLY BIZARRE QUESTION: What if cooling your rice, potatoes, and pasta before eating them could change the way your body digests them, potentially improving their effects on blood sugar, gut health, and even weight loss?

While the idea of refrigerating your dinner before eating it sounds like another ridiculous nutrition trend, this one (gasp!) actually has some legitimate research behind it.

It all comes down to resistant starch, a type of carbohydrate that escapes digestion in the small intestine and has shown to have some interesting effects on gut health, blood sugar, and more.

MOST STARCHES (AKA complex carbohydrates) are broken down into glucose in the small intestine. That glucose is then absorbed into the cells and used for energy.

Resistant starch, as its name suggests, resists that process, passing through the small intestine intact. Once in the colon, it’s fermented by good gut bacteria. Although technically a starch, resistant starch functions similarly to dietary fiber in that its fermentation confers health benefits to the host. There are five types of resistant starch; two are found in food, the remaining three are created through cooking and cooling or chemical modification.

WHEN YOU COOL or freeze starchy foods like bread and cooked pasta, rice, and potatoes, the temperature drop causes some of the starch molecules in these foods to rearrange themselves in a process called retrogradation. This makes them more difficult for digestive enzymes to break down.

The amount of resistant starch formed depends on the food, its starch composition, how it’s cooked, and how long it’s cooled. Reheating a starchy food does not destroy the resistant starch that has formed, so yes, you can toast your frozen bread and still theoretically reap the benefits.

SOME OF THE strongest evidence for resistant starch’s health benefits comes from its role as a prebiotic, meaning that it serves as food for beneficial gut bacteria. When these gut bacteria ferment resistant starch in the colon, they produce short-chain fatty acids including butyrate, acetate, and propionate. These are important for maintaining the integrity of the gut barrier, modulating inflammation, and supporting intestinal health.

Resistant starch has also shown some potential with blood sugar regulation. Because resistant starch isn’t readily turned into glucose, replacing some digestible starch with resistant starch can reduce the amount of glucose entering the bloodstream after a meal. Research found that this can be potentially helpful for people who have diabetes or prediabetes.

There are plenty of social media videos describing how cooking and cooling your starch can help with weight loss, but these claims are mostly overstated.

Although resistant starch provides fewer calories due to its poor absorption in the gut, it’s important to understand that in the majority of cases, this doesn’t translate into a significant calorie deficit. Only a portion of the starch in a dish converts to resistant starch, so the actual calorie differential between hot and cooled starch is unlikely to be calorically meaningful.

There’s also this: Cooling your carbohydrates also doesn’t magically cancel out their calories or make them into low-carb foods.

ASIDE FROM COOKING and cooling your starchy foods, there are foods that already contain resistant starch. These include green bananas, Jerusalem artichokes, beans and lentils, oats and barley, whole grains, and seeds.

Resistant starch supplements are also available, but it’s worth keeping in mind the fermentation process that resistant starch undergoes in the colon can produce side effects such as gas, bloating, and cramps. Individual tolerance levels vary.

FOODS THAT CONTAIN resistant starch are also high in nutrients and fiber, which most of us don’t eat enough of. Adding more of these foods to your diet is a good idea for most healthy people, but resistant starch isn’t a miracle ingredient that is going to improve your health on its own. If you enjoy leftover or cooled starches, that’s great. If not, they aren’t an essential for health.

To prevent foodborne illness, always cool cooked starches in the refrigerator.

If you want to increase your resistant starch intake, start with whole food sources such as beans and lentils, whole grains, and potatoes. These foods also offer nutrition benefits beyond their resistant starch content.

Not everything we eat has to have health benefits, and there’s really no benefit to overloading your system with resistant starch. Resistant starch can be one part of a healthy diet, but not the only part.

Regular aerobic exercise may help postmenopausal women strengthen executive function, according to new research.

The study, published in the Journal of Alzheimer’s Disease on August 1, suggests that cognitive benefits may appear within 3 to 6 months. This highlights that exercise can be a practical strategy for preserving brain health after menopause.

Brain health strategies are crucial during and after menopause, with two-thirds of cases of Alzheimer’s disease occurring in women. Menopause can influence cognitive decline, as well as resilience. 

Dung Trinh, MD, a physician at MemorialCare Medical Group and Chief Medical Officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, said the findings are encouraging, but noted some limitations. 

“This was a secondary analysis of a relatively small study, so I would view it as important evidence that deserves confirmation rather than as definitive proof,” he told Healthline.

Aerobic exercise improves executive function after menopause

The study included 93 cognitively healthy females ages 20 to 67, 43% of whom were postmenopausal. Each participant had below-average cardiorespiratory fitness and didn’t exercise regularly. 

The participants were randomly assigned to groups for either a 6-month aerobic exercise program or a stretching-and-toning program. 

The greatest cognitive benefits were seen in individuals who engaged in 45 minutes of aerobic activity four times a week, who saw improvements in executive function. These cognitive gains were first observed at 3 months and strengthened through the remainder of the program. 

Executive function involves working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control. In daily life, this includes managing tasks, making decisions, staying organized, and maintaining independence. 

“Stretching and mobility work have important benefits — namely, flexibility, comfort, range of motion, balance, and stress reduction, to name a few. However, aerobic activity produces a more sustained cardiorespiratory and metabolic stimulus,” said Kecia Gaither, MD, a double board certified OB-GYN and maternal fetal medicine specialist, and associate professor of Clinical Obstetrics and Gynecology at Weill Cornell Medicine. Gaither wasn’t involved in the study.

What counts as moderate aerobic activity? 

Aerobic activity is rhythmic and continuous, increasing your breathing and heart rate. Moderate intensity means you can talk but not sing while participating in the activity. 

Examples of aerobic activities include: 

  • brisk walking
  • cycling
  • water aerobics
  • dancing
  • active gardening, like mowing, raking, or digging
  • doubles tennis or pickleball

How exercise benefits brain health after menopause

Aerobic exercise can affect the brain in various ways.

“Exercise may help by improving cardiovascular fitness, reducing vascular risk factors, and supporting brain plasticity, all of which are relevant because vascular and metabolic health are closely tied to later cognitive decline,” Trinh said.

While aerobic exercise doesn’t prevent Alzheimer’s disease or slow the progression of dementia, it can help improve overall cognitive function as you age.

One of the ways aerobic exercise can affect the brain is in its structure. It can help protect brain regions such as the hippocampus, which is important for memory.

Aerobic exercise can also benefit the brain’s white matter. Regular aerobic activity may support connections in the brain that help with focus and processing information.

Short-term workouts can also have immediate cognitive benefits, like boosting thinking skills right after you work out. 

Gaither told Healthline that aerobic exercise can be extremely helpful in multiple areas, such as:

  • Cerebrovascular function: Aerobic exercise increases cardiac output and challenges vascular regulation. It may improve endothelial function, cerebral perfusion, and increase the brain’s capacity to deliver oxygen and glucose where neural activity is greatest.
  • Metabolic health: Aerobic exercise can improve blood pressure, glucose handling, and lipid profiles — these same cardiometabolic factors are strongly entwined with small vessel brain disease and later cognitive risk.

  • Brain pathways: Aerobic exercise can help to improve pathways involved in synaptic remodeling, neuronal survival, and learning.

Cognitive decline appears to occur more frequently in women than in men. This suggests that hormonal factors likely contribute to this decline. 

Cognitive complaints are more common among postmenopausal women than premenopausal women. This may be due in part to reduced ovarian function and decreased serum estrogen levels. 

This leads to menopausal symptoms that have an emphasis on vasomotor symptoms. Vasomotor symptoms include hot flashes, sweating, and dizziness. 

It’s possible that estrogen has a neuroprotective role in the body, which may also help explain why cognitive decline is more common in women, especially those who are postmenopausal.

Trinh noted that the study highlights a gap in research: Menopause status is often overlooked in exercise and brain-health trials, even though women have a higher lifetime burden of dementia.

#4233. Tired of Swiping? What Evolution Suggests Instead

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

In my last post, I explored why dating apps leave many people exhausted, pointing to a mismatch between our evolved minds and endless choice. Here, I look at who feels that exhaustion most, who the apps still serve well, why run clubs (or any club, really) may feel different, and what an exhausted dater can do.

Dating exhaustion is not the same for everyone. Take, for example, the results from a 2022 Pew Research Center survey of 6,034 people in the USA. Of those who had used a dating site or app in the past year, about 54% of women felt overwhelmed by the number of messages they received, compared with 25% of men, while 64% of men felt insecure about receiving too few messages, compared with 40% of women. Among everyone who had ever dated online, women were also less likely than men to describe their experiences as positive (48% versus 57%).

These differences fit an evolutionary view. One of the core tenets of the field is that the sex investing more in offspring becomes a limiting resource, courted by the other sex and choosier about whom it accepts (Trivers, 1972); in humans, this logic has been used to explain why women tend to be the choosier sex. An app that gives many women a flood of interest and many men a trickle may simply magnify an asymmetry already present.

There is a competitive side, too. Much of my own research concerns intrasexual competition, meaning competition with members of one’s own sex for mates. In one early study, I found that women rated other women’s faces as less attractive when they were near peak fertility, while their ratings of men’s faces did not change (Fisher, 2004). Apps make the competition feel ever-present, given that every profile you admire is presumably being admired by others as well. I would predict that the sense of a crowded field adds to the exhaustion, but this idea needs proper testing.

None of these findings means the apps are failing everyone. By 2017, meeting online had become the most common way heterosexual couples in the USA met, accounting for an estimated 39% of couples (Rosenfeld et al., 2019). In Pew’s 2022 survey, about one in 10 partnered adults had met their partner online, rising to about one in five among those under 30 and about one in four among lesbian, gay, and bisexual adults. LGB users were also more likely than straight users to describe their experiences as positive (61% versus 53%). For people whose local pool of potential partners is small, a wider net may be helpful.

I recently spent a month in Chile as part of a partnership-building grant. While I was there, everyone in the university labs I interacted with seemed to have taken up running. Many group members were running long distances or even running marathons. Truthfully, I was surprised. They were all using Strava, the fitness-tracking app, which reported a 59% increase in running clubs on its platform in 2024. In Strava's own survey of more than 5,000 active people, more than half of Gen Z respondents said social connection was their main reason for joining a fitness group, and four times as many respondents preferred to meet new people through a fitness group than at a bar. My experiences in Chile certainly point to this change.

But why are we turning to fitness groups when we want to meet new people? A group or club shrinks the pool of new people to a number of faces you can actually come to know, and it provides repeated exposure, which tends to build liking. In a classic study, Moreland and Beach (1992) had four women of similar appearance attend a large college course 0, 5, 10, or 15 times without interacting with anyone. At the end of the term, students reported greater attraction to the women who had attended more often and saw them as more similar to themselves. The attraction measured was not specifically romantic, and those women never spoke to anyone, whereas a run club involves real conversation, but the study shows how much familiarity alone can do.

Shared activities also reveal what a profile cannot, such as how someone treats other people, copes with a hard hill, or makes a group laugh. Joel and colleagues’ work suggests that compatibility tends to show itself only once people meet. Whether run clubs actually produce more lasting relationships than apps do is, as yet, unknown, but I suspect the answer is “yes.”

#4228. MIT Researchers Found That Listening to This Kind of Noise Might Help “Flush” Your Brain

The latest study has some intriguing results.

Your brain juggles a lot of tasks at once, from critical thinking and managing your body's movements to regularly cleaning itself. But new research suggests that you may be able to support your brain’s waste clean-up process by doing something super simple: listening to noise while you sleep.

There's a lot that happened in this study, including a very strategic use of pink noise to try to rid the brain of toxins and possibly even support deeper sleep. This is also one small study, making it a stretch to say flipping on an ambient noise machine at night will make you smarter or healthier.

But if you already enjoy listening to something like pink noise at night, it's helpful to know that there may be an added perk for your brain. Here's what the study found, plus why a sleep medicine specialist says more work is needed.

Meet the experts: Joshua Levitt, PhD, researcher and study co-author and Christopher Winter, MD, a sleep medicine specialist, neurologist, and author of The Sleep Solution: Why Your Sleep Is Broken and How to Fix It.

For the study, which was published in the journal Science Translational Medicine, researchers from MIT recruited 14 healthy volunteers and monitored their brain activity while they napped in an MRI scanner.

The researchers used closed-loop auditory stimulation, which is a brain modulation technique that detects changes in brain activity and then delivers a low-volume acoustic cue (in this case, pink noise) to enhance or reset brain activity. For this study, participants were specifically subject to a 50-millisecond (or 0.05 second) burst of pink noise when their brains showed a peak in EEG slow waves. (EEG slow waves include delta waves, the slowest and highest-amplitude brain waves that are a defining characteristic of deep sleep.)

The researchers discovered that the short burst of pink noise was linked to an increase in the amplitude of delta waves, along with the flow of cerebrospinal fluid, or the protective clear fluid surrounding the brain and spinal cord that’s thought to help get rid of waste.

The findings can be "explored as a potential translational tool in clinical populations," the researchers wrote, which basically means there may be some health-boosting value to the general public, but exactly what isn’t clear yet.

The study didn’t look into that, but there are some theories. The EEG slow waves are probably the biggest factor, according to Joshua Levitt, PhD, researcher and study co-author.

"They represent moments of widespread, cyclical activation and deactivation of the neurons in our brain," he says. "When parts of the brain are less active, they consume less oxygen, and the body shunts oxygen-supplying blood flow away from them." When this is widespread, which is what happens when there are slow waves, it means there's a large flow of blood away from the brain, Levitt says.

"Because our skull is a fixed volume at constant pressure, if one liquid flows out, another must flow in to take its place," Levitt says. "So, when blood flows out following deactivation, cerebrospinal fluid flows in." There, it can help deliver nutrition to your brain and clean out waste.

Levitt says the findings are less about the pink noise itself and more about the technique of closed-loop auditory stimulation. "We chose pink noise to better match with some previous literature," he explains.

Closed-loop auditory stimulation is "basically playing sounds at night—it could be white noise or other frequency tones—that seem to affect the electrical signature of sleep, in this case, slow wave sleep," says Christopher Winter, MD, a sleep medicine specialist, neurologist, and author of The Sleep Solution: Why Your Sleep Is Broken and How to Fix It.

Closed-loop auditory stimulation is an area of neuroscience that's being heavily researched. But Dr. Winter points out that the practical real-world benefits of this—such as having enhanced memory or preventing disease—are still up for debate. "The theory is that playing the right tone/frequency at the right time in sleep will elevate the quality of sleep, [or] enhance deep sleep,” Dr. Winter says, noting skeptically that it’s often sold as biohacking or a way to extend longevity.

#4225. Can Statins Give Your Diet Some Wiggle Room?

Doctors explain just how many liberties it’s OK to take.

IF YOU’RE TAKING a statin to lower your cholesterol, you may have wondered whether the medication gives you a little more freedom at the dinner table. After all, if a pill is lowering your low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol—the so-called “bad” cholesterol—maybe that means a burger, steak, or plate of fries won’t clog your arteries as much. It’s an idea that guys have discussed online: if a statin is keeping your cholesterol in check, why not eat whatever you want? On Reddit, posts occasionally come up about using statins to intentionally order fast food guilt-free. Have people discovered a junk food loophole?

Surprisingly, docs say they might have a point. When you’re on a statin, you don’t have to stomach a bland plate of grilled chicken and veggies for the rest of your life. The trick is to not go too far.

Juan Carlos Rozo, MD, cardiologist at Houston Methodist The Woodlands Hospital, says the goal isn’t to eliminate the foods you enjoy. Rather, use statins as a “seat belt” to keep yourself maneuvering safely when you start to veer off-lane. More on this below.

STATINS ARE A class of prescription medications that lower LDL cholesterol. Common examples include atorvastatin (Lipitor), rosuvastatin (Crestor), and simvastatin (Zocor).

The reason lowering LDL matters is that when too much circulates in the bloodstream, it could contribute to the buildup of plaque inside artery walls. Dr. Rozo says too much plaque increases the risk of heart attack and stroke. One factor that can push LDL levels higher is diet. We’re talking foods high in saturated fat, such as butter, full-fat dairy, sausage, bacon, and ribeye, Dr. Rozo explains.

Fortunately, statins help by lowering the amount of cholesterol the liver produces. They do this by blocking an enzyme called HMG-CoA reductase, which plays a key role in cholesterol production. Arash Bereliani, MD, a cardiologist and medical director of the Beverly Hills Institute for Cardiology and Preventative Medicine, says the liver pulls more LDL out of the bloodstream, lowering the amount circulating in the body. Depending on the statin and dose, research shows that LDL can fall by roughly 30 to 50 percent or more. That’s a big deal, but LDL isn’t the whole story when it comes to your ticker.

Statins are a powerful tool for lowering the risk of heart disease, but while a statin can significantly lower one important risk factor—LDL cholesterol—it doesn’t address everything that contributes to heart disease.

Think of your heart health as a dashboard with several warning lights. You have one for LDL, blood pressure, blood sugar, and triglycerides. A statin can do an excellent job of bringing down LDL when it’s elevated. But warning lights for other health issues can still pop up because of the problems high-calorie junk food brings.

While we might all wish statins can excuse all the greasy, fried foods we want to eat, that reality is fictional. But statins do give you a little more leeway. Cardiologists tell MH that you aren’t banned from eating a burger, milkshake, or fried chicken sandwich.

“An occasional indulgent meal isn’t going to undo good habits,” Dr. Bereliani says. So, if you love pizza and burgers, you can still have them—just maybe once a week in reasonable portions rather than making them staples of your diet.

A seatbelt can significantly reduce your risk of serious injury if something goes wrong, but you wouldn’t put one on and throw safe driving out the window. A statin works much the same way. It can meaningfully lower your cardiovascular risk, but it doesn’t eliminate the other risks that come with an unhealthy diet.

THE BIGGER GOAL is to make the foods you eat most often do the heavy lifting for your health. According to Dr. Bereliani, that means building your everyday meals around high-fiber foods. Think of oats, beans, and lentils, along with vegetables, fruit, nuts, olive oil, and fatty fish, while cutting back on processed meats, fried foods, and refined carbohydrates like white bread, white rice, and pastries.

That’s ultimately why a balanced diet and a statin work best together. A heart-healthy diet can help lower LDL on its own, while a statin can bring it down further, giving you more protection than either approach alone, says Dr. Bereliani. The key is to see medication and healthy habits as complementary, not competing approaches. “Statins reduce risk significantly, but they don’t eliminate it, because lifestyle is what addresses the rest of the picture.”

#4222. The Beauty of Limitations

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Scarcity is a pervasive condition of human existence. Everyday circumstances of limited resources (money and time) can make individuals experience a sense of scarcity. Their scarcity functions like an obstacle to goal pursuit, which intensifies the value of goal.

Scarcity prioritizes our choices and it can make us more effective. The time pressure of a deadline focuses our attention on using what we have most effectively. Distractions are less tempting. When we have little time left, we try to get more out of every moment. For example, we are more frugal with the toothpaste as the tube starts to run empty, and college seniors tend to get the most out of their time before graduation.

Scarcity makes everything more desirable. We will enjoy our lunch more for being deprived of breakfast—literally, hunger is the best sauce. Sometimes people want things precisely because they cannot have them: “The grass is always greener on the other side of the fence." Playing hard to get is a most effective strategy for attracting a partner, especially in the context of long-term love, in which a person wishes to be sure of their partner’s commitment. A “hard to get” player likes to appear busy, create intrigue, and keep their suitors guessing.

Scarcity also contributes to an interesting and a meaningful life. A life without limits would lose the beauty of its moments and become boring. Abundant time destroys the urgency for anything and need for quick action. Research shows that reminding individuals of the reality of death increases the value of life. Death represents life’s scarcity, which may influence subsequent efforts to protect the threatening aspects of death.

Midlife often heightens the feeling that there is not enough time left in life to waste. We overcome the illusion that we can be anything, do anything, and experience everything. We restructure our lives around the needs that are essential. This means that we accept that there will be many things we won’t do in our lives. Overcoming a midlife crisis requires accepting that life is finite (Strenger, 2011).

Like scarcity, impermanence refers to the concept of limitation. There is a beauty in impermanence. Knowing that a moment won’t last allows us to cherish it more fully. In fact, beauty is heightened by the very fact that it cannot last. Cherry blossoms, mist, rain, snow, autumn leaves, and other fleeting materials or phenomena especially signify aging and transience.

Impermanence is core to Japanese culture (Juniper, 2003). The concept of wabi-sabi, central to Japanese aesthetics, embraces the transient and imperfect nature of life, emphasizing simplicity, humility and the beauty found in natural flaws and impermanence. In this context, the concept of impermanence highlights the transient nature of all things. When many Japanese speak of beauty, they link it directly to the transience of all things: An object that lasts forever has far less aesthetic appeal than something fragile and short-lived.

This worldview frames impermanence not as a limitation but as a source of aesthetic. It offers a framework in which change is not feared but embraced, allowing impermanence to inspire wonder, reflection and a deeper connection to life’s fleeting moments. Impermanence, in that sense, makes it possible for beauty to exist.

There’s a potential solution for women struggling with orgasms—you’ve just never heard of it. Until now.

One day, not long after her 43rd birthday, Meg woke up and her orgasm was gone. Poof. Disappeared.

“The things that used to work suddenly didn’t work anymore,” she says. “It was hard to get excited, and even when I’d get turned on, it felt like the volume had been turned down so low it was on mute.”

She Googled some of her other symptoms—which ranged from dryness to feeling almost as if she were being stabbed in the vagina whenever her boyfriend touched her—and self-diagnosed perimenopause. Her doctor prescribed hormone replacement therapy, but Meg says it didn’t do anything for the sex part. She couldn’t bring herself to make an appointment specifically to complain about that; instead, Meg went down more Internet rabbit holes, finding her way to Reddit and, eventually, a telehealth company that prescribed a topical cream she was told to rub into her vagina, particularly the clitoris, about a half-hour before sex. It contained sildenafil, which increases blood flow—and is the key active ingredient in Viagra.

“It worked, thank god,” Meg says. “But why did I have to find out about this from Reddit?”

Indeed. It’s estimated that one in five women will experience female sexual arousal disorder (FSAD), defined as a distress-causing inability to attain or maintain sexual excitement, which often includes lack of lubrication and decreased genital swelling. And although sildenafil is, of course, approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat erectile dysfunction­—it famously received “Priority Review” status in 1997 and then was given the green light six months later—the drug is not approved as a topical cream for women. In fact, there are zero FDA-approved treatments for FSAD.

That means women like Meg often have to DIY their sexual health care, including seeking out information about “scream creams,” the colloquial term for topical formulations applied to the clitoris and genital area. The creams are typically available from telehealth platforms such as Wisp, which debuted its sildenafil formulation—dubbed OMG! Cream—in 2022.

According to the company, demand (including repeat sales) was so strong that in 2025, Wisp launched an extra-strength formulation with 3 percent sildenafil compared with 1 percent in the original. (Trying the 1 percent version is not a prerequisite for trying the extra-strength, says the company’s lead nurse practitioner, Andrea Sleeth. “Some people just want to start with 3 percent right off the bat.”)

Before the telehealth boom, you had to be lucky enough to see a clinician who specialized in sexual health and also worked with a compounding pharmacy that could mix up a cream for you. These people existed—for example, Anna Cabeca, DO, a triple-board-certified ob-gyn in Dallas, has been offering patients what she calls a “joy gel” for more than 20 years—but even in 2026, prescribers remain few and far between.

Before you can even think about treating FSAD, you need a diagnosis—and many women slip through the cracks at this stage. Few doctors feel comfortable diagnosing patients with the condition, says Maria Sophocles, MD, a gynecologist and the author of The Bedroom Gap. More than half of ob-gyn residents were not able to describe the disorders of sexual function or list common medications that affect sexual function, according to one study. That’s because most residencies have, at best, limited education around female sexual dysfunction, says Tami Rowen, MD, an ob-gyn in San Francisco and president-elect of the International Society for the Study of Women’s Sexual Health.

This is mainly due to a lack of investment from the medical establishment; the field of sexual medicine “does not reimburse particularly well,” says Dr. Rowen. “So, to be a specialist within an academic center [and] to maintain the goals of the enterprise, you end up doing a lot of obstetrics and other types of gynecology outside the realm of sexual medicine.”

There’s also the matter of bias. Doctors can “bring their own family, sociocultural, religious layering of their own sexual beliefs” into the exam room, says Dr. Sophocles. “That can make sexual questions or issues in their patients something they really either avoid asking about or are sort of demure in terms of talking about.” Some women feel dismissed by their doctors’ answers (“Of course, you’re not having good sex; you have three kids”). Others feel embarrassed—or the secondhand embarrassment of their providers (again, partly due to the lack of education)—to ask questions in the first place. “This happens very commonly, and it shuts women down,” says Dr. Sophocles. “They just blame themselves.”

Beyond having sildenafil as the active ingredient, scream creams aren’t standardized, and because of that, results vary. Some women say the creams live up to their name, while others say the only thing they arouse is intense frustration that nothing seems to be happening. “It was so disappointing,” says one user, who tried a cream about six times—or until she got so annoyed, she threw it out. “I wasn’t expecting anything miraculous, but I got nothing but the faintest tingle.” (Women are apparently frustrated with the cream’s results often enough that the telehealth platform Strut published “Scream Cream Not Working?,” a long blog post with six application tips, which include trying the cream multiple times and not expecting it to “do 100 percent of the work.”)

While the creams aren’t recommended if you’re pregnant, breastfeeding, or have a heart condition, they have no known side effects beyond irritation (relatively rare), making them low risk and potentially high reward.

But just how high remains a matter of debate, even among sexual health specialists. These creams are typically prescribed to women for whom the struggle to climax is something new, often related to menopause. But they’re usually not the first line of defense for Lori Davis, a doctor of nursing practice and a sex counselor in Ithaca, New York. Although she’s been prescribing arousal creams (Davis’s preferred term) to patients for more than 10 years, she often prescribes testosterone cream first, which can take several months to take effect.

In addition to the T cream, she’ll often prescribe an arousal cream to patients who come in saying their orgasm is meh, that it’s “really hard to get there, and when I get there, it’s sort of like, ‘what’s the point?’  ” Davis says. She’ll also offer the cream—as a kind of Hail Mary to patients who are really struggling—as another way to enhance the neurofeedback from their clitoris to their brain, and to work with that natural arousal process. “I think it’s worth a trial for any woman who has orgasm trouble,” Davis says. “The only thing you’re doing is wasting money if it doesn’t work.” (The pharmacy Davis uses to compound hers charges about $90 for a tube with 30 applications.)

Dr. Rowen has also been prescribing compounded creams to her patients for 13 years, usually to those experiencing a loss of sensation, often because of age. “I would love for it to work better,” she says. It can also be “a little bit of a hard sell” to patients, Dr. Rowen adds, since you have to apply the cream about a half-hour before sex. (The same can be said for oral sildenafil—men are told to pop their little blue pill 30 minutes to an hour before sex for optimum results.)

But that added time may actually help some women who struggle with sexual desire in addition to arousal. Sameena Rahman, MD, a gynecologist and menopause specialist in Chicago, says that every person she talks to about their lack of desire usually has some form of arousal dysfunction—and that women, especially in long-term relationships, tend to lose spontaneous desire. Instead, they have responsive desire, meaning they have to get aroused before they have the desire for sex. Scream creams can kick-start that arousal process by increasing blood flow to the vulva, and particularly the clitoris (why that little nub becomes engorged), creating the conditions for responsive desire to show up. “When you can fix an element of arousal, you’re actually fixing their whole sexual function experience,” she says.

As with many aspects of women’s health, the creams also suffer from a research problem—essentially, the lack of it. Unfortunately, this isn’t unusual. “In the field of sex medicine, we don’t have a lot of research on most of the things that we’re working with,” says Davis. Add to that the fact that orgasms are multisensory and multifactorial and include a strong psychological component. As a result, they are not easily replicable, even in the same woman.

Studies involving sex are difficult to conduct and present novel problems, something you can glimpse even in the researchers’ neutral clinical language. In one Chinese study, women were “instructed to attempt vaginal intercourse five times between visits 2 and 3 [a four-week period]”—but what if they didn’t feel like it or, say, had had a fight with their partner? Another piece of research involving a sildenafil cream stopped far short of the desired sample size partly because it was so hard to sign up volunteers. Women didn’t want to tell their partners that they were using the cream, but the study required that partners answer questions about any adverse effects they may have had from contact with the cream within 72 hours of any sexual event.

What little research there is tends to show a strong placebo effect (which isn’t unusual—in a meta-analysis of studies involving oral sildenafil and other PDE5 inhibitors for men, the authors concluded that participants receiving a placebo exhibited gains in erectile function). In the Chinese study, women using the strongest cream formulation achieved an “arousal success rate” of over 50 percent, but the rate was still 33 percent for patients who got the placebo cream. Another study showed no “statistically significant differences” in the mean number of “satisfactory sexual events” between women who used the cream and those who used the placebo. It doesn’t help that critics charge that it is the rubbing-in action, not the creams themselves, that gets results, and at least one study instructed participants “to engage in foreplay to facilitate the action of the medication”—but defenders are doubtful the rubbing is the secret sauce. “Massaging it in definitely helps with blood flow, but that’s where the controlled trials make a difference,” Dr. Cabeca says. “In those, we definitely see the benefit with the ingredient compared to the placebo.”

Kelly Casperson, MD, a urologist who specializes in female sexual health, points to the placebo effect seen in both Viagra and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRI) studies. (In the latter, the placebo response is estimated to account for up to approximately 75 percent of the overall clinical improvement.) She says, “We don’t get rid of all SSRIs because there’s a significant placebo effect. Picking on women’s sexual health because of the placebo effect is gender bias.”

And doctors trained in treating sexual dysfunction know sildenafil—actual sildenafil, not the placebo—makes a real difference for some women. Daré Bioscience, a company focused on women’s health, did some initial testing on a sildenafil cream to see if, and how quickly, it increased blood flow. The company stopped the study after six women because it was clear it was having the intended effect. (If you need further proof of the difficulty of testing sex-related products, and of lining up volunteers, this particular test used a heat-sensing camera, while the study volunteers, feet in stirrups [and hands to themselves], watched curated porn for women. The women had to agree to be tested three times, randomized between the product, no cream, and a placebo cream.) And Dr. Cabeca, who uses a compounding pharmacy to make her “joy gels,” says some patients can get a similar effect from a cheaper version that uses a mix of non-sildenafil vasodilators, including L-arginine. If that doesn’t work, she gives them one with the sildenafil added, which does the trick for about half her patients who try it.

Many doctors who focus on women’s sexual health say they began working with compounding pharmacies in part because of the long history of general dismissiveness toward their specialty. Dr. Cabeca, for example, says she got into it in the early 2000s because other doctors were telling her breast cancer patients “just be thankful you’re alive—come back next year to see if the cancer comes back.” (Breast cancer treatments can drastically lower estrogen levels, leading to vaginal dryness, which can make sex painful.)

This was back when hormone replacement therapy was villainized—a result of research revealed to be seriously flawed—so Dr. Cabeca got into bioidentical hormones and sexual health issues. “My approach was to restore the vulva, the vaginal tissue, the vaginal health, because that is key to the quality of life of a woman, especially as we age,” she says. “We didn’t have options, so I had to compound.”

Scream creams aren’t a perfect solution, but they are a relatively easy one to try. FDA approval would bring much wider access (and, potentially, insurance coverage); however, the approval is slow in coming (no pun intended). Daré Bioscience has been working on a sildenafil cream—one with a novel absorption technology—since 2018. In 2023, it completed its phase 2 trial, which is one of the biggest hurdles for a new drug application because that is where companies have to show the drug can actually work. This one showed improvement in sexual satisfaction, arousal sensation, lubrication, and achievement and pleasure of orgasm—though it fell short of being statistically significant. (One of the biggest takeaways from this trial was that there “were no statistically significant differences in the mean number of satisfactory sexual events between sildenafil cream and placebo cream users.” This standard has been criticized, however, by women’s sexual health advocates because it’s a very male-centered metric.)

But—far from the Viagra fast-track—three years later the company is still in discussions with the FDA about what would be an appropriate phase 3 study design. The argument isn’t about the validity of the questionnaires around arousal sensations (these are similar to the kinds of questions men in the Viagra study answered about their erections). According to Sabrina Martucci Johnson, Daré’s founder, the FDA wants a component to the assessment that shows whether the drug improves the interpersonal distress that is a criterion for the diagnosis of female sexual arousal disorder—a completely subjective measure. But the FDA required no such thing of Viagra, even though causing such distress is a criterion for the diagnosis of erectile dysfunction too.

Johnson says she doesn’t see this as a function of the FDA’s reluctance to move on women’s health issues. (The organization’s history of sluggishly approving other sexual health medications designed for women suggests otherwise.) Instead, she speaks with the diplomacy of someone who has to keep working with the FDA: “I think it’s really challenging for any company in any disease area when you’re trying to innovate where there’s no already FDA-approved product you can point to as a road map.” Dr. Casperson is more forthright: “It’s embarrassing,” she says of the FDA’s slowness. (The FDA said its policy was not to comment on new drug applications.)

Some doctors say if Daré can clear the FDA hurdles and the cream is approved, it will make a huge difference in awareness and use of the product. “It’ll make it massively easier and less complicated for doctors to prescribe it,” Dr. Rowen says.

Dr. Rahman, who describes herself as a “true believer” in the cream, takes it one step further. “I think this is probably going to be a game changer for a lot of people,” she says.

She isn’t the only one who thinks so. Daré says that when it published the results of its phase 2 trial in 2024, doctors reached out asking for the formula, wondering if they could give it to a compounding pharmacy. The enthusiastic response pushed Daré to find a compounding pharmacy itself. Since February, doctors have been able to write prescriptions for the cream, called Dare to Play, though it won’t be dispensed until later this year.

Patients “are waiting,” says Dr. Sophocles. “In a clinic like mine, they all want it. They’re all excited about the possibility of it.”

And that’s the thing: the possibility. Doctors don’t agree about how effective sildenafil cream is—how effective any arousal cream is—or even how significant it would be if the FDA approved one. But many agree on one thing: Women deserve a chance to try it.

Japan's groundbreaking mission to Mars is officially underway, headed to the Red Planet's mysterious moons Phobos and Deimos for a historic sample-return attempt.

The Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission launched today (Oct. 19) from Tanegashima Space Center in Japan, riding an H3 rocket that lifted off from Pad-2 at Tanegashima's Yoshinobu Launch Complex at 3:41 p.m. EDT (1941 GMT; 4:41 a.m. local time in Japan on Oct. 20).

The JAXA (Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency)-led MMX mission is bound for Mars orbit, where it will study the planet's two moons, Phobos and Deimos. If everything goes according to plan, MMX will become the first spacecraft to land on and return samples from Phobos for study back on Earth, which scientists think could shed light on a decades-old mystery about the moons' origins and reveal clues about the formation of the early solar system.

It was the 10th liftoff of Japan's expendable, two-stage H3 rocket, which was jointly developed by JAXA and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries. After clearing its launch pad today, the 207-foot-tall (63 meters) launch vehicle flew a nominal trajectory through its ascent corridor over the Pacific Ocean, jettisoning its four solid boosters in pairs just before the flight clock reached two minutes. The rocket shed its payload fairings about one minute and 20 seconds later, exposing MMX to the vacuum of space for the first time.

H3's first stage, powered by two LE-9 rocket engines, channels a mixture of liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen to produce a combined thrust of 331,000 pounds (1,471 kN), according to Mitsubishi Heavy Industries. As the mission clock approached T+5 minutes, H3 executed its main engine cutoff procedure and separated from the second stage, which ignited its engines for a 5.5-minute burn about 10 seconds later.

All continued to go to plan after that. The H3's upper stage ignited its engine for a second time for a trans-Mars injection burn about one hour and four minutes after liftoff, a maneuver that lasted roughly six minutes and 25 seconds and took MMX to a maximum velocity of about 24,145 mph (38,858 kph).

MMX was released from the rocket's upper stage right on schedule, about one minute after the completion of that burn and roughly 71 minutes after liftoff. The success elicited loud cheers in mission control, which viewers saw and heard on JAXA's launch webcast.

MMX is now under its own power, and on course for Mars. The interplanetary transit will last about 10 months, with the spacecraft scheduled to arrive at the Red Planet in August 2027. When it does, MMX's propulsion module will execute an orbital insertion burn to place itself in a quasi-satellite orbit (QSO) around Phobos, which places MMX within the space around the moon as both circle Mars.

MMX will spend about three years in the Martian system, devoting much of that time to studying the 14-mile-wide (22 km) Phobos with its science instruments. The spacecraft also carries the IDEFIX rover, a joint effort of the space agencies of France and Germany, which is scheduled to deploy to the surface of Phobos in late 2028 or early 2029.

After a mapping campaign to select suitable landing sites, MMX will perform two separate touchdowns on Phobos to collect and stow samples in the spacecraft's return capsule. Following its second landing and sample collection, MMX will briefly return to QSO with Phobos before transferring to a more elongated orbit around Mars that intersects with the planet's smaller moon, Deimos, which is just 8 miles (14 km) in diameter.

During the last several months of its time around Mars, MMX will perform a series of Deimos flybys before departing for Earth in November 2030. If all goes according to plan, the MMX sample-return capsule will land in Australia in 2031.

Scientists are counting on those samples, and other data collected during MMX's time around Mars, to answer a decades-old question surrounding the Martian moons' origins. Competing theories and conflicting evidence point to two possibilities for how Mars got its moons.

Their near-circular, equatorial orbits suggest a narrative similar to how Earth's moon is thought to have formed — as a result of a massive planetary impact between Mars and some other giant space rock, which broke off pieces of the planet that eventually coalesced to form the natural satellites that orbit there today.

Neither Phobos nor Deimos is spherical, isntead; both are oblong and lumpy, like potatoes. That fact — along with their chemical composition, as far as we have been able to tell using telescopic spectroscopy — point to the possibility that Phobos and Deimos were asteroids from the early solar system that were captured by Mars' gravitational pull. Figuring out the moons' true origin story would provide key clues about Mars' geological past, the solar system's early history and the formation of the inner planets, mission team members have said.

Once the MMX samples are safely returned to Earth, scientists will study their composition and mineralogy, then compare such chemical fingerprints with those of Martian rocks and asteroid material. NASA's contributions to MMX include the Mars-moon Exploration with Gamma rays and Neutrons (MEGANE, pronounced "meh-gah-nay") device, which is a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer built by the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory (APL); a Honeybee Robotics-built pneumatic sampler; and communications support using the agency's Deep Space Network.

#4210. FDA Approves Lilly’s New Oral Combo Pill for Common Breast Cancer Mutation

  • The FDA approved a new oral combo drug for a common breast cancer mutation on September 18.
  • The treatment includes Inluriyo (imlunestrant) taken with Verzenio (abemaciclib) for adults with ER-positive, HER2-negative advanced or metastatic breast cancer with ESR1 mutation that has spread after hormone therapy.
  • Results from a trial show that among 159 participants with ESR1 mutations, those taking the combination went a median of 11.1 months before their cancer spread, or they died, compared with 5.5 months for those taking Inluriyo alone.
  • A blood test can show whether breast cancer has an ESR1 mutation, according to the FDA.

People with advanced breast cancer that has spread despite hormone therapy, and with tumors that carry a gene change called an ESR1 mutation, have a new treatment option. 

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the use of an all-oral combo pill on September 18: Inluriyo (imlunestrant) and Verzenio (abemaciclib), both manufactured by Eli Lilly and Company. 

Inluriyo blocks estrogen’s signal in certain breast cancer cells and helps break down the receptor that receives that signal, according to the drug’s FDA-approved prescribing information. Verzenio blocks two proteins, CDK4 and CDK6, that can help cancer cells grow and divide, according to its prescribing information.

Verzenio first won FDA approval in 2017, and Inluriyo was approved as a stand-alone treatment in September 2025.

The FDA approved Inluriyo and Verzenio for use together. Inluriyo shuts down the estrogen receptors that fuel the cancer, while Verzenio slows how fast cancer cells multiply.

Neil Iyengar, MD, a breast medical oncologist at Winship Cancer Institute of Emory University, told Healthline that, “the individual treatments are not necessarily new, but the combination is a new concept.”

Some cancer specialists already prescribed the combination, according to Komal Jhaveri, MD, a breast medical oncologist at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York and lead researcher on the clinical trial that led to its approval.

“Now FDA has backed that with an official approval, so more patients can receive it without having any issues,” Jhaveri told Healthline.

Who is eligible for Inluriyo and Verzenio?

The combination is approved for adults whose breast cancer meets all of these conditions:

  • The cancer is locally advanced, meaning it has grown into tissue or lymph nodes near the breast, or metastatic, meaning it has spread to distant parts of the body.
  • The cancer is ER-positive, meaning estrogen helps drive its growth.
  • The cancer is HER2-negative.
  • An FDA-authorized test has confirmed an ESR1 mutation.
  • The cancer grew after at least one round of hormone therapy, also called endocrine therapy.

What is an ESR1 mutation in breast cancer?

In ER-positive breast cancer, estrogen attaches to proteins called receptors, which can lead to cancer growth, according to the National Cancer Institute.

Hormone therapy either prevents estrogen from binding to those receptors or lowers estrogen levels, according to the American Cancer Society (ACS).

An ESR1 mutation changes the estrogen receptor in a way that “makes it stay turned on,” Hope Rugo, MD, chief of breast medical oncology for City of Hope, told Healthline.

“And that mutation can develop while a person is receiving hormone therapy, and ultimately it makes the cancer able to resist the hormone therapy,” Iyengar said.

ESR1 mutations appear in about 1% of breast tumors at diagnosis but in 10% to 50% of metastatic cancers that have stopped responding to hormone therapy, according to the College of American Pathologists.

How the new drug combo differs from earlier treatments

People with metastatic ER-positive breast cancer usually start treatment with a hormone therapy paired with a CDK4/6 inhibitor, such as Verzenio, according to Iyengar.

The CDK4/6 inhibitor doesn’t work on its own, he said. When an ESR1 mutation makes the hormone therapy stop working, doctors can’t continue the CDK4/6 inhibitor by itself.

Until now, those patients typically switched to a newer hormone therapy pill, such as Inluriyo, alone, according to Iyengar.

The new approval allows patients to keep a targeted therapy as part of their treatment.

Pairing a hormone therapy with a targeted therapy helps overcome resistance and controls the cancer longer, according to Rugo.

“We don’t like giving single endocrine therapy all by itself anymore because we like to give patients combination therapy with targeted agents,” she said.

Pills instead of fulvestrant shots

Before the newer pills, the standard treatment after an ESR1 mutation was fulvestrant, a hormone therapy given as two shots per visit, according to Rugo.

Patients receive the shots every two weeks for the first three doses and then monthly, she said.

“But people hate the injection,” Rugo said. “Most patients will do better with the pill because they don’t have to have the injection.”

How effective is the Inluriyo-Verzenio combo?

Researchers added the drug combo to the clinical trial 6 months after it began, Jhaveri said.

To extend how long patients went before their cancer grew, “we understood that maybe we need a little bit more than one drug,” she said.

The team set out to answer the question, “Can we do a better blockade by giving them more than one drug that might target pathways better?” Jhaveri said.

The clinical trial that led to the approval, called EMBER-3, included 874 adults, according to the FDA.

Eli Lilly funded the clinical trial. The approval is based on results from a cohort of 159 participants with tumors that had ESR1 mutations.

Jhaveri said the study showed “that despite getting a CDK4/6 inhibitor in the first line, the combination of imlunestrant plus abemaciclib was effective.”

In that group, those taking the combination went a median of 11.1 months before their cancer grew or they died, compared with 5.5 months for those taking Inluriyo alone, according to the FDA.

Tumors shrank in 35% of people taking the combination and in 15% of those taking Inluriyo alone, the FDA reported. The FDA noted that these results come from a closer look at part of the trial, not from its main research question.

When the FDA reviewed the results, it was too early to tell whether the combination helps people live longer, according to the agency.

“There isn’t any survival data from any of these trials. The trials weren’t powered to look at survival,” Rugo said.

Side effects of Inluriyo and Verzenio

In the EMBER-3 clinical trial, 86% of people taking both Verzenio and Inluriyo had diarrhea, and 9% had severe cases, according to Verzenio’s prescribing information.

Diarrhea is a known side effect of Verzenio, according to Jhaveri. Adding Inluriyo didn’t increase the incidence of diarrhea compared with pairing Verzenio with fulvestrant, she said.

About 6% of people taking the combination stopped treatment due to side effects, according to a 2026 analysis of EMBER-3 published in npj Breast Cancer.

People taking Verzenio should start anti-diarrhea medication, drink more fluids, and contact their care team at the first sign of loose stools, according to the prescribing information for the drug.

The FDA’s safety warnings for the combination also include:

  • low white blood cell counts
  • liver problems
  • lung inflammation
  • blood clots
  • harm to a developing fetus during pregnancy

Doctors check blood counts and liver function before a patient starts Verzenio, then every 2 weeks for the first 2 months, monthly for the next 2 months, and as needed after that, according to the prescribing information.

Testing for an ESR1 mutation

A blood test that looks for tumor DNA can show whether breast cancer has an ESR1 mutation, according to the FDA. The FDA approved one such test, Guardant360 CDx, to identify people who may benefit from the combination.

“We would typically test for it when somebody was diagnosed with metastatic breast cancer, and then we would test for it again if their scans showed that the cancer was growing again,” Iyengar said.

Ask your doctor about Inluriyo and Verzenio

Verzenio and Inluriyo, plus an aromatase inhibitor, are currently listed as a first-line treatment option for hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer, according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN).

Breast cancer specialists are likely to be aware of the new two-drug combo, but those treated by general oncologists may need to bring it up with their healthcare professional, Rugo said.

Rugo suggested asking when it makes sense to check your blood for an ESR1 mutation, and whether the combination is a good option if your cancer is growing on your current treatment.

Questions to bring to your next oncology appointment

  • Has my cancer been tested for an ESR1 mutation, and when should I be retested?
  • Am I eligible for Inluriyo and Verzenio?
  • I’ve taken a CDK4/6 inhibitor before. Is this combination still a good fit for me?
  • How does this combination compare to my other treatment options?
  • What’s our plan if I get diarrhea?

What’s next for ESR1-mutated breast cancer treatment?

The FDA granted accelerated approval to Etcamah (camizestrant), developed by AstraZeneca, on September 4.

Etcamah is for people whose ESR1 mutation shows up during their first treatment with an aromatase inhibitor, a type of hormone therapy, and a CDK4/6 inhibitor.

Doctors start Etcamah and keep the CDK4/6 inhibitor at the same dose the patient was taking when the ESR1 mutation showed up, according to the FDA’s approval notice.

The agency granted Etcamah accelerated approval based on how long patients went without their cancer worsening, measured from the point when the mutation first appeared in the blood, according to the FDA.

The FDA has required follow-up studies because it isn’t yet confirmed that switching treatment at that point, rather than when scans show the cancer is spreading, gives patients a meaningful benefit.

Patients in the clinical trial that led to the approval had blood tests every 2 to 3 months, according to Iyengar.

“So technically speaking, what the FDA has approved is two different strategies,” Iyengar said.

Early results show a short-term benefit from switching treatment based on blood tests rather than waiting for scans, but researchers are waiting for long-term results, he said.

Researchers are also studying whether Inluriyo helps people with early stage breast cancer. That study, EMBER-4, has enrolled more than 8,000 patients, and initial results are expected in 2027, according to Eli Lilly.

Rugo said patients should watch for more drug approvals over the next six months.

“I would just emphasize here that it’s a really exciting time because people are living longer after a diagnosis of breast cancer,” Iyengar said.

#4209. Crypto for Advisors: Stablecoins are ready for prime time

A year ago, the stablecoin debate centered on whether regulatory clarity and a compelling combination of speed, cost and liquidity benefits would drive meaningful adoption. The evidence now points to a market moving decisively from experimentation toward implementation.

EY-Parthenon’s 2026 Blockchain Payments Survey of 521 corporate and financial institution decision-makers found that 18% of corporates have used stablecoins in production, double the share reported in our 2025 survey. Tokenized deposits are close behind, with 15% of corporates reporting adoption.

Financial institutions are also making firm commitments. A total of 69% offer stablecoin services or plan to launch them within two years, up from 30% in 2025. Among banks, 49% already offer or plan to offer tokenized deposits over the same period.

Although blockchain payments have not yet reached mass adoption, the survey findings provide clear evidence that the market is shifting from technology trials to integrating capabilities into existing payment workflows and treasury operations.

Cross-border payments are the entry point

Cross-border business payments remain the most common use case. Across stablecoins and tokenized deposits, 50% to 53% of all respondents have used or are exploring each for cross-border supplier payments or cross-border business-to-business (B2B) payment acceptance.

Overall blockchain payments exploration & adoption

Cross-border B2B flows lead blockchain payment use cases for most respondents, while interest in payouts is concentrated among banks and payments firms.

These applications directly address frictions corporates experience in cross-border payments. A total of 56% cite slow processing and settlement; 52% cite foreign exchange costs or spreads; 48% cite regulatory, sanctions and compliance delays; and 36% cite correspondent banking fees. Financial institutions see the corresponding opportunity, with faster settlement and lower costs ranking among the leading drivers of interest in both stablecoins and tokenized deposits.

The opportunity extends beyond basic money movement. Tokenized asset settlement was the most frequently selected opportunity to add value on top of blockchain payment rails, cited by 63% of respondents from financial institutions, reflecting strong interest in using blockchain-based money as a settlement mechanism.

Adoption is moving beyond a binary choice between stablecoins and tokenized deposits. Among respondents with an interest in blockchain payments, 45% of corporates and 53% of financial institutions favor using both rails. Respondents cite flexibility and the expectation that the two methods will coexist as leading reasons, suggesting that different payment needs will call for different forms of digital money.

That preference reflects the different roles the two methods can play. Stablecoins provide reach across wallets, platforms and public blockchain networks, while tokenized deposits can bring commercial bank money into programmable environments. Rather than selecting a single rail, organizations can choose between them based on the use case, counterparty, geography and liquidity requirements.

For financial institutions and payment providers, the opportunity is to make that choice seamless for clients, enabling them to move between payment methods, both traditional fiat and blockchain payments, through integrated processes and a unified experience.

Corporate expectations favor embedded delivery of blockchain payment services. Forgiveness as a concept can be incendiary and polarizing.

Some view forgiveness as surrender, a form of exonerating an abuser and accepting bad behavior. They see it as a shrugging of the shoulders and giving someone who has hurt them deeply a free pass.

Yet others view it as anything but. They see it as liberation, a way to free themselves from the hold of someone who has caused them harm, a path to moving on.

At its core, forgiveness is about releasing your resentment over a past hurt and giving yourself permission to move from a place of pain to a life where you can flourish without being weighed down by an injustice. It is like removing a proverbial backpack that has been weighing you down. It’s a mindset that releases you from being chained to the past and to the event that harmed you.

Of course, some wounds are too deep, some acts too horrific, to forgive the person who inflicted them. That choice deserves respect. But there is another form of forgiveness that can be restorative: forgiving ourselves.

When we have been deeply hurt, at times we turn the blame inward. We ask, Could I have prevented what happened? Self-forgiveness means accepting the event was beyond our control and that we cannot alter the past. It releases blame that was never ours to bear, and extends to ourselves the compassion we might readily offer others.

After all, forgiveness is not about what we owe another person. It's a choice we make for ourselves. We forgive so that the experience does not take up precious space in the story of our life.

Whatever our personal view of forgiveness, science offers a compelling reason to consider it. In addition to reclaiming our peace, research suggests that letting go of resentment can benefit our mental and physical health and may even be associated with greater longevity.

While this is not evidence that forgiveness extends life, it raises a compelling possibility. Holding on to resentment while waiting for someone else to make amends may take a toll on our physical health.

In one study of nearly 4,600 adults across five counties, those who participated in a brief forgiveness intervention experienced reductions in symptoms of both. And there are even greater benefits. Research suggests that practicing forgiveness can improve self-esteem and is associated with greater life satisfaction.

#4207. In 1976, a college paper blamed the Salem witch trials on moldy bread. It wasn’t true.

Published Oct 15, 2026 9:02 AM EDT

In the mid-1970s, Linnda Caporael was a college student facing a seemingly mundane decision.

A science major at U.C. Santa Barbara, she needed a humanities course to graduate. The course she chose, on women in American history, came with a term paper, and she had to pick a topic.

Caporael’s first choice—Lydia Wardell, a Quaker woman who protested Puritan persecution by walking naked into a meeting house in the 1660s—was intriguing, but already taken. So Caporael instead settled on her second choice: Ann Putnam, one of the accusers in the 1692 Salem witch trials. In doing so, Caporael reshaped the way millions of people view a nearly 300-year-old chapter of American history.

Caporael’s paper introduced the theory that the visions, convulsions, and distress the “afflicted” girls reported might not have been supernatural after all. Instead, she proposed, they could be explained by ergot, a fungus that grows on rye grain and produces a hallucinogenic toxin. In other words, blame moldy bread.

The ergot theory, despite being debunked within a year of being published, has continued to spread and thrive like fungus on rye. As recently as 2023, comedian-turned-podcasting-juggernaut Joe Rogan discussed the theory enthusiastically on his show, effectively reanimating a story that seems destined to never fully die.

“It’s sexy,” says historian Margo Burns about the enduring appeal of Caporael’s ergot theory. “It’s about drugs. It’s about witches. It’s about killing people. Also, we like to think that when there’s an unjust sentence or execution, there’s someone or something to blame.”

Burns, a Salem scholar who has spent years tracing how the ergot theory took hold and persisted, says she rarely gets through a talk on the trials without someone in the audience bringing up moldy bread.

The fact that a single undergraduate college paper could have such a lasting and wide-ranging impact on our understanding of the Salem witch trials seems bizarre, but it can be explained in part by a thoroughly modern phenomenon: virality. Although the World Wide Web was still decades away, Caporael’s paper found a way to spread fast through newspaper wire services.

After she started graduate school, Caporael submitted the paper to Science, which, to her surprise, published it in April 1976. That alone might have kept the theory confined within academic circles. Instead, newspaper editors saw a story with mass appeal.

“Editors and newspapers just grabbed it, saying, ‘Oh, here’s a human interest thing,’” Burns says. “And when they put it in their papers, like The Washington Post, The New York Times, The Boston Globe, it got syndicated. And the syndication of those articles went through the whole country and Canada within a matter of weeks.”

Caporael, who had explored the topic through the lens of the scientific method, wasn’t claiming to know what had happened at Salem. At the suggestion of a pre-med friend, she began looking into ergot poisoning as a possible explanation, not a definitive answer.

According to Burns, who has spoken with Caporael directly, she was only testing whether ergot could be ruled out. Working from the limited sources available to her, Caporael determined that she couldn’t rule it out. Even then, though, she called her own findings “suggestive,” not conclusive.

But that nuance didn’t survive exposure to the public, which in 1976 was already fixated on LSD and what it might do to young people. The press coverage essentially retrofitted 1692 with the anxieties of 1976, recasting the Salem girls as teenagers on a bad trip.

“You give that to the American public, which doesn’t understand there’s a difference between scientific inquiry and historical storytelling, and it’s just so compelling,” Burns says.

Not all the viral attention was positive, though. Within that same year, researcher Nicholas Spanos and graduate student Jack Gottlieb published a rebuttal in Science, dismantling Caporael’s argument point by point. By their reckoning, various aspects of the Salem girls’ symptoms did not add up.

“There are a couple of different kinds of ergot poisoning, and one of them is gangrenous, so people’s fingers would start rotting,” Burns says. “And they would have gastrointestinal problems, and we don’t have any instances in the records that either of those things happened.”

The problems for the theory didn’t end there. Ergot poisoning was thought to hit hardest among people with vitamin A deficiencies, but Salem’s residents had dairy cows and the sea nearby, and likely plenty of vitamin A. (Ironically, Burns notes, that the vitamin A argument was itself based on a discredited 1930s source that relied on circumstantial evidence and has never been replicated.) And while whole families ate from the same supply of rye, usually only one or two people per household fell ill.

The rebuttal, though methodical and logical, was little more than a momentary speed bump for the runaway popularity of the psychedelic bread story. In fact, it may have helped.

Arguing with a claim, however insubstantial, implies that it’s worth arguing about, and a formal rebuttal in Science can make a student’s term paper look like one side of a valid scientific debate.

“It’s really hard to refute something,” Burns says. “If anything, it just sort of nails it and makes it more permanent.”

Whatever was causing young women in Salem to convulse, the atrocities that followed are much harder to grasp. By the time the panic ended, 152 people had been formally accused, 19 had been hanged, and one man, Giles Corey, had been pressed to death with stones for refusing to stand trial. Several more died in jail.

“The people who were responsible were the adults in the room,” Burns says.

In such a charged social climate, suspicion could fall on just about anyone, and it did. The accused weren’t just outcasts, Burns says. Many were fully covenanted members of the Puritan church.

When jurors asked her what she meant, the courtroom was in such an uproar that Nurse didn’t hear the question. At the time, failing to answer an accusation was treated as a confession, and the jury returned a guilty verdict. In a later appeal, Nurse wrote that she was “something hard of hearing, and full of grief,” and didn’t realize she was being asked to explain herself.

It was the kind of injustice no mere fungus could cause.

“[Chief Justice] Stoughton was singularly, individually responsible for her execution,” Burns says.

The question of who is to blame is rarely so straightforward, though. Which might be part of the ergot theory’s appeal; it offers “one silver bullet” that tidily explains everything.

“There are just so many reasons that all came together,” Burns says.

#4201. Astronomers discover first green star and the reddest star ever recorded

Published Oct 15, 2026 10:26 AM EDT

Despite the name, the universe’s white dwarfs can glow in a wide range of colors—even green. For the first time, astronomers have spotted a star that appears green to the human eye, and the reddest ever recorded. These outlier stars help explain how certain white dwarf stars would shine in green, pink, cyan, yellow, and purple if we actually saw them up close in space. These colorful findings are described in a study recently published in the journal RAS Techniques and Instruments.

White dwarf stars are the dense remnants left behind after stars like our sun reach the end of their lifespan. For most stars, their color gives a hint to their temperature. Cooler stars appear orange, warm stars like our sun look white. The hottest glow pale blue, similar to how a blacksmith’s iron will go from red to orange to white hot. Astronomers believed that white dwarfs followed this same rule. They even earned their name after the earliest examples were surprisingly hot and glowed in a white/pale blue.

“Most stars, including most white dwarfs, behave like ‘perfect radiators,’ so their colour is set almost entirely by temperature. But nobody had really looked into what else might be possible for white dwarfs,” Mark Hollands, a study co-author and astronomer at the University of Warwick in England, said in a statement. “It turns out these incredibly dense stars can produce colours nobody expected, and some of them genuinely surprised me.”

According to the team, the key to these colors is density. The atmospheres surrounding the coolest white dwarfs can reach about the same density as water (roughly 1 gram per cubic centimeter). They are also millions of times denser than the sun’s photosphere, the lowest layer of its atmosphere. Under these higher density conditions, any trace elements present (carbon, calcium, sodium, etc.) interact with the surrounding dense helium plasma. As they interact with the plasma, they can absorb light across a wider spectrum of colors than they would at lower densities. This then shifts the star’s color away from the more typical orange, white, and blue.

Unfortunately, no white dwarf sky is close enough for its color to be seen directly. Instead, the team calculated their color from data astronomers routinely collect—a star’s spectrum. The spectrum is a “fingerprint” that’s produced when starlight splits apart by wavelength, similar to how a prism splits sunlight into a rainbow. Astronomers ran these spectra through a computer model of what colors human vision can see to check what color a star’s light would appear to our eyes.

The team applied this method to several white dwarfs across the cosmos. They found that their colors fall into distinct groups linked to what makes up a star’s atmosphere. Carbon-rich white dwarfs generally appear pink or purple. Stars enriched with debris from busted asteroids and comets, the remains of old planetary systems, tend to be more yellow. Cooler white dwarfs with atmospheres of mixed hydrogen and helium show a more cyan or pale blue tinge. This cyan-pale blue is caused by hydrogen molecules colliding with helium atoms inside their ultra-dense atmospheres.

The most striking example is WD J1330+6435. This star is about 7,232 degrees Fahrenheit (4,000 degrees Celsius) and around 284 light-years away from Earth. Over time, it has gathered rocky material from its planetary system. All of this extra material enriched its atmosphere with calcium, lithium, and sodium. The result—a chartreuse green appearance in the team’s models. As far as the researchers are aware, no other star (living or dead) has ever been credibly reported as green.

“The traces of sodium in the atmosphere of this white dwarf absorb the orange part of the spectrum, leaving behind a ghostly green glow,” added Hollands. “Imagine standing on a planet and looking up at a green star. It feels like something out of science fiction.”

On the opposite end of the spectrum, the team identified what could be the reddest star ever recorded—WD J1011+2845. This white dwarf is only 48 light-years away from Earth, and has a deep convection zone. This zone is full of carbon that’s been lifted up from the star’s core to its surface. The carbon molecules then distort the star’s light so much that almost all of it is squeezed into wavelengths beyond 600 nanometres. This wavelength produces an unusual deep salmon-pink glow.

The team also studied 151 white dwarfs located within about 65 light-years (20 parsecs) from Earth. Only five of these showed unusual colors, two yellow and three pink. This suggests that about 1 in 30 of the universe’s white dwarfs shine with an unusually colored light.

The team then measured the color temperature of the remaining 146 white dwarfs. Color temperature is commonly used to describe light bulbs using degrees Kelvin—warm white at 3,000K, cool white at 6,000 K, etc. The median color temperature of the sample was approximately 6,800K, which our eyes perceive as white. This measurement corrects a common misconception that white dwarfs are typically blue. The hotter and bluer white dwarfs are mostly brighter, so they can be seen from further away and are over-represented in surveys that only look at a star’s brightness.

“One in thirty might not sound like a huge fraction, but across the entire population of white dwarfs in the Universe, that is still a lot of oddly coloured stars out there,” added study co-author and University of Warwick astronomer Boris Gänsicke. “Yet if we take the median across a representative population of white dwarfs, we found that the average white dwarf really is white.”
