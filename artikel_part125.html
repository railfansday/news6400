#198. Hackers Exploited the PHP Vulnerabilities Days Before They Were Patched

The internet’s mad scramble on Wednesday to patch a pair of critical vulnerabilities in PHP, a widely used web scripting language, was already too late to stop every attack, newly released details reveal.

Wednesday’s release of fixes for two critical vulnerabilities in PHP caused administrators and security staff to race to identify and patch vulnerable websites and apps, while attackers inevitably reverse engineered the fixes to learn how to exploit the flaws. Some governments and other groups even proactively took key websites down in anticipation of the release, so they wouldn't be targeted before they could apply the patches.

But in addition to detecting such active exploitation on Wednesday, research published Wednesday evening by Amazon Web Services indicates that at least one actor had started attempting to exploit the bugs—known collectively as PHP2Shell—on October 17, four days before Wednesday's public revelations.

AWS analysts spotted the October 17 exploitation on Wednesday while looking back at activity on one of their so-called honeypots, or intentionally vulnerable web servers or other digital lures that help researchers study attack methods and trends. The attempt came from an IP address that AWS says is linked to a “commercial anonymization network,” in other words a proxy network or VPN.

“While the vulnerability was under embargo, the targeting of a honeypot from an anonymized IP address suggests the activity was conducted by a threat actor, though it remains unclear how the actor acquired exploit information prior to disclosure,” CJ Moses, AWS's chief information security officer, wrote in the research post.

Elizabeth Barron, executive director of the PHP Foundation, tells WIRED that the organization was unaware of AWS's research prior to publication and did not immediately have a comment on the findings. She added, “Although we agree that upgrading is absolutely imperative, to our knowledge, there has been no successful attempt at remote code execution.”

Proof-of-concept exploits for PHP2Shell began emerging publicly on Wednesday, with some seeming to appear hours before the public disclosure at 10 am ET. In the hours leading up to the disclosure, AWS researchers say their honeypots were hit with multiple exploitation attempts anonymized using various providers and seemingly with various attack goals, likely indicating multiple different actors. “Technical analysis is ongoing, and it’s unclear if any of these attempts would have succeeded in real-world systems,” Moses wrote. He added that “PHP2Shell is now being broadly exploited in the wild.”

Though it is unclear how the exploitation on October 17 happened or where it came from, AWS emphasizes that the findings underscore how coordinated vulnerability disclosure must evolve and speed up, given global access to AI tools that can accelerate vulnerability analysis and allow less technically proficient actors to quickly develop new exploits.

“The time from patch to working exploit is shrinking for structural reasons,” Moses wrote. “In the past, most exploitation we observed came after a public proof of concept was released. A small number of capable actors built exploits, and everyone else waited for them. That dependency is weakening. AI-assisted analysis makes it cheaper to turn a patch diff or advisory into a working exploit. As a result, more actors can act within hours or even minutes once technical details of a vulnerability are disclosed.”

#196. This Flock Camera map shows more than 300,000 devices

You probably know where the coffee shops and gas stations are in your neighborhood. But the cameras logging your license plate as you drive past? Those can be a little harder to keep track of.

Proton, the Swiss privacy company behind Proton Mail and Proton VPN, is helping make them easier to find by hosting a free map of Flock surveillance devices on its servers (you can explore the map on Proton's website). Flock tried to get the researcher behind the original project to take it down, according to The Intercept.

If you need a refresher on Flock, the company makes automated license plate readers used by police departments, businesses, schools, and homeowners associations. The cameras photograph passing vehicles whether or not their drivers are suspected of anything. Flock devices can record license plates, times, and locations, plus identifying details such as a vehicle’s make, model, color, and bumper stickers. According to Flock, its cameras log more than 20 billion vehicle sightings each month across 49 states.

Those records then go into Flock's cloud, where police can search them and share them with other agencies across city and state lines. You don’t have to be pulled over for your car to become part of a searchable record of where you’ve been — and that access has been abused. A Washington Post investigation found at least 50 officers accused or charged with misusing license plate readers, often to track people in their personal lives. (Flock's system was involved in 46 of those cases.)

On Proton's map, you can enter your ZIP code to see devices in your area, or zoom and pan around the country. Note that the data is a snapshot from December 2025, though. Cameras installed or removed since then won’t be reflected.

This isn't the first effort to put surveillance cameras on the map (literally). As Mashable previously reported, DeFlock Maps lets people look up license plate readers in their neighborhoods, including devices from Flock, Motorola Solutions, and other manufacturers. Volunteers report cameras they spot and add their locations to OpenStreetMap, the open-source mapping project.

MapQuest also added a "Surveillance Layer" to its app this month, letting users see known license plate reader locations, including Flock cameras. Turn it on under Map Options, and camera markers appear alongside the roads you’re traveling.

The difference here from other crowdsourced projects is that the locations come from Flock's own systems, according to the map's creator.

Security researcher Joshua Michael discovered in November 2025 that Flock's servers were exposing an access token anyone could obtain without logging in. He used it to retrieve an inventory of device locations, reported the issue to Flock, and published his findings and a map in December. That inventory included more than 170,000 cameras and over 130,000 other Flock devices.

Proton has questioned Flock's leadership directly about how its technology is used. In an interview published in August, CEO Garrett Langley said, "Any level of abuse greater than zero is unacceptable and makes me sick," pointing to audit logs and an optional tool designed to detect potentially abusive activity. But he declined to say how many police department contracts Flock had suspended or terminated over abuse. "I don’t think you should hold the entire city accountable for just the bad decisions of a few," he said.

When it came to Michael's research, Flock's response included legal threats, The Intercept reported. The company fixed the exposed-token flaw, while also publicly maintaining that it had never been hacked and that no Flock information had leaked. On his website, Michael says Flock also sent him a cease-and-desist letter (which he published) threatening litigation and told customers it had notified law enforcement, describing him as a "malicious actor."

Michael says that customer email acknowledged Flock had found no indication of unauthorized access when it closed the issue in December. He argues that this illustrates the very concern behind his research: that information could be taken without the company detecting it.

"I’m not backing down," he recently wrote on Instagram.

In September, brand-protection company Doppel also filed a trademark complaint on Flock's behalf. It argued that Michael's website could confuse customers or harm the company, even citing the disclaimer saying it wasn't affiliated with or endorsed by Flock — though Flock had not disputed the map's accuracy, as CyberNews reported.

Proton rejected the trademark argument and published a copy of the map on its Swiss servers in order to keep it available.

"This is an attempt to suppress information, not to prevent consumer confusion," Proton head of legal Marc Løebekken said.

For residents, that means another way to find out what's watching their streets — and a starting point for questions to local officials. As Proton puts it on the map's website: "You have a right to know who's recording you when you leave home."

#194. Albania's student protests are only getting bigger

As police try to suppress anti-corruption protests by young Albanians, Prime Minister Edi Rama is claiming that "antifa" is behind the unrest. Few are buying that explanation.

The fallout from the October 13 protests was still visible when students and faculty from the University of Tirana gathered outside the Education Ministry in Albania's capital on Tuesday to express their continued discontent. Smashed windows have yet to be replaced; the walls are still black with soot from fires that broke out during the protests.

Albania has seen nearly five months of peaceful anti-government protests, sparked initially when plans were made public that Jared Kushner — a son-in-law of US President Donald Trump — and associates intended to build a luxury hotel complex within a nature reserve.

Since then, anti-government and anti-corruption protests have escalated. Now, college and high school students clash with police forces almost daily, with the fiercest clashes so far occurring last week.

Protesters denounce corruption, mismanaged universities and the rising cost of education in Albania — and call for the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama, who has been in office since 2013.

Wearing a black hooded jacket and a scarf across her face to conceal her identity, a woman who asked to be called Sara because she fears repercussions for speaking to the press told DW that protesters "communicate via text messages — that's the only way."

She cut the interview short as some protesters began flinging stones at the Education Ministry. Police pushed demonstrators and journalists out of the way, using water cannons to disperse the crowd.

"Social media is the most important public space today — more so than physical spaces," Besmir Semanaj, a prominent Albanian cybersecurity advocate told DW. "Young Albanians no longer organize through traditional structures such as political parties, associations or public appeals. Instead, they organize themselves where they spend much of their daily lives: on their cellphones."

"The most important communication channels include Instagram, WhatsApp groups for academic-year cohorts, departments and dormitories, Telegram channels for general announcements, and TikTok for sharing videos," Semanaj said. He added that people who are especially concerned with security use the Signal messaging app.

"This is a horizontal organizational structure, without individual 'leaders' and without a central command structure," Semanaj said. "That is exactly why the government is having such a hard time understanding and controlling the situation."

Semanaj said the Telegram messenger app had proven particularly useful for protesters. "Telegram channels allow you to reach tens of thousands of people at once," he said. "Notifications are sent out within seconds, and external control is more difficult to establish than on traditional platforms."

Rama has called the youth uprising a "hybrid war." He said protesters thought they could "make Albania a better country through TikTok and without even being able to string together more than seven coherent words." Though not all are terrorists, he said, he believes that "they are hostages to a certain mindset, a new ideology that has only one goal: to destroy, to turn everything upside down, to raze everything to the ground."

Over nearly five months, Rama has accused various "true" masterminds of organizing the anti-government and anti-corruption protests. He has alleged that the governments of Greece, Russia and Iran are stirring up dissent.

Most recently, Rama said it was "antifa" — or a decentralized movement of global antifascists — that had stoked anti-government and anti-corruption sentiments in Albania. "These are young people who have been radicalized and ideologically indoctrinated and who are acting under the pressure or influence of a new, dark force that has emerged in Europe and the United States, a force that the President of the United States has designated as a terrorist organization: Antifa," Rama claimed.

Redion Qirjazi, a security affairs researcher who focuses on violence prevention and conflict resolution, told DW that Rama's claim was not likely to prove true. "That is a serious accusation — one that requires equally compelling evidence," Qirjazi said. "To date, no verifiable evidence has been presented to prove the existence of an organized Antifa structure in Albania, let alone its involvement in coordinating the protests."

Qirjazi said Rama's reference to Trump's calling antifa a terror group was problematic. "Antifa is not a unified international organization with a centralized structure or leadership," he said, "but rather an umbrella term for various movements and groups united by the fact that they are, at their core, anti-fascist."

Sara said she rejected the "terrorist" label that Rama had attempted to slap on her and her fellow protesters. "There are groups that are willing to use violence — that's true — but I have nothing to do with them," she said. "As soon as I see violence, I walk away; I'm against it."

Arlind Qori, the chairman of the Together Movement, a social-democratic opposition party with just one seat in Albania's parliament, said youths had taken to the streets out of dissatisfaction with the status quo rather than any particular ideology. "Young Albanians are outraged by the current socioeconomic conditions: the poverty, the fact that many of their parents have emigrated because they couldn't support their families, the decline of the education system, the lack of postgraduation prospects," Qori said. The students' central demand is for Rama to resign, he added  "not a socialist revolution."

"People aren't demanding anything radical," Qori said. "They merely want a corrupt head of government to resign — that's a fundamental and moderate demand. But Edi Rama continues to meet this demand with police violence and arrogance."

Qirjazi said Rama's allegations of foreign interference could be a strategy. "When a government faces protests and public discontent, linking demonstrators to extremism and foreign influence may serve to divert attention away from the actual causes and toward a national security narrative," he said.

"This can serve several political purposes: to delegitimize the protesters, to weaken their support among the public, and to justify a tougher crackdown in the name of law and order," Qirjazi said.

Semanaj said this was a common pattern in governments' efforts to dismiss dissent. "Protests are portrayed as acts of violence," he said. "Young people are cast as manipulated, and their legitimate concerns — conditions at universities, tuition fees, corruption and a lack of prospects — vanish from the news agenda."

#192. Capcom data breach confirmed: More than 11 million accounts impacted

Do you play video games like Street Fighter, Resident Evil, or Monster Hunter online? If so, you probably have a Capcom ID — and you should know that there has been a major data breach that has affected millions of accounts.

Capcom, the developer of the aforementioned titles, posted a notice on Thursday informing users of a major data breach affecting more than 11 million accounts.

"Capcom Co., Ltd. has determined that certain customer information associated with CAPCOM ID, an online membership account service operated by the company, was subject to unauthorized access by a third party," the company said in a statement. "Capcom sincerely apologizes for the complications and concern this has caused its customers and other stakeholders."

Data breaches are seemingly becoming a regular occurrence, which may result in some becoming desensitized to this news. However, the specific information Capcom has shared should be concerning to those with a Capcom ID, as the company has confirmed that some accounts have experienced unauthorized changes due to the breach.

According to Capcom, the company first noticed suspicious activity on Capcom IDs on Oct. 19, then began blocking access and opened an investigation. Capcom's investigation found that approximately 11,774,247 accounts were impacted in a data breach. The account information exposed to the unauthorized party includes email address records, the date and time an account was created, the total number of logins, the date and time of the last login, and the IP address used for that last login.

Of those more than 11.7 million accounts, 43 had their date of birth, gender, country/region, and nickname affected, and 196 had unauthorized changes to their Capcom ID login password.

Capcom says it has already reset the passwords associated with those 196 accounts.

One positive piece of news is that Capcom says it has yet to find any evidence that the passwords originally set by its users were leaked in the data breach. In addition, Capcom shared that it does not appear that its games were affected in the data breach.

While Capcom continues its investigation, it is warning its users to be wary of phishing emails that weaponize data leaked in the breach.

"If you receive any suspicious email or message, please exercise caution and refrain from accessing any URLs contained therein or opening any attached files," Capcom said in its breach notice.

#190. Oxford scientists discover how viruses build themselves without a blueprint

Researchers at the University of Oxford have observed virus-like particles assembling themselves one molecule at a time, uncovering the surprisingly simple rules that allow complex biological structures to form with remarkable reliability. The findings, published in Nature, offer a detailed look at a process that scientists have struggled to observe directly for decades.

Understanding how viruses construct their protective protein shells, and how that construction might be interrupted, could lead to new antiviral drugs, improved vaccines, and other medical treatments. The discovery helps explain how individual proteins consistently organize themselves into the correct structures despite having thousands of possible ways to connect.

The researchers found that viral assembly resembles navigating a complicated maze, where only certain paths lead to lasting progress. At first, protein components form weak connections that can easily break apart if the arrangement is unsuccessful. This flexibility allows the molecules to keep trying different combinations until they form particular closed structures. Once multiple connections hold these structures together, they become far more stable.

These stable arrangements serve as molecular checkpoints, narrowing the enormous number of possible assembly routes into a much smaller set of successful pathways. One especially important milestone occurs when five larger protein building blocks connect to form a closed pentagonal ring. This is the first highly stable structure in the assembly process. Once it forms, each subsequent stable stage requires fewer additional building blocks, allowing the particle to grow more quickly.

Co-lead author Dr. Roi Asor (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) explained: "A virus has to solve an extraordinary construction problem. Its components somehow have to find the right arrangement among a huge number of possibilities, without a blueprint or machinery directing the process. We can now watch what happens molecule by molecule and see the physical rules that make it possible."

Watching Virus Particles Assemble Molecule by Molecule

Viruses store and protect their genetic material within organized protein shells known as capsids. Depending on the virus, these shells may consist of tens or even thousands of individual protein components. Remarkably, in many cases, the proteins can arrange themselves into the correct structure without any external machinery directing their assembly.

Although scientists have investigated viral self-assembly for decades, capturing the process as it unfolds has been a major challenge. Many of the intermediate structures exist only briefly and appear in very small quantities. As a result, traditional methods have generally allowed researchers to examine the starting materials and finished particles without clearly showing how one transforms into the other.

To overcome this limitation, the Oxford researchers studied an engineered virus-like particle composed of 60 protein units. They used mass photometry, a technique developed at Oxford that determines the mass of individual molecules by measuring the light they scatter. By combining this technology with a new approach for keeping individual molecules confined and continuously observable, the team could repeatedly measure the mass of a growing particle as additional proteins attached.

This approach allowed the scientists to follow the construction process as it happened, effectively tracking the addition of individual molecular building blocks over time.

Co-first author PhD student Dan Loewenthal (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) said: "This method lets us study the assembly process directly, we just take a video!"

Co-lead author Professor Philipp Kukura (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, Oxford University) said: "Until now, much of our understanding of how these structures assemble has had to be reconstructed from snapshots or theoretical models. Being able to both quantify the underlying interactions and follow one particle as it grows changes that. We can see the important intermediate structures appear and deduce a detailed model of the construction process."

The Surprising Trick That Makes Viral Assembly Work

The results help explain how a process involving an overwhelming number of possible molecular arrangements can consistently produce the correct structure. Rather than exploring every possible route, the proteins progress through a relatively small number of stable intermediate stages that guide assembly toward completion.

The findings also clarify why the earliest stage of construction, called nucleation, is particularly difficult and why growth becomes substantially faster after the first stable structure appears.

A key part of this efficiency comes from the ability of protein building blocks to make mistakes without permanently disrupting the process. Weak initial connections allow incorrect combinations to separate, giving the proteins another opportunity to find a better arrangement. When enough components eventually connect in the correct closed configuration, the resulting structure becomes stable enough to support further growth.

Dr. Asor added: "The weak interactions give the system room to make mistakes. Most encounters don't have to be successful: the components can separate and try again. But once enough of them come together in the right closed arrangement, the structure becomes stable, and assembly can move forward. That combination of trial and error followed by locking in successful structures is what makes the process so reliable."

New Possibilities for Antiviral Drugs and Vaccines

By identifying the molecular interactions that control viral assembly, scientists may eventually be able to develop treatments that interfere with the construction of viral protein shells. The same insights could also support efforts to engineer virus-like particles for vaccines and other therapeutic applications.

Co-author Dr. Jack Tan (MRC Weatherall Institute of Molecular Medicine and CAMS-Oxford Institute) noted: "The ability to understand these processes at the molecular level could have important applications in vaccine development and antivirals that disrupt viral assembly."

The implications may extend far beyond viruses. Self-assembly is essential throughout biology, helping create everything from the internal skeletons of cells to specialized molecular compartments and large protein complexes.

By making it possible to observe these intricate construction processes one molecule at a time, the Oxford team's approach offers researchers a powerful new way to investigate how complex biological structures emerge from relatively simple interactions.

#186. This Preamp Costs More Than a Fancy Sports Car

In the world of hi-fi, there’s “high end,” and then there’s Goldmund. Since 1978, the Swiss manufacturer has produced exquisitely designed, astonishingly high-performance and staggeringly expensive audio equipment. As it nears half a century of proudly uncompromised engineering, its determination to position its products as credible alternatives to jewellery, art, or other pricey indulgences for the superrich shows no sign of abating—quite the opposite.

I was recently invited to Goldmund's headquarters in Geneva, Switzerland, to experience the new Mimesis Statement, the company’s flagship analog preamplifier. Goldmund's HQ, incidentally, features the sort of high ceilings, generous use of concrete and glass, and fanatical tidiness that puts me in mind of a Bond villain who's decided to devote their time to audio gratification rather than global tyranny.

In a traditional stereo chain, a preamplifier takes the low-level signals from your source gear, handles volume and input selection, and feeds that clean signal to a power amp to drive your speakers. Most people use an integrated amplifier, one that combines pre-amplification and power amplification in the same box.

Those who want to take things to the next level often separate pre-amplification and power amplification into two boxes—to keep the precious audio signal away from any electrical activity that might compromise its integrity. Goldmund, naturally, is convinced that a preamplifier alone needs to occupy three separate boxes.

The first box, the one with the buttons, dials, and display, houses the pre-amplification electronics. It’s where your source equipment—turntable, network streamer, whatever you fancy—connects using the generous selection of balanced and unbalanced inputs. The other two boxes are external power supplies serving the left and right channels of your stereo signal.

Goldmund reckons this radical separation, along with painstaking internal architecture featuring complete symmetry, voltage regulation stages, and optimized heat dissipation, guarantees signal integrity and utterly consistent operating conditions in all circumstances.

It’s apparently not enough to make a preamplifier with such a low total harmonic distortion figure (0.0002 percent) that it’s extremely difficult to measure in the first place. No, it has to be housed in three chassis with mirrored-glass displays, metal-coated controls, and Goldmund’s signature slender gold-line design flourish to confirm its identity.

In the US, the Goldmund Mimesis Statement analog preamplifier costs $240,000. No, I didn't accidentally add a few zeros. We’re already into “fairly fancy European sports car” territory here, but a preamplifier by itself doesn’t constitute an audio system. For the Mimesis Statement to do its thing, it needs a music input signal, which it will output to a power amplifier. From there, it goes to a pair of speakers.

Thankfully, Goldmund offers plenty of options. During my listen to the Mimesis Statement, it is connected to the company’s Eidos network streamer (yours for $27,000) at one end and a pair of Thelos 9800 power amplifiers (nearly 800 pounds per amplifier, 1,560 watts per channel, an imposing aesthetic, and $500,000 each) at the other. The amplified signal then moves to a pair of Theia Passive four-way floor-standing loudspeakers ($215,000 for two). That’s a total system price of $1,482,000—not so much “sports car” as “very nice apartment, probably with a balcony.” And I haven’t even added in the price of cables.

What does all that money buy you? Well, it most certainly buys a distinctive industrial design language, attention to detail and precision where fit and finish is concerned that’s beyond OCD, hand-soldered circuit boards, military-grade alloys crafted using established Swiss watchmaking techniques, and the sort of overall physical presence that’s more commonly associated with the stuff they build at NASA.

But can a stereo system possibly be worth $1.5 million? The answer has to be “no.” I’ve been a part of the audio equipment appraisal process for over two decades, and I know full well that the law of diminishing returns kicks in long before you’ve spent six figures, let alone seven. But Goldmund isn’t selling you a hi-fi any more than Rolex is selling you a wristwatch—it’s selling you an unmistakable symbol of status, wealth, and success. It’s selling you exclusivity. It’s selling you what it sincerely believes is the pinnacle of artisanal engineering.

And it’s also selling you a serving of detailed, tremendously insightful, and eloquently communicative sound. I am not a prospective Goldmund customer, but if I had the disposable income, I really think I might be.

#184. PHP vulnerability: PHP security issues today explained

Updated on Thursday, Oct. 22 at 12:25 p.m. ET — The PHP security issues known as PHP2Shell sent the cybersecurity world into a frenzy yesterday, and now we have some data on the fallout.

VirusTotal, a cybersecurity tool owned by Google, reported that there are about 5.1 million IP addresses running PHP, showing just how widespread the vulnerability is.

Cogent Security, an agentic AI cybersecurity company, released data on how the issue affected big companies. Cogent reported that the average Fortune 500 company had 2,266 affected assets, with an average of 16 assets demanding immediate action. As of 8 p.m. ET on Wednesday, Cogent reported that less than 1 percent of affected assets had been patched.

Cloudflare CEO Matthew Prince reported a surge in attempts to exploit PHP2Shell across the company’s network: thousands in the 10 hours before the public announcement, tens of thousands within 45 minutes afterward, and hundreds of thousands within two hours. By the time of his post, the count had reached millions.

PHP2Shell caused outages across the web, including at Wikipedia, and many government, gaming, and business websites required emergency maintenance. By Thursday morning, most services had been restored.

The PHP Foundation, the non-profit team behind the PHP coding language, released emergency security updates on Wednesday to fix two critical vulnerabilities, a day after details about the flaws leaked online.

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), the Prime Minister of Japan, and the U.S. National Security Agency all urged organizations to install the fixes immediately.

The newly exposed PHP vulnerability led to a number of outages on Wednesday, as webmasters around the world scrambled to update their websites.

PHP is a programming language that runs on web servers. When you load a webpage, PHP often runs behind the scenes, retrieving data from databases and building the page before it reaches your browser. BleepingComputer notes that it powers many websites, including content management systems, online stores, and custom web services. According to the NCSC, PHP is also built into a range of other products.

The NCSC says the two flaws, tracked as CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, could allow an attacker to run their own commands on a PHP-based server without logging in. This is called remote code execution, and it’s one of the most serious kinds of security bugs.

According to the NCSC, an attacker could overload PHP’s memory to corrupt it, then, under certain conditions, use that corruption to take control of the server. One of the flaws involves how PHP processes form submissions and file uploads.

PHP rated each flaw 9.2 out of 10 on CVSS, the industry’s standard severity scale. Security firm watchTowr described them as a perfect 10, BleepingComputer reported, and so did an anonymous Hacker News post that has since been deleted. Researchers have started calling the bugs "PHP2SHELL."

The fixes were originally planned for a coordinated release later this week, so companies would have time to prepare. Then, information about the flaws leaked early. Speaking to BleepingComputer, watchTowr CEO Benjamin Harris said the vulnerabilities are "indeed real" and affect PHP versions 5 through 8. The firm said it had reproduced one of them.

PHP moved its release up to 10 a.m. ET Wednesday. As of Tuesday, watchTowr said it hadn’t seen anyone exploiting the flaws. By Wednesday, Cloudflare CTO Dane Knecht said on X that the company was seeing early attacks, according to BleepingComputer.

The NCSC says organizations running PHP 5 or later are affected and should update to the latest fixed version. Anyone still using an older, unsupported version should move to a supported one.

Wikipedia went down for a time on Wednesday, and the Wikimedia Foundation, the nonprofit that runs the site, confirmed to Mashable that the outage was tied to the PHP vulnerabilities.

In a statement to Mashable through email, the foundation said it is working to address the flaws in PHP, which it described as the programming language behind most wikis, including its own projects. "This issue affects all known versions of PHP, and is not specific to the Wikimedia Foundation," the statement said.

The foundation added that all of its PHP-based systems are affected, along with volunteer-run systems that use PHP. It has also taken several other systems offline as a precaution until they can be safely patched. Wikipedia was back up as of this writing.

If you only browse the web, there’s nothing for you to install. The fix is up to the people who run websites.

If you run a website, blog, or online store, check whether it uses PHP and make sure it’s up to date. If a hosting company manages your site, contact them to confirm they’ve applied the patch.

As CNN has reported, the US intelligence community last year developed a method of tracking what it says is a fleet of these Chinese balloons operating across the globe, controlled by the Chinese military.

The FBI is still examining the balloon, but so far officials have been able to glean additional information about how the device worked, including the algorithms used for the balloon’s software and how it is powered and designed.

CNN has reached out to the National Security Council at the White House and the Pentagon for comment. NBC was first to report on the new intelligence.

#179. Birds are more afraid of women than men. Scientists have no idea why

Birds living in cities appear to react differently to men and women, and scientists are struggling to explain why. An international research team discovered that urban birds, including great tits, house sparrows, and blackbirds, tend to fly away sooner when approached by women than by men. The surprising behavior raises new questions about how birds perceive the people around them.

The study took place in five European countries, where male and female participants (matched for height and clothing) walked directly toward birds in city parks and other green spaces.

On average, men were able to approach about one meter closer than women before the birds flew away. The researchers measured this response by recording how close a person could get before a bird took flight (termed flight initiation distance).

Published in the British Ecological Society journal People and Nature, the findings showed the same pattern in Czechia, France, Germany, Poland, and Spain. The difference also appeared across all 37 bird species examined, including easily startled magpies and pigeons that typically allow people to approach much more closely.

The researchers believe their findings indicate that city birds can distinguish between male and female humans. However, they have yet to determine which characteristics the birds recognize or why they appear more cautious around women.

Professor Daniel Blumstein of the University of California, Los Angeles, who helped conduct the research, said the results were convincing despite the lack of an explanation.

"I fully believe our results, that urban birds react differently based on the sex of the person approaching them, but I can't explain them right now. We used bleeding-edge comparative analysis techniques that showed our findings were consistent across cities and species, but we simply don't have a conclusive explanation yet."

Dr. Yanina Benedetti of the Czech University of Life Sciences Prague, another member of the research team, said the discovery challenged her expectations about how birds respond to human observers.

"As a woman in the field, I was surprised that birds reacted to us differently. This study highlights how animals in cities 'see' humans, which has implications for urban ecology and equality in science. Many behavioral studies assume that a human observer is neutral, but this wasn't the case for urban birds in our study."

Scientists have proposed several possible explanations for the difference. Birds might be responding to chemical signals such as pheromones, variations in body shape, or differences in how men and women walk. For now, however, these ideas remain speculative, and additional experiments will be needed to identify the actual cause.

Dr. Federico Morelli of the University of Turin, a co-author of the study, said the unanswered question is one of the discovery's most fascinating aspects.

"This is maybe the most interesting part of our study," said Dr. Federico Morelli from the University of Turin, a co-author of the study. "We have identified a phenomenon, but we really don't know why. However, what our results do highlight is the birds' sophisticated ability to evaluate their environment."

The findings suggest that urban birds may be sensitive to human characteristics that people themselves rarely notice. Identifying those signals could help researchers better understand how wildlife assesses potential threats in environments shared with humans.

Dr. Benedetti said: "Urban birds clearly react to subtle cues that humans do not easily notice. Follow-up studies could focus on individual factors such as movement patterns, scent cues, or physical traits, testing them separately rather than grouping them under observer sex. This approach would help identify the specific cues birds detect."

More Research Is Needed to Explain the Bird Behavior

Although the investigation included 2,701 observations, the researchers emphasize that their findings are still preliminary. Further studies will be necessary to establish whether the difference between birds' responses to men and women consistently appears in other settings and populations.

For now, the discovery points to an intriguing possibility: Birds sharing our cities may be paying attention to subtle differences between people that scientists have yet to fully understand.

#178. Cyberpunk: Edgerunners 2 ending, explained: Who dies, and who lives to tell their story?

The ending of Cyberpunk: Edgerunners 2 might be even more ambiguous than its predecessor. And according to writer and showrunner Bartosz Sztybor, that's exactly what the creative team intended.

"We had a lot of conversation. There were a lot of endings," Sztybor told Mashable about the nearly two years spent developing the season. Ultimately, they arrived at a conclusion he described as "very ambiguous, even more ambiguous than the ending in the first season. But also somehow hopeful."

That ambiguity is especially apparent in D's final moments. After spending years pursuing revenge for the destruction of his clan, the Nomad rides off with Capri, a chip containing digitized human consciousnesses, toward an orbital space station, seemingly sacrificing himself to keep her out of Arasaka's hands.

But does he actually die? And why does his ending feel so hopeful?

Major spoilers for the Cyberpunk: Edgerunners 2 finale follow.

D has spent years hunting the people responsible for killing his clan, only to discover that they're already dead. Worse, he learns that his clan was targeted because of him. His unusual tolerance for cyberware made him a potential host for Capri, part of an Arasaka experiment that also identified David Martinez and Talia as possible candidates.

Suddenly, the revenge that has defined D's entire existence is impossible. "I think we started with this idea that what would happen [if] your whole personality is based on that revenge thing," Sztybor explained. "And suddenly you see that, oh shit, I didn't fulfill my journey. So, what now?"

Omi, the only surviving member of D's clan, puts it more simply: "You should have followed your own path. You should have lived your own life, David."

D has spent so long living for revenge that he's forgotten how to live for himself. But his relationship with Capri changes something. Despite existing as digitized human consciousnesses stored on a chip, Capri is curious, playful, and eager to experience the world. She also has little patience for D's obsession.

"Why is everyone so obsessed with getting revenge anyway? Is getting revenge, like, fun?" she asks while controlling Talia's body.

The surprisingly simple question cuts to the heart of D's story. Revenge has given him a reason to keep going, but it hasn't made him happy.

Capri, on the other hand, does. For the first time in years, D seems to be having fun. His time with her offers a reprieve from the nightmares that have haunted him since his clan's destruction, and there's something almost intoxicating about that relief. Their relationship can even be read through the lens of addiction, with its initial rush of euphoria and the danger of becoming consumed by the thing that makes you feel good. In Capri, D seems to rediscover a part of himself that existed before his life became defined by trauma.

It's not hard to understand why he'd become so attached to her. His revenge has reached a dead end, and his actions have left him with few options in Night City. But with Capri, he gets to experience something he thought he'd lost. And in keeping her out of Arasaka's hands, he offers Capri a chance at freedom, too.

Their final rampage is violent, reckless, and, in its own twisted way, almost joyful. D isn't suddenly a hero, and his decision to take Capri toward the orbital space station may ultimately cost him his life. The series leaves their fate uncertain — and it's incredibly possible that D and Capri are somewhere in the Net — but there's something liberating about those final moments. For once, he's no longer acting out of anger toward the people who destroyed his past. He's doing something because he wants to.

And in his final recording for Roman, D smiles. It's the very expression Roman has spent the season trying to capture on camera. After everything D has endured, that small moment of happiness feels like its own kind of resolution.

Talia's story explores a different side of that same idea. Born with a terminal illness, the teenager has spent much of her life having her failing body parts replaced with cyberware, all in an effort to prolong her life. But those decisions were made by her parents, not Talia herself. And throughout the season, she tries to escape their control, adding even more chrome to her body in an attempt to reclaim the autonomy she's never really had.

It's what makes cyberpunk fiction feel so relevant. Beneath all the futuristic technology is a very real conversation about medical intervention, bodily autonomy, and the difference between keeping someone alive and allowing them to actually live.

When her mother seeks a treatment that would remove the cyberware from her body to extend the time she has left, Talia protests, "I wanted to die beautiful."

And that's what makes her friendship with Roman so meaningful. With him, Talia gets to experience something resembling an ordinary teenage life. She makes a friend closer to her own age. They go to the movies. It's such a mundane thing to do, especially in Night City, where so much of life is defined by violence and survival. But for Talia, those moments are precious because they're hers.

In the finale, Talia tells Roman that seeing herself through his camera made her feel beautiful. "You are beautiful," he replies.

It's a small exchange, but it brings her story full circle. After spending so much of her life having other people decide what her body should look like and how long she should live, Talia finally gets to see herself through the eyes of someone who appreciates her exactly as she is.

Her condition is irreversible. But when she leaves with Weak, she's finally making a choice about how she wants to spend whatever time she has left.

Weak has spent much of the season trying to protect Talia after her mother tells him that he's her biological father. But Talia's father later claims that story was a manipulation, leaving the truth uncertain.

By the finale, however, their biological relationship matters less than the one they've formed.

Once a legendary edgerunner known as King, Weak has spent years living in the shadow of his former glory. His relationship with Talia gives him something meaningful to care about beyond his own reputation.

And despite knowing that he can't save her, he chooses to stay. "Come on, let's live!" he tells Talia as they drive away. Weak can't promise her a cure or a long future. But he can give her the freedom to spend the rest of her life on her own terms. And in choosing to be there for her, he finds something worth living for, too.

Before leaving with Weak, Talia shares a tearful goodbye with Roman, the young aspiring filmmaker whose analog camera has documented much of the season's events. Along the way, he's captured something more personal, too: the lives of a group of misfits whose stories might otherwise have been lost to Night City.

"Roman, we'll see each other again. I know it," she tells him. "For now, you just keep that camera rolling, and you can come show me what you've made. Scroll something so great, it will make all this stuff feel small."

Her words echo D's own farewell to Roman: "This is the high point of my story, not yours. You still have a lot ahead of you."

Both characters, whose futures have become so uncertain, encourage Roman to imagine something bigger for himself.

Sztybor described Roman as "the only perfectly innocent character" in the violent world of Night City. While the first Edgerunners was, in his words, "a story about the end of innocence," Roman's presence helped the creative team arrive at a more hopeful conclusion for the second season. Roman's love of filmmaking became central to that decision. Roman has witnessed terrible things, but those experiences haven't extinguished his desire to create.

After Talia and Weak drive away, he looks at his camera, smiles, and takes off running with an exuberant yell.

The series then closes on a flickering space station in the sky, an image evoking D and Capri's final journey without confirming their fate.

That's the beauty of Edgerunners 2's ambiguous ending. We don't know whether D and Capri survive, how much time Talia has left, or what Roman will eventually create. But the finale isn't really about promising these characters a happy ending. It's about giving them the freedom to decide what to do with their lives. Because even in Night City, where so much of your life is decided for you, there's still something powerful about getting to choose how your story unfolds.

#175. Hugging Face’s New AI Biology Model Lets You Chat With DNA

The AI platform Hugging Face on Thursday released a new open model for understanding DNA that can run locally on a laptop.

Developed in collaboration with researchers at Zhongguancun Academy in China and the University of Naples Federico II in Italy, the model is trained on genomic sequences and natural language so that people can ask questions about the effects of DNA changes on gene activity and health and get conversational answers in return.

Lewis Tunstall, a senior research scientist at Hugging Face, says the tool is meant to help researchers design new drugs and genetic therapies, and it could potentially aid patients in interpreting their own genomes.

“We've unlocked a new capability in language models, which is fundamentally about being able to chat with DNA,” he says of the new model, dubbed Carbon-X. Using natural language means even nontechnical users “can actually ask questions about DNA that wouldn't be accessible through any other tool.”

Known as essentially GitHub for AI models, Hugging Face provides a library of readymade AI tools and a platform for running primarily open-weight and open-source AI applications for developers, researchers, businesses, and people who want to experiment with AI without building everything from scratch. In September, Nvidia agreed to acquire the company for nearly $13 billion. (Hugging Face was also at the center of a major hack by OpenAI’s models earlier this year.)

DNA is more like computer code than natural language. Instead of 0s and 1s, though, it uses As, Cs, Gs, and Ts to encode information. A human genome contains 6 billion of these letters. While the human genome has been sequenced end to end, scientists still don’t understand how most of it works.

Now, AI models are helping researchers decipher some of those mysteries. Last year, Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome, which predicts the effects of DNA sequences and genetic mutations but does so in a numeric format. It also requires more compute power. Evo 2, a model developed by the Arc Institute and Nvidia, along with academic partners, speaks the language of DNA and can generate entire genomes from scratch.

Carbon-X integrates both DNA and natural language in a conversational interface. Think of it like a ChatGPT for DNA.

The team started with an existing language model and expanded its vocabulary to read DNA in groups of six letters. They then continued training on 300 billion chunks of data, or tokens, that combined DNA sequences, English text, and examples linking DNA to biological descriptions. The DNA sequences came from mammals (including humans), other vertebrates, invertebrates, plants, fungi, and protozoa. Tunstall says the team didn’t train the model on viruses because they didn’t want it to be used for potentially dangerous applications.

Users won’t be able to upload full human genomes. The model was trained to be able to ingest DNA sequences of roughly 50,000 base pairs, so users will only be able to query small parts of the genome at a time.

One of the ways scientists could use Carbon-X is to help understand whether a genetic mutation is benign or disease-causing, a key step in developing a targeted drug or genetic therapy. If an intervention targets the wrong mutation, it could be totally ineffective or, worse, cause unintended harm.

Researchers face restrictions when it comes to uploading identifiable or sensitive patient data to frontier AI models, so Tunstall says his team intentionally designed a small model with 14 billion parameters. That will allow it to run locally on the user’s hardware, rather than rely on cloud-based models. No data is sent to Hugging Face or any other company, he says.

The developers of Carbon-X caution that they haven’t validated the model yet with actual wet lab experiments, so they can’t say how accurate its predictions are.

#151. I Worked for a Whole Day Using Only the iPhone Duo. Here’s How It Went

I wrote my entire iPhone Duo review on Apple’s new handset. I used a note-taking app called Bear, which was mercifully optimized for the Duo’s larger screen. (Read my review and you’ll hear about how most apps aren’t optimized; it could take weeks or months for everything to look right.) But surprisingly, I didn’t hate the experience.

I have run this experiment before on Android foldables and even smart glasses with virtual workspaces, but iOS has its own unique hurdles. Notably, iOS doesn’t recognize a Bluetooth mouse or trackpad by default. Instead, you have to go to the phone’s accessibility settings and enable AssistiveTouch—then you’ll get an iPad-like cursor that lets you navigate the Duo’s interface and select text without constantly reaching for the touchscreen.

Even with the cursor, small tasks like pasting hyperlinks into a written piece or quickly Googling topics for additional research are slower and more cumbersome, so I left those for the end when I moved everything into WIRED’s content management system. Even then, I had to switch over to my desktop for the final touches.

Apple also imposes arbitrary multitasking restrictions in iOS because, well, Apple likes to control how people use its devices. For example, if you open two apps in split screen mode, you can only view them in an equal 50-50 split. Unlike most operating systems that let you adjust how much of each app you can see by dragging the border in between the windows, iOS leaves you stuck with a view that gives both apps equal screen space. If you want to use the iPhone in portrait orientation and stack two apps vertically, one on the top part of the screen and the other on the bottom, you’re out of luck. In portrait mode, you can only pin video apps to the top with another app below.

One thing Apple did get right is app pairs. You can “pin” two apps together, and they will live in your Recents, ready when you need them. Say you always want to look at your email and calendar together; you can pair them and find them in Recents whenever you need them. (Tap the bar in the middle to lock and save the pair.)

My keyboard of choice is an old Seenda folding Bluetooth keyboard that is admittedly not very good. I imagine we'll see an array of compact keyboards hitting the market soon—like Nillkin or ProtoArc keyboards already available now—to cater to workaholics who need to work everywhere. I also used one of Apple's cases, the iPhone Duo Folio with Kickstand, to keep the phone propped up. You can use various MagSafe kickstands, tripods, or cases with the Duo.

I typed most of the day, but I also had to juggle between apps to respond to coworkers or hop on a Zoom call. Thankfully, the Slack iOS app updated the day before my testing to fully support the iPhone Duo's larger screen and vertical navigation, making it very easy to bounce between multiple conversations in the two-pane view. I still think Apple should add a feature like the iPad's Slide Over, where you can “hide” a floating app off to the side and bring it back in when you need it. It’d be the perfect spot for messaging apps like Slack, WhatsApp, or Messages, and it wouldn’t interrupt your split screen experience.

Zoom wasn’t optimized for the Duo, though my editor said the video quality from the inner and outer selfie cameras was “really good.” I used the folio kickstand to Zoom on the big 7.6-inch screen until we realized my video was stuck in an awkward portrait orientation, so I laid the phone down on the table and pointed the 5.4-inch outer screen toward my face, which made it feel like a traditional laptop experience.

The app that annoyed me the most was Gmail. Even though Google updated the Google Search app to be compatible with the iPhone Duo early this week, the rest of Google's workspace apps currently look awful on the Duo. I imagine full support will come very soon, if not on Friday, when the Duo officially launches. The two-pane view in Gmail (or any email app) is a game changer for clearing a bunch of emails when you’re on the go.

By 6 pm, battery life was sitting around 45 percent, which is very impressive. That's with around six hours of screen-on time.
