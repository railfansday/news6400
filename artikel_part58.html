#3550. Dispatch's Anniversary Update Brings Commentary Track, Storyboard Mode, And Mobile Port

Dispatch, a superhero-themed workplace comedy from last year, celebrates its one-year anniversary today. While it was released episodically, the first batch dropped on October 22. It was received well, with strong performances from a star-studded cast (including Breaking Bad star Aaron Paul) and filled a niche left vacant by Telltale Games' absence from the gaming world in recent years. Today, developer AdHoc announced a batch of anniversary content, and some of it drops today.

First, there's a new way to play Dispatch: storyboard mode. This option lets you play through the whole experience using the original animatics – rough placeholder sketches that the artists used to sync images up to the voice-over. It's a fun behind-the-scenes look at the game's development that we don't get very often. In that same vein, the cast and crew recorded DVD-style commentary tracks to play over the game, making jokes and sharing insight into the development process. A press release says there are over 16 hours of commentary from the game's development team (like art director Derek Stratton and creative directors Dennis Lenart and Nick Herman) as well as some of the cast, including Aaron Paul, Laura Bailey, and Erin Yvette.

Storyboard and Commentary modes are included in the game's deluxe edition, which you can upgrade to for $10.99. Both modes are available today.

If you haven't played the game yet, AdHoc has announced a new way to do so; Dispatch is coming to mobile platforms on December 9. It's been rebuilt to support touch controls and vertical play, and it will be available on iOS and Android. You can even play the first half of the game for free – the first four episodes are included, but you'll need to pay $19.99 to play the rest.

Finally, AdHoc announced new merch, including a book created alongside publisher Lost in Cult called Dispatch: Design Works, which is both an art book and a "making-of" book. There's also a live game soundtrack concert in Los Angeles featuring Thot Squad, Bershy, Primer, Caroline Kingsbury, Fartbarf, and Dispatch composer Andrew Arcadi with the SDN Company Band. It will be held at Avalon Hollywood on December 3, but it won't be livestreamed, so you'll need to grab a ticket to see the event. The press release states that people who signed up for updates from adhocstudio.com will be the first in line to purchase tickets.

#3547. Tenebris Somnia Review – A Hair-Raising And Entertaining Throwback

Tenebris Somnia channels the old-school appeal of classic point-and-click adventures, survival horror, and full-motion-video (FMV) experiences, then smartly modernizes them to create a creepy, entertaining experience. Surviving the twisted threats of a demonic dream world is a tightly packed adventure that rewards you with what’s essentially an impressive indie horror film peppered throughout. It’s a nightmare you may not want to wake up from.

The blend of live-action and in-game storytelling works well in Tenebris Somina’s adventure, which begins when protagonist Julia visits the apartment of her filmmaker ex-boyfriend, Ivan, after suffering recurring nightmares of him being massacred by monsters. What begins as a well-meaning check-in on his status spirals down an unsettling rabbit hole when Julia’s worst fears are realized and then some. The dialogue-free tale presents a scary but fascinating exploration of obsession, both in art and in romance, that constantly delivers surprising, and at times upsetting, revelations during its eight-hour runtime. While Tenebris Somnia sometimes leans a little too far into “tell, don’t show” territory when explaining the cool lore of its supernatural threats, I never felt lost or confused about the wild things that regularly unfold on screen.

FMV experiences are nothing new, but Tenebris Somnia’s live-action scenes are a highlight. They’re well shot, sport impressively gruesome practical effects, and even present visual clues that make them important to gameplay. These scenes are additive, never taking too much time away from the in-game action, and they feel well placed to elicit the strongest emotional response. I also appreciate how the live scenes fit thematically, as much of Tenebris Somnia’s tale centers on a horror film shot by Ivan and Julia that significantly impacted their former relationship; at times, it’s like watching a movie about the movie. While I enjoy smaller moments, like opening a door to trigger a live-action jump scare, the filmmaking succeeds in selling Julia's emotions and the cast's reactions. Seeing real fear, concern, and determination in Julia’s eyes as she navigates a dreamworld made up of Ivan’s memories helped endear me to what would otherwise be a featureless pixel sprite.

Gameplay may look unimpressive due to its old-school adventure game presentation, but Tenebris Somnia’s spin on familiar survival-horror mechanics hits the right notes. Taking place entirely within a dreamlike version of a four-story apartment building, you’ll traverse floors collecting and combining objects and deciphering clues to solve well-crafted puzzles. I’m impressed by how well the game’s puzzles make the most of its limited graphical presentation, including clever scenarios involving mirror/screen reflections I won’t spoil, and how consistently creative they are. One devious exercise even utilizes the game’s limited save points as an unexpected resource in a way that changes how you approach your adventure going forward. No matter how strange puzzles become, they are never opaque, requiring just enough out-of-the-box thinking to make me feel smart when I connect the dots.

Battling grotesque monsters (which, naturally, look even creepier in live-action) also feels good, with melee and gunplay sporting deliberate animations reminiscent of classic adventure games without feeling mechanically clunky. Taking on multiple enemies by swinging a pipe or blowing them away with a shotgun feels better than it looks, and the most intense encounters end just as they approach the line of becoming overwhelming.

Boss battles, perhaps most unexpectedly for a largely puzzle-focused adventure, are fun and distinct from each other, be it wave-based bouts against small gremlin-like foes, protecting a fragile effigy of yourself from a parade of demented house servants, or battling literal mirror images of a tentacled flesh monster the Resident Evil series would be proud to claim. Managing limited ammo and health strikes a good balance of feeling tense but not oppressive. Playing conservatively is rewarding, as few non-boss threats need to be engaged if you can evade them well enough. But when I did find myself down to my last bullet or on the verge of death, Tenebris Somnia lets you feel the pinch just long enough before dropping a box of bullets or a health vial at the right moment.

Tenebris Somnia mines the enjoyable nostalgia of playing the horror games of yesteryear without feeling dated in the worst way. Weaving a solid indie horror film adds to the experience rather than feeling like a gimmick. The result is a captivating, well-executed survival-horror romp that maintains a hair-raising atmosphere with a fraction of the pixels of other genre giants.

#3546. I have seen the future of sports games and it is called just volleyball

Earlier this year, I embarked on a pivotal journey, one that I'm sure upon finishing will change me for good: I started watching Haikyu!! It's my first sports anime (when you're a chubby little muffin like I was, you take more interest in Not Moving over Sports), an abscess in the lexicon of genres I've dipped my toes into, one I'm rectifying after all these years. And friends, it's good eatin'. I even, incredibly briefly, floated the idea of taking up volleyball. Yet now I don't have to, and it's all thanks to a delightful, incredibly Wii Sports coded game called just volleyball.

just volleyball [sic] is a sports game that is exactly what it says on the tin. You are immediately greeted, in its demo, with an avatar customising screen not too dissimilar to the Mii Channel of Wii days gone by. There's lots of hair options, some charmingly skewy face designs, you can set how your voice sounds, choose the number on your jersey, it's all good stuff. And then comes the volleyball.

Like in real volleyball, you can jump, bump, dive, spike, block and serve. Unlike Wii Sports, however, you have to be a lot more on the ball than just having a controller around. I played using a controller, and the controls work thusly: you move your funny lil guy around using the left stick, and with the right stick you aim where you want to hit the ball. That might be across the net to your opponents' side of the field, or it could be towards one of your teammates. None of it is as easy as it sounds.

You have to be constantly vigilant of where the ball might land, and if you're the one receiving the ball, you have to make sure you're pointing your cursor towards a teammate, as it's more than unlikely that you'll score yourself a point that way. Serving is finicky too, requiring precise timing and tight skills so as to trick the opposing players. I never quite got the hang of it, but I at least made some headway with the other components.

Mostly what I feel towards just volleyball is delight. There's hardly any frills to it, it's clearly inspired by Wii Sports yet isn't trying to be Wii Sports, just it's own thing. Just, volleyball! If you're interested in giving it a go yourself, then you can try out the demo on Steam ahead of its release on November 4th.

#3545. The next Call of Duty will introduce a new sub-series to the franchise

Next year's Call of Duty game will not be part of any of the franchise's existing sub-series. In an interview with The Game Business, Activision confirmed it's finally ready to create a new sub-series.

"We've already kind of stated that a future title is going to be a new sub-franchise and we've already kind of laid the groundwork there," Activision chief marketing officer Tyler Bahl said.

This year's game, Modern Warfare 4, is the final chapter in one of Call of Duty's most popular sub-franchises, which followed on from 2025's Black Ops 7, the 7th entry in the other popular Call of Duty sub-series.

Activision announced last year it's no longer going to release two back-to-back games form the Modern Warfare or Black Ops sub-series, and this presumably goes for future sub-series that get created. Bahl admitted that releasing back-to-back games in the same sub-franchise was something the community rejected.

"Yeah, I think we were pretty public about the idea of us going back-to-back with Modern Warfare 2 to Modern Warfare 3 and Black Ops 6 to Black Ops 7 was not something that our community was looking for," Bahl said.

"And even though there were different time settings and different ways to play the game[s] and lots of new features, that tonal shift is something that they were looking for us to [do]."

The most recent Call of Duty game in a new sub-franchise was 2021's Vanguard, which performed poorly and failed to establish a new sub-franchise within the larger brand. Incidentally, Vanguard was developed by Sledgehammer Games, which is understood to be the maker of next year's game - itself also a new sub-franchise.

2027's Call of Duty is rumoured to be set in the late 90s and early 2000s featuring a new setting with a new cast of characters. It's going to be critical to see how well the new sub-series sets itself apart from the existing duo, and whether it ends up getting a sequel.

It turns out the kids can't get enough of John Steinbeck. The Florence Pugh-led, Zoe Kazan-created adaptation of his novel East of Eden has been a massive hit for Netflix, its epic story covering decades in the lives of two California families in the run-up to World War I. And why not? It's got grand family drama and plenty of sex, set against a massive historical backdrop and stuffed with biblical allusions that make it all feel like less of a guilty pleasure. If this is your bag, here are more similar shows to stream after you've finished your binge-watch.

One generation will inevitably influence those that follow, for better and worse—sins and strengths in equal measure, dramatized most succinctly, perhaps, by Steinbeck's East of Eden and in Isabel Allende's seminal novel, adapted here (indicentally, both books are among the most frequently challenged in the United States). Told from the perspective of Alba (Rochi Hernández), House of the Spirits starts in the 1970s and works backward as Alba explores four generations of family history through he journals of her grandmother, Clara (Dolores Fonzi). Though their country is never named, Allende based it all on the real history of Chile through those decades of political violence and upheaval, and on her own family's triumphs and tragedies in its wake. Stream The House of the Spirits on Prime Video.

The most recent adaptation of Leo Tolstoy's epic, and a pretty good one. We begin in 1805, during the height of the Napoleonic Wars, meeting Pierre (Paul Dano), the idealistic illegitimate son of an oligarch; romantic and impulsive dancer Natasha (Lily James); and thoughtful, high-minded Andrei (James Norton), destined to become a soldier. Throughout the years of war that follow, the three of them and their families move in and out of each other's lives, as tragedy alternately unites and divides them. Stream War and Peace on Peacock and Prime Video.

Starting in 1915, this multigenerational saga follows one woman (played in different time periods by Youn Yuh-jung and Kim Min-ha) and her family, taking us from the Japanese occupation of Korea through the decades of the Korean diaspora. With her opportunities limited, Sunja leaves her home and family in Busan to pursue a life in Japan, even as racism and anti-immigrant sentiment are rampant amid the war. In a parallel narrative that begins in 1989, we see what has and hasn't changed for Sunja and her family. It’s a journey as personal as it is epic, with better location cinematography than most movies. Stream Pachinko on Apple TV.

I haven't been the biggest fan of the newer adaptation of the John Galsworthy novels—though The Forsytes has already been renewed for a further two seasons, so I'm apparently in the minority. Nevertheless, I'll direct you back to 2002 and this extended miniseries led by Damian Lewis. It starts off in 1884 when the slightly scandalous engagement of one of the wealthy new-money Forsytes is marred by the revelation of an affair between cousin Joylon (Rupert Graves) and the governess, kicking off an impossibly complicated series of events that plagues the family for decades. Author Galsworthy was inspired to write by his own extramarital affair, so the drama is in the show's DNA. Stream The Forsyte Saga on Netflix.

An epic love story that cleaned house at the 2025 Baeksang Arts Awards (that's South Korea's most prestigious entertainment award ceremony), Tangerines stars IU as Oh Ae-sun (and as Ae-sun's daughter and the series' narrator, Geum-myeong), a young woman born in the 1960s on rural Jeju Island. The series spans decades in her life with friend and later romantic interest Yang Gwan-sik (Park Bo-gum), as South Korea changes around them. The focus is more on Ae-sun's own love story than some of these broader narratives, but the scope is epic all the same. Stream When Life Gives You Tangerines on Netflix.

Not to be confused with the American Civil War miniseries North and South from the 1980s, this series adapts Elizabeth Gaskell's 1851 novel that follows Margaret Hale (Daniela Denby-Ashe) and her family, forced to leave their Hampshire home and travel to the industrial north of England to avoid religious scandal involving the clergyman father. Poor but well-educated, Margaret runs up against the wealthy owner of the local mill, John Thornton (Richard Armitage), whose family sees her as haughty despite her circumstances; meanwhile, hers sees John and his peers as gruff and inhumane where workers are concerned. Despite their very opposing views, you won't be surprised to learn that Margaret and John find themselves growing closer over the subsequent years, even as their families remain divided. Stream North & South on Prime Video and Tubi.

There's plenty of upper-crust family drama in this loosely-based-on-real-events series, but it comes from Steven Knight (Peaky Blinders), so expect bloody fisticuffs, too. The first episode begins shortly after the death of Sir Benjamin Guinness, who became the wealthiest man in Ireland by expanding the trade in Guinness beer throughout the British Empire. The cracks begin to show on the way to the funeral: Religious leaders deplore all of the beer-drinking, while Irish Republicans want the company brought down for colluding with the Empire. Sir Benjamin's four heirs have wildly different goals and/or hate each other, so, you know—lots of drama, and lots of beer. The show has been renewed for a second season, so a second round is on the way. Stream House of Guinness on Netflix.

Subtitled The Saga of an American Family by Alex Haley in his novel, Roots, in any of its incarnations, is hard to top as an epic family drama grounded in history. Absolutely nothing can match the cultural importance of the 1977 miniseries, also an easy recommendation, which centered the generations-long story of a Black family at a time when such narratives were rare on television. The 2016 version couldn’t possibly have that kind of impact, but it works just the same: it's impressively acted and adds a modern polish that doesn’t detract from the story’s power. Covering much of the same ground, the newer version goes further in focusing on the American family at its center, spending less time leading white viewers into the story that begins with Kunta Kinte (Malachi Kirby) captured in Gambia and sold as chattel. There’s a very reasonable debate about the preponderance of slave narratives in film and television and the purpose they serve for white audiences; while both versions of Roots are unflinching in that area, they also go beyond it by engaging with what came before and after. Stream Roots on Prime Video and Tubi.

A dark turn down an alternate path in American history, The Plot Against America asks: what if Charles Lindbergh had succeeded in his bid for political power in the 1930s, bringing to bear his vision of an America that followed in the footsteps of Nazi Germany by halting the “infiltration of inferior blood” (by which he meant, mostly, Jewish people). Adapted from the book by Philip Roth, the series bends imagination with real history, depicting Lindbergh's successful campaign for the American presidency against Franklin D. Roosevelt, which ultimately keeps the U.S. out of World War II—and which results in things at home growing increasingly dangerous for the Jewish family at the show's center, which splinters between members who advocate for resistance, quiet acquiescence, or active participation. Morgan Spector, Zoe Kazan, Winona Ryder, and John Turturro star. Stream The Plot Against America on HBO Max.

Rarely has so much drama been sparked by a herd of sheep, but here we are with this blockbuster miniseries adapting Colleen McCullough's novel in suitably grand style. It's set on a sheep station in the Australian outback over several decades in the 20th century. Rachel Ward plays Meggie Cleary, a tough but damaged young woman who becomes the de facto leader of her family after her father uproots the family from New Zealand to New South Wales to live and work on a ranch owned by her wealthy, widowed Aunt Mary (the legendary Barbara Stanwyck, whose performance lives on in "Kiss me on the mouth!" gif reactions). Both Mary and Meggie have eyes for the same man, Ralph de Bricassart (Richard Chamberlain), who just happens to be a priest. It's expensive, star-studded melodrama par excellence. That is bastardising it. "Meticulously tuned for airflow." That's what Cooler Master claims for the HAF II 500 on its product page. The good news is that this case absolutely has the hardware to back that claim up.

In the front, two Mighty40 F220 fans. These are the largest (220 mm) and thickest (40 mm) consumer fans that Cooler Master could install into this case. The frame is made from PBT and the blade from liquid-crystal polymer (LCP)—that's the same as Noctua's NF-A14x25 G2 and is ideal for this application due to a high tensile strength and stability. Back at Cooler Master HQ, I snapped a pic of my hand in front of this goliath to give an idea of the size (see below).

This fan size offers a clear advantage in terms of airflow. Cooler Master rates this fan to 203.8 CFM, which is a mighty figure for a fan that spins so slowly. Just 1,050 RPM at max speed. For reference, an Arctic P12 Pro is rated to 77 CFM at 3,000 RPM; an Arctic P14 Pro to 110 CFM at 2,500 RPM.

The guidelines cover four major points. The first is focused on consent and establishes that permission must be granted at "every stage" of the generative AI process. This would mean AI companies require permission from actors before models are trained on their performances.

The second point, meanwhile, states that AI replications of an actor's work are to be counted as a new performance under the guidelines. This would mean royalties or compensation must be paid by AI companies to the actors involved.

Next up comes generative AI-powered translations, which takes an actor's performance and automatically changes it to another language. These must maintain the "artistic integrity" of the original performance, the guidelines state, and may not be used without permission in overseas regions.

Lastly, the Japan Actors Union covers generative AI use regarding a dead actor's work. The union's guidelines ban the use of generative AI here, unless that actor had given express permission for the software's use while they were alive. Even with this, an AI company would require permission from the deceased actor's family, the agency or company the actor was affiliated with, and to pay royalties.

It is worth noting that these guidelines are not laws and therefore are not binding rules AI companies must follow. Even so, it marks a major push from the Japan Actors Union for this to change. #3506. Dawn of War 4 clearly loves the Warhammer 40K strategy games that came before, but it misses that they loved to reinvent themselves

I may have set my expectations for Dawn of War 4 too high, but it's a series that, to my mind, never retreads the same ground. It's therefore been disappointing to feel, having played it across two its campaigns that this long-awaited sequel may have taken too few risks - or been too reverent of the games that came before.

Back in the hands of Relic Entertainment, the original Dawn of War was closest to a traditional RTS game. Yet its use of cover mechanics, unit customisation, and onscreen violence felt distinctly new compared to other games I was playing at the time. As someone who'd grown up on Age of Empires and Command & Conquer, seeing units pair off and take part in animated scuffles brought the battlefield to life, especially after spending years of Saturday afternoons sliding static Warhammer figurines around a tabletop.

Then, in Dawn of War 2, Relic dropped traditional basebuilding and pushed the camera in to focus the action on commanding just a few squads. It upended the tried and tested mechanics of the genre I was used to. The hard unit cap meant it simply wasn't possible to assemble a vast army with which to overwhelm the enemy. The only way to succeed was to use each differently-specced squad as it was designed: heavy bolters supplying cover, assault marines launching into the fray to break up the enemy's defensive positions, recon units circumventing the frontline to attack a vulnerable flank. It was a bold reinvention when they could have just offered more of the same.

Admittedly, I played much less of Dawn of War 3, but it too was responding and reacting to what was happening in the RTS genre at the time. Dota 2 and League of Legends were in their ascendency and, rather than ignore the former mods of RTS games, Relic tried to thread their DNA back into the Warhammer-y battlefield. Hero characters were beefed up and each unit gained special abilities to be used in combat. Armies were made both larger and weaker, meaning there was a much higher death toll in each mission. And base building returned, but cover was ejected, changing the dynamics we had got used to in the previous two games.

I run through all this history so you can see why Dawn of War 4 is both a good game and an unexciting one. From what I've played of the Orks and Adeptus Mechanicus campaigns, it can produce enjoyable afternoons of pushing virtual soldiers around a sci-fi battlefield, but all the while it feels too familiar to what came before it. King Art Games have made no secret that of the Dawn of War games, they've mostly closely been cribbing from the original. The battles aren't as large as Dawn of War 3, but the unit cap is significantly larger than Dawn of War 2, and both base building and cover make a return. As a result of this deference to the 2004 original, m first impression – and this is a preview rather than a review – is that Dawn of War 4 is a competent RTS that immediately feels dated.

I went back to the original Dawn of War to compare the two. Dawn of War 4 doesn't entirely miss the opportunity to expand: your units, for instance, have far more customisation options, allowing you to swap out their weapons and equip them with additional abilities. Plus, there are more tools available for grouping and selecting your units, making it a little easier to manage your forces. And, in all the ways you would expect, it looks like a more modern game, with sharper looking textures, more detailed models, and a camera that lets you peer even closer upon the violence. But while the original Dawn of War is fun to play today, it has decades-old issues that nonetheless crop up in Dawn of War 4 as well.

Despite your being able to group units, select all on screen, and select all units of single type, the larger your army grows, the less your ability to make meaningful tactical choices becomes. The unit toolbar, which Dawn of War 4 places at the bottom of your screen, fills with squashed unit portraits that eventually become indistinguishable. The Orks suffer from this more even than the Adeptus Mechanicus, as their roster is so cheap to recruit. With the growing inability to be selective, in mission after mission, I just grabbed as many units as I could and threw them at the enemies' defences. It was effective and I don't want to complain too much about the victories, but they weren't satisfying when attaining them involved so few of my own, meaningful choices. This was also the weakness of the original Dawn of War's design and why the move to smaller squads to focusing on squads was so effective in its sequel. The ability to roll over your enemy with a large army was removed entirely.

The Adeptus Mechanicus, at least, make a much better first impression. Unlike the Waaargh-obsessed boyz, it's hard to grab a lump of soldiers, chuck them at the enemy, and expect results. I was more inclined to play around the specialisations of certain units, like the Skitarii Rangers, excellent long-range anti-infantry snipers who - when plonked into cover - will whittle down any greenskins or Necrons that march on their position. Meanwhile, the Skitarii Vanguard, armed with radiation-emitting ammunition, can vapourise the more heavily-armoured troops. However, both Skitarii ranged units are truly awful in melee, so to keep them effective, I paired them with units of Sicarian Ruststalkers. These blade-armed cyborg assassins are brutal in close combat, and can hold back infantry while the Rangers and Vanguard finish them off. Combine and position your units well, and you'll scythe through enemy troops, but do it haphazardly and they'll crumble just as quickly.

The early missions in the Adeptus Mechanicus campaign introduce you to Potentia Delta-9, a Technoarcheologist exploring the surface of Kronus to learn about what stirs in its depths, and Skitarii Marshal Sek-IX-23, who is doing his best to put down the Ork uprising. It was the opening where the Adeptus Mechanicus were most enjoyable to command., the shape and smaller scale of the battlefields giving a lot more opportunities to embrace the tactics of managing their extreme strengths and weaknesses.

In one early defence mission, Potentia finds herself surrounded by newly-awoken Necron troops. (If you've seen Dawn of War 4's trailers or know the history of planet Kronus from the previous games, you won't be surprised to learn it's Necrons that are stirring from a decades-long slumber, like Sleeping Beauty if she were clad in self-repairing battle armour.) You must hold out against waves of the immortal robots with their fizzing green eyes and even fizzier laser cannons. It's a set up that pressures you into thoughtfully applying your resources, ensuring that each entrance to your base has a firm defensive line composed of mixed troops. Then, as the waves against you grow stronger, and the attacks come against you from different angles, you must relocate your forces to bloody the nose of the attackers. Complicating things is the appearance of trapped Adeptus Mechanicus units around the map; if you can break out from your base and rescue them, they'll bolster your army, but at the risk of depleting your defences.

It's a mission design I've seen before in many RTS games, which does undermine the enjoyment, but it hints at the tactical play that's missing in the Ork campaign. Sadly, the further I get into the Adeptus Mechanicus campaign, the larger the battles become, with fewer opportunities to focus on unit-specific tactics. The result is that these too are won by treating your forces as a bolter-armed blob that just rolls over the enemy. It doesn't matter whether you're fighting Necrons or Orks, they all fall down before the bolter-blob.

As I say, I may have set my expectations for a new Dawn of War game too high. Over the past year, I've spoken to the team about their attention to detail in capturing units in the Adeptus Mechanicus and Necron armies, like the perpetual motion machine walkers that can't stand still, or the mega unit you can summon who makes no secret of the fact they hate you. Those conversations only stoked my hopes, which also might point to why I'm feeling a little deflated after what I've played.

I will still be playing through the full four campaigns when I get the opportunity (the preview build is limited to two of the four factions and I'm only able to talk about the opening six missions in each of those stories). And one change I will be making will be to whack the difficulty up. If I can make whomping the enemy harder, perhaps it will push me into thinking more tactically.

Still, while that may make Dawn of War 4 more enjoyable, it won't hide the fact this feels like a missed opportunity in a series that has marked itself by its reinvention, especially as it's recreated weaknesses of the original game which were addressed in its sequels.

#3502. Final Fantasy 7 Revelation's Hard Mode is available from the start this time, so you won't need to replay the mammoth RPG to get to the good stuff

Final Fantasy 7 Revelation will let players access Hard Mode from the very beginning of the journey, unlike FF7 Remake and Rebirth, which made folks roll credits before unlocking the tougher difficulty option.

The tidbit comes courtesy of director Naoki Hamaguchi, who told Hardcore Gamer that Final Fantasy 7 Revelation's Hard Mode "won't function in the exact same way" as it did previously. For next year's third and final chapter, the team "reimagined the high-difficulty experience to align with the new game design."

You see, this time around, the world of Gaia supposedly opens up really early in the game. Cloud and company can fly straight to some of the game's toughest battles as soon as they jump into their flying airship, whether they're ready for the fight or not, so Square Enix's approach to difficulty had to change too.

"Hard Mode is selectable from the very start of this game," Hamaguchi explained. "Therefore, players who want to tackle challenging battles and high-difficulty gameplay right from the beginning can enjoy that experience from their very first playthrough. This is because, unlike Remake and Rebirth, Revelation offers a wide variety of end-game content even after you've beaten the game. Rather than relying solely on the traditional flow of challenging Hard Mode after beating the game, we aimed to create a game where players can enjoy various new content and challenges even after completing the main story."

As much as I loved both Remake and Rebirth, the combat never properly pushed me to my limits and tested my creaky reflexes until I finally reached that second playthrough - in the case of Rebirth, that meant playing the game for at least 50 hours. FF7 Revelation is shaping up to be another behemoth that could easily take 100 hours to fully complete, apparently, so I'm very relieved to hear that I can get to the good stuff from the jump. Hard Mode is truly where these games shine.

In a separate tweet, Hamaguchi also shared there'll be a Dynamic Mode at launch, like in Rebirth, "allowing players to enjoy consistently challenging battles throughout the game." It also looks like we'll be able to adjust how tricky those many, many mini-games are. (Damn you, Moogles!)
