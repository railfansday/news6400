Servant Toinette (Rachel Bloom) is more than happy to assist Angélique in any way possible, in part because she knows true love when she sees it but more likely because she enjoys getting one over on the demanding old fool who employs her. There are accommodating doctors (the wonderful Brooks Ashmanskas), forever ready with the cure-all enemas upon which Argan insists, and a goofy doctor’s son (Hiram Delgado) who Argan wants for a son-in-law.

Farce, as you might expect, ensues, with various plots and plans and motivations slamming into one another as the brilliantly attired cast comes and goes and swirls around the sedentary Argan. Beowulf Boritt’s 17th Century great room is as lovely as it is efficient, with panels and doors providing easy access in and out of the action for the ever-busy characters. (Their comings and goings are easy to keep track of, thanks to the color-coded costumes – each character is assigned a dominant color scheme, so much so that brother Béralde is once referred to as “the green man.”)

No need to point out that complications get ever more complicated, that it’ll all work out in the end and that this fine cast knows just how to maneuver the farce and deliver the bons mots. Bloom, as the jester-like truth-teller, is a stand-out, pointedly delivering her lines with a modern-sounding ease rather than the amusing Mid-Atlantic upper crusty thespianism the posher characters utilize.

This, after all, is a man who can upstage even while all-but-permanently seated on an otherwise motion-filled stage. When he was last on Broadway in the brilliant Eureka Day, Irwin proved that he could convincingly inhabit a normal human absent as any trace of the harlequin, but no such restrictions are required for The Imaginary Invalid.

Indeed, the man who once held his own against both Robin Williams and Steve Martin in an Off Broadway production of Waiting For Godot gets free reign to clown around here, especially in a lovely moment that closes the show. That La-Z-Boy doesn’t stand a chance.

If you use Google Chrome, you can turn on a new safety feature that will warn you if you navigate to a suspicious website that looks like others already known for malicious behavior. The alerts are part of Enhanced Protection mode in Chrome's Safe Browsing, which checks your activity against potentially harmful sites and warns you if you're at risk of encountering malware, phishing, intrusive ads, or social engineering attacks.

Most internet users are familiar with the full-page alerts that appear if you navigate to a site that's known to be malicious. These red "dangerous site" warnings indicate a high risk of harm to the user and can't really be ignored since they take up the entire screen and urge you to go back to safety.

The new "suspicious site" alerts indicate possible risk of harm based on patterns consistent with other known malicious sites. This means that Google isn't as confident that the website is actually dangerous, but it looks a lot like others that are spreading malware or running phishing scams. These suspicious site warnings are smaller pop-ups with the text "This site doesn't seem right" and two button options: Mark as safe or Back to safety. The different colors, text, and formatting make it easy for users to distinguish between what Google deems dangerous versus suspicious.

Safe Browsing in Chrome has three options: standard protection, enhanced protection, and no protection at all (which isn't recommended). Standard protection checks your browsing activity, extensions, and downloads against Google's list of unsafe sites, which is updated periodically. By contrast, enhanced protection works in real time and uses AI analysis. Note that enhanced protection does require you to share more information about your activity to Google, and the data are linked to your Google Account.

While privacy-conscious users may be hesitant to send more info to Google, enhanced protection is likely worthwhile for those who aren't as savvy to advanced scam tactics (and therefore more likely to enter credentials or financial information on or download a file from a malicious website). To enable Enhanced Protection and receive suspicious website warnings, go to Settings > Privacy and security in Chrome and toggle Enhanced protection on under the Safe Browsing section.

#3084. What to Wear When Exercising Outdoors, Based on How Cold It Is

I fell in love with cold weather running while I was training for a spring marathon. It wasn’t a choice at first: Some weeks, the weather was bad for days on end, and if I stayed home, I would fall behind in my training. Everybody else in my training group was braving the elements, so I did too—with no regrets.

I got to run on quiet mornings while snow was softly falling. I signed up for a winter race that herded finishers into a heated barn for hot chocolate and soup. I set a personal record at a spring race, during which I pushed myself hard without breaking a sweat. I now find it sad when somebody tells me they’re afraid of running in the cold, or that they would run in the winter but don’t know what to wear.

No, cold weather doesn’t have to stop you from running, but getting dressed can be a puzzle: How do you keep from freezing your butt off at first and avoid overheating once you start to sweat? Fortunately, it isn’t as tough as it looks. With the right gear, your fingers and ears can stay toasty while you jog through a winter wonderland. Below, I’ll give you a temperature-by-temperature running cheat sheet, and then dive into the specifics on how to buy the best socks, shirts, and more.

When I get dressed to run in winter, I take a look at the “feels like” temperature, not the actual high or low. I also make sure to check the weather for the time I’ll go running, not for the whole day. It doesn’t matter how warm it will be at 2 p.m. if my run will be happening at 6 a.m., before the sun is up. I use the Carrot Weather app, but any app that gives you hourly feels-like temps will work just fine.

Next, use the 20-degree rule. Imagine that the temperature is 20 degrees (Fahrenheit) warmer than it really is. What would you wear then? Here's an example: if it’s 50 degrees out, you should dress as if it were 70: in a T-shirt and shorts, most likely. If it’s 30 degrees, you’d dress as if it were 50: long pants and maybe a light jacket. Get the idea?

Everybody has their own preferences. Some people wear shorts even when it’s snowing, while others will pop a beanie on as soon as there’s a detectable chill in the air. I’m a pretty middle-of-the-road runner when it comes to keeping warm, so here’s what I wear at each “feels like” temperature, starting with warmer weather and working our way colder. I'll give a guide to each type of garment or accessory at the end.

At 50+ degrees Fahrenheit, I’m wearing basically the same things I would for a summer run.

Head and hands: Nothing special. No gloves and no hat, unless it’s a baseball cap to shade my eyes from the sun.

We’re starting to feel the first bit of a chill. This is where I may start bringing gloves, although they’ll probably get stuffed into a pocket by the end of the run. Remember, with the 20-degree rule, it will feel like 60 degrees by the time you're warmed up.

Bottoms: Leggings, lightweight long pants, or shorts.

Top: Something with long sleeves, but ideally made of thin material (think long-sleeved T-shirt, not a full-on sweatshirt or jacket). If you wore a sun shirt in the summer, that's a good layering option for these cool temps. I like my REI Sahara Shade hoodie here, but any long sleeve will do. If you run fall and winter races, you'll probably be handed a free long-sleeved version at some point.

Hands: I usually just pull my shirt's sleeves over my hands, but you could wear knit gloves if you want. Consider something like Flipmits for an intermediate option (they can be folded down into a wrist cuff when your hands warm up).

Head: A headband if you need protection for your ears.

You may see a few snowflakes drifting down, but don’t bundle up in a snowsuit. Once you’re warmed up, it will only feel like 50 degrees—so go for something in “light jacket” territory.

Bottoms: Leggings or running tights. The fleece-lined kind can be nice. (I have these.)

Top: You’ll want some kind of jacket or sweatshirt. A lightweight, reflective rain jacket is great here, or just throw on a regular hoodie over your T-shirt. (I go with cotton sweats in dry weather or light snow, but I'd go for synthetics or wool if there's rain or sleet.)

Hands: Definitely wear gloves. The cheap knit ones are usually good enough, but consider treating yourself to a nice pair of running gloves.

Head: Your choice of hat or headband; I’d stick with the headband.

Feet: This is around the temperature you may start to want warmer socks. That goes double if snow is in play: snow that falls onto your shoes will work its way through the mesh, melt, and make your feet wet. Wool is a good material here, so consider something like these from Smartwool.

This is where I’ll officially say it’s “cold.” Whatever warm-weather gear you were getting away with at warmer temps, you’ll probably have to swap it out now. Shorts people will break out their long pants. Headband people will wear a real hat.

Bottoms: Leggings and shorts. I don’t care if you think it looks goofy to wear both; this is an A+ hack. Or try a puffer skirt.

Top: It’s definitely jacket weather. Rather than getting a warmer jacket, I like to wear the same light jacket as I did at 30 degrees, but this time with a long-sleeved fleece or wool base layer underneath.

Hands: Real gloves, please. You can keep wearing your little knit gloves if you’re able to tuck your hands inside your jacket sleeves, or if you have a pair of mittens to wear over top; otherwise, get windproof gloves.

Head: A hat, and maybe also a buff for your nose and mouth. (If you have any cloth masks still hanging around from the early COVID days, they’re perfect for cold weather runs.)

Feet: Warm socks for sure. If you’ll be doing a lot of running in these temperatures, you may want shoes that are specialized for the elements. Look for ones with Gore-Tex uppers, like the Brooks Ghost GTX.

This is where I tap out. If a run is just barely in the teens, I’ll make do with the 20-degree items I mentioned above, adding an extra layer wherever needed. But if that isn't enough, I opt for the treadmill. You certainly can run in this weather if you are determined. You’ll want windproof everything, some kind of face covering, and you may need eye protection such as goggles.

Whenever the weather gets to your own personal “too cold” temperature, remember that you still have options. The early afternoon is often the warmest part of the day, so a run may be tolerable then. There’s also no shame in using a treadmill (even if you have to join a cheap gym for this reason alone).

Everybody has different preferences, and with practice you’ll figure out if you’re the kind of person who wears three shirts before it hits freezing or the type to run through the snow in shorts. But this should give you a starting point when you head out for your first (or next) cold weather run.

As you continue on your cold-weather running journey, you may need to buy some new gear or repurpose things from elsewhere in your closet. And yes, the first few minutes of your run will feel chilly. But if you’re wearing the right shoes and clothes (and planning routes with a few extra factors in mind, which I’ll discuss below), winter running is not just feasible; it’s actually fun. So here are the items you may want to consider.

Your feet need to be warm and, if possible, dry. Warm socks go without saying, but there are some extra considerations when you’re picking socks to run in. Wool socks are ideal because they keep your feet warm even when they get wet. You may not think of winter as a wet time, but any snow that meets up with your body heat will turn into water. If you don’t have wool socks, synthetic wicking fabrics are a good second choice. Cotton is a no-no

First, the socks have to be able to fit into your shoes. If you’ve been running in super thin cotton or synthetic socks all summer, thick socks may not fit. Smartwool run socks are available in a thin version that can fit no problem—but in colder weather you’ll want some thickness for extra warmth. That thickness, in turn, may mean that your winter running shoes need to be roomier than the shoes you wear in summer. If you’re buying a new pair, make sure to try them on with the type of socks you intend to run in.

If your warm socks fit, you can make do with the same shoes you wore in the summer. Your feet will stay dry if the weather is dry and you stay out of puddles. If you encounter snow and slush, though, your feet will have to endure warm-but-wet conditions for the rest of your run. That can be okay for short runs, but for serious winter running, consider upgrading your shoes.

Some running shoes are available with water-resistant uppers. This level of water resistance will keep stray snowflakes out of your socks, but it isn’t reliable if you’ll be stepping in slush puddles. In that case, you’ll want something more waterproof, like Gore-Tex shoes, many of which are made for running trails.

If you expect to run on a lot of ice or packed snow, especially on trails, spikes can give you a little extra grip.

Yaktrax makes a rubber harness that stretches to fit your shoe, with steel coils and small spikes, for about $40.

Microspikes are a notch up from there, costing around $90 for what look like a miniature version of the chains you’d put on car tires.

And if you’re really dedicated—and are seeking a purpose-built pair of snow shoes—you can attach individual spikes: either these traction spikes from IceSpike, or via a DIY approach using sheet metal screws. (There’s a tutorial here.)

The versions that include rubber, like Yaktrax, can wear out quickly on pavement, so it’s best to save these for runs on trails or on unplowed paths. You’ll also want to be careful about the fit. I tried a pair that were similar to Yaktrax, but they were a tad too big. Shortly into my run, I found that the rubber stretched enough to pull the front set of spikes up over the top of my shoe. I've since gotten better-fitting ones, and they work great.

Spikes and specialized shoes help you go more places, in worse weather—but remember that they’re not required to just head out on a chilly day. Regular shoes are fine if the weather is cold but dry, or if you’re willing to put up with a little moisture.

I actually love when the weather gets cold enough that I need a jacket, because then I get to wear something that gives me extra pockets! Stick with thin jackets that have some room inside. By the time temps dip into the 20s or below, you’ll be doing some serious layering: for example, an undershirt, a long-sleeved warm layer, and a jacket. A great option for that warm layer is a wool base layer like this one. (Don’t worry, it’s not itchy.) If you can’t wear wool, a synthetic fleece is a good alternative.

Before you run out to buy all new clothes, experiment with what’s in your closet. Try repurposing a thin sweater, for example. Some people would never wear cotton for running (it can be cold when wet), but I wear it as my undershirt layer and have lived to tell the tale. Just make sure to shower or change after your run, so you aren't sitting around in cold, wet clothes.

The jacket is the most important layer. You can grab a sweatshirt in a pinch, but to be really well-equipped for nasty weather, you’ll want a jacket that is windproof and water-resistant. Jackets made for running are lightweight, for layering. Consider ones that are visible at night, with bright colors and reflective areas, and that come with zippered pockets.

Once it’s too cold for shorts, you’ll want to start layering your bottoms, too. Loose pants let cold air in, which is why leggings or “running tights” like these are standard winter wear for all genders. Running tights come in different thicknesses; go for something fleece-lined for your coldest runs, or layer two pairs.

You need to keep your ears warm, but don’t reach for a winter hat right away: an ear warmer or headband can cover your ears while still letting heat escape from the top of your head. And if anything is falling from the sky (say, snow), a baseball cap will keep it out of your eyes.

Fleece or knit hats are great for cold enough days, of course. The dividing line between earwarmer weather and hat weather is a personal one, and depends on whether you’re overheating. If you’re wearing a hat but sweating into your base layers, the hat is probably overkill.

Gloves, however, are essential. I keep a pair of cheap knit gloves in my jacket pockets, but windy or very cold days call for something stronger. You can layer gloves; I find gloves under mittens work well. You can also get windproof insulated gloves, which are warmer than any number of layers of holey knit gloves.

Your face will freeze on windy or very cold days, so this is where your old stash of pandemic face masks really shines. Consider a balaclava or even a fleece-lined bandana. A neck gaiter stays on better than a scarf if your neck is cold. At this point, every inch of you is covered but your eyes. Sunglasses can take care of that.

In recent weeks, Google has been automatically connecting Android users' phone numbers to every Google account they are signed into on their devices—without requesting consent to do so. This means that if you are logged into personal, work, and disposable accounts on your Android phone, Google may have silently verified and linked your number with all of them even if you've deliberately kept them separate. If you that's not something you want, there's a way to fix it.

As Android Authority reports, some Android users have recently received confirmation emails from an official Google address stating that their phone number has been verified. Google has required occasional phone number re-verification but appears to have updated its support documentation about a year ago to make this feature opt-out by default. As part of this process, Google also adds your phone number to all Google Accounts that are signed in on the device, including current accounts and phone numbers and any that are added or updated in the future.

If you have an Android device, you can disable automatic phone number verification, which should prevent Google from linking sign-in accounts together. You can find this toggle in your Settings app under Google or Google services and preferences > All Services > Privacy & Security > Phone number verification. (Note that the exact path may differ by device.) You should periodically check to ensure this toggle remains off.

Updating this setting does not disconnect phone numbers and Google accounts that have already been linked. You can find out which are which via your Google Account Personal Info page. Select your profile picture in the upper-right corner to go through each signed-in account and open the Phone section to select and delete any numbers you don't want associated.

Finally, Malwarebytes advises scrolling down to the Usage section on the Phone page to view which services are listed as well as reviewing your Security & sign-in settings. If you added your phone number for two-step verification or to a separate app like Google Pay, deleting it from the Phone page won't remove it entirely from your account, and you'll need to update those settings separately. Automatic phone verification shouldn't be the culprit here, but it's useful to audit what your number is being used for and on which accounts if you want to keep things separate.

#3054. A lost Schrödinger manuscript reveals a twist in quantum physics

For more than 80 years, a manuscript penned by one of the founders of quantum physics has been gathering dust in an Austrian library.

Now brought to light by physics historian Flavio Del Santo of the University of Geneva, the three-page document is the very soul of brevity – but on it is scrawled a long-lost contribution to one of the most contentious debates in quantum mechanics.

And it offers a surprising insight into what Erwin Schrödinger himself believed about the nature of reality.

Rather than the discomfort with quantum randomness he is often portrayed as espousing, Schrödinger believed that there were aspects of the Universe that couldn't be predicted – a belief he held even before the emergence of quantum theory in the 1920s.

"The strongest plea in favor of indeterminism is older than quantum mechanics," Schrödinger wrote in the unpublished manuscript.

In classical physics, there's no such thing as true randomness. Dice may have nicks and flaws that influence how they roll. Computer random-number generators are usually driven by algorithms. Even coin flips are governed by physical forces that, in theory, could be predicted.

This phenomenon is known as determinism, and it plays an important role in our understanding of the physical Universe, stating that every classical behavior is determined by the conditions that precede it, even if we don't know what all those conditions are.

(The absoluteness of classical determinism has been questioned, but it remains a foundational assumption of classical mechanics.)

But quantum mechanics – as is its wont – marches to the beat of a very different drum.

At the quantum scale, the behavior of particles such as photons and electrons can only be predicted in terms of probabilities. We can calculate the likelihood of a particular outcome with extraordinary precision, but we can't definitively predict what an individual particle will do.

This raises a profound question: Is the Universe fundamentally random, or are there hidden factors that determine particle behavior that quantum mechanics hasn't yet accounted for?

These hypothetical hidden factors are known as hidden variables, and they are what they sound like – unknown properties or conditions that, if we did know them, would allow physicists to predict the quantum behavior of individual particles.

In 1932, mathematician John von Neumann – one of the leading authorities on quantum theory's foundational math – published what was widely interpreted as a mathematical proof that such hidden variables were impossible.

His argument carried considerable weight, and for more than a decade, research into hidden-variable models largely stalled.

Not everyone was convinced. In 1935, mathematician Grete Hermann poked significant holes in von Neumann's reasoning. However, her critique was mostly ignored, and you can probably guess why without much difficulty.

By the mid-1940s, however, two prominent physicists had inadvertently revived the argument for hidden variables – by, ironically, speaking out against them, in agreement with von Neumann.

In May 1943, mathematician and physicist Edmund Taylor Whittaker delivered a lecture arguing that quantum mechanics had finally demolished the classical notion of determinism.

Unlike the apparent randomness of a coin toss, he argued, quantum randomness was genuine, and no amount of additional information could make individual outcomes predictable.

Then, in January 1944, physicist Satyendra Nath Bose made a similar argument in an address to the Indian Science Congress, declaring that "physicists have gained knowledge but lost their faith" in the deterministic laws of nature.

In July 1944, mathematician Henry Piaggio published an article in Nature titled "Collapse of Determinism," reporting on Bose's arguments. It prompted a flurry of responses from physicists who weren't nearly so convinced that determinism was dead and buried.

Over the ensuing months, the argument blossomed across the journal's pages, with researchers picking apart the supposed impossibility of hidden variables – including Whittaker's seemingly straightforward example involving photons and polarized light.

In his 1943 lecture, Whittaker had proposed a seemingly simple demonstration of why hidden variables couldn't exist, using photons – individual particles of light – passing through a polarizer, a device that filters light according to its orientation.

Quantum mechanics can predict what proportion of photons will pass through the polarizer at any given angle.

But Whittaker argued that if each photon carried a hidden instruction determining whether it would pass through, changing the angle of the polarizer while the photons were in flight would render that instruction useless.

Therefore, he concluded, the outcome couldn't be predetermined by hidden variables.

Enter Schrödinger. Or rather, stay in the wings, Schrödinger.

The physicist had been following the debate, and sometime after November 1944, he penned a response of his own, also titled "Collapse of Determinism" (there were several articles published as part of the debate that used the same title).

In it, he identified a significant flaw in Whittaker's argument.

Whittaker assumed each photon carried a hidden variable with only two possible values, one determining whether it would pass through the polarizer and the other whether it would be reflected.

But Schrödinger pointed out that there was no reason to restrict the hidden variable to just two values. What if it could take any value between zero and one?

He then constructed a mathematical model in which the outcome depended on both the photon's hidden variable and the polarizer's angle.

With the right rules, the model reproduced the probabilities quantum mechanics predicts, without requiring each individual interaction to be random.

There was a second part of the model that was also important. Schrödinger devised a deterministic rule for updating the hidden variable after a photon has passed through the polarizer – which meant his model could be used to reproduce the predictions for subsequent measurements.

Importantly, his aim was not to defend determinism because he necessarily believed in it. He was defending the possibility of determinism against specific claims that it was impossible.

"Experimental evidence + logical deduction can never prove that either the deterministic or the indeterministic view is impossible," he wrote.

Schrödinger's response, however, never saw the light of day.

Why he never published it remains a mystery, although Del Santo has a possible explanation.

In 1944, physicist Heinrich Pelzer published a similar hidden-variable model, showing that Whittaker's argument didn't hold up. Schrödinger may have learned of Pelzer's work and decided that the point had already been made.

Yet Schrödinger's model went further, providing an explicit mathematical rule for how a photon's hidden variable would change after passing through a polarizer, allowing it to account for successive measurements.

Whatever his reasons, the manuscript remained unpublished, preserved in an Austrian archive until Del Santo brought it to light more than eight decades later.

Although Schrödinger is often portrayed as a champion of determinism, his own philosophical position was rather different.

As early as the 1920s, before modern quantum mechanics had even emerged, Schrödinger had argued that the laws of nature might be fundamentally statistical, rather than governed by an underlying clockwork of perfectly predictable events.

Influenced by physicist Franz Exner, he even described classical determinism as a "philosophic prejudice" and an "a priori belief".

And his unpublished manuscript suggests that his position hadn't fundamentally changed by the 1940s – nor did it later.

Although the "Collapse of Determinism" flurry of the 1940s is largely forgotten today, physicists remain divided by its core positions.

Perhaps what we can learn from Schrödinger's belated contribution is to keep an open mind. In a rather fitting twist, the man who gave us Schrödinger's cat has left us with a question that remains, in its own way, both alive and dead: Is the Universe deterministic… or isn't it?

"Schrödinger … had developed a remarkably strong commitment to indeterminism even before, and independently of, quantum mechanics, which he maintained throughout his career," Del Santo writes.

Models such as Schrödinger's, Del Santo concludes, "serve as proofs of principle against widespread beliefs or alleged impossibility claims, as strikingly illustrated by the case of Schrödinger reconstructed here, but never as expressions of a dogmatic commitment to determinism."

His paper is available on preprint server arXiv.
