#250. Southwest Airlines cuts 2026 profit forecast on higher fuel

Southwest Airlines became the third major U.S. airline this month to cut its 2026 profit forecast due to higher fuel prices, but CEO Bob Jordan said travelers continue to book despite higher fares.

The carrier expects to earn between $2.60 and $2.85 a share on an adjusted basis this year, down from its forecast in July of between $3.25 to as much as $4.25 a share.

Southwest forecast per-share adjusted earnings of between 55 cents and 80 cents in the fourth quarter and unit revenue, a gauge of airfare, up as much as 21% from a year earlier. It said it expects to grow capacity by up to 4% year over year.

"Demand remained strong as we lapped last year's bag fee and other commercial initiatives, reinforcing our belief that the earnings power we've built is durable, even with significantly higher fuel costs," Jordan said in an earnings release.

The airline said its average passenger fare was $231.69 in the third quarter, up almost 27% from the same quarter of 2025. A spokesman said that number does not include add-on fees. The number of revenue passengers carried fell 3.2% to 33.47 million people.

But Jordan told CNBC's Phil LeBeau that demand was strong across every geography and product.

"The consumer is incredibly strong and we do not see that abating," Jordan said. "We're seeing strength in both the holiday period and the non-holiday period."

Under pressure to grow revenue, Southwest has been remaking its longtime business model over the past several years, scrapping its old policies like two free checked bags per passenger and open seating. In its place, Southwest has started charging for many seat assignments and added a bag fee, similar to major competitors.

For the third quarter, Southwest posted net income of $270 million, or 55 cents a share, up from $54 million or 10 cents a share a year earlier. Revenue rose more than 21% to $8.42 billion.

Adjusting for one-time items, Southwest reported 66 cents a share.

Delta Air Lines and United Airlines also cut their profit outlooks for the rest of the year when they reported third-quarter earnings. Both of those airlines reported top- and bottom-line misses for the third quarter.

#242. Bose Is Buying Skullcandy. It’s Not as Weird as It Sounds

Longtime audio giant Bose announced on Wednesday that it's acquiring Skullcandy, the lifestyle brand known for its bass-heavy headphones. Skullcandy will remain a separate company within Bose's Premium Consumer Audio business and will still be based in Park City, Utah.

The acquisition isn't out of left field. Bose has lent its audio expertise to Skullcandy over the past year under its Sound by Bose licensing program. Skullcandy designs the headphones and then works with Bose on the audio front. The relationship began with the Skullcandy Method 360 ANC earbuds in 2025 and more recently, with the Crusher 1080 ANC headphones in July.

When WIRED spoke with Skullcandy CEO Brian Garofalow in July about the partnership, Garofalow highlighted the 23-year-old company's ability to build community and culture, specifically around the board sports community (the first Skullcandy product was designed on a ski chairlift). But he admitted the company wasn't “the greatest at the engineering part of innovation with products.”

This is hardly Bose's first audio acquisition (though it may be the most mainstream). Bose acquired McIntosh Group, owners of luxury audio brands McIntosh Laboratory and Sonus Faber, back in 2024. But Bose seems to be after Skullcandy's connection to its community.

“What does the brand stand for? Does it have meaning for consumers? Is it relevant to a group of consumers that, frankly, we otherwise aren’t reaching?" Bose CEO Lila Snyder tells WIRED. "If you think about McIntosh, Sonus Faber, and Skullcandy, they all fit into that realm. Skullcandy gives us access to a customer base that we think we’re largely missing with the Bose brand.”

That's why Bose has no plans to relocate Skullcandy from Park City, Utah, which Snyder says is the epicenter of board sports culture. “We want to make sure we’re right at the heart of it, as they always have been,” she says. Skullcandy products are also affordable, especially compared to other Bose products and Bose-owned brands, which lets Bose compete at both ends of the market.

As WIRED's headphone reviewer, I find Bose and Skullcandy an odd pairing. In consumer audio, Bose pioneered active noise-cancelation (ANC) in headphones, and maintains that legacy with ANC-leading products like the Bose QuietComfort Ultra Headphones (2nd Gen) and Bose QuietComfort Ultra Earbuds (2nd Gen). Skullcandy, on the other hand, has often been viewed as the butt of the joke when it comes to audio quality. It has had a reputation for middling sound, bass-over-everything headphones, and a loud, bold aesthetic.

Recent collaborations have started to change those perceptions—WIRED rated the Method 360 ANC earbuds an 8/10, noting good sound quality and ANC for the money. Snyder says those buds and the recent Crusher collab are just the tip of the iceberg. “It’s the beginning of what we think we can do together,” she says.

I’m very curious about what comes next from Skullcandy, headphones or otherwise. Either way, we’ll see if Bose's acoustic muscle can give the lifestyle brand the permanent glow-up it’s been chasing.

#241. LE SSERAFIMs Huh Yunjin and creator Emily Fan know that fandom is personal

At Spotify's New York City office, LE SSERAFIM's HUH YUNJIN and podcaster Emily Fan are comparing notes on what it means to be a creator in 2026. One is a K-pop star accustomed to sold-out concerts, choreographed performances, and a devoted global fandom. The other has spent more than a decade building an audience around her personality, turning her humor and observations about internet culture and everyday life into videos that keep people coming back for more.

But the two have something else in common: They're fans of each other.

The playful moment captures the spirit of their conversation, which you can watch in full below. For all the differences between the highly produced world of K-pop and the personality-driven world of internet creators, YUNJIN and Fan have a lot to learn from each other, particularly when it comes to building and maintaining a fandom.

Mashable was invited behind the scenes of the Show Notes recording, which centered on YUNJIN's recent move into podcasting with HMM (Huh's Memo), a personal podcast on STAN:A, HYBE's artist-led podcast channel on Spotify. Unlike a traditional interview show, HMM finds YUNJIN reflecting on her thoughts and experiences in familiar spaces, like recording studios and rehearsal rooms. Fan is no stranger to the format herself, hosting her own podcast, Much to Discuss...!

Of course, K-pop idols already share an enormous amount of their lives with fans. Between livestreams, social media, dedicated fan platforms, and vlogs, the industry has built an entire ecosystem around keeping artists and their audiences connected. But podcasting offers another way to deepen that relationship, giving artists like YUNJIN room to slow down, reflect, and explore their thoughts at greater length.

And as both women explained, building a fandom isn't simply about creating something people want to watch or listen to. It's about giving them a reason to care about the person behind it.

Following the Show Notes taping, Mashable sat down with YUNJIN and Fan to talk about podcasting, creative freedom, the surprising moments that resonate with fans, and what separates an audience from a fandom.

Emily Fan: I really feel the same. When you're catering too much of yourself to external factors, that's only going to end up biting you in the butt in the long term. Being able to express what you authentically want to express and then trusting that the audience will follow is the more beneficial route to go. If you start making stuff for the sake of following what's mainstream or what you think other people are going to like, you're going to cultivate an audience who maybe wouldn't actually be interested in the things you want to say.

Fan: And with content creators, there's a sense of feeling like you're on the same level. There's less of that degree of separation. #240. David's Bridal plans to spin off AI platform Pearl into publicly traded company

David's Bridal is planning to spin off its artificial intelligence platform, Pearl, into a stand-alone publicly traded company targeting a valuation of roughly $350 million, CNBC learned exclusively.

The company expects the deal to close in the first half of 2027.

David's Bridal filed a nonbinding letter of intent on Thursday to separate out Pearl. The AI-centered business would own David's Bridal's intellectual property and look to build on its growing AI model, while the original company would try to strengthen its physical retail business.

"We have a fast-growing, high-margin business generating positive cash flow right now in Pearl, and going public now will help us attract the talent and give us more optionality to fuel the growth that we believe that we have," David's Bridal CEO Kelly Cook, who will take over as CEO of Pearl after the deal closes, told CNBC in an interview.

Cook said the companies will work hand in hand to serve customers throughout the wedding-planning process, adding that the split is "positioning both companies for strategic growth."

Pearl's services include an agentic AI-powered wedding-planning platform, a proprietary tool to streamline appointments and communications and a digital content network with original programming. It uses AI to personalize its suggestions based on its data collection for each client.

David's Bridal expects Pearl to generate an EBITDA margin of more than 30% this year and is projecting revenue growth of more than 100% in 2027, exceeding $85 million. It's expected to be debt-free after the deal closes and have positive free cash flow over the next two years, according to the company.

"Pearl has earned the right to stand on its own because it's created more value than we've expected," Cook said.

Following the transaction, Pearl will own David's Bridal's intellectual property and license it back to the chain.

"It's a very healthy business model," Cook said.

She added that the demand remains strong for both of the businesses. Grand View Research estimates the U.S. wedding services market will top $96 billion in 2026 — and both Pearl and David's Bridal want to be at the forefront of that segment.

The companies will continue to have a "relationship built on reciprocity," she said, sharing proprietary data and pointing customers to the other business for the next steps in their wedding planning. The split will allow each company to refine its focus, she added.

Still, the plan to go public comes at a time when AI stocks have been struggling. The initial public offering market for consumer companies has been lackluster, more companies are postponing their public debuts and investors are beginning to lose confidence in the AI boom.

Cook said the differentiator for Pearl is that the company is "not AI for AI's sake."

"Most AI companies have to find a customer," she said. "We already have her ... we have her information, and it's less about the AI side and more about modernizing this entire ecosystem for her that desperately needed modernization."

David's Bridal is no stranger to financial struggles. The company filed for bankruptcy protection in 2018 and emerged a few months later. It then filed a second time in 2023, citing a significant business slowdown from the pandemic and high inflation. To save itself, the company then sold its business to asset manager Cion Investment Corp. to avoid a shutdown.

As part of the spinoff deal, Cion has proposed to invest $25 million as an anchor investor in the separate company.

Cook, who took over as CEO last year to help it transition from a legacy retailer to a more cohesive online and in-store experience, said the company has "been through a hard chapter," but those struggles led to the decision to split the business.

"That's no secret, we've talked about that openly," she said. "What's extraordinary is what came out of that process is a leaner David's, a more focused David's ... I think it's a move from strength."

#225. NASA’s Curiosity rover reveals a spectacular new view of Mars with a mystery on the horizon

NASA's Curiosity rover has captured a spectacular new panorama of Mars, providing its clearest look yet at a series of mysterious cliffs shaped by wind. Scientists have been eager to examine these distant formations up close, and the latest images offer an exciting preview of what lies ahead. Taken at 8:30 a.m. local Mars time, the scene reveals striking blue tones across the foreground while morning sunlight illuminates rugged cliffs known as yardangs along the horizon.

Curiosity recorded the panorama using its Mastcam on August 11, during the mission's 4,982nd Martian day, or sol. Six separate photographs were transmitted to Earth and combined to create the sweeping view. Unlike most Mastcam photographs, the images were processed without white balancing, allowing the scene to retain the distinctive colors and lighting of the Martian morning.

Curiosity Approaches Mysterious Martian Cliffs

The yardangs stretch approximately 10 miles (16 kilometers) across the northwestern portion of Mount Sharp, a 3-mile-tall (5-kilometer-tall) mountain that Curiosity has been climbing since 2014. The rover recently achieved a major milestone, gaining 0.6 miles (1 kilometer) in elevation, a greater climb than any other rover has completed on Mars.

As Curiosity continues toward these unusual formations, researchers anticipate increasingly detailed views of the cliffs. More importantly, they hope that examining the rocks firsthand will help explain how the remarkable landscape developed.

What Mars' Ancient Rocks Reveal About Its Past

Mount Sharp contains numerous layers of sedimentary rock, each preserving evidence from a different chapter of Martian history. Investigating these layers has helped scientists reconstruct a time billions of years ago when lakes and streams existed in this region of Mars.

As the planet's environment changed, those bodies of water eventually disappeared, leaving deposits of salty minerals behind. Once additional material stopped accumulating on the mountain, powerful winds may have gradually eroded portions of the existing rock, carving out the distinctive yardangs visible today.

Yet the unusual appearance of this particular layer has raised questions about its origins. Its color differs from the surrounding terrain, and its rock layers are tilted in an unexpected direction. Scientists suspect that processes beyond ordinary wind erosion may have contributed to its formation.

"The yardang layer looks out of place. It's the wrong color, the layers tilt at an odd angle, and it almost appears plastered on," said Ashwin Vasavada, Curiosity project scientist of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California. "But that's what makes it exciting to reach. No one is sure exactly what created this layer, but one idea is it may be ash deposited by ancient volcanic eruptions."

NASA Plans to Investigate the Cliffs in 2027

Curiosity is now in the second year of its fifth extended mission, which began with an investigation of unusual spiderweb-like boxwork ridges. Over roughly the next year, the rover will continue traveling through rock layers containing abundant sulfates and carbonates, minerals that provide evidence of ancient drying conditions on Mars.

Mission scientists aim to reach the bottom of the yardang formations sometime in 2027. Once there, Curiosity should be able to examine the mysterious cliffs using its robotic arm, gathering unique measurements that could help determine how they formed and reveal more about the environmental changes that shaped Mars billions of years ago.

#217. 96% of world's coral reefs face critical risk of collapse, IUCN warns

Almost all of the world’s shallow-water coral reefs are at risk of collapse by 2075, with climate change the biggest threat. A new study found that 96% of reef systems are critically endangered.

Nearly all of the world's shallow-water coral reefs face an extremely high risk of collapse by 2075, with climate change being the greatest threat, the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) warned on Thursday.

In the first systematic survey of the world's 127 tropical and subtropical coral reef systems, the IUCN said 96% were critically endangered, which is its highest risk category.

Shallow-water reefs are colorful coral ecosystems found at depths of up to around 40 meters (131 feet), and are home to a high level of biodiversity.

Among the reefs affected are Australia's Great Barrier Reef, the Coral Triangle in the western Pacific and Southeast Asia, the Mesoamerican Reef System and the reefs in the Red Sea.

"Heat stress from climate change is the single most consistent threat driving coral reef decline worldwide," the IUCN said when it presented the study at the United Nations' COP17 biodiversity summit in Yerevan, Armenia.

Worldwide, shallow-water reefs cover around 188,000 square kilometers(73,000 square miles).

Their corals are highly sensitive to temperature changes. This means when the water gets too warm, the corals expel the tiny algae living in their tissues, which provide much of their nourishment.

This then leads to coral bleaching and can ultimately lead to their death.

Overfishing is another major threat. In particular, the overfishing of parrotfish and groupers affects reef ecosystems since these fish help maintain ecological balance.

Coastal development, pollution, tourism and increasingly severe storms also contribute to reef degradation.

When hard corals disappear, their structures are often replaced by algae, rubble and sponges, leaving less diverse ecosystems that do not sustain as many species. Affected animals include reef fish, mussels, sea turtles, and sharks.

"The future threat of climate change is looming and severe, and that poses a very high risk to the survival of these ecosystems," according to Mishal Gudka, scientific lead of the IUCN Red List of Coral Reef Ecosystems assessment.

The study uses a climate scenario developed by the UN's Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, which expects global warming of around 2.7 degrees Celsius (4.86 degrees Fahrenheit) above pre-industrial levels by 2100.

Despite the bleak outlook, the IUCN said most reefs had not yet been lost. Without projections of future climate change and based on the changes over the past 50 years, only 24% would qualify as critically endangered.

"This gives us a critical window for action for us to scale up conservation efforts and climate action to prevent the worst outcomes from occurring," Gudka explained.

"The actions we take over the next few years will determine what these ecosystems look like at the end of the century, whether they persist as we have known them, or whether they transform into a different ecosystem, which is worth less to both nature and people," he added.

IUCN Director General Grethel Aguilar said it was "not too late to give coral reefs a chance to recover."

"We know what is needed: ambitious emissions reductions, stronger protection and restoration efforts, and sustained investment in resilient coastal ecosystems and the communities that depend on them," she said.

#214. 40 years later, Chernobyl's nuclear fallout is not what scientists expected

Four decades after the Chernobyl (known today as Chornobyl) nuclear disaster, microscopic radioactive particles remain scattered throughout the surrounding soil. The explosions that devastated the reactor released enormous quantities of debris, leaving behind contamination that continues to pose serious health risks. Even after 40 years, particles measuring just 8 to 50 micrometers remain highly radioactive, and entering affected areas still requires protective clothing.

Scientists have now taken a closer look at these tiny remnants and uncovered a surprising finding: some have preserved much of their original internal structure despite decades of exposure to the environment.

"There are three classes of these particles," explains Tobias Weissenborn, a physicist and doctoral candidate at Leibniz University Hannover. "First, there are particles that are chemically and physically still very similar to the nuclear fuel uranium dioxide. Then, there are particles that are partially or fully encased in, or completely fused with, their zirconium layer."

The second category originated during the extreme temperatures reached inside the reactor. As the nuclear fuel melted, it combined with zirconium, a highly resistant metal used in the fuel's protective outer layer.

"The third type of particle was created when the graphite-moderator caught fire," Weissenborn adds.

Under normal operating conditions, the graphite moderator slows fast neutrons, helping sustain the nuclear chain reaction. During the disaster, however, the graphite ignited and continued burning for ten days.

"In the process, the fuel oxidized into various uranium oxides" - such as U3O8.

Unlike more stable materials, this uranium oxide is mechanically fragile and can readily break apart into microscopic fragments. Wind can then carry these radioactive particles through the environment, where inhaling them presents serious health hazards.

Scientists Examine Chornobyl's Radioactive Dust in Unprecedented Detail

Despite decades of research, scientists still do not fully understand why radioactive particles from Chornobyl break down at different speeds. As these materials weather, they can produce several forms of uranium oxide, making their long term behavior difficult to predict.

Investigating these changes requires exceptionally precise equipment, including synchrotron X-ray diffraction, a technique that reveals the arrangement of atoms inside crystalline materials.

Dr. Christoph Hennig, a crystallographer at the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf (HZDR), has now completed the first successful structural analysis of radioactive fragments of this kind at the Rossendorf Beamline in Grenoble.

Working with Weissenborn, Hennig analyzed six particles recovered from the Chornobyl region. By identifying the different crystalline phases within them, the researchers gained information that could help determine how quickly radioactive substances escape into the surrounding environment.

"The colleagues from Hanover and I used various methods to isolate the particles from Ukrainian soil samples and attach them to tungsten electrodes," Weissenborn explains.

Because the samples were highly radioactive, they had to be enclosed within several layers of containment before being transported to Grenoble. There, Hennig's research team used X-ray diffraction to examine their internal structures by measuring how X-rays scattered as they passed through the material.

"First, we had to figure out how to examine the particle structure," Christoph Hennig explains. "Ultimately, we fully rotated the particles in an X-ray beam focused down to 100 micrometers - about the thickness of a human hair. We measured each particle from 2,000 different angles to seamlessly capture all the reflections."

The measurements allowed the scientists to distinguish different uranium oxide phases within the radioactive fragments, revealing details that had previously been inaccessible.

A Surprising Discovery About 40-Year-Old Nuclear Fallout

The results revealed something unexpected. Despite spending four decades in the environment, the nuclear fuel inside the six examined particles had retained much of its original crystal structure.

This suggests that at least some of the radioactive debris produced by the Chornobyl disaster is more chemically stable than researchers previously believed.

That stability could have important environmental implications. Rather than rapidly releasing radioactive substances, the particles appear capable of retaining fission products within their structures. If this behavior occurs more widely, it could help limit the movement of radioactive contaminants into nearby soil and water.

However, the researchers emphasize that their findings should not be interpreted as evidence that Chornobyl's radioactive contamination is becoming harmless.

"However, every single particle has a different structure," warns Weissenborn, "and our experiment only studied six such particles from two different locations."

The relatively small sample means the findings cannot yet establish how radioactive particles behave throughout the entire contaminated region. To understand their stability more comprehensively, scientists would need to investigate many additional particles collected from a much broader range of locations.

Why Chornobyl's Radioactive Legacy Remains Unpredictable

Even with substantially more data, predicting the long term dangers of Chornobyl's radioactive debris would remain challenging. Individual particles can differ in composition, structure, and resistance to environmental weathering, potentially affecting when they release radioactive materials.

"And even if we obtained some averages at some point, we still wouldn't be able to make universal statements about health risks in the region," Weissenborn cautions. "Because even if the particles decay in a largely uniform patten, there will always be outliers, more persistent particles, that will release radionuclides at a later point in time."

These persistent particles could continue releasing radioactive substances long after other remnants have broken down, complicating efforts to assess future contamination and health risks.

For that reason, the new findings do not suggest that restrictions on the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone can be removed anytime soon.

Research into the disaster's enduring consequences is continuing. Weissenborn and Hennig are already conducting additional experiments focused on highly radioactive transuranic phases found in the remaining debris. Their work could provide a clearer picture of how Chornobyl's nuclear materials change over time and how long their environmental effects may persist.

#202. Nearly 3,000 exploding stars challenge our understanding of the Universe

The largest collection of exploding white dwarf stars ever compiled is providing fresh evidence that dark energy, the mysterious force responsible for the Universe’s accelerating expansion, may change over time. The findings challenge a central assumption of modern cosmology and could help scientists better understand how the Universe evolves.

An international research team led by scientists at the University of Queensland’s School of Mathematics and Physics brought together observations of 2,884 Type Ia supernovae. These powerful stellar explosions serve as important tools for measuring distances across the Universe, allowing astronomers to trace its expansion history.

PhD candidate Ryan Camilleri said the project establishes a new international standard for supernova cosmology. By bringing decades of measurements into a unified dataset, researchers have created their most detailed picture yet of how cosmic expansion has changed over time.

“We’ve rebuilt 3 decades of astronomical observations into a single, consistent framework,” Mr. Camilleri said.

“We combined our data with other cosmic measurements including relic light from the Big Bang and maps of how galaxies are distributed through space.

“Instead of confirming the standard model of cosmology which assumes dark energy is fixed and unchanging, we have more evidence that dark energy may change over time.”

The new collection brings together historical supernova measurements and findings from the Dark Energy Survey (DES) published in 2024.

To make observations collected over different periods directly comparable, the scientists revisited older data using updated analytical methods. Advances in understanding supernova behavior allowed the team to improve the consistency and accuracy of measurements gathered over decades.

“Over the years we’ve learned a lot more about how supernovae behave so we’ve been able to go back and apply that improved understanding to older data,” Mr. Camilleri said.

The researchers also had to account for differences among the telescopes used to collect the observations. Other factors, including cosmic dust and the masses of galaxies, can influence the light astronomers receive from distant supernovae and therefore affect distance measurements.

“Extensive work has been done to link observations from different telescopes with different capacities and take into account issues such as cosmic dust and galaxy mass which can affect the light coming from a supernova.

“We also incorporated more subtle effects such as gravitational lensing, which is the bending and magnification of light around large objects as it travels from a supernova to Earth.”

Astrophysicist Professor Tamara Davis said the expanded dataset represents an important advance in efforts to understand the nature of dark energy.

Under the standard model of cosmology, dark energy is treated as a constant property of the Universe. However, recent observations have raised questions about whether that assumption fully explains how cosmic expansion has evolved.

“Our supernova data from DES in 2024 first showed hints that dark energy may be time varying, and this new compilation also sees a deviation from the standard model although in a slightly different direction,” Professor Davis said.

The findings are especially interesting because a separate astronomical project has also produced evidence suggesting that dark energy might not remain constant.

“Similarly results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) found hints of variations in dark energy in its surveys of relic sound waves from the early Universe.

“So, 2 completely independent measurements have found hints of time variation in dark energy, challenging the standard model that dark energy doesn't change.

Although the different measurements do not point to exactly the same departure from the standard model, they provide additional reasons to investigate whether dark energy changes as the Universe ages.

Understanding dark energy could have implications extending far beyond the expansion of the Universe. Researchers believe that learning more about its behavior might also provide insights into one of the greatest unresolved problems in theoretical physics: reconciling gravity with quantum physics.

“All of this research may also hold the clue to explain how gravity and quantum physics fit together.

“We know these 2 theories are each immensely successful in their own realms, so if we can figure out how to put them together that would be a huge step in theoretical physics.”

The researchers plan to expand the dataset as additional observations become available. One important source will be the Dark Energy Bedrock All-Sky Supernova program (DEBASS), which is identifying hundreds of supernovae at distances closer to Earth than those surveyed by DES.

Incorporating these nearby explosions will provide further measurements that scientists can use to investigate the Universe’s expansion and test whether dark energy truly changes over time.
