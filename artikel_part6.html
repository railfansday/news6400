#6150. David Bowie at Home: 10 Photos of the Rock Legend Off-Duty

He wrote songs about space exploration, but David Bowie was a master of time travel, bringing the future of music, style, and culture to an astonished public. He was a postwar baby; born David Robert Jones on January 8, 1947, the young Bowie grew up in London’s Bromley borough playing in the ruins of bombed-out buildings. As a budding musician, he performed in a series of 1950s rock-and-roll bands before going solo as David Bowie, naming himself for what he told Rolling Stone was the ultimate American knife.

Not just a rock star but an entire galaxy unto himself, Bowie became a singular force in glam rock, electronic and ambient sounds, dance pop, art rock, and gender-bending fashion. By the time of his death from liver cancer in 2016, he had become a rock icon, worked as an actor, married twice (the second time to supermodel Iman), and raised two children. And he did it all from crumbling British Victorians, chic New York apartments, and an island getaway in Mustique.

Read on to see the artist at home in the 1960s and ’70s, with his distinctive gaze always set on the future.

Young Bowie with sheet music in a promotional shot.

When this photo was taken in 1966, Bowie was still living at his parents’ home in Bromley, London. The Heritage of London Trust’s Proud Places program aims to turn the icon’s childhood residence into a museum called Bowie’s House in 2027, preserving the site where, as their website says, “before there was a global superstar, there was a bedroom where a teenager listened to Little Richard records and felt the first shock of possibility.” The rising star would move into manager Ken Pitt’s apartment in 1967.

According to The Pitt Report, Pitt’s book about Bowie, the young rocker moved into his flat during the summer of 1967 because his parents’ home had become too crowded. “On Sunday, June 11, Mr. Jones drove David and his belongings up from Bromley,” Pitt wrote. “After his father had gone, David said, ‘Dad liked the flat. He said it’s very masculine.’” Between 1967 and 1968, Bowie would stay at Pitt’s top-floor residence in a Georgian terraced house, which was often used for photo shoots with musicians.

It was Pitt who suggested that the aspiring musician change his name from David Jones to avoid confusion with Davy Jones, who rose to fame playing the Artful Dodger in the musical Oliver! on the London stage before bringing the role to Broadway and eventually joining the Monkees.

It wouldn’t be until 1972 that Bowie would release The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars, introducing his alter ego, an alien rock star visiting Earth. In this 1966 photo of the musician and his instrument, with glam rock still in the future, one of its superstars is still firmly surrounded by mod, mid-1960s fixtures including his skinny tie and massive ashtray.

Bowie’s penetrating gaze is on display in this 1970 photo.

In late 1969, Bowie and then girlfriend Angie Barnett moved into the ground floor of Haddon Hall, a historic home on South London’s 42 Southend Road. The Beckenham building was made of Victorian brick and boasted a handful of architectural flourishes, including a prominent stained-glass window. By February 1970, when this photo was taken, Bowie had suffered both a devastating personal loss (the death of his father in August 1969) and sudden professional success: After seeing the Stanley Kubrick movie 2001: A Space Odyssey, he wrote the song “Space Oddity” about an astronaut lost in the cosmos.

Bowie was photographed painting the molding of his Haddon Hall flat silver in this 1972 photo.

“The building was the epitome of crumbling magnificence,” biographer Paul Trynka wrote of Haddon Hall in Starman: David Bowie. Trynka described a staircase “dominated by a magnificent Gothic stained-glass window.” As royalties from the “Space Oddity” single started rolling in, Bowie and Barnett “became familiar figures at the antiques shops on Old Kent and Tower Bridge Roads, acquiring Art Deco lamps, William Morris screens, and mahogany chests of drawers,” per Trynka.

David Bowie at his creative hotbed of a home, Haddon Hall, in Beckenham, Kent, in 1971

Bowie wore this ensemble during a photo shoot at Haddon Hall for the cover of his 1970 album The Man Who Sold the World. The UK album cover shows him reclining on a blue chaise, but in the United States, Mercury Records refused to release an album featuring a man in a dress on the cover, and went with a photo of a cowboy instead. When asked by Melody Maker magazine journalist Michael Watts why he wasn’t “wearing [his] girl’s dress today” during a 1972 interview, Bowie replied, “Oh dear. You must understand that it’s not a woman’s. It’s a man’s dress.”

Angie and David Bowie relax with a friend at Haddon Hall in 1971.

Bowie and Barnett married in March 1970. They’re shown here in 1971 with a friend, unwinding in their Haddon Hall flat. The pair brought bold style to the neglected Victorian, painting the sitting room silver, the living room green, and the bedroom pink, and installing a recording studio in the basement. On May 30, 1971, their son, Duncan Zowie Haywood Jones was born.

In The Man Who Sold the World, biographer Peter Doggett described Haddon Hall as “a bohemian enclave shared with Angie, producer Tony Visconti, and assorted musicians, roadies, and associates.” The rock star’s half-brother Terry also called the space home on occasion. In her own memoir, Backstage Passes: Life on the Wild Side with David Bowie, Angie wrote, “As you might expect, Haddon Hall is a thoroughly Victorian edifice: solid red brick, ornately embellished with solemn white fasciae, and, of course righteously, haughtily church-like in basic aspect, so much so that the dominant feature of the rear face is a huge stained-glass window. The front is almost as imposing. The door opens, and the first thing you see is that magnificent stained-glass window rising above a short staircase at the far end of a central hallway fully forty feet wide by sixty feet long.”

Bowie spent much of 1972 and 1973 touring in the US and Japan. When his album Pin Ups hit number one in the UK in late 1973, his fame skyrocketed, with admirers camping out in front of Haddon Hall. After one managed to break into the home, Bowie and his family moved to escape the crowds, first to an apartment in Maida Vale and later to a house on Oakley Street in Chelsea.

The decaying Haddon Hall was demolished in the 1980s and replaced with an apartment block.

Bowie in London’s Dorchester Hotel in 1977

Bowie moved to LA in April 1975, pursuing an acting career while continuing to record, then relocated to Berlin in 1976. He was still living in Germany in 1977 when he returned to England to promote his new album, Heroes. The musician is shown here in his home away from home, the Dorchester Hotel, where, at age 30, he reflected on finding his place in the world. “Towards the end of my stay in America, I realized that what I had to do was to experiment. To discover new forms of writing. To evolve, in fact, a new musical language. That’s what I set out to do,” he told Allan Jones during that interview. “That’s why I returned to Europe.”

Over the next 39 years, he acquired new homes in New York City (where he lived with his second wife, Iman, and their daughter Alexandria) and in the private island Mustique, where he flexed his interior-design muscles.

#6148. 7 Renovations Designers Say Homeowners Regret the Most

These changes may look pretty, but they don’t live well.

Sometimes, a design trend that looks stunning on your Pinterest board doesn’t translate as seamlessly into everyday life. It’s easy to fall for a beautiful image online, but real homes have real-world needs, and those two elements don’t always meld. Take oversized shower “rooms,” for example. They may look sleek and spa-like, yet they can leave you shivering in a drafty bathroom. Or consider a mirrored tile backsplash that sparkles in photos but quickly reveals every fingerprint, splash, and smudge once it’s installed.

Because of this disconnect between inspiration and reality, renovation regrets than you might think. Design and construction professionals have seen it all, from impractical layouts to beautiful but high-maintenance finishes that homeowners grow tired of almost immediately. To better understand which ideas cause the most frustration, we asked contractors and interior designers to share the design missteps they’re often called in to correct. Ahead, they break down common pitfalls, plus what they recommend instead.

Marble countertops are classic, but the soft, porous stone is not always practical for every home. Laura Hammett, founder and creative director of Laura Hammett Interiors, often finds herself swapping out the material in heavily used kitchens, and instead recommends a sintered stone, like Neolith, instead.

“Clients love the look of marble but then find the reality is you can’t spill a drop of food or drink before it gets stained,” she says. “For clients who love to cook, especially with colorful spices, we recommend stones that still look beautiful, but are much more durable and hardier."

With the rise in multi-generational households and more people working from home, Bethany Adams of Bethany Adams Interiors says her clients no longer like open-plan living and crave a little more alone time. She first noticed this trend during the pandemic when people spent a lot of time together. “To accommodate these requests, I've added hidden pocket doors behind new built-ins and sleek glass-and-steel-framed partitions and doors to section off more open spaces,” she says.

Adams has found plenty of ways to create separation without building full walls. Glass doors and partitions strike the right balance between the privacy that clients want and the openness that they may have originally desired, she says. Cased openings can also be a good option, as they allow for design delineation without eliminating sightlines.

You may imagine yourself lounging outdoors with a book or enjoying al fresco meals and dips in a pool, but homeowners often change their minds about expansive loggias and open-air lounging spaces, Robert W. Burrage, founder of Florida-based RWB Concierge Builder, says.

Factors like humidity, heat, and seasonal storms can make it tough to enjoy those outdoor living spaces to their fullest potential. “As a result, we often find ourselves enclosing these outdoor spaces to create comfortable, usable areas that can be enjoyed year-round,” he says.

Tile selection has been an issue on several projects that Alexis Woodbury Earman, an interior designer with The Woodbury Home, has worked on. “Many homeowners choose tiles based on inspiration photos and current trends,” she says. “Though these tile choices can be beautiful in pictures, the owner can grow tired of the trend and want to replace it with something more classic.”

She remedies these types of designs by choosing neutral-colored tiles and adding color through paint. That way, when they want to change the color in the space, they can do so much more easily.

When the farmhouse trend exploded around 2015, everyone asked their contractor for barn doors and faux shiplap walls, even if the features didn't suit the home's architecture or their family's needs, says Lesley Myrick, an Atlanta-based interior designer. She then, she has removed several barn doors from clients’ homes, noting that they’re a terrible choice because they let sound and light through. She has also removed plenty of shiplap walls, which can collect dust between the horizontal boards.

“Many people saw the farmhouse trend as both current and timeless and rushed to ‘upgrade’ their homes with these design elements,” she says. But when these additions aren’t functional for your home or lifestyle, it's a design disaster. Some of her projects have included removing barn doors and tracks and installing single-paneled doors, and in at least one case, removing a floating shiplap wall, which made room for a kitchen reconfiguration with a large kitchen island.

Kitchen cabinets or pantry doors with clear glass doors can be beautiful, but they come with a few practical challenges that make them hard to live with, says designer Ashley Macuga of Collected Interiors.

“Since glass doors reveal what's inside, any clutter or mismatched items are easily visible, which can make even a new kitchen look messy if not perfectly maintained,” she says. A quick fix is to update the clear glass to reeded glass, which obscures the visual, while still keeping the elegance of the material,” she says.

Doorless showers may look great, but homeowners who had them installed often find them more trouble than they’re worth, says interior designer Shiva Samiei. Water tends to splash outside of the shower area, causing moisture damage and slip-and-fall hazards. They can also be decidedly un-cozy, leaving you feeling exposed—not a pleasant sensation when you're bathing.

Custom glass panels and new drainage solutions usually strike a middle ground, helping bathrooms preserve a modern look while containing water.

#6146. The Biggest Trends of Fall 2026 Are All About Subverting Expectations

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

This season, we’re wearing satin to the office and plaid to cocktail parties

The best trends from the fall 2026 shows were all about disrupting expectations. Knitwear became an evening layer; tuxedos took a spin in the sunlight. From Miuccia Prada’s satin dresses worn with scarves to Chloé’s chiffon skirts in folksy plaid, so-wrong-it’s-right statement dressing ruled the runways.

Even the more subtle styling tricks and silhouettes felt a little bit tweaked. Michael Rider at Celine and Cate Holstein of Khaite made the case for pulling your outfit together with pops of red. Simone Rocha, Tory Burch, and Chanel’s Matthieu Blazy dropped waistlines on dresses lower than we’ve seen before, giving us the shape of the season. And Julian Klausner revived Dries Van Noten’s usual waist theory with statement cummerbunds, a hero accessory of fall.

Still, whether you gravitate towards the classics or take an experimental approach, the fall 2026 collections had something for everyone. Below, I break down seven of the key trends of the season.

The waist has been a focal point for multiple seasons now. Back in the spring, it was all about tying a boho scarf around your middle. Now, designers are taking inspiration from menswear. There were cummerbunds all over the runway, and while some felt quite traditional, others came in unexpected colorways or were adorned with surprising embellishments.

For an easy twist on the look, try securing a silky opera scarf around your waist to add interest to an outfit of black trousers and a blue button-down. Or, if you want the effect built in, try Róhe’s double-waist pants (personally, I can’t stop wearing them). These small details can make your typical office uniform feel that much more thoughtful and chic.

This season, plaids were not giving your typical pumpkin patch energy. Plaid was chic. Dressed up. Sophisticated. The colors felt brighter (as opposed to dark hues for the fall) and the silhouettes, such as dresses, skirts, going-out tops, and evening bags, felt elevated. Not to mention the textures: chiffons and silks made the usually down-home pattern feel luxurious. Whether you wear a plaid skirt to the office with knee-high boots and a neutral blazer or choose a plaid dress for a cocktail party, this trend can breathe new life into your wardrobe.

This season, the runways embraced ladylike pieces styled in a way that reflects the messiness of everyday life. Sweaters and trench coats were thrown over arms, gloves were clutched in hands, and Prada even gave us a cuff stained with coffee. The formula here is quite effortless—that’s the point—but investing in a top-handle couchette bag is always a good idea. Ditto a pair of leather gloves. Look for silhouettes with imperfect cutouts, like Jil Sander’s pencil skirt with a sliced waist, and try replacing your sweater top with a more ladylike shell top. It’s the contrast that makes it all work.

Red became a new neutral this season, taking every look to the next level. Brooches, belts, and jewelry appeared in crimson, while basics like sweaters and denim took on new life in scarlet. A great starting point is in the accessories. Whether you add a brooch to your favorite black jacket for the office, or finish off a pair of raw jeans with a white T-shirt and red belt, it’s the easiest way to make your entire wardrobe feel refreshed.

Sweaters are a fall perennial, but this season, designers made them feel exciting by tapping into less-traditional categories (like Dries Van Noten’s Fair Isle sweater set, or Chloé’s matching balaclava and sweater) and opposites-attract styling. From satin dresses worn with sweaters and chunky knit scarves to a floral-embroidered sweater with dressed-up bottoms, there were plenty of ways to wear sweaters for evening.

One of the coolest tailoring trends this season was the way designers interpreted tuxedo dressing for daywear. Whether you replace your oversized shirting with a tuxedo button-down or trade your blazer for a tuxedo jacket to wear with jeans, the look borrows from the boys in a distinctly feminine way. The cummerbund trend fits into this category too, offering a refined way to break up a silky dress or pull together denim and a jacket.

Looking for a dress silhouette with high style? The key is to go low. Drop-waist dresses were everywhere, with the shape giving otherwise polished or uptight pieces an edgy twist. Some of the waistlines came with belt loops, which really accentuate the drama of the drop. For others, a straightforward approach did the job on its own. Either way, a classic pump heel (pointed- or almond-toe) streamlines the look, balancing the proportions of the dress for an elegant finish.

#6145. Convergence of AI, Quantum Risks Is Impacting Cybersecurity

Recent research from Thales highlights how AI adoption and emerging quantum computing threats are putting pressure on existing security measures. The 2026 Thales Data Threat Report: Quantum & AI Trends, based on responses from 3,120 security and IT management professionals across 20 countries, found that 98% are considering how the two technologies affect each other. Thirty percent reported a significant increase in security budgets specifically for AI.

The research, conducted by 451 Research by S&P Global, examines both the risks and potential benefits of the technologies. Fifty-seven percent believe quantum computing will enhance machine learning, while 54% expect it to enable advanced simulations of complex systems. The security findings span data governance, cloud infrastructure, and preparations for quantum-resistant encryption.

Cloud-based storage, cloud-delivered applications, and cloud management infrastructure were the three most frequently cited asset types targeted by attackers, at 35%, 34%, and 32%, respectively. The report connects that exposure to the large datasets required for AI training and the rapid expansion of AI infrastructure.

Organizations are also looking to cloud providers for protection: 67% reported investing in their cloud provider's AI-specific security tools, compared with 63% using an established security provider. Respondents could identify multiple sources of protection, including startups, large language model providers, and internally developed controls.

Encryption coverage remains incomplete. Only 7% of organizations reported encrypting more than 80% of their sensitive cloud data, while 29% reported encrypting more than 60%. The report's chart puts the average share encrypted at 47% in 2026, down from 51% in 2025.

The findings extend concerns raised in coverage of Thales' 2025 cloud security research, when AI security already ranked second to cloud security in spending priorities and 52% of respondents reported that AI security spending was displacing existing security budgets. The latest report again places AI security second, while its historical comparison shows average sensitive cloud data encryption coverage falling from 51% in 2025 to 47% in 2026.

AI security was the second-highest prioritized security spending category overall, behind cloud security. But protecting data ranked much lower among the measures organizations use to judge AI projects: Reducing or managing risks of data loss or noncompliance placed sixth among seven success criteria, at 35%.

Improving customer experience led those measures at 68%, followed by reducing employee toil through greater task automation at 63%. Meanwhile, poor data quality or initial data governance was the leading inhibitor to AI adoption, cited by 65%; security risks from exposed data followed at 61%. Another 51% cited AI initiatives moving too quickly to be properly secured.

#6144. All About EJAE, Audrey Nuna, and Rei Ami, the Singing Voices of KPop Demon Hunters

KPop Demon Hunters is Netflix’s most-watched film ever, and its big single “Golden” has gone platinum. The animated phenomenon won two Oscars, a Grammy, two Critics Choice Awards, and two Golden Globes, with its song “Golden” winning the ceremonies’ Best Original Song category. With the movie’s massive success, the three singing voices of its characters, HUNTR/X members Rumi, Mira, and Zoey, have stepped into the spotlight. They even performed “Golden” at the 2026 Academy Awards.

At the Critics Choice Awards on January 4, EJAE (the voice of Rumi) reflected on her journey, saying, “When writing the song, I found myself living vicariously through my character, Rumi. Her life and journey in the movie felt deeply familiar to me.” She added, “The song needed to be an expression of hope to convince herself that she could rise and reach for her dreams. In many ways, it did the same for me. But most importantly, for it to be giving hope to so many people around the world is truly the greatest honor.”

The musical stars have been lighting up the red carpet this award season, including the Grammys in February. Nuna and Ami wore white looks by Thom Browne and Guvanch respectively, and EJAE was in a purple gown by Dior.

EJAE and her co-stars Audrey Nuna (Mira) and Rei Ami (Zoey) had their own individual careers in music before their work for the Netflix film took off, but now they have fans wishing they were a proper girl band themselves. Get to know each of the stars below.

EJAE, born Kim Eun-jae, is a 34-year-old Korean-American singer and songwriter based in New York City. She didn’t just sing Rumi’s parts for Kpop Demon Hunters; she also co-wrote “Golden,” “How It’s Done,” and “Your Idol.”

#6142. Pest Experts Reveal How to Get Rid of Stink Bugs and Prevent an Infestation

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

One of the easiest ways to deal with a bug in your house is by, well, squashing it. It’s not a pleasant experience for anyone, but it gets the job done. With a stink bug, however, doing this will only cause a bigger problem as the putrid odor it’s named for releases when it’s crushed. And if you have an infestation on your hands, you’ll need to resort to alternative methods. These insects thankfully aren’t harmful to people, pets, or your property, but they’re definitely a nuisance, especially if you’ve been seeing them around your house or yard.

To find out how to get rid of stink bugs and prevent future infestations, we reached out to Dr. Chad Gore, an entomologist and the market technical director at Terminix, for his expert insight. He tells House Beautiful that it’s sometimes difficult to stop a stink bug infestation, so it’s “important to hire a professional to help you determine your risk, identify opportunities for sealing the building and excluding stink bugs, and treating the building with insecticides at the right time of year to reduce the likelihood they'll bother you in the winter and early spring.”

Keep reading to learn more about these bugs and how to get rid of them.

There are many types of stink bugs in the United States, but the kind that usually comes inside your house is the brown marmorated stink bug. It's an invasive species that originated in East Asia and was accidentally introduced in Pennsylvania in the late 1990s, according to Penn State Extension. Since then, the shield-shaped, winged bugs have spread across the country. They can't do structural damage to your home, but they can damage your plants and potentially trigger your allergies.

Stink bugs are attracted by warmth, food, light, and moisture. Like pretty much any living organism, stink bugs are simply looking for what they need to survive. You'll likely notice stink bugs in your house when the weather starts to cool in the fall and early winter. They'll look for any opening to get in.

You may feel compelled to, but don't step on or crush stink bugs the way you might other creepy-crawlies. Remember, getting smashed is what releases their revolting stench. You can trap a stink bug in a cup (just put it on top and slide a piece of paper underneath) and release it outside, or sweep or vacuum them up. If you use a vacuum cleaner, just make sure to replace the vacuum bag and throw the used one out or empty the dust bin right away.

“One of the biggest mistakes made when dealing with stink bugs is waiting until they're already inside to try and do anything about them. By the time they're inside, it's too late,” Gore explains.

Though it’s hard to pinpoint exactly how stink bugs are entering your abode, there are some common things you can do to prevent them from coming in.

It's probably difficult to figure out where exactly the bugs are coming in, but that's exactly why you should seal off any and all potential entry points. It could be a screen window or door, a crack in the countertop, or really any hole or niche. Stink bugs can squeeze into very small spaces. According to Orkin, they most often sneak in through “cracks, crevices, gaps, and holes in foundations, window and door frames, soffits, attics, and underneath siding.”

Reinforcing these areas with clear silicone is a must. Pay extra attention to entry areas near fruit trees, gardens, or other agricultural hot spots since they tend to attract stink bugs.

Sometimes, the only way to truly prevent insects is with a chemical deterrent. Gore recommends consulting a professional to determine the most at-risk areas of your home—usually windows, exterior doors, crawl spaces, and attics—and line those spaces with an insecticide at the end of the summer to try to avoid the bugs coming inside to escape the dropping temperatures.

“Spraying insecticides might kill some as they emerge from attics, around fixtures, or from wall voids (assuming you've been lucky enough to guess accurately where they crawl out of and they walk across the material),” Gore says. If you really want to get rid of stink bugs, though, you need to treat the exterior of your home in the fall.

“The best approach is to target them in the fall on the outside of a building. This means applying residual insecticides to the exterior of a building, to the cracks and crevices they may try to enter (gaps around windows and doors, under fascia, gaps in soffit, attic vents, chimneys, around utility penetrations, etc.).”

If you've just been using chip clips, you might want to reassess and switch to airtight food storage containers. Food attracts stink bugs just like other pests. The goal is to make it harder for stink bugs to survive and thrive once they're in your home, so eliminating their access to food will make it less appealing to them. Trust us, you don't want to find a stink bug in your brown rice or tortilla chips.

Super-humid places with water buildup or poor ventilation are breeding grounds for stink bugs. Moisture attracts stink bugs, too. So dry it up, whether that means investing in a great dehumidifier or fixing a leaky faucet. You can also temporarily tape over any emergency drainage points if you think the bugs are coming in through the drain.

Like most insects, stink bugs are drawn to lights. If you keep your porch light (or any light for that matter, once they make their way inside) on after dark, they'll flock to it. The simple answer? Turn off the lights or put them on a motion-detection timer so they turn on only when someone walks by—that's more energy efficient and cost-effective anyway.

Like any creature, stink bugs can't survive the winter cold, so they search for a warmer place to wait the season out.

“The arrival of cool weather triggers them to search for someplace to spend the winter,” Gore explains. “In the spring, stink bugs that passed through the winter successfully and are able to leave their overwintering habitat will be attracted to plants of many varieties (host plants) in order to lay their eggs. This gives the newly emerged (hatched) insects a head start by starting life out on a plant they can feed from.”

Infestation might be the wrong word. Stink bugs aren't like ants or bedbugs that can cause damage; they're mostly just a nuisance.

“If you do find that you have a lot of stink bugs outside, then it's important to hire a professional to help you determine how best to treat and where on your home/building needs to be sealed up,” Gore says. “The best prevention is to exclude them and make it difficult for them to get inside. If you've missed the opportunity to seal them out and find yourself with a lot of stink bugs inside, then it's best to vacuum them up and discard the vacuum contents.”

#6139. 8 Secrets of ‘Gilmore Girls’ Sets You Need to Know Ahead Of Your Annual Rewatch

We hate to break it to you, but Stars Hollow, Connecticut, is not a real place. Yet the endearing small town depicted in Gilmore Girls was so flush with quaint details—falling leaves, quirky gatherings, and sweet little nooks such as Taylor’s Olde Fashioned Soda Shoppe, Weston’s Bakery, and Stars Hollow Books—that it’s easy to imagine Lorelai and Rory Gilmore (played by Lauren Graham and Alexis Bledel) gabbing over coffee at the retro-style Luke’s Diner at this very moment.

“The show was never about something being slick and glossy and trendy,” Lauren Crasco, the show’s production designer for 42 episodes, tells AD. “We were always very nostalgic and aimed for an existing vintage feel. The few times we had to do something contemporary, it was a little jarring.”

That enveloping sense of coziness is one reason why Gilmore Girls—which originally aired from 2000 to 2007—continues to feel like a communal dreamscape, with scores of fans making rewatching it as much of an autumn tradition as Halloween costumes and pumpkin spice lattes. Even Crasco was sucked through the screen when binging early episodes in preparation to start her job on the production in 2001: “I thought I lived in Stars Hollow!”

’Tis the season to bask in the warm-and-fuzzy show, so AD caught up with the production designer to go behind the scenes of our favorite small town. Below, she shares some secrets fans may not know.

Alexis Bledel as Rory Gilmore, with a painting of her grandfather Richard Gilmore (played by Edward Herrmann) in the 2016 miniseries Gilmore Girls: A Year in the Life.

The palatial home of Emily and Richard Gilmore (played by Kelly Bishop and Edward Herrmann) was supposed to convey blue-blood wealth. “The decorative items in the parents’ house were more subdued because we wanted to skew it very New England,” Crasco says. “We would only change things surreptitiously over a few seasons.”

The set itself required a few adjustments, as the interiors proved to be too cramped for the six-foot-five Herrmann. “He’s such a tall man, and the [old] set was always so small,” creator and executive producer Amy Sherman-Palladino has said. “He would walk through an archway and it would look like a dollhouse.” However, the production team gradually enlarged it as the budget allowed. “Every [season] we kept making the set a little bigger and the ceilings a little higher,” she added.

The scale issue was finally resolved for the 2016 sequel series Gilmore Girls: A Year in the Life, when the interiors received a bona fide upgrade. Alas, Herrmann, who died in 2014, wasn’t around to appreciate it. “It was [finally] big enough for Ed,” Sherman-Palladino said. “And he’s [not here].”

Scott Patterson as Luke and Lauren Graham as Lorelai in season one of Gilmore Girls.

The warm heart of Stars Hollow was actually anything but. Because Luke’s Diner was essentially a wooden frame without insulation—and the cast and crew constantly shuffled in and out the door—the December and January shoots on the backlot could be brutal. Yes, even in Southern California. “I do remember it would get really cold,” Crasco says. “We had to find these little pockets between the sets and behind the building façade to at least be out of the wind for a few minutes.”

She even has a chilling anecdote to prove it. One day inside the diner set, she and the crew bundled up beside a propane heater with cups of coffee in hand. Suddenly, she took a whiff and became alarmed: “I looked at one of the set dressers and I said, ‘Do you smell anything burning?’ He literally looked over and said, ‘Yep, your coat’s on fire’ and took another sip of his coffee. Keiko Agena as Lane, with Bledel in an early episode of Gilmore Girls.

One thing that gave Stars Hollow that authentic feel? All the storefronts had a personalized touch. The credit goes to graphic designer Casey Kasemeier, who painted awnings and signs for both the Stars Hollow stalwarts and new businesses. (Welcome to the neighborhood, Bessie’s Butcher!) “If we needed anything, whether the music shop or tailor’s or soda shop or whatever,” Crasco notes, “we just gave it to Casey to do it in-house. We never sent it out to a sign shop.”

Kasemeier even created signage for the planned (but ultimately unrealized) Gilmore Girls 2003 spinoff, Windward Circle. When that production went to Venice, California, to transform the iconic boardwalk eatery Hot Dog on a Stick into a fictional spot called Dante’s Inferno, Crasco notes that he and the painters came up with “this grinning devil hot dog!” The series, by the way, was intended to center on Milo Ventimiglia’s Jess character.

Production splurged on a set for the season two episode “The Road Trip To Harvard.”

During their onstage reunion at the 2025 Emmy awards (to mark the show’s 25th anniversary), stars Graham and Bledel joked that the show had a small budget compared to its higher-profile neighbors like The Drew Carey Show and ER on the Warner Bros. backlot in Burbank, California. “We saved up all year long to have one snow episode,” quipped Bledel.

“It definitely was the smallest art department I’ve ever had,” Crasco says, noting that the shooting schedule—a whopping 10 pages of that rapid-fire dialogue was filmed each day—made the job extra challenging.

Still, Sherman-Palladino “really cared” about certain details, and the money was stretched. The exterior of Lorelai’s house as seen in the 2016 miniseries Gilmore Girls: A Year in the Life.

Lauren Graham with Kelly Bishop as Emily Gilmore–supporting Yale—in a season four episode.

#6138. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6108. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

What Thread Count Is Best for Soft Sheets?

Thread count is the number of threads per square inch of fabric. So, the higher the thread count, usually the softer the sheets—though the average cotton sheet is between 200 and 600. For example, “Sateen sheets in the 300 to 600 thread-count range are best for someone looking for softness over durability, and better for cooler climates where a heavier feeling sheet is more comfortable,” Katie Glusica, professor in the fibers department at the Savannah College of Art and Design, previously told AD.

What Is the Difference Between a Flat sheet, a Fitted Sheet, and a Top Sheet?

Most sheet sets come with a flat sheet, a fitted sheet, and two pillowcases. When you’re layering your bedding, secure the fitted sheet—a sheet with elasticized, rounded edges—over your mattress or mattress pad. Then, top it with the flat sheet, a.k.a. the top sheet. This might be enough for people who run hot, but most finish their bed with a comforter or quilt.

For soft sheets with an airier feel, consider a set of 200 to 300 thread count sheets, like the Quince 100% percale cotton sheets or bamboo bedding from Cozy Earth or Bearaby. #6105. My Husband’s Transition

For most of our relationship, nobody paid us a second look. Now, a few years into August’s new life, holding hands is a political statement. I wouldn’t have it any other way.

A few weeks ago, after we got home from work and the Atlanta weather had cooled off, my husband, August, and I were out for a walk, as couples have done since time immemorial. As each of us absentmindedly reached out for the other’s hand, we shared a subtle nod with two well-dressed men who passed us on the opposite side of the path. I thought it was nothing more than an unspoken, gentlemanly, “How d’you do?” But August interpreted it as something else.

“It’s weird that for the first 15 years of our relationship, nobody paid any attention to whether or not we were holding hands,” August said. “Now it’s a political statement.”

What had changed? Three or four years ago—it’s hard to pin down the exact date—August wasn’t my husband. They were my wife.

August and I first met in Asheville, North Carolina, in 2006, when they were a 22-year-old, female-presenting barista. I was a few years older, working as a dishwasher. One of my earliest memories of August, before I even knew their name, occurred at the coffee shop we both frequented. I had a mohawk and mutton chops at the time and wore a heavy, utilitarian kilt. August was petite with a cute smile and a mane of curly brown hair. We were hanging out with mutual friends, but when August came to sit down at our table, all the seats were occupied. I very gentlemanly offered them my chair, but to my surprise, they simply took a seat on my lap. That, as they say, was that.

About a year later, we announced that we were running off to San Jose so August could attend grad school for library science. We should’ve known the plan would fail for a few reasons: Neither of us had ever been to San Jose, August had never even been on a plane before, and the Great Recession had just begun a few months prior. We couch-surfed with friends in Berkeley until the companies we worked for went bankrupt within a few weeks of each other. Still, we powered through to San Jose, moving into what could be best described as a hovel with a sink and a shower. We filled out applications for weeks before we realized there were no jobs to be had.

Every couple should start their relationship with an utter failure to make sure they’re both in it for the long haul. We had previously purchased plane tickets to visit August’s parents in Tennessee and asked ourselves, What if we just go and don’t come back to California? If we could survive this disaster, we thought, then we can survive anything. Maybe we needed something to look forward to, but around this time, we started planning our wedding. Neither of us ever popped the question. We both just knew.

Moving in with our parents or back to Asheville, however, felt like too much of a setback. August’s brother helped us find jobs and a place to stay where he lived, in Greensboro, North Carolina. Sure enough, a month later, we were living with a roommate and working service-industry jobs.

We got married in 2008 at my parent’s farmhouse in middle-of-nowhere Kentucky. The wedding theme was “garden party at which we get married.” My father had an Amish butcher roast a goat for the occasion, and my cousins made a vat of our secret-family-recipe German potato salad. We had cupcakes instead of wedding cake, before that idea was cool and then not cool. There was so much homemade wine and moonshine that two people passed out in the living room before all was said and done.

Less than a year later, August was accepted to another graduate program, this time in Iowa. Thankfully, it worked out a lot better. August finally began their career as an academic librarian after getting a job at the University of Georgia and later Georgia Tech.

I don’t remember the first time they mentioned wanting to transition. Like our decision to marry, it seemed like something that was going to happen eventually. Back when we lived in Asheville, August would do this thing they called Mustache Thursday. Every week, on that day, they would wear an obviously fake handlebar mustache to work. People did that sort of thing in mid-aughts Asheville, but for August, it hinted at what they were feeling inside themself.

In 2023, we settled into a comfortable middle-class existence in Atlanta. August realized they wanted to talk to the doctor about microdosing testosterone. What I remember most about their first shot is the immediate pep in their step and the way they described the feeling: “It was like when an old radio dial is stuck between two stations. You tap it, and one comes in clear.” They almost immediately went from a microdose to a regular dose.

August has a big family, so they sent out a group email to inform everyone. We knew my sister would be cool, so we just called her. I told my parents in person, since I was going up for a visit anyway. I was probably a bit dramatic with them; I don’t have a lot of experience delivering Big News. They were sitting on the couch watching Jeopardy! while I was doing something in the kitchen. I asked them to turn off the television because I had something important to share.

Everyone was cool or at least feigned coolness; I expected some muttering behind the scenes. But we made it pretty clear that we weren’t interested in feedback. The Big News was not phrased as a question. There was no “What do you think?” or “How do you feel?” Only: “This is what’s happening.” We didn’t want to create the impression that anyone else got a vote on our relationship or August’s gender.

The top surgery was a different story—not that people were opposed. But no one, myself included, was thrilled about the idea of August getting major surgery. If anyone in our families announced they were having an elective procedure with a long recovery time, we’d all probably have about the same supportive but nervous reaction.

To prepare, August and I watched a lot of the plastic-surgery show Botched, read up on top-surgery recovery, and talked to friends who’d had it done. My day job is at a lactation clinic, so I already knew a lot more about breast and chest surgery than most men. One thing I didn’t know about top surgery, however, was that they ask you to bring in images of what you want your chest to look like. August went with Bill Skarsgård; we agreed that Alexander would have been a bit much.

My mom is a retired RN with a Pride-flag tattoo, so of course she came down to help out the week after surgery. One afternoon, while August was having a nap, my mom asked me a question on a drive to the grocery store: “So, given everything that’s been going on, do I need to prepare myself for an amicable divorce between you two?”

It was, I suppose, a fair question, but honestly the thought had never occurred to me. We’d been through so much together. If the California debacle and multiple grad schools didn’t split us up, why would a little thing like transitioning?

I don’t begrudge people who end a relationship after their partner changes genders. If you’ve always considered both yourself and your marriage to be straight, breaking up seems like a fairly reasonable reaction. My situation is not exactly unique, but it’s certainly rare. We know a few male couples in which one is trans and the other cis, but the former had almost always transitioned before meeting their current partner.

As much as I loved August pre-transition—their cute voice, their big hair—I love their deeper voice and broader chest and shoulders. What I love most, though, is their newfound self-assurance. I always used to tell them, “I wish you knew how great you are.” Whether it’s because of testosterone’s known impact on confidence or because now they just feel more comfortable with who they are as a person, I feel like now they finally do.

Recently, August and I were again walking through our neighborhood when we had another interaction with a passerby. An older man by himself saw us, two men nonchalantly holding hands. He said nothing but dramatically placed his hand on his heart and smiled at us.

Others may disagree, but I don’t think I necessarily come off as gay. That being said, people don’t look too surprised when I say, “My husband…” This affords me the privilege of being largely anonymous and occasionally invisible. I’m also a pretty big guy, so I never feel as though anybody is going to target me even if they see me only as a gay man. The only time I’ve ever been called a faggot is when the speaker was already in a car, driving away from me.

There’s a comfort in that invisibility that I didn’t recognize until it was gone. Now, a solid three years into August’s transition, even if people don’t clock August as trans, we appear as middle-aged gay guys. Although we live in an area that could fairly be referred to as Gay Country, it’s slightly unnerving for random bystanders to have an opinion on our sexualities, even if it’s only visible through a look or a pointed lack thereof.

Historically, this is the best time and probably one of the best places to be gay or trans. But that didn’t happen by magic, and we still have a lot of work to do. Part of that work is just making ourselves visible. Because when the two of us, one trans, one cis, walk down the street and hold hands, we’re making a political statement—whether we mean to or not.

#6104. Is Wood “Tile” Replacing Hardwood Floors? Here's What Designers Think About the Trend

The newest porcelain planks blur the line between faux wood and the real thing.

At this year’s Cersaie, the annual international exhibition of ceramic tile and bathroom furnishings in Bologna, Italy, I noticed something surprising: wood was everywhere—except it wasn’t actually wood.

Among the latest tile launches were porcelain planks mimicking everything from the subtle grain of oak to the rich, distinctive markings of walnut and teak. And these weren't the obviously artificial wood-look tiles you might remember from years past. With realistic knots, nuanced color variations, and finishes designed to replicate the texture of actual timber, some were convincing enough to warrant a second look—or touch.

Of course, wood-look tile isn't new. It's been around for decades, offering homeowners the appearance of hardwood without some of its maintenance demands. But as tile trends continue to favor texture and material experimentation, manufacturers are making a compelling case for giving it another look, introducing increasingly sophisticated collections that blur the line between natural and manufactured materials.

The question is: Just because tile can look like an authentic hardwood floor, does that mean designers actually want it to?

If there's one thing the latest collections have in common, it's an obsession with realism. Manufacturers are going beyond printed wood grain, developing surfaces that attempt to replicate the way natural timber looks and feels.

At Cersaie, Italian manufacturer Atlas Concorde debuted Log Anthology, an expansion of its wood-effect porcelain offering that introduces six new wood species, including Douglas fir, ash, elm, walnut, teak, and zebrano. The collection moves beyond the familiar oak aesthetic, offering everything from understated, Nordic-inspired finishes to richly grained exotic woods.

"Realism today is as much about touch as it is about sight," says Viola Pagani of Atlas Concorde. The collection incorporates the company's Sensitech surface technology, which combines slip resistance with a softer feel underfoot. High-definition decoration also allows the brand to reproduce the natural irregularities of different wood species.

Meanwhile, Marca Corona introduced Elisir Pura, a porcelain collection inspired by Slavonian oak. Available in two variations, one with minimal knots and subtle veining and another with more pronounced natural markings, the collection features a soft-touch, wax-effect finish designed to mimic the tactile qualities of wood.

According to Francesco Giusti of Marca Corona, the collection's distinctive texture comes from a custom-blended glaze that creates subtle depressions along the grain, similar to the way natural wood feels after sanding.

And it's not just luxury Italian manufacturers investing in the category. Floor & Decor has introduced its NatureMatch line, which emphasizes more realistic wood visuals and textures.

"Wood-look tile has matured since its original debut in the market," says Ashley Biscan, design and trend expert at Floor & Decor, who reports growing demand for the material as its appearance improves.

The most popular styles? Think light wood tones, minimal color variation, and understated grain patterns. "Fabricated, exaggerated knots and grains are a thing of the past," Biscan says.

Beyond appearances, wood-look porcelain has a few practical advantages over the real thing. Unlike natural hardwood, porcelain is highly resistant to moisture, scratches, and everyday wear, making it particularly appealing for bathrooms, kitchens, mudrooms, and other hardworking spaces.

Climate also plays a significant role. Biscan notes that wood-look tile is especially appealing in coastal and desert regions, where moisture, humidity, and environmental conditions can make traditional hardwood more challenging to maintain.

Britney Stein, designer at Florida-based Sketch Design Studio, has seen those benefits firsthand. For an oceanfront residence, her team specified wood-look porcelain rather than hardwood to better withstand the home's exposure to humidity, salt air, and moisture.

"As designers, we always want to find that balance between creating something beautiful and ensuring the materials will perform well in their environment," Stein says.

The material can also create visual continuity between spaces where real wood might not be practical. Stein is currently working on a project featuring wood-look chevron porcelain tile in an outdoor loggia, carefully color-matched to the natural hardwood floors inside. When the home's large sliding glass doors open, the two spaces will appear to flow together.

Atlas Concorde is taking a similar approach with Log Anthology, offering coordinating indoor planks, outdoor pavers, herringbone formats, and large-format slabs that can extend the wood aesthetic onto walls and even furniture.

Still, wood-look tile isn't without drawbacks. Porcelain is harder and cooler underfoot than hardwood, and while it can be a more affordable alternative, that isn't always the case. Stein points out that higher-end porcelain products, particularly when installation is factored in, can cost just as much as natural hardwood.

Here's where things get interesting. While manufacturers are pushing the boundaries of what wood-look porcelain can achieve, not every interior designer is convinced it deserves a place in their projects.

"I tend to gravitate towards more timeless materials and finishes rather than those that feel trend driven," says Lauren Weisner, founder and principal designer of Shoppe Weis. "While I can certainly appreciate the appeal and practicality of wood-like tile in certain spaces, it's not something I would reach for."

For Weisner, the issue isn't necessarily how convincing the imitation looks. It's what gets lost in translation.

"Natural wood is irreplaceable. Its warmth, texture, and tactile quality bring a depth that man-made materials simply can't replicate," she explains.

Designer Evan Millárd shares that sentiment, pointing to repetitive grain patterns, visible grout lines, and textures that don't quite match the wood they're imitating as common giveaways. He also worries that wood-look tile could date a space more quickly than natural materials, particularly as preferences for plank widths, finishes, and installation patterns change.

"There's something to be said for letting materials be what they are," Millard says.

But that doesn't mean wood-look tile is entirely off the table. Even Weisner acknowledges that it could make sense in a basement, mudroom, or bathroom if executed exceptionally well.

Stein, who has incorporated the material into several projects, takes a more flexible approach. She believes the latest iterations can come remarkably close to natural hardwood, especially when manufacturers prioritize realistic grain variation, quality printing, and textured finishes.

"The size and proportion of the planks, grout selection, and installation also make a significant difference in the final result," she says.

In other words, a convincing wood-look tile floor requires more than simply picking the prettiest plank.

That depends on what we're expecting it to do.

If the goal is to replace natural hardwood entirely, designers aren't exactly lining up to make the switch. Even those who appreciate the practical benefits acknowledge that there's something about the warmth, character, and authenticity of real wood that porcelain can't fully reproduce.

But as an alternative for spaces where hardwood isn't necessarily the smartest choice? That's a different story.

"I do believe wood-look tile has staying power, particularly in projects where durability and practicality are just as important as aesthetics," Stein says. Rather than viewing it as a replacement for hardwood, she considers it another valuable option in a designer's material palette.

And perhaps that's the most compelling argument for the material's future. Wood-look tile doesn't need to fool everyone into thinking it's hardwood to earn its place in a home. It simply needs to look good, perform well, and make sense for the space.

With manufacturers continuing to refine its appearance and texture, the gap between the two materials may keep narrowing. But whether wood-look tile becomes the next big flooring trend or remains a practical alternative, one thing is clear: It's come a long way from the faux wood floors of the past.

And while I wouldn't necessarily trade beautiful hardwood floors for porcelain, after seeing the latest collections at Cersaie, I'm certainly more open to the idea.
