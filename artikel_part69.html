Americans are mostly negative on AI and oppose new data centers near them, another poll shows.

As far as public opinion goes, artificial intelligence is not a bed of roses. With the Nov. 3 elections looming nationwide, an issue that was barely a blip a year ago has become a full-blown backlash and a likely spoiler in the midterm elections.

A new poll from ABC News, the Washington Post and Ipsos found that a majority of Americans (57%) believe AI is bad for society, with many more pointing to its dangers than the potential benefits the tech could bring. An even larger number of those polled (66%) are opposed to the construction of new data centers in the US, and more (72%) would be opposed if it were in their community.

Opposition to AI crosses party lines. The technology has faced a surge in public disapproval this year, putting political candidates on blast and potentially affecting outcomes in key races, political and polling experts told CNET.

Ambivalence about AI also feeds into negativity toward data centers, which have, in a short time, become a rare bipartisan issue everyone’s talking about, according to Christopher Lee, partner and senior political strategist for Foresight Strategic Advisors.

Lee, who has closely followed election trends in southwestern states such as Nevada and Arizona, said the issue of data centers — massive AI server and compute farms that consume vast electricity and water resources — is going to “hammer” some candidates, especially those who weren’t transparent with constituents in the past about the cost to communities. If a politician voted for the construction of a data center earlier this year, their “political career is going to run into a stone wall,” he said.

The ABC News/Washington Post/Ipsos survey asked a wide range of questions about how Americans will vote in the midterms and what they think of the political parties. Alongside the economy, immigration and the war against Iran, the AI rollout is a critical issue.

As noted by CNET reporter Katelyn Chedraoui, AI regulation and data center construction have been the subject of controversy, protests and increased legal action in communities.

The poll confirmed as much: 67% of respondents felt the government isn’t doing enough to regulate AI, with risks including widespread job losses, psychological dependency, economic inequality and even human extinction. Fears and negativity toward AI also extend to the election process itself, with AI election interference among Americans’ top 10 fears for 2026.

A national PR campaign funded by tech leaders and supported by the Trump administration, meant to tout the alleged benefits of data centers, such as job creation and technological advancements, doesn’t seem to be having the intended effect. Some politicians, especially those running for congressional seats, seem to be better at reading the room.

According to Lindsey Cormack, an associate professor of political science at Stevens Institute of Technology and co-host of the podcast Government That Doesn’t Suck, many candidates are paying close attention to how they communicate about AI developments, with a particular focus on the problems they present rather than the benefits they provide.

“It’s difficult to persuade people with promises of economic development when they’re worried about their electricity bills or water supply,” she said.

Cormack, creator of the congressional newsletter archive DCinbox, has tracked a sharp rise in lawmakers linking data centers to consumer energy costs. In an email to CNET, she noted that between January and September 2025, just 5% of Republican newsletters discussing data centers mentioned electricity bills or ratepayers (4 of 76). Over the same period in 2026, that figure surged to 54% (97 of 180). Democrats showed a similar focus, with 40% of their data center newsletters (71 of 176) raising the issue in 2026.

The midterms won’t be the end of the battle. AI and data centers are “going to be an issue in 2028 and 2030 and way beyond that,” Lee said.

#2998. Microsoft AI tool beat generalist doctors at step-by-step diagnosis

A Science study found Microsoft’s orchestrator more than twice as accurate as generalist physicians on 304 complex case reports.

Microsoft logo at the Microsoft Experience Center, New York

Microsoft has developed an AI diagnostic tool that solved complex medical cases more than twice as accurately as generalist doctors in a study published in Science.

Unlike many medical AI systems, it works through a case step by step, deciding which questions to ask and which tests to order before reaching a diagnosis.

The tool, called the MAI Diagnostic Orchestrator (MAI-DxO), simulates a panel of clinical experts, Reuters reported.

It gathers information, reviews test results, and revises its working diagnosis as new evidence emerges. Many medical AI systems instead assess symptoms, laboratory results, and scans together.

In a peer-reviewed study published in Science, MAI-DxO, running on OpenAI’s GPT-5, reached the correct diagnosis in 83.2% of 304 challenging cases published in the New England Journal of Medicine. Generalist doctors, who had access to textbooks and the internet, scored 36.1%.

The system also outperformed doctors on 325 emergency department admissions, scoring 80.3% compared with 46.8% for the human group.

Researchers said its diagnostic work-ups cost less, too, although those figures are estimates based on the cases rather than actual medical bills.

Dominic King, a corporate vice president in Microsoft AI’s health unit, told Reuters that medical AI has largely been evaluated using multiple-choice tests.

“AI has become very good at getting a 100% pass rate on those,” King said. “No patient ever came to see me with a nice summary.”

Harsha Nori, head of AI at Microsoft AI Health, said the researchers also measured how effectively the system selected tests, rather than looking only at whether it reached the right answer.

“We’re not just measuring the accuracy of the systems,” Nori told Reuters.

The results come with important limitations. The doctors in the comparison were generalists, not specialists, and the cases were presented as text.

Neither the AI nor the doctors had access to physical examinations or medical imaging in the study.

MAI-DxO can also work with different AI models. An earlier preprint, published in June 2025, reported improvements using models from Google, Anthropic, Meta, and other companies.

The system is not yet being used in clinical practice. Microsoft’s researchers plan to work with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston to test whether it can help triage patients in emergency department waiting rooms.

#2995. Cristiano Ronaldo’s Al-Nassr faces uncertain ownership future as PIF weighs two options to avoid AFC conflict

With new Asian Football Confederation (AFC) rules approaching, Al-Nassr, Al-Ahli and Al-Ittihad may need changes to their ownership structures before the 2027-28 season to protect their eligibility for continental competitions.

Cristiano Ronaldo’s Al-Nassr could face a significant change behind the scenes as Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund (PIF) considers how to address a growing regulatory challenge involving the country’s leading football clubs. With new Asian Football Confederation (AFC) rules approaching, Al-Nassr, Al-Ahli and Al-Ittihad may need changes to their ownership structures before the 2027-28 season to protect their eligibility for continental competitions.

The issue stems from regulations designed to prevent conflicts of interest when multiple clubs under the same ownership participate in the same Asian competition. According to 365Scores Arabic, PIF is examining ways to ensure the three Saudi clubs can operate independently while preserving their financial value.

Under Article 8 of the AFC Club Competitions Entry Regulations, restrictions apply when a single entity has ownership, management authority or significant influence over multiple participating clubs in circumstances that threaten competitive integrity. If Al-Nassr, Al-Ahli and Al-Ittihad qualify for the same continental competition, their shared ownership could create an eligibility problem unless the required separation is achieved.

The two options on PIF’s table

PIF is reportedly weighing two potential solutions: selling investment stakes to private investors or transferring administrative control to establish greater independence between the clubs. Both approaches could help Saudi Arabia’s major teams comply with the upcoming regulations while allowing PIF to protect the value of its investments.

Selling stakes would follow a model already seen at Al-Hilal, where PIF agreed to sell a 70% shareholding to Kingdom Holding Company, linked to Prince Alwaleed bin Talal, for approximately SAR 840 million while retaining a 30% minority stake. Alternatively, transferring administrative ownership could involve restructuring boards and management arrangements, although any changes would need to satisfy the AFC’s requirements on control and influence.

How Saudi Arabia’s ownership model reached this point

PIF acquired 75% stakes in Saudi Arabia’s so-called Big Four clubs in 2023 as part of the country’s broader Sports Clubs Investment and Privatization Project under Vision 2030. The remaining 25% was held by non-profit foundations representing the clubs’ traditional membership structures.

In August 2026, Saudi authorities began transferring those remaining stakes to PIF, consolidating ownership and dissolving the foundations’ boards. Al-Hilal’s partial sale to a private investor has given it a different ownership structure, while Al-Nassr, Al-Ahli and Al-Ittihad now face questions about how their shared control can be reconciled with the AFC’s rules.

What the changes could mean for Ronaldo’s Al-Nassr

For Al-Nassr, the developments carry particular significance because Ronaldo has played a central role in the club’s international profile since joining in 2023. Any ownership restructuring could influence its commercial direction, recruitment strategy and ambitions to compete regularly for major Asian honours.

Al-Ahli and Al-Ittihad face similar considerations, as all three clubs seek to remain competitive while adapting to stricter governance requirements. The wider objective is to attract private investment, strengthen club independence and make Saudi football more sustainable over the long term.

For now, PIF’s plans remain under consideration, and no final restructuring decision has been announced. The coming months could bring greater clarity as the Saudi authorities and the clubs prepare for the new regulations.

#2992. A Talent for Murder review: Alden Ehrenreich gives sexy menace in this Patricia Highsmith-inspired thriller

Carol. Deep Water. Strangers on a Train. The Talented Mr. Ripley. American author Patricia Highsmith has inspired filmmakers for decades with her lurid tales of sex, deception, and murder. Now, she herself is a character in such a thriller, thanks to A Talent For Murder.

Adapted for the screen by Australian playwright Joanna Murray-Smith from her own play, Switzerland, this film imagines what might have happened if Highsmith's biggest fan got the chance to meet his hero. Enter Edward Ridgeway (Tony winner Alden Ehrenreich), a handsome young man with carefully coiffed dark hair, a strong jaw, a pressed shirt, and polished glasses, but little poise. On a train through Switzerland, he seems like he stumbled out of the first chapter of a Tom Ripley novel, sure to be felled or corrupted by the iconic con man.

Now, the girl. Clementine Harriet Balfour (Olivia Cooke) is pretty, with a bouncy brunette bob, and a fashion sense that hints at an old-money family and independent ambitions. They meet as strangers on a train, but are sure to cross paths again in the small Swiss village where she's visiting a miserly dowager aunt (Juliet Stevenson), and he's tasked with getting Highsmith to sign a contract to write a sixth Ripley novel.

Director Anton Corbijn expertly lays out a spring-loaded trap that's sure to capture the interest of Highsmith fans. A handsome couple flirts, while wealth and opportunity glitter on the horizon. What might they do to get there?

Enter a surly Highsmith, played with gnarly certainty by the one and only Helen Mirren.

The English Dame has done Shakespeare and Prime Suspect; she's played Queen Elizabeth II and a Fast and Furious baddie with equal aplomb. Plus, just last year, she played a sultry spy in the cozy crime treat The Thursday Murder Club. But there's no lightness in Mirren's Highsmith.

In 1995, atop a Swiss mountain, the heralded author has become an eccentric recluse, rumored to have poisoned a neighbor's dog for barking too much, and to have driven an American man so mad he fled her home while only wearing his pajamas. Dressed in men's slacks, a crisp men's dress shirt, a cravat and vest, paired with a fuck-off bob, Mirren's Highsmith is imposing, even introduced ironing a shirt.

When Edward appears, eager with compliments, gifts, and talk of opportunity, she regards him with such scorching disdain the audience at the Toronto International Film Festival gasped and guffawed. The ruthless wit of the Ripley novels is sharply replicated in Murray-Smith's screenplay, and Mirren makes the most of every line, savoring the sharp barbs. To his credit, Ehrenreich doesn't balk in the face of this storied actress. And thank the gods, because failing to match her onscreen would make A Talent for Murder a miss. Instead, it's a twisted thrill: sexy, salty, and wickedly fun.

Earlier this year, Ehrenreich earned praise and a Tony award for his phenomenal performance in the Broadway revival of Becky Shaw. Watching A Talent for Murder, I thought often of this stage performance, as his characters have a lot in common. Young men who've come from a family with no means and no reputation worth a damn, who both cozy up to a wealthy family, determined not just to enter their world but to rule it.

At first glance, Edward looks like Matt Damon's version of Ripley, from the juicy 1999 adaptation The Talented Mr. Ripley. His hair floppy but fashionable. His smile earnest but awkward. The eyeglasses, a perfect twin. This feels intentional, as if Corbijn wants Highsmith fans to immediately wonder if this eager social climber might have the root of evil in him, which could make Highsmith's criminal mastermind real.

Smartly, the screenplay doesn't pitch Edward as some rube. He's come ready to impress Highsmith, even if that means tantalizing her with twisted tales from his past, and the possibility of criminally corrupting him. Enticed by his game, the two begin to collaborate on a murder mystery about a blue-collar boy who meets an old-money girl, and is determined to make her family fortune his own. Their thought experiment gets joltingly real as Highsmith haunts him in the night and inspires him to treat the reckless heiress Clementine as source material.

From there, A Talent for Murder begins to blur what's real and imagined in pursuit of bringing fresh blood to Highsmith's typewriter. And it just wouldn't work if Ehrenreich weren't on fire. Where Mirren is delicious in her snarling cruelty (think Tilda Swinton in Problemista), Ehrenreich is intriguing in his slipperiness. Edward and Highsmith's imagined novel plays out as we watch, recasting the former as her anti-hero, with Clementine playing the role they write for her. In these sections, Ehrenreich has the smoothness of Ripley, his confidence edged with a misanthropic disgust at all who aren't as clever and calculating as he. But as Edward, Ehrenreich trembles with insecurity, particularly in a scene where he's delicately called out by an Irish lady (Brid Brennan), who spots him as an outsider from his shoes alone.

To her credit, Cooke is not outshone in the slim role of Clementine. Whether the imagined version where she is a yearning virgin or the real one, where she is a spoiled rich girl looking for a cheap thrill, she brings a full-blooded verve to this girl. Together, this trio weaves a tale of sex, death, and desire that touches on the best of Highsmith's works, without feeling like a shallow retread of them. Instead, A Talent for Murder plays like a superb fan fiction, imagining the inner world and grisly desires of the seminal author, without idolizing her or whitewashing her well-documented defects.

A Talent For Murder is darkly funny, freshly thrilling, and unrepentantly seductive. It's not a Ripley sequel, but it's damn close.

#2985. The Only Living Pickpocket in New York review: John Turturro gets the movie he deserves

John Turturro has a storied career that stretches across 45 years, and boasts unforgettable performances in iconic films like Spike Lee's Do the Right Thing and the Coen Bros' The Big Lebowski and O Brother, Where Art Thou? More recently, he awed streaming audiences with his bittersweet romance as Irving Bailiff in Apple TV+'s Severance, for which he's earned two Primetime Emmy nominations.

Across more than 100 films and television series, Turturro has played clowns and villains, lovers and bowlers. But in his latest, The Only Living Pickpocket in New York, this heralded character actor and celebrated New York City native gets the role that he was born to play.

Writer/director Noah Segan entrusts Turturro with the leading man role in a crime thriller that evokes 1970s classics like Taxi Driver, The French Connection, and The Taking of Pelham One Two Three — movies that predate Turturro's screen career. And yet, The Only Living Pickpocket in New York doesn't feel like a throwback. Instead, this big-hearted and hard-nosed thriller bridges the gap between past and present with a modern awareness of how things change, even for a classic New York character.

It's a cliché to call a movie a love letter to New York. The Only Living Pickpocket in New York is more of a confession of affection scribbled on the back of a way-too-long CVS receipt. It's passionate and a bit grimy, just like the city itself.

Turturro stars as Harry Lehman, an aging Bronx resident who's been a career criminal specializing in pickpocketing for decades. This day begins as any other, lifting a wallet from a finance bro on the subway. But times have changed. The city has moved away from paper money and toward Apple Pay and cryptocurrency. So, it's harder than ever for Harry to provide for his paralyzed wife, Rosie (Karina Arroyave). So, it seems like a boon when he successfully boosts the wallet of a smug 20-something named Dylan (Will Price). But while Harry is fencing the goods to his pawnbroker buddy Ben (a pitch-perfect Steve Buscemi), he learns this mark is practically made. Okay, so he's more of a nepobaby-gangster than a goodfella. But this is still bad news.

Harry's got to hustle to get back what he sold, or else Dylan and his posse of puffed-up Gen Z goons will kill Rosie. In this quest, Harry rides the train from the Bronx to Chinatown, to Sheepshead Bay in Brooklyn, and up into cozy Queens, where big houses and yards shake up the presumed urban landscape. (Sorry, Staten Island, no time for you!)

In each place, Harry collides with a quintessentially New York figure, be it a no-nonsense neighbor (Echaka Agba), a shop owner (Kelvin Han Yee) more focused on his TV than a pesky customer, a wise-cracking detective (Giancarlo Esposito), a finger-wagging grannie (Lori Tan Chinn), or a slick-fingered could-be protégé (Mark Cayne). Like in classic '70s New York City cinema, each of these folks adds a new texture to this portrait of this messy and magical city. The cinematography by Sam Levy showcases this setting with a understated wonder B-roll of rumbling subways and bustling streets reflects Harry's never-say-die energy, as he strides out of pawn shops, police precincts, and community centers with an easy confidence. Before he dashes off, Levy's camera will zoom in, catching him as if he were a native animal on the run. Or else, a simple pan as Harry pauses to think reflects the big, urban world around him that won't stop for a moment.

This is not the romantic landscape of Manhattan showcased in countless rom-coms. The only view of the skyline or even NYC's rivers in this film is tinged with a sinister tone, as Harry and his detective friend recall an old thief whose head was found "bobbing in the Hudson!" The Only Living Pickpocket in New York instead captures a city that is both repulsive and beautiful, heartbreaking and inspiring. Segan even kicks off with a gut-punch of an opener, playing LCD Soundsystem's "New York I Love You, But You're Bringing Me Down" at such an intensity that you can't escape the raw vulnerability in its lyrics or the trembling voice of James Murphy.

Before the title card hits, Segan shows he understands this city like a New Yorker. This is a place that will build you up and knock you down, over and over. And somehow — for those of us who truly love it — it feels like a privilege every time you get back up to try again.

This is a story of a man who's lived past his prime. A brassy score of trumpets and plinking piano puts Harry Lehman in the space of Travis Bickle, Popeye Doyle, or Transit Police Lt. Zachary Garber. But Segan's film willfully lacks the intensity of these reference points. That's because the Fear City days are behind New York, and Harry's hard edge has softened over the years of caring for his wife.

Harry, Rosie, Ben, and even Detective Warren are relics of a "bad days" era that they miss, because the days were rough, but the wild nights still make them smile. Now, kids half their age are talking about how the only worthwhile terrain for theft is through computers. But Harry doesn't even have a cell phone. A friendly stranger marvels when he knows a phone number "by heart." A crypto-bro chides him for not understanding he's part of "planned obsolescence." Over and over, the youth of this city are telling Harry that is time is over. There's a quiet resignation in his response, but even that doesn't mean he'll go down without a fight.

Turturro, who has famously seethed, snarled, and caterwauled to dramatic and comedic effect, plays closer to the vest here. When not lifting wallets, his long, agile fingers hide in the pockets of his tweed overcoat, its salt-and-pepper herringbone mirroring the color of his silvered, curly hair. Harry strives to not stand out, all the better to ply his craft. But he is coolly dashing. And in that, he has a facade of composure. But cuddled next to his wife, his passion burns. For her, he will coo and dance, showing that this old man still has moves. In front of old friends, he is gruff but caring. To their grandchildren, he is warm and paternal. And when confronted by mercurial thugs, he is alert and patient. Turturro offers a phenomenally nuanced performance that subtly speaks to what it takes to survive decades in this city.

When the Knicks won the NBA Championship this year, Turturro went viral for a dance he did at the celebration parade, candidly caught on camera. I thought of that scene throughout The Only Living Pickpocket in New York, not because his genuine joy in that moment is reflected in the movie, but because in both of these on-camera moments, Turturro succinctly captures what it is to be a New Yorker. It is grappling with grief and joy in the same crazy day. It is making a family of the misfits you treasure. It is embracing this city — and the outer boroughs — with appreciation and respect. It is building a life few can understand, and frankly, they don't need to. This is a space for all kinds of dreams, all kinds of people, and all kinds of ways to lose and win.

As a crime thriller, The Only Living Pickpocket in New York is a solidly entertaining ride, being sharply paced, thoughtfully shot, and stacked with a terrific cast that also includes Tatiana Maslany. But more than just another fun crime movie set in New York City, this one is also resolutely aware of the greats who came before, paying tribute without aping them. And best of all, Turturro offers a turn that showcases his humor, sexiness, pathos, and a cutting subtlety that's absolutely riveting. In a career full of outstanding performances, The Only Living Pickpocket in New York offers one of Turturro's very best.

The city has been completely rebuilt following the battle with Kuvira at the end of The Legend of Korra, in which Korra created a new spirit portal within the city's Spirit Wilds. Now, the yellow beam of that portal appears to emerge straight from a Future Industries tower, suggesting that the people of Republic City have built over or around the Spirit Wilds. That couldn't possibly have a negative impact on the physical world's relationship with the Spirit World, right?

In Pavi's anomaly vision, she also sees a concerned-looking Korra, one who's much older than when we last left her, flying from Air Temple Island to Republic City. When Nisha joins Pavi in the vision, the scene changes drastically. The twins find themselves in the city streets, surrounded by fires and explosions. As the smoke clears from a motorcycle crash, the twins see Korra again. This time, though, she's changed: She's in the Avatar State, and she sports a menacing smirk. That can only mean one thing. The twins are seeing the moment Korra somehow turned and broke the world.

As Pavi and Nisha look on, Korra reaches up to pipe-like structures running along Republic City's skyscrapers and tears them down. The breaking of the pipes unleashes massive bands of destructive purple energy beams, which look very similar to the spirit vine energy Kuvira harnessed in her campaign to conquer Republic City.

That's all we get of the Cataclysm, but there's already a lot to unpack here, from Republic City's new look to the purple energy beams. Most fascinating of all might be the pipe structures Korra is tearing down. Their design is not rigid and pipe-like, but instead resembles vines. In rebuilding Republic City, could its citizens have tried to harness spirit energy from the Spirit Wilds as an energy source? Did they build around the vines climbing up buildings, and if so, did that strategy backfire spectacularly, contributing to the Cataclysm? Based on Republic City's skyline and these mysterious pipes, it seems like the physical world's relationship with the Spirit World was already way out of whack by the time the Cataclysm happened. That's odd, given that much of Korra's time as the Avatar was focused on maintaining that balance. After all, she opened the spirit portals at the poles and in Republic City.

That brings us to Korra herself. Let's be totally real here. That smirking woman in the Avatar State might look like Korra and bend like Korra, but that frightening smile and the intent on destroying the world just doesn't feel like her. Obviously, a lot has changed since we last saw Korra on TV, but I refuse to believe she had an entire personality transplant!

Instead, this version of Korra in the Avatar State recalls a more sinister figure from Book 2 in The Legend of Korra. In the climax of that season, villain Unalaq merged with the darkness spirit Vaatu to create a dark Avatar whose eyes glowed red, contrasting with the white glow of the light spirit Raava, who lives within the Avatar. In the Seven Havens vision, Korra's eyes still glow white, yet her unsettling body language screams that the Avatar State has been corrupted in some way.

By the end of Season 2, Korra defeated Vaatu, but did not destroy him outright. Instead, his spirit is set to regrow within Raava over a period of 10,000 years, until the next Harmonic Convergence. That's a ways off within the Avatar timeline, but could the rapid acceleration of technology or Korra's proximity to the Spirit Wilds have accelerated that growth somewhat? Or alternatively, did some Future Industries technology create the anomaly and trigger some dark turn in Korra? I could very well be barking up the wrong spirit vine, but I have a feeling Book 2 of The Legend of Korra is about to become very, very important.

Clearly, we haven't gotten the full story yet, and Seven Havens is being very careful in how it doles out Korra's memories. In this episode alone, it leaves a gap between Korra's flight to Republic City and the Cataclysm. (Assuming the visions happen on the same night, that is, as Korra is wearing the exact same outfit in both.) That gap could be key to understanding Korra's motives in bringing on the Cataclysm, and hopefully in clearing her name somewhat in the timeline of Seven Havens.

#2952. Apple’s iPhone Duo goes on sale with tight supply and mixed reviews

Apple’s $1,999 foldable reached stores on Friday, with few phones for walk-in buyers and reviewers split on its value.

Apple’s first foldable phone, the iPhone Duo, went on sale on Friday, October 23, at a starting price of $1,999. Sydney was the first Apple Store to sell it, opening its doors at 08:00 local time (21:00 GMT on Thursday) to a queue of buyers.

The launch will show whether Apple can grow a niche that Samsung and Huawei have served for years, Reuters reported.

The phone is also an early test for John Ternus, who became Apple’s chief executive on September 1 and unveiled the Duo eight days later.

Buyers will struggle to find one in a shop. Apple said walk-in stock is limited in the US, Canada, Mexico, Australia and much of the UK and Europe. In China, France, Hong Kong, India and Japan, stores will only hand phones to customers who ordered online.

In Sydney, Simon Krishnan, a 48-year-old engineer, paid A$3,899 (about US$2,734) for a 512GB model.

“This is a bit of a luxury item for me, because it’s so expensive, but I just think, you know what? Life’s too short,” he told Reuters.

Delivery dates for new orders slipped to late January within minutes of pre-orders opening on October 16.

“The pre-order demand for iPhone Duo, our first foldable iPhone, has been nothing short of extraordinary,” Apple told the Financial Times.

Early reviews praised the design and a hinge that leaves almost no visible crease, long a weak spot for folding screens. Reviewers were less sure about the rest.

The Duo has no telephoto camera, uses Touch ID instead of Face ID, and many third-party apps still need updating for its 7.6-inch inner screen.

John Gruber of Daring Fireball wrote that using the Duo felt like using “an extraordinarily polished prototype,” not a finished product he would want to buy and carry.

Marques Brownlee said in his YouTube review that the phone was “probably a no” for people who mostly text, use social media, and browse the web.

Analysts are more upbeat. IDC expects Apple to take 44% of foldable market value in 2026 on 31% of units shipped, and to lead the category on both measures by 2027.

“Apple has made a convincing case for this form factor,” Francisco Jeronimo, a vice president at IDC, told Reuters after a week with the phone.

The next test, he said, is “turning excitement into availability, adoption and a lasting new chapter” for the smartphone.
