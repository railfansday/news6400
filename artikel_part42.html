#4348. Blockchain Association Presses Congress on Crypto Taxes as Clarity Act Stalls

The Blockchain Association asked congressional leaders Thursday to pass bipartisan crypto tax legislation, but with the Clarity Act near dead, the House bill stripped of mining and staking relief, and Congress out until after the midterms, the ask lands with little room to move.

The Blockchain Association sent a letter to Senate and House leadership on Thursday asking Congress to pass bipartisan digital asset tax legislation, the group’s first broad push on taxes since the Clarity Act failed in the Senate last month.

The letter, signed by Chief Policy Officer Lindsay Fraser and addressed to Majority Leader John Thune, Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, Speaker Mike Johnson and Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, argues that the rules governing the 67 million Americans who hold digital assets “were written for a different era.”

The group paired it with a new site, TaxClarityforAmerica.com, built for constituents to contact their representatives.

The asks will sound familiar to anyone who has followed the tax fight: a de minimis exemption for small transactions, cash treatment for stablecoins, tax on mining and staking rewards at sale instead of receipt, broker reporting limited to firms that hold customer assets, and wash sale rules that close loss harvesting without catching everyday transactions.

The letter adds a safe harbor for foreign traders on U.S. exchanges, a mark-to-market election, and R&D credit eligibility for blockchain development. What the letter leaves out is an endorsement. Two bills are somewhat in play, and the association named neither.

The House moved first. One day after the Clarity Act failed in the Senate, the Ways and Means Committee approved the Digital Asset Tax Certainty Act, H.R. 10357, a 114-page package from Chairman Jason Smith (R-MO). It exempts network fees under $10 from capital gains reporting, with a carve-out for taxpayers who made more than 5,000 transfers in the prior year, and extends wash sale and constructive sale rules to digital assets. Qualified dollar stablecoins are exempt from the wash sale rule.

The package left out the provision the industry wanted most. Rep. Mike Carey’s Tax Clarity for Mining and Staking Act, H.R. 9175, which would have let miners and stakers defer tax until they sell, was in an early discussion draft and gone from the text the committee released on September 14. Instead the bill classifies validation rewards as ordinary income, taxable on receipt. Rep. Steven Horsford (D-NV), who had proposed capping the deferral at five years, voted for the package and said it leaves the question open for further work.

The Blockchain Association, Crypto Council for Innovation and Digital Chamber had asked the committee in June to pass that bill without changes. The committee did the opposite.

The Senate has its own version. Sen. Steve Daines (R-MT) introduced the ADAPT Act on September 30 with Sens. Cynthia Lummis, Bernie Moreno and Tim Scott as cosponsors. It exempts purchases made with GENIUS Act stablecoins from gain or loss, sets the same $10 fee threshold, and applies wash sale rules with grandfathering for existing holdings and exemptions for mining and staking rewards. It sits in committee.

Lummis’s earlier bill, S. 2207, with a $300 per-transaction de minimis exemption capped at $5,000 a year, has been in the Finance Committee since June 2025.

Neither chamber’s bill contains the exemption for small bitcoin purchases that the industry made a centerpiece at Bitcoin 2026. The House bill’s $10 threshold covers fees, not purchases. The Senate bill’s spending exemption covers stablecoins, not bitcoin.

The Bitcoin Policy Institute and others have argued that any de minimis rule should cover bitcoin, and Thursday’s letter asks for a “meaningful” exemption without naming a figure.

Congress is in recess until after the November 3 midterms. When members return, they will have a lame-duck session measured in weeks, and crypto taxes compete with government funding and whatever else leadership decides to clear before the 119th Congress ends.

Lummis, the Senate’s loudest voice on digital asset taxes, retires in January 2027.

The Clarity Act’s collapse shapes all of this. The market structure bill passed the House 294-134 in July 2025, then stalled in the Senate for more than a year while the banking lobby fought the crypto industry over stablecoin yield and Democrats pressed for ethics language aimed at the Trump family’s crypto ventures.

The cloture vote on September 15 failed 49-50. No Democrat crossed over, and Republicans Susan Collins, Josh Hawley and Jerry Moran voted no. Sen. Thom Tillis switched his vote so he could file a motion to reconsider, which keeps the bill alive on paper and little more.

With market structure off the table, the industry has shifted its lobbying to what might move. Tax is the obvious candidate: the House package cleared committee with bipartisan votes, and the wash sale provisions raise revenue, which gives tax writers a reason to attach them to a larger vehicle. Punchbowl News reported Thursday that the association’s refusal to back either bill signals the industry wants more than what Congress has produced.

Regulators keep filling the gap in the meantime. The OCC issued its final GENIUS Act rule on Wednesday, and the SEC and CFTC have been setting market structure policy through rulemaking and enforcement in the absence of a statute.

StarkWare plans to release a draft whitepaper in November as it explores an independent, quantum-resistant blockchain.

Starknet’s community has voted to support research into becoming an independent, post-quantum layer-1 blockchain.

The network’s potential departure from Ethereum gained overwhelming support. The governance vote that ran for seven days closed on Thursday. The poll drew about 3,200 votes, with 93.52% of voting power in favor of the transition.

The non-binding poll recorded the highest participation in Starknet’s governance history, according to StarkWare, which said it abstained from voting to leave the decision to the community.

The outcome allows StarkWare and the Starknet Foundation to proceed with research into an independent L1. The actual shift itself would require a separate, binding governance vote. For now, Starknet will continue operating as an Ethereum layer-2 network.

“We’re beginning work on a whitepaper even before we start the weekend, and hope to release a draft for public scrutiny in November,” StarkWare CEO Eli Ben-Sasson told Cointelegraph.

StarkWare and the Starknet Foundation have established a working group to examine the proposed transition and develop more detailed technical proposals.

The governance proposal states that STRK will remain the network’s native token, with no new token planned, while existing bridges between Starknet and Ethereum will remain open.

StarkWare first raised the potential transition on Oct. 8, citing concerns about the network’s dependence on Ethereum for security. Although Starknet uses hash-based STARK proofs and supports programmable accounts capable of adopting post-quantum signatures, its bridge and data availability infrastructure remain dependent on Ethereum’s upgrade timeline.

"Regulator-led measures can encourage institutional participation in the crypto ecosystem over the near term, but only legislation would make the framework more durable over the longer term," the JPMorgan analysts led by Nikolaos Panigirtzoglou said in a Wednesday report.

"...elevated political and legal risk premia are likely to persist until comprehensive market-structure legislation is eventually passed. For now, the crypto industry has to rely on a patchwork of regulatory actions that could be overturned in as soon as two years, if a Democrat wins the White House," the analysts added.

The Senate failed to move the Clarity Act forward last month. The analysts said the Clarity Act is unlikely to pass this year and may return in 2027.

Crypto market sentiment has still remained "relatively constructive." For now, the focus is on steps the SEC, CFTC, and OCC can take to make it easier for institutions to enter crypto.

The SEC released a five-year "Innovation Exemption" for tokenized stocks last month. The tokens would need to carry the same financial and voting rights as the original shares. Synthetic tokens would not qualify, and trading platforms would face limits, the analysts noted.

Companies could also choose not to have their shares tokenized. The analysts said more firms have recently chosen to do so, hurting sentiment for application tokens such as ether as ETF flows and futures positioning weakened.

"This contrasts with the initial period following the September 15th SEC announcement, when both ETF flows and our futures-position proxy had increased," the analysts said.

The CFTC is looking at ways to allow leveraged crypto trading on regulated U.S. platforms. The plan is still at an early stage and is unlikely to move forward before 2027, the analysts said.

"Activity that migrates onshore can deepen liquidity, improve price formation, and eventually support the development of more liquid regulated venues that institutions are more likely to use," the analysts said.

The Independent Community Bankers of America has sued the OCC over parts of the approvals.

"These charters are not immune from challenge, as community banks, through the ICBA, have sued the OCC over aspects of the approvals. Even so, once granted, charters tend to be difficult to unwind in practice," the analysts said.

Meanwhile, the Financial Accounting Standards Board is considering allowing companies to classify certain stablecoins as cash equivalents on their balance sheets. The proposal is open for comment until Nov. 19.

"Adoption would support broader use beyond crypto trading, toward payments and liquidity management for corporates and institutions," the analysts said.

They concluded that regulators can help the crypto market for now, but clear long-term rules will still require a law from Congress.

#4335. In 1960, scientists predicted the world would end on November 13, 2026

Published Oct 13, 2026 9:02 AM EDT

In exactly a month, on November 13, 2026, the world will end. Fittingly, our impending apocalypse falls on Friday the 13th.

At least, that was the prediction made by University of Illinois physicist Heinz von Foerster in a highly cited Science paper published in 1960. Though talks of supposed doomsday have renewed in recent months thanks to hyperbolic anxieties over runaway, sentient artificial intelligence, von Foerster had a much different calamity in mind.

For more than a century, the prevailing fear, dating back to English economist Thomas Malthus who lived between 1766 to 1834, was of a booming population outrunning its food supply. Famine would ensue. But Von Foerster broke from that view. He assumed (correctly, as it turns out) that human ingenuity would keep the world mostly fed. The problem, he argued, was that the population would keep accelerating, toward “infinity.”

In a now infamous quote, von Foerster predicted, “Our great—great—grandchildren will not starve to death,” von Foerster and his co-authors wrote. “They will be squeezed to death.” Homo sapiens’ last gasping breath, they predicted, would peter out in the fall of 2026.

It’s pretty clear that von Foerster’s estimate was dead wrong. Not only is the global population (around 8.3 billion people) not expanding near the rate it was when he was writing, but there are actually concerns it’s moving in the opposite direction. In dozens of countries around the world, from South Korea and Japan to much of Western Europe, the concern among demographers has shifted from worries about overpopulation to declining birth rates.

In the United States, President Donald Trump has jumped on the issue, calling himself the “fertilization president” offering tax incentives for new families and even exploring medals for women who give birth to six or more children. Joseph Stalin previously championed that last idea.

So how was a prediction so wrong and still so influential? Human population experts speaking with Popular Science say von Foerster and his contemporaries based their prediction on real trends from the past while utterly failing to see the world changing right before their eyes. Though they may have accurately measured growth rates up to that point, that only tells part of the story.

“He got the picture right just as that picture ceased to be right,” Rockefeller University and Columbia University professor and mathematical biologist Joel Cohen tells Popular Science. Cohen has studied human population changes for years and published the influential 1995 book How Many People Can the Earth Support?

“Von Foerster didn’t know at the time, and in fact, nobody knew” that the all-time population growth had already happened, Cohen adds. “The all-time peak happened in the early 1960s.”

Von Foerster and his colleagues published their “Doomsday” paper on November 4, 1960. The dramatic pronouncement hinges on an equation that projects the human population into the future based on past trends.

The team gathered 24 estimates of world population dating back 2,000 years. The key assumption underpinning their logic was that humans differ from other animals because of our unique ability to cooperate and innovate.

Fruit flies in a jar eventually level off as crowding and competition take their toll. Humans, on the other hand, the authors argue, work the other way. The more of us there are, the better we get at cooperating. And the better we get at cooperation, the faster our population grows.

Follow that curve forward, and the population doesn’t just grow; it rockets toward infinity, hitting the so-called mathematical wall on November 13, 2026, give or take five years.

Von Foerster wasn’t alone in sounding the alarm either. Eight years later, biologist Paul R. Ehrlich would publish The Population Bomb, arguably the most influential single text fueling overpopulation fears.

“Both of those guys were right looking backward, but wrong looking forward,” Cohen says.

At a glance, thinking the future will resemble the past seems pretty reasonable. The human population grew steadily but slowly over much of our roughly 300,000 years of humanity. But that changed radically with the onset of globalization, colonization, and the Industrial Revolution.

As Darrell Bricker and John Ibbitson note in their 2020 book Empty Planet: The Shock of Global Population Decline, human population passed the one billion mark somewhere around 1800. That doubled to two billion by 1927. Then doubled again just less than 50 years later in 1974. It would then only take another 13 years to cross the five billion mark. And in 2022, we surpassed eight billion.

That remarkable explosion had many causes, Cohen says, but among the most important was the spread of cheap, calorie-dense crops like potatoes and corn from the Americas.

Crucially, though, humans weren’t necessarily having way more kids. They were just far better at keeping them alive.

“Humanity got better at keeping the people who were born alive through simple, but important innovations like the germ theory of disease, sanitation, nutrition,” University of Texas Associate professor and economic demographer Dean Spears tells Popular Science.

Demographers like Spears study human populations and how they change over time. He published a book on the topic titled After the Spike, earlier this year. Advances in nutrition and medicine “caused early life mortality rates to fall, so more of the humans who were born survived and that caused the population to grow.”

Factoring in longer life expectancy and reductions in child mortality around the world blows a huge hole in Von Foerster’s calculations. Death rates can only fall so far, and once most children survive, families tend to have fewer of them.

The population boom was a one time explosion, not an ever-accelerating loop.

Von Foerster’s assumption of endlessly accelerating growth, Spears says, was “based on a misunderstanding of where population growth comes from.”

Experts specializing in human population studies at the time actually knew that was the case, Spears notes.

Neither von Foerster’s nor Ehrlich (who wrote The Population Bomb) were human population experts. Von Foerster’s background was in physics and philosophy. Ehrlich was a biologist. But they were both great at getting attention.

Indeed, academics at the time reportedly sent flustered letters to the editor at Science, calling the prediction “ridiculous” and “manifestly absurd.”

“There was never, even at that time, any reason to think that it would keep growing fast like that.” Spears says. “Paul Ehrlich was a biologist who studied butterflies, not human demography.”

In the later 20th century, population growth actually started declining quite rapidly thanks to a convergence of a variety of factors.

The “Green Revolution” in farming meant that more food could be grown on the same plot of land, which extinguished fears of humanity suddenly starving itself. The first birth control pill, approved by the Federal Drug Administration (FDA) in the U.S. in 1957, also gave women more autonomy to decide when and how many children to have.

These trends, along with global population shifts away from rural areas and towards more urban areas, would eventually shift concerns away from overpopulation and towards declining birth rates.

Although demographers and policy experts debate how much people should worry about declining birth rates, it is generally agreed that an older population that significantly outnumbers younger people can lead to long-term strains on the country’s economy and social services.

With fewer workers needed in the field, more and more people moved to cities. Historically, families living on farms or agricultural communities tend to have more children than those in urban areas because those children could work the land and, in a purely economic sense, provide more labor and, hopefully, make the family more money. In more urban areas, another child meant another mouth to feed rather than an extra pair of hands.

In the later part of the 20th century, women in many parts of the world gained far more autonomy through education, work, and access to contraception, which demographers agree is among the biggest drivers of demographic change over the past half century.

Some governments went further. China’s one-child policy (introduced in 1979) and India’s forced sterilization campaigns (between 1975-1977) were heavy-handed responses fuelled by overpopulation panic. What makes those coercive policies all the more tragic, Spears notes, is that birth rates likely would have fallen without them.

“What we’re seeing now, a world of low and falling birth rates, is actually nothing new, but just the latest moment in a decades-long or longer trajectory of falling global birth rates on average happening essentially everywhere,” Sears says.

“I would say that the coercive [state-sanctioned] school [of thought] was counterproductive,” Cohen adds. He went on to say he believes similar government-led initiatives encouraging  families to have more children today, like the Trump administration’s proposed baby medal, are “counterproductive.” Real, meaningful generational changes in population, Cohen notes, are more often the product of society-level macro trends beyond the scope of any one politician or party.

Today, about a month shy of von Foerster’s doomsday, around 55 percent of the world’s countries, home to more than two-thirds of its people, have fertility rates below replacement level. That’s the rate (about 2.1 children per woman) demographers use to denote where a population replaces itself from one generation to the next.

In other words, not only is the human population not hurtling towards infinity, but in much of the world, it’s actually on track to eventually shrink. Experts largely agree birth rates are falling. Whether that’s a problem to be solved is less clear-cut.

At the family level, having fewer children can mean more money, resources, and personal attention invested in each one. It can also mean more independence, opportunity, and choice for those who decided parenthood just isn’t for them.

At the broader economic or societal level, though, Spears argues there are good reasons to want birth rates to stabilize rather than keep falling. Pay-as-you-go social programs like Social Security depend on young workers to fund older people’s retirements. Fewer people also means fewer shared resources and services for everyone else.

“If you want to go out and eat lunch at a taco truck, but you’re the only person nearby who wants it, then the taco truck’s not going to be there,” Spears says. “And if you’re a school child wanting to go to school, there might not be enough other children for there to be a classroom nearby that’s suitable for you.”

Those are legitimate concerns, though Cohen warns that focusing too much on abstract numbers and trends can blur a more immediate issue directly in front of us. Globally, children are still regularly going underfed, so much so that the World Health Organization estimates around 22 percent of children under the age of five experience stunted growth from malnutrition.

“We are doing so poorly by the children we have that I can’t understand why the emphasis is on making more children,” Cohen says. “We’re talking about having more children when we don’t take care of the children we have. What the heck is that?”

But even for those who do worry about falling birth rates, Spears cautions against politicians promising quick fixes. This isn’t a short-term emergency, he says, but a “generational challenge.” One thing is clear though: Neither Spears nor Cohen are losing sleep over von Foerster’s November 13 death date.

“I’m sure not acting as if I’m too concerned about it,” Cohen says. “I’m still paying my bills. I’m still paying my taxes.”

#4330. Solana’s biggest network change since 2020 is live. Here’s how users benefit

Solana switched on its Alpenglow upgrade Wednesday, introducing a new voting system designed to make payments and trades final in a fraction of a second.

The upgrade targets about 150 milliseconds before the network considers a transaction locked in, replacing the multi-second wait under its previous system. That point is called finality, when an application can treat the payment as settled.

Previously, they recorded votes as transactions on the blockchain and accumulated approvals across successive blocks.

Alpenglow lets them send votes directly to one another and combine those approvals into certificates, allowing a block to become final after one or two voting rounds.

The handover from the old system took about 900 milliseconds, according to the Solana Foundation. Votes from 446 of the network’s 654 participating validators certified the block used as the starting point — a timing that measures the switch between systems.

Many Solana apps already showed a transaction as confirmed before the network had finished locking it in, which made payments feel fast while leaving a further wait for full settlement.

Alpenglow actually makes those transactions faster, giving apps a way to show users their money has arrived with stronger assurance that the payment will hold. Such a benefit is especially useful when a service needs to release funds or goods immediately after receiving payment.

An exchange could credit a deposit sooner, letting a customer trade with the funds. A shop accepting a stablecoin payment could confirm it without keeping the customer at the counter waiting for settlement.

How quickly that reaches a user’s screen also depends on the service handling the payment. Anza, the company behind Solana’s Agave software, told CoinDesk in a release that some computers receive finality information later than the validators voting directly.

The 150-millisecond figure remains a target under normal network conditions, rather than a guaranteed time for every payment.

Users can continue sending funds through their existing wallets. Transaction fees and the way applications run remain unchanged.

The speed comes from changing how validators, the computers checking transactions and agreeing on the network’s records, communicate.

Meanwhile, there is also a change readers may notice on network dashboards. Validator votes no longer appear as blockchain transactions, so services that counted them will report lower transaction totals.

A chart could therefore show a sharp drop even while users continue making just as many payments and trades. Comparisons with earlier activity need to count user transactions separately from the votes that previously filled the blocks.

#4326. Monarch butterfly saved after landing in refried beans

Published Oct 13, 2026 11:06 AM EDT

A migrating monarch butterfly (Danaus plexippus) recently brought into Sweetbriar Nature Center in Smithtown on New York’s Long Island came with a unique backstory. The male butterfly flew into a nearby house and landed on a pot of refried beans. The man living there took the butterfly out of the pan, but by morning the butterfly still could not fly. There was only one thing left to do for the injured insect—bring him to the professionals.

“Butterflies are incredibly delicate, and even handling them can cause additional damage or leave them unable to fly,” Janine Bendicksen, Sweetbriar Nature Center’s Director of Wildlife Rehabilitation, tells Popular Science. “That makes butterfly rehabilitation especially challenging—we have to be extremely careful with every step.”

The team got to work on fixing this unusual patient. They started by checking the butterfly for a parasite called OE (Ophryocystis elektroscirrha) that can live on monarchs. If the insect is parasite-free, the team can then assess the injuries and determine whether rehabilitation is possible. Butterflies are very delicate, and just handling them can cause additional damage.

“In cases where a monarch has lost part of a wing, we may be able to transplant a portion of a donor wing,” says Bendicksen. “When the circumstances are right, we can carefully attach the donor wing to the injured butterfly.”

The team gently sprayed the butterfly’s wings with water several times to remove the bean residue that prevented him from flying. Then, a challenge arose. How do you remove the greasy residue from such delicate wings? The solution came from a common powdery substance found in kitchen pantries—cornstarch.

“We use starch regularly in wildlife rehabilitation to help dry out materials, and we had also previously used starch when replacing a wing on another monarch,” Bendicksen explains. “Because we already knew it was safe and effective in that context, we felt it was a good option for this particular situation. We were looking for something that would help remove the remaining moisture and residue without causing additional damage to the delicate wings.”

The cornstarch trick worked. The monarch was able to fly again. The team gently transported him outside and he took to the skies. Monarchs are currently on their way to the overwintering grounds in the forested mountains of Central Mexico. Their migrations are multigenerational, with roughly four generations of monarch butterflies emerging throughout the year. This butterfly may have been in the third generation (lays eggs in the north, but does not migrate) of the fourth generation that treks south.

“It’s not often that we are called upon to rehabilitate a butterfly,” Bendicksen says. “Most butterflies have relatively short lifespans, but when we receive a monarch in the fall—especially one that will travel thousands of miles on its migration to Mexico—we do everything we can to give it the best possible chance.”

#4319. The UK needs a workable route to regulated DeFi access

The UK’s 2027 crypto rules risk a de facto ban on regulated firms providing access to DeFi, argues Coinbase’s European policy head Katie Harries.

Opinion by Katie Harries, head of policy, Europe, Coinbase

The United Kingdom is approaching a choice that could define its financial future: should decentralized finance (DeFi) be allowed to develop through regulated gateways or will it be constrained to a silo?

This is not just relevant to crypto enthusiasts; it goes to the future of capital markets, the competitiveness of the City and the UK’s ability to shape the next generation of financial infrastructure.

DeFi is software that lets people borrow, lend and trade directly with one another without relying on an intermediary like a bank or broker. It works less like a financial institution and more like the internet, promising cheaper, faster financial services that are accessible to everyone, anytime.

While DeFi will always be accessible through open-source or decentralized routes, regulated providers can also offer a route to DeFi, allowing customers to engage with the technology while receiving key information about the services they are accessing and the risks they should be aware of.

However, the Financial Conduct Authority’s (FCA) proposed crypto rules could make this impossible. The FCA will expect regulated platforms to ensure customer orders are only executed on an FCA-authorized venue. While this is designed to prevent overseas firms from illegally accessing the UK market, it also risks blocking off access to DeFi because the decentralized protocols and exchanges where users execute orders do not have a person behind them who could become authorized.

Overview of FCA’s cryptoassets regime policy statements. Source: FCA

No rule explicitly says that firms cannot offer DeFi integrations to their customers; however, the FCA has made clear that it is not seeking to provide rules specific to these products, opting instead to apply the same rules as to fully centralized trading. The effect could be a de facto ban.

From October 2027, regulated platforms may no longer be able to provide customers access to DeFi services unless the FCA changes its approach.

The government has pledged it wants the UK to “lead the world on digital assets.” It will struggle to realize this ambition if British firms are cut off from the decentralized infrastructure digital asset markets are built on.

Related: UK FCA sets crypto authorization guidance ahead of September application window

Regulators in other jurisdictions recognize the opportunities and are exploring ways to enable DeFi innovation. In the European Union, the European Securities and Markets Authority has proposed allowing regulated firms to provide access to DeFi, saying it could “allow innovation in decentralized finance in a regulated and transparent environment.”

In September, the United States Securities and Exchange Commission granted an “Innovation Exemption” for the trading of tokenized stocks peer-to-peer, using automated trading systems and liquidity pools.

In short, the US and EU are exploring how traditional finance and DeFi infrastructure can work together. The UK should be exploring the same potential before it closes off this option completely.

Onchain tokenized finance cannot reach its full potential if it is confined to closed and fragmented systems that struggle to interact with the decentralized technology that underpins them.

The UK has set out its first full rulebook for crypto regulation. From Oct. 25, 2027, firms offering crypto services to UK customers will need authorization from the FCA. The FCA is now consulting on guidance on how its rules will apply to firms that provide financial services that use or interact with DeFi.

Potential concerns around DeFi integrations are threefold. First, a customer using a familiar regulated platform may not realize when they have moved into an environment where the usual protections do not apply. Second, certain firms could use DeFi integrations to avoid regulatory obligations. Third, DeFi could bring risks related to financial crime and market resilience.

The UK wants to lead the world on digital assets. Source: Gov.uk

But these risks point to the need for clear information for consumers and responsibilities for firms — not a prohibition.

A de facto ban would produce the opposite of its intended effect. It would not eliminate DeFi. Instead, it would push more activity beyond the FCA’s practical visibility and reduce regulated firms’ ability to shape how customers interact with it.

Regulated platforms can provide a useful point for supervision. They can make clear to customers when they are leaving a regulated environment and require their consent before allowing this. Regulated firms can also curate access to selected protocols, assess operational and governance risks, and report incidents and usage to regulators.

The UK already recognizes, in other areas of finance, that regulated institutions can provide access to products or markets that do not carry the same level of oversight.

Many retail trading platforms or so-called “super apps” now offer consumers access to a range of products, from stocks and money market funds to commodities and foreign exchange, with different regulatory protections. The FCA permits this, as long as platforms make clear to consumers which protections apply to different products and services.

This is why DeFi integrations require a better solution. The FCA should protect consumers, but it should not seek to achieve protection with total exclusion.

Responsible integration would give consumers clearer information, give supervisors greater visibility and give UK financial services the chance to compete in the next phase of financial innovation.

Related: ESMA seeks evidence tokenized collateral can be cashed out in crisis

DeFi protocols can already be accessed directly without the use of regulated firms. The question is whether consumers will also be able to access DeFi through a regulated gateway, with clear disclosures and guardrails.

The stakes are broader than crypto trading. DeFi underpins the greatest potential cost savings as financial services move onchain, with fewer costly intermediaries and systems that support 24/7 activity across borders. Regulation that provides a clear route to support DeFi is crucial to give firms the confidence to invest in UK innovation in the long term.

If barriers to access are imposed in the UK, tokenized capital markets and financial innovation more broadly could develop in countries that are decisive in supporting DeFi infrastructure.

The UK cannot credibly aspire to lead in digital finance while closing off the decentralized infrastructure on which the future of finance is being built.

NASA’s Perseverance Mars rover has officially traveled 28.10 miles—farther across another planet’s surface than any other vehicle in history. Not only that, but the six-wheeled science lab accomplished this impressive feat almost entirely autonomously and in barely a third the time it took the previous recordholder.

“The original mission called for no more than 12.4 miles of driving. We’ve blown well past that,” Steven Lee, Perseverance and Curiosity project manager at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory, said in a statement.

Perseverance began operations on Mars on February 18, 2021, after landing in an ancient river delta selected for potential past microbial life called Jezero Crater. Since then, the rover has slowly but steadily made its way across the region, while conducting multiple sediment drilling surveys. These samples are then chemically analyzed using onboard laboratory equipment before the results are sent back to mission specialists at NASA.

Perseverance surpassed the previous travel record of 28.06 miles set by NASA’s Opportunity rover, before its communications with NASA ended on June 10, 2018. However, it took Opportunity nearly 15 years to accrue its distance while Perseverance exceeded the same mileage in less than six years, and on the 2,006th Martian day (sol) of its mission. Perseverance is currently traveling towards an area named Idubi through an expanse nicknamed “Lac de Charmes.” The region near Jezero Crater’s western rim hosts what may be the oldest landscape ever investigated in the solar system. Mars does not feature tectonic plates like Earth, which means its terrain is never eroded and subsumed into the planet’s substrates.

Mission engineers have not spent the last six years remotely steering Perseverance, either. The rover arrived on Mars equipped with a self-piloting system called AutoNav, which constructs 3D maps of the immediate surroundings to flag any potential hazards. AutoNav then charts a route between specific waypoints chosen by NASA specialists back home. Perseverance has completed 90 percent of its total excursion with AutoNav. Opportunity and its sibling rover, Spirit, only relied on a first version of the piloting system about five percent of the time.

Perseverance has a long road ahead of it, if all goes according to plan. In October, NASA approved the first mission extension that will continue the rover’s operations through about the end of 2028.

“With all mobility systems fully operational and favorable extended-ground-life test results, we expect Perseverance to keep adding to this record for many years to come,” added Lee.

#4309. Bitcoin ETF buyers are trend followers, not setters, DWF Labs says

DWF Labs research finds that Bitcoin ETF flows follow past BTC price movements rather than predict future returns, with investors buying rallies and selling dips.

US spot Bitcoin exchange-traded fund (ETF) daily flows aren’t reliable guides to BTC’s next move as investors tend to chase rallies and sell into slumps, according to DWF Labs.

In a research report published Thursday, DWF found that investors pulled money from the funds during seven of the eight times Bitcoin’s price dropped at least 15%. All nine rallies of that size drew net inflows. The analysis reviewed 694 trading days dating back to January 2024, when Bitcoin ETFs first launched in the US.

“ETF flows confirm moves; they don’t predict them,” DWF said in its report. “The evidence points mostly to investors reacting to price, not driving it.”

Investors tended to put money into Bitcoin ETFs after prices rose and pull it out after prices fell. Those flows offered almost no clue to whether Bitcoin would rise or fall over the next five days.

Each day’s ETF flows had a moderate relationship with Bitcoin’s price changes over the five trading sessions before that day, with a correlation of 0.54. On a scale where zero means no relationship, the correlation for the following five days was 0.03.

ETF outflows in seven of eight Bitcoin sell-offs. Source: DWF Labs

The heaviest withdrawals came during the two most recent sell-offs. Investors withdrew a net $6.9 billion between October 2025 and February 2026, followed by $8.2 billion during the May–June decline.

The figures do not show whether institutions or retail investors drove the selling. DWF did not separate the two groups in its analysis.

“We don’t have a clear split on the exact buyers/sellers of the ETFs,” a DWF Labs spokesperson told Cointelegraph.

#4308. How to watch Churchill’s famous polar bears live

Published Oct 13, 2026 2:01 PM EDT

The internet’s favorite Fat Bears in Alaska are not appearing on wildlife cameras quite as much these days. The salmon has been eaten, the Fat Bear Week crown has been won, and it’s time to survive the winter ahead. However,  some other bears from the great white north are getting their close-up.

Explore.org and Polar Bears International run eight different livestreams from Churchill, Manitoba, Canada. Known as the polar bear capital of the world, Churchill’s Wapusk National Park is home to one of the world’s largest concentrations of polar bear (Ursus maritimus) maternity dens. During October and November, viewers can watch bear families wait for sea ice to form.

Twenty populations of polar bears live in the Arctic. The population living along the Western Hudson Bay is one of the most comprehensively studied and best known. It is part of Canada’s “Seasonal Ice Ecoregion.” In this area of the Arctic, the sea ice completely melts in the summer, typically in July. While the bears may remain sporadically active on land, in the summer, they generally create day beds or curl up in the bush to rest. The pregnant females will often settle in or near their dens for several months to rest where it is cooler and with less mosquitoes. Many of these dens were first excavated by bears generations ago.

In the fall, all of the bears except the pregnant female will increase their activity and move closer to the coast. Here, the bears will hunt for seals once the bay freezes over again. From early October onward, they will gather on the tundra along the shores of Hudson Bay, waiting for ice to form. As soon as the first coastal ice is thick enough to support their weight, they will head out to find seals. They may continue to trek back and forth between ice and land at first, but they primarily head out onto the bay until it begins to thin and they ashore again in the summer.

These cameras are one way for scientists and the public to aid in real scientific research. In December 2025, scientists here confirmed that a wild female polar bear adopted a cub that was not her own. This rare behavior was captured on cameras during the polar bear’s annual migration along Western Hudson Bay.

The mother bear (designated as bear X33991) was first spotted in spring 2025, when she came out of her maternity den with one tagged cub. When scientists saw her again in the fall, she had two cubs. One cub had a tag and one did not, indicating that the untagged cub was not hers. The survival rate for a polar bear cub into adulthood is about 50 percent, so only time will tell how this blended family will do.

Viewers can also take a virtual ride on the Tundra Buggy Lodge from two different angles. The lodge is made of tundra buggies that are linked together like a train. This moving hotel includes staff quarters, a dining area, a supply cart, and two carts for guests.

By night, viewers can also take in another of the north’s natural wonders with the aurora borealis cam. The northern lights livecam is on top of the Churchill Northern Studies Center and located directly underneath the aurora oval. This spot is considered one of the best places to watch the aurora dance across the night sky. The aurora season is only just beginning, with late winter and early spring being the best times to tune into this camera.

And if birds are more your thing, a raven cam is streaming from Cape Churchill Wapusk National Park. The resident ravens selected the topside of a radar reflector attached to the Cape Churchill observation tower for their nest. This “cabin in the sky” has also been home to numerous biologists researching the local wildlife since the 1970s. This camera will be switched off at night to preserve power.

The organizations also provide highlight reels to help viewers catch up on what to watch for this winter. Happy polar bear season!

#4301. Use Crossword Generator to create challenging custom puzzles in 27 languages

Published Oct 13, 2026 3:09 PM EDT

Crossword puzzles range from those easy elementary school-level varieties up to nightmarish labyrinths of text. But crafting even the most simple versions remains its own artform. Seamlessly combining dozens of answers into a coherent, functional grid often requires years of practice, but digital strategist and creator Tex Jernigan has designed an impressive tool to level the playing field, so to speak: Crossword Generator.

“Making my own puzzles is something I wanted to do for my neighborhood coffee shop, and [I] started working on it many years ago,” Jernigan, who also recently debuted a vintage Blockbuster VHS sleeve maker, tells Popular Science. “I thought I would be able to have a huge list of theme words, but it can be quite difficult to get more than a small handful of theme words to work.”

To use Crossword Generator, creators first pick a grid size, then choose either a pattern with pregenerated or manually entered black squares. Next, creators have the option to fill the grid with a few specific words, or simply let the program create an entire puzzle itself. Either way, Crossword Generator employs a constraint solver to scour a database of hundreds of thousands of words, then starts plugging in potential options on a trial-and-error basis. Preference is given to more common, everyday words instead of particularly obtuse selections. Any words entered by a user remain locked in during the generation process, and the prom works around those as it fills in the blanks. Crossword Generator starts in English, but supports 27 other languages including Spanish, Russian, and Japanese.

While Jernigan’s constraint solver doesn’t rely on artificial intelligence, another generative program does use Google Gemini to crossmatch every word with a dictionary-based clue. That said, anyone can simply erase those and create their own hints to ensure their customized crosswords are as human-centric as possible.

“In my experience, AI isn’t very good at building a crossword from scratch, because it can’t reliably make every word cross,” Jernigan explained on his website. “It works much better as a partner to the solver.”

Crossword Generator is free for creating and printing up to 13×13 puzzles, but a subscription service opens up New York Times-style grids ranging from the 15×15 through 23×23 Sunday editions. But if solving crosswords is more your thing than creating them, you can start working on Jernigan’s exclusive Popular Science-themed puzzle.
