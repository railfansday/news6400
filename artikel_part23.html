#5300. Ukraine Can Turn Imminent Peril to Its Advantage

ERIC CIARAMELLA is a Senior Fellow at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace and previously served at the National Security Council, National Intelligence Council, and Central Intelligence Agency.

How a True “Energy Cease-Fire” Can Curb Putin

Ukraine is facing its most precarious moment since the early days of the war. Since late July, Russia has used its growing stockpile of ballistic missiles and a new generation of jet-powered drones to dramatically intensify its long-range attacks. It is now targeting Ukrainian population centers all over the country, with a focus on logistics, transportation, and commercial infrastructure. According to Ukrainian intelligence reports, the Russian government is planning an even more intense campaign against energy and water supplies this winter in an attempt to decisively break Ukraine’s economy and society.

On October 9, U.S. President Donald Trump seemed to make Ukraine’s situation even more perilous when he announced a deal with Moscow to unilaterally lift sanctions on Russian diesel and called on Ukraine to suspend strikes on Russian oil refineries. Those strikes have been one of Ukraine’s most potent forms of counterpressure in recent months. But Trump’s unexpected announcement is not necessarily a major setback for Ukraine. It comes amid a broader shift by the Trump administration in recent weeks to move away from pursuing an elusive comprehensive peace agreement in favor of a mutual cease-fire on strikes against energy infrastructure—a shift that could ultimately be beneficial to Kyiv. Moreover, in his angry retort to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky’s criticism of the diesel deal, Trump revealed a potential way forward: as long as Ukraine stopped hitting Russian refineries, the president said, “you can do whatever you want to Russia.”

Ukraine should take Trump at his word. If upheld by Moscow as well as Kyiv, an energy cease-fire would be a crucial step toward containing the war. Framed in the right way, it would also leave open other forms of pressure by Ukraine that could help compel Russian compliance. Ideally, a reciprocal halt in long-range air and missile attacks could then be replicated across other sectors—maritime, commercial, and transportation—thus forming the basis of a broader aerial cease-fire.

Thus far, Russian President Vladimir Putin has given no indication that he is prepared to halt or even curtail his strike campaign. He sees the widespread destruction of Ukrainian cities and infrastructure as his most direct path to victory. But Putin is also under pressure from the widening war. In part, his accelerating air campaign reflects weakness on the frontlines, where Russia has achieved negligible progress throughout 2026 and Ukraine has even begun to make some gains in the Donbas. Russia also faces growing costs at home from Ukraine’s long-range strikes, which have disrupted energy and commercial infrastructure and heightened public anxiety over a prolonged war.

The United States and Europe can capitalize on Russia’s mounting challenges if they allow Ukraine to apply targeted pressure to convince Moscow to agree to pause or significantly limit its reckless air campaign. To be effective, this approach needs to be combined with focused diplomacy aimed at that specific objective. In fact, there are tools available for this purpose, and the barriers to putting them quickly into play are far more surmountable than has been commonly recognized.

The key will be credibly signaling that critical Russian military and economic assets will be at risk if Russian forces continue pummeling Ukraine’s civilian economy and society. Only then might Putin conclude that his current approach has reached the point of diminishing returns—and then pivot, as he has done at other junctures in the war. At the same time, this diplomatic play must be hitched to a broader effort by Kyiv’s Western partners to help it urgently shore up its air defenses and finances ahead of winter. Such a strategy may not immediately succeed, but failing to try would all but ensure that Ukraine suffers an extraordinary degree of damage that will be much harder to reverse.

The Trump administration’s repeated failure to advance its stated goal of brokering peace between Russia and Ukraine has led many observers to write off diplomacy as a waste of time. Over the past 21 months, the Trump administration has gotten nowhere in its attempts to mediate a comprehensive peace settlement. Russia’s war aims and Ukraine’s requirements—on such thorny issues as territory, Ukrainian security arrangements, and Russia’s future relations with the West—are simply too far apart. Washington also missed opportunities to build meaningful leverage over Russia, instead lavishly courting it with potential commercial deals and other incentives while pressuring Kyiv to accept Moscow’s conditions.

Yet that experience has helped drive the administration’s current efforts to achieve more plausible and narrowly defined goals. Following their visits to Moscow and Kyiv in early September, Trump’s envoys, Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, pivoted toward a more limited energy cease-fire. Since then, the president has also reiterated his support for such a deal. Trump’s interest in an energy cease-fire is largely self-serving—he has attributed spikes in fuel prices to Ukraine’s strikes on Russian oil refineries—but this shift could serve Kyiv well, especially if it becomes the basis for a coordinated drive to secure broader reciprocal limits on air and missile attacks.

The core challenge is convincing Russia to embrace such an agreement. To date, Putin has rejected calls for restrictions on aerial strikes of any sort. He views the damage he is inflicting on Ukraine as outstripping the pain Russia is sustaining from Kyiv’s attacks. But the United States and European countries have several ways to change this calculus. Crucially, the United States and Europe must enable Ukraine to strike missile launchers and other military targets inside Russia—or at least allow it to credibly threaten to do so. Thus far, Ukraine has largely focused its long-range attacks on energy infrastructure, because targeting Russia’s hardened military installations and mobile launch systems is technically much more complicated. But it is exactly these harder-to-replace assets that Putin is most concerned about losing.

In fact, it would not be difficult for the United States to facilitate strikes on military assets. For months, Ukraine has pressed the Trump administration and the SpaceX CEO Elon Musk to enable Starlink access over a swath of Russian territory opposite the Ukrainian border from which most ballistic missiles are launched. Kyiv needs such access to allow its drone teams to target mobile launchers. As soon as Ukraine acquires this ability, Russia would likely be compelled to pull the rest of these systems farther away from the border, thereby considerably reducing the ballistic threat to Ukraine. Thus far, Trump and Musk have both deflected Ukraine’s request—each one claiming the decision rests with the other. But neither has foreclosed greenlighting such access.

Kyiv’s case for acquiring Starlink access over Russia might be more persuasive if Ukrainian officials focused on how it could be deployed as negotiating leverage, as part of a wider U.S.-led diplomatic strategy to secure an aerial cease-fire—especially if it were an element of an agreement with Washington to refrain from striking Russian oil refineries. Specifically, Trump could authorize access without public fanfare while signaling to Moscow that he is doing so only until an aerial cease-fire is in place. There is reason to think such a tactic would succeed. Earlier this year, Musk worked with Ukraine to create a registration system that effectively limited Starlink use to Ukrainian drone operators, a step that for a short period sent Russian forces into operational disarray along the front. Washington missed a chance to exploit that moment of Russian weakness to push Moscow toward a tactical de-escalation, however, because U.S. attention shifted just weeks later to the war against Iran.

The German government has a similar card to play. Kyiv has long sought to get Germany’s Taurus air-launched cruise missile, which would allow it to target fixed sites hundreds of miles into Russia, such as defense plants that produce jet-powered drones and ballistic missiles. Thus far, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz has resisted sending the Taurus system to Ukraine, arguing that it would be escalatory and would require a level of on-site technical assistance that would make German forces party to the conflict. But Merz might be persuaded to relent—or at least to authorize Ukrainian troops to start training on the system—if he believed that doing so would create momentum toward an aerial cease-fire, ideally in lockstep with a move from Trump on Starlink. In turn, other European partners could also contribute to such a plan by leaning in more with intelligence and technical expertise to enable Ukraine to target launchers inside Russia.

Any such leveraged diplomacy, of course, will depend on Ukraine’s partners showing that they remain committed to addressing Ukraine’s immediate war needs and that Russia will not succeed in its stepped-up campaign to break the country this winter. And this means having a coherent immediate strategy for shoring up both Kyiv’s air defenses and its economy, irrespective of U.S. involvement or support.

Above all is the question of Patriot interceptors. For Ukraine, the Patriot’s PAC-3 variant is by far the most effective protection against Russian ballistic missile attacks. Yet Kyiv’s stockpile has dried up as the United States husbands an arsenal that has been significantly depleted by the Iran war. Kyiv’s European partners have been scouring the world for Patriot missiles but have so far been unable to meet Ukraine’s modest request of 300 interceptors to get through the winter. Countries with robust Patriot stocks, such as Greece, Japan, South Korea, and Spain, have been reluctant to transfer them to Ukraine for a variety of security and legal reasons.

Here, Europe should take a page from an earlier U.S. playbook. During the Biden administration, U.S. officials cajoled recalcitrant partners and engineered creative multiparty swaps and backfill agreements—many of them subsidized by the United States—to free up critical munitions for Ukraine. For example, the United States bought 155-millimeter artillery shells from South Korea and PAC-3 interceptors from Japan to backfill American munitions for Ukraine. This time around, Ukraine can finance the purchases itself through the loan package it is receiving from the EU.

On the economic front, Ukraine’s most acute needs could be met if the EU agreed to frontload the disbursement of the tranches of its 90 billion euro loan that are scheduled for next year. EU leaders have made clear that Kyiv must first meet Brussels’ reform conditions to secure the tranches of the loan already promised this year. But the EU will also most likely need to find additional resources because the loan itself, expected to last through 2027, will fall short of meeting Ukraine’s needs.

In August, the Netherlands, Poland, Spain, and Sweden again proposed the option of tapping into Russia’s frozen sovereign assets, an estimated $240 billion of which reside in the EU, mostly in the Belgium-based financial services company Euroclear. The legal and political obstacles to taking custody of those assets for onward transfer to Ukraine may be too formidable to resolve in time to help Kyiv in the coming year. But Ukraine’s partners have come up with creative solutions in the past. In 2024, the G-7 designed a $50 billion loan to be serviced by the future windfall profits generated by the frozen Russian assets. The EU’s 90 billion euro loan is another creative example: it is financed from EU borrowing on capital markets, to be repaid by Ukraine only if Russia pays reparations; otherwise, the EU has reserved the right to reimburse itself with the frozen Russian assets. The EU and likeminded partners—notably Canada, Japan, and the United Kingdom—can revisit and expand both mechanisms to generate additional funding for Ukraine while steering clear of political poison pills.

Ultimately, each of these steps needs to be coordinated to have maximum influence on Putin’s thinking. The fact that the Trump administration invited top European officials to join last week’s talks with Ukrainian negotiators in Miami is a sign that such transatlantic coordination is possible, even if Washington’s ties with Europe are otherwise strained. On the other hand, reports that the Trump administration has threatened to cut off intelligence support and supplies of air defense missiles and other critical munitions for Ukraine underscore the reality that Washington is at best a capricious interlocutor. In any case, European leaders who have constructive working relations with the White House, such as Finnish President Alexander Stubb, should continue arguing that granting Starlink access, among other steps to pressure Moscow, would advance Trump’s stated aim of de-escalating the conflict.

Putin is committed to his overall war aims, and no amount of external pressure is likely to force him to abandon those. But he is an opportunist and has shown himself adept at making tactical adjustments in pursuit of his broader ambitions. In early 2022, he gave up his ground attack on Kyiv when it became evident that it would fail. Later, he pulled the Black Sea Fleet away from Ukraine’s shores after Kyiv proved it could sink valuable Russian naval assets; in 2022, he also entered the Black Sea Grain Initiative, which for nearly a year allowed the safe passage of grain shipments from Ukrainian ports and was brokered by the United Nations and Turkey, under pressure from African nations suffering from grain shortages.

For Putin, these were not goodwill gestures but measures of self-preservation designed to advance his broader aim of subjugating Ukraine. With the right kind of leverage applied by the United States and Europe, there is no reason to assume he would rule out a similar pullback from attacks on Ukrainian civilian populations and economic infrastructure. He would be even more inclined to view an aerial cease-fire as advantageous if he saw an expansion of Ukraine’s ability to upend life in Russia’s major population centers—a goal Kyiv no doubt is intent on pursuing, as shown by its effective recent strikes on Russian data centers.

Of course, there is the possibility that, even faced with these new costs, Putin will still refuse to budge. But in that scenario, enabling Ukraine to target Russian missile launchers and defense production sites—combined with continued economic pressure—will be even more essential to helping Ukraine counter Russian escalation. In addition to relieving immediate pressure on Ukrainian cities by making it harder for Russia to launch strikes, it will also undercut Russia’s military capacity. That would be beneficial even if it falls short of containing the air war.

Paradoxically, the adaptability that has enabled many of Putin’s successes also gives Kyiv and the West more space to maneuver than they realize. Trump, Zelensky, and European leaders need to organize their actions in a way that relieves pressure on the Ukrainian economy while showing growing willingness to exploit critical Russian vulnerabilities. Only then might Putin consider that he is better off making a tactical adjustment—in this case, agreeing to an aerial cease-fire—than maintaining the present course. He would do so not to concede but to find a more effective way to achieve his broader aims. In that adjustment period, Kyiv and its partners will gain urgently needed breathing space to regroup, fortify, and prepare for the next round.

Last week, NATO began its annual Steadfast Noon exercise to ensure the alliance’s nuclear readiness, even as the Trump administration pursues a policy that threatens to undermine it.

Launched in February, NATO 3.0 is the brainchild of Elbridge Colby, the undersecretary of defense for policy. At its core is the assumption that the United States can rapidly wind down its conventional commitments to Europe while retaining its leading role in the alliance, so long as Europe continues to rely on the U.S. nuclear guarantee. Colby presents this shift as a rebalancing of the alliance, enticing European states to take responsibility for the continent’s conventional defense as Washington turns its attention to the Indo-Pacific.

The approach is Washington’s attempt to have it both ways—to cut back without sacrificing its influence or affecting extended nuclear deterrence. In reality, however, it would compromise Europe’s defense, diminish Washington’s sway, weaken the U.S. nuclear guarantee, and generate proliferation risks—ultimately undercutting the policy’s objectives and U.S. national interests.

Colby’s vision is already reshaping the U.S. footprint in Europe. In May, the Department of Defense announced plans to withdraw roughly 5,000 troops from Germany and cancel the deployment of a long-range strike battalion there. The U.S. rotational brigade in Romania returned home without replacement last October, and reports suggest another 25,000 to 40,000 troops could leave Europe.

Washington has also proposed cutting its contribution to the NATO Force Model—the pool of forces made available for NATO operations—by one-third to one-half. The United States is reportedly planning to significantly reduce the aircraft and warships available in a crisis, including an aircraft carrier, reconnaissance aircraft, dozens of F-16 and F-15E jets, and potentially half of the current U.S. armed MQ-9 drone supply currently in Europe.

As conventional forces recede, nuclear forces are expected to play a larger role. Colby has pledged Washington “will continue to provide the U.S. extended nuclear deterrent,” and there are reports that the United States is considering expanding the basing of dual-capable aircraft and the deployment of nuclear warheads beyond the alliance’s current six host countries, potentially to Poland and the Baltic states. According to two anonymous sources cited by the Financial Times, the additional nuclear presence is meant to demonstrate the resilience of the U.S. nuclear umbrella despite the conventional drawdown.

Yet convincing an adversary that the U.S. president would risk New York to save Riga has always been a tall order. Historically, Washington has bolstered the credibility of that commitment by ensuring that a conventional attack on NATO territory could trigger unpredictable nuclear escalation. U.S. troops in Europe thus serve as a trip wire: A president might hesitate to order a nuclear strike to defend Germany or Poland but calculate differently if U.S. soldiers were threatened. During the Cold War, stationing U.S. forces on European soil was a deliberate tactic to couple European defense with the protection of Americans.

Colby’s vision, by contrast, treats conventional and nuclear forces as separable domains, assigning responsibility for the former to Europe and the latter to the United States. Such a tidy division may make sense on paper, but it is unrealistic in practice. A hasty U.S. conventional withdrawal would weaken Europe’s ability to defend itself below the nuclear threshold while simultaneously eroding the credibility of the U.S. nuclear guarantee. It is a dangerous, double-edged experiment that undercuts both pillars of deterrence at once.

Partitioning conventional and nuclear responsibilities also creates new challenges for escalation management. For instance, Russian military doctrine reflects concerns about NATO using conventional weapons to conduct counterforce strikes against Russia’s nuclear arsenal—a scenario in which the two domains overlap. Assigning conventional responsibility to Europe and nuclear responsibility to the United States does not produce a clean split. Conventional strikes may hit strategic targets, and Moscow’s threshold for a nuclear response cannot be known in advance. NATO 3.0 would bifurcate the escalation ladder, fragmenting command, complicating allied operations, and giving Moscow new openings to exploit divisions within the alliance.

This problem is compounded by Europe’s dependence on the United States for conventional long-range strike capabilities. The canceled deployments of Tomahawk and Dark Eagle hypersonic missiles underscore the extent of this gap. European alternatives have encountered setbacks; the Kiel Institute estimates, optimistically, that Europe will need at least three to five years to field such capabilities, assuming the necessary funding materializes. Until then, Europe will not be able to hold its own in a high-end conventional conflict with Russia.

Moreover, the notion that the United States would enter a crisis only at the brink of nuclear escalation defies logic. NATO 3.0 effectively hands the president a ready-made rationale for withholding conventional support (“That’s Europe’s job.”) while requiring an affirmative decision to threaten nuclear escalation. It is inconceivable that Washington would avoid the risk of a conventional war on behalf of Europe but somehow accept the far greater risks of a nuclear war. Without meaningful U.S. forces’ involvement in conventional operations and a central role in managing escalation, credible nuclear signaling from Washington is highly dubious. After nearly two years of intense hostility toward European allies, the clearest signal coming from Washington is that Europe is now on its own.

By insisting that the U.S. nuclear umbrella can continue to protect Europe, NATO 3.0 also attempts to push Europe toward greater burden-sharing while keeping a lid on European nuclear proliferation. In March, Colby said the United States would “strenuously oppose” an independent European nuclear program. But if NATO 3.0 deepens allies’ sense of vulnerability, it could backfire and produce precisely the result Colby hopes to avoid.

NATO’s three nuclear powers—the United States, the United Kingdom, and France—all retain independent control over their arsenals and nuclear decision-making. But while Washington and London coordinate through the NATO nuclear planning structure, Paris does not. NATO has historically viewed these separate centers of decision-making as an asset that complicates Russian calculations. In reality, though, the U.S. arsenal remains the backbone of the alliance’s nuclear deterrent, giving Washington immense influence over NATO strategy, planning, and operations. As the U.K. and France strengthen their cooperation on a more explicitly European approach to extended deterrence, the United States risks losing the influence conferred by its long-standing role as the alliance’s ultimate guarantor.

The prospect of extending U.K. and French nuclear protection to the rest of Europe is already gaining traction as doubts about Washington’s reliability grow. In July 2025, the United Kingdom and France committed to independent but harmonizable nuclear forces and established a joint nuclear steering group. In March, French President Emmanuel Macron announced that France would expand its warhead numbers and adopt a forward deterrence framework to deepen European nuclear security cooperation. France and Germany have also set up their own nuclear steering group, and 10 European states are now holding structured bilateral discussions with Paris on nuclear consultation and potential hosting agreements.

For now, these efforts primarily serve as an insurance policy against the possibility of U.S. abandonment. But there is also a danger, albeit still far off, that other NATO states begin to consider acquiring nuclear weapons of their own. Poland is already debating exactly this question. In an address to parliament in March 2025, Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk suggested the country “pursue the most advanced capabilities, including nuclear and modern unconventional weapons.” He has also brought Warsaw into France’s forward deterrence initiative. Polish President Karol Nawrocki, self-described as “a great supporter of Poland joining the nuclear project,” has argued that the country’s security strategy should rest on developing its own nuclear potential.

New European nuclear states remain a distant prospect, but if NATO 3.0 is intended to reassure allies while discouraging proliferation, the evidence suggests it is having the opposite effect.

NATO 3.0’s underlying intuition—that the United States should encourage greater European burden-sharing without encouraging nuclear proliferation—is sound. The problem is that these objectives cannot be achieved together in such a short window. There are better ways to balance burden-sharing, European security, and Washington’s position in the alliance, and if the Trump administration presses ahead with NATO 3.0, several steps could mitigate the risks.

First, Washington should adopt a formal no-surprises policy of the kind that Dutch officials have proposed, under which allies would receive advance notice of U.S. redeployments and have a voice on potential troop withdrawals, if not a veto. Washington should then establish a phased, multiyear drawdown schedule in consultation with allies, linking reductions to specific European capability benchmarks and joint assessments of the threat from Russia. Washington should also commit to maintaining its support of capabilities that most deter Moscow—specifically for intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance; air defense; long-range precision strike; and logistics—until European forces can provide equivalent capabilities, which may be years away.

Congressional consultation is equally important. Support for NATO remains strong on Capitol Hill, and any new trans-Atlantic security bargain will be more durable if it enjoys bipartisan backing. Further, as the Trump administration’s experience has shown, making unilateral cuts risks congressional blowback: The 2026 National Defense Authorization Act restricts the use of funds to reduce U.S. troop levels in Europe below 76,000 for more than 45 days without NATO consultation. A methodical approach that includes both allies and Congress would also help avoid embarrassing moments of policymaking on the fly, such as when Washington withdrew forces from Poland only for Trump to reverse the decision, all at a cost to the American taxpayer.

To address operational challenges, NATO should establish a standing body within its Nuclear Planning Group dedicated to escalation management and other issues created by the proposed division between conventional and nuclear responsibilities. The Defense Department’s six-month review of U.S. force posture in Europe, which is tasked with ensuring that “Europe [is] taking primary responsibility for its conventional defense,” should explicitly assess the implications for conventional-nuclear coordination and extended deterrence.

Washington should also seek greater nuclear coordination within and beyond NATO. One option would be to establish a mechanism linking the NATO Nuclear Planning Group with the French-led bilateral nuclear steering groups. Despite France’s long-standing neuralgia about participating in NATO’s nuclear structures, it would be prudent for these groups to discuss crisis coordination and establish regular communications channels, lest divergences surface for the first time under conditions of considerable danger.

The Trump administration is right that the traditional division of responsibility for European security was unsustainable. But NATO 3.0 rests on an unworkable and dangerous partition between conventional and nuclear capabilities, one that runs counter to the lessons of history and deterrence theory. A phased, consultative redistribution of responsibilities would better preserve deterrence, shift burdens, and protect U.S. influence. Pull back too quickly, and Washington risks leaving behind a weaker NATO and an emboldened Russia eager to test its seams.

#5291. Segway launches the $1,999 Xyber Air, bringing its futuristic moped-style e-bike to the masses

When Segway launched the original Xyber last year, it was one of those e-bikes that immediately turned heads. With its futuristic styling, oversized frame, and high-performance off-road ambitions, it looked more like a concept vehicle than something you could actually buy.

Now the company is hoping to capture a much wider audience with the new Segway Xyber Air, a lighter, street-legal version that keeps much of the original’s aggressive styling while fitting neatly into the familiar Class 1, 2, and 3 e-bike categories.

At first glance, you’d be forgiven for mistaking the Xyber Air for its larger sibling. It retains the angular frame design and moto-inspired proportions that made the original stand out, complete with Segway’s signature X-shaped front and rear lighting.

But beneath the familiar styling is a much more approachable machine.

Power comes from a 750W nominal rear hub motor producing 80 Nm of torque, with peak output reaching 1,600W.

Riders can choose between Class 1, Class 2, and Class 3 operation depending on local regulations, with speeds of up to 20 mph (32 km/h) in Class 1 and 2 modes or 28 mph (45 km/h) in Class 3.

Segway has even included a Hybrid Mode that allows throttle operation up to 20 mph while pedal assist continues to 28 mph without requiring riders to switch between ride modes.

That’s still kosher in many states, but some are starting to require true Class 3 e-bikes to have no throttle operation at all, so check your local regulations to make sure you’re compliant.

The bike is powered by a 717 Wh battery that Segway claims can deliver up to 58 miles (93 km) of range, though as always, real-world mileage will depend heavily on rider weight, terrain, and how enthusiastically that throttle gets used.

Suspension duties are handled by an 80 mm dual-crown front fork, paired with 20×4-inch fat tires and hydraulic disc brakes with 180 mm rotors.

The bike also borrows several features more commonly associated with motorcycles, including traction control, hill-start assist, regenerative hill descent control, and automatic lighting.

Whether or not an e-bike requires traction control or hill hold remains to be seen, but it’s neat to know that such moto-inspired tech is heading into the e-bike space.

#5288. A Democratic Reckoning in India

PRATAP BHANU MEHTA is University Lecturer at Princeton University and a Senior Fellow at the Centre for Policy Research. He is a co-editor of The Oxford Handbook of the Indian Constitution.

The Battle on the Streets Over the Foundations of the State

India is in rebellion. At stake is the very future of the country’s democracy and the potential of its renewal. The mass protests that have unfolded in New Delhi since July and flared again this month are nothing less than a contest over the foundations of the Indian state and whether it lives up to its constitutional commitments.

Demonstrations began in the summer as students decried perceived unfairness in the country’s examination system. They have now widened to a struggle over the integrity of India’s elections, after authorities began to purge over 130 million people from draft voting lists over the last year. Many of those stripped from the electoral rolls are appealing their removal—an often laborious process—but the possibility of mass disenfranchisement is real. Tens of thousands of mostly young people have taken to the streets in New Delhi in the past week, with protests now spreading to other major cities. The demonstrators are demanding the resignation of India’s chief election commissioner and questioning the credibility of India’s ostensibly independent institutions. And they are holding Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi directly responsible, accusing him of “stealing democracy.” In their rejection of the country’s current trajectory, the protests pose the most significant challenge to Modi’s authority since the prime minister came to power in 2014.

The main opposition parties, which have never come close to dislodging Modi in national parliamentary elections, put up a united front under Rahul Gandhi, the leader of the Congress party, and an impressive show of force at a rally in New Delhi on October 12. But the real heart of the protest is the intense political energy and inventiveness shown by Gen Z students. These young people have defied prohibitions on protest, breached draconian security clampdowns, and endured detentions to relentlessly question the government’s authority.

The government, for its part, also sees this confrontation as an existential battle. This is evident in its determination to contain the protests, deploying over 40,000 security personnel, closing down metro and train stations, barricading central New Delhi, suspending mobile Internet, and detaining over 7,000 people. It has doggedly defended the chief of the Election Commission and it has refused to talk to the opposition or the youthful protesters about their demands.

Modi is in no danger of falling; this will not be a rerun of recent Gen Z protests in neighboring Bangladesh and Nepal that successfully toppled governments. But the Indian government faces a subtler danger: its writ no longer carries unequivocal sway. A strongman such as Modi likes to project unquestioned personal authority. Since 2014, his ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) has consolidated and centralized power to an unprecedented degree. For the government, the open defiance of young people—who come from all class, religious, and caste backgrounds and cannot easily be dismissed or stigmatized as belonging to an “antinational” group—is an unprecedented challenge. It makes the Modi government’s once adamantine grip on power seem more fragile. The future of Indian democracy hinges on whether this contest opens up more democratic space or leads to greater repression.

Modern India is founded on two legitimizing institutions: elections and exams. Since its independence in 1947, India has maintained a reputation for conducting free and fair elections. The Election Commission of India, an independent constitutional body, has sweeping powers to ensure that the polls remain beyond reproach. The credibility of the commission has been central to the credibility of Indian democracy.

India also conducts recruitment for public-sector jobs and admissions to public and several private colleges through competitive national exams. For millions of young people, the stakes in these exams are high. Success can send candidates scrambling up the ladder of opportunity, but failure can consign them to never climbing it at all. It is important, therefore, that these exams be conducted fairly and have the imprimatur of honesty and impartiality.

India remains a doggedly unequal society in which money, kinship, and power distort almost all institutions. In this context, elections and exams are the only two processes that make the state’s promise of equal treatment tangible. Elections give even the poorest Indians the prospect of dignity and agency, and as a result, voter turnout rates are routinely high. Exams sustain a parallel promise: they keep alive the idea that academic diligence and performance can trump other forms of social capital. To be sure, the staging of both elections and exams has never been perfect, but the overall integrity of the processes has never been in doubt.

Over the last year, however, the actions of the Indian government have cast doubt on elections and exams. In 2024, the main nationwide entrance examination for medical schools was engulfed in controversy over a slew of irregularities, including the advance leak of the exam’s questions. Protests ensued, and students raised serious questions about the reliability of the examination system. The episode also put the National Testing Agency, the central body that under Modi had become responsible for conducting many important exams, under intense scrutiny. Centralization was meant to ensure greater efficiency and regularity. Instead, it made the crisis deeper by concentrating responsibility at the level of the federal government. In July, antigovernment protests forced the resignation of the education minister over frustrations with the examination system.

Similarly, over the last year or so, the Election Commission decided to undertake what is known as a Special Intensive Revision, or SIR, an assessment of India’s voter lists to ensure their accuracy. The exercise is well within the powers of the election commission; the last such revision was conducted between 2002 and 2004. But the process has become quite controversial this time because of concerns that it places excessive burdens on citizens to prove their eligibility to vote; the lack of transparency about why names are being added or deleted from the lists; and the use of new software that has given the Election Commission the ability to strip citizens from the voter rolls on trivial grounds, often overriding established procedures and the recommendations of those local officials who do the actual verification.

According to his critics, Gyanesh Kumar, the chief election commissioner, acted unilaterally and recklessly in pursuing the revision of voter rolls in ways that violated normal procedures. Since June 2025, the SIR has tagged roughly 130 million names for removal from the voter rolls. Although many of the deletions were duplicates and the names of people who had died or left the country, millions of Indians have found themselves stripped of the right to vote and now burdened with the difficult task of appealing to a tribunal to prove their citizenship. In this light, the protests have already had one constructive effect: the Election Commission is trying to placate its critics by making it easier for those excluded to lodge appeals.

Gandhi has alleged that the SIR is an instrument for manipulating elections. Already, in the state of West Bengal, where nine million names were taken off, millions of Indians found themselves unable to participate in state elections in March because their appeals were yet to be heard. The elections brought Modi’s BJP to power in the state for the first time. But even if the Election Commission is not systematically targeting voters based on their identity or their political preferences, the exercise still risks disenfranchising millions through sheer ineptitude.

To make matters worse, India’s Supreme Court, another vital democratic institution, has also played an unfortunate role in this train of events. The Supreme Court effectively collaborated in the disenfranchisement of voters in West Bengal by blithely claiming that those whose petitions were under appeal could just vote in the next election after their appeals had been adjudicated; it didn’t matter if they missed voting in the current election.

The sense of the Supreme Court’s callousness was compounded in May after Surya Kant, the chief justice, compared a group of young lawyers pressing for change to “cockroaches.” That stray remark precipitated a social movement, with the creation—initially as a joke but then in earnest—of the “Cockroach Janata Party,” a group that has galvanized young Indians both on social media and in the streets. It played a key role in forcing the resignation of Dharmendra Pradhan, the education minister, in July, after mass protests in New Delhi and across the country. It is now driving young Indians to call for the resignation of Kumar, the chief election commissioner.

Most significantly, it has linked the credibility crisis directly to Modi, encouraging a veritable explosion of public mockery of the prime minister on social media and the Internet. That has punctured Modi’s carefully cultivated image of an invincible leader who delivers on every promise. He has prided himself on his dominance of social media, but the loss of control over the political narrative, especially on the image-sharing platform Instagram, was palpable. The prime minister has feebly tried to make his own reels in response, but that failed to stem the tide of public irreverence. The ridicule was noteworthy not simply as political satire. It lifted the pall of fear that had hung over Indian democracy since Modi became prime minister, as critics have been silenced through intimidation and opposition leaders targeted through legal proceedings. In mocking power, young people found their voice.

The threat they pose to Modi is significant. These young Indians are not saddled with the historical baggage of ties to conventional opposition parties. The issues they focus on—exams and elections—cut across social divides and even partisan political affiliations. And the protesters’ grit, wit, inventiveness, and fearlessness have dented Modi’s authority like nothing else has in the past 12 years.

The movement must still contend with the brute reality of state power, which has been evident in the streets of New Delhi this past week. Authorities tried to physically suppress and contain dissent by prohibiting it and preventing protesters from entering certain public spaces. That in turn has given young Indians another reason to go to the streets, to protest the right to protest and reclaim public spaces. The government has deployed thousands of security forces in the national capital, as if it were facing some invading foreign army, not young people making reasonable demands, and all signs are that Modi will continue to maintain a hard line on the protests.

For the government, the movement’s principal demand, the resignation of Kumar, is not simply a question of one official’s tenure. Modi has secured extraordinary loyalty within his BJP through an implicit assurance that those who act at the government’s behest will be protected from the consequences of their actions. If that assurance begins to fray, ministers and party functionaries may worry about what might happen when they can no longer take for granted the protection of the leadership. Modi’s defense of Kumar, then, may be less about the SIR than about preserving loyalty to the government within the ranks of his party.

Moreover, Modi has built much of his political persona around turning perceived slights into demonstrations of strength. To yield in the face of public mockery would be especially difficult and out of character. Kumar’s removal could be read as an admission of guilt. Such a retreat could then embolden Modi’s rivals and encourage further protests—and raise the prospect that a galvanized opposition might finally unseat the prime minister in the next parliamentary elections scheduled for 2029.

To be sure, the future electoral consequences of the protests remain uncertain. Public anger does not automatically translate into votes. The Election Commission may yet blunt the controversy by admitting voters whose appeals are pending or by making procedural concessions that reduce the immediate grievances. The BJP may still benefit from a fractious opposition and from the fact that voters in a country as large as India are rarely united in their motivations and perceptions of the political stakes. The next major electoral test, in the state of Uttar Pradesh, is still some months away, in March 2027. A victory there for the BJP would allow the party to claim that its authority remains intact; a defeat would make it much harder to contain the political implications of the present controversy. The government is likely to double down on repression against dissenters nationwide, trusting that it can succeed in limiting public expressions of anger.

The BJP is hoping that the more disruptions there are in New Delhi, the more it can cast the current upheaval as the work of rabble-rousers and anarchists and rally Indians behind the prime minister. The government and its supporters are already seeking to discredit the movement as the work of foreign hands, invoking conspiracy theories and examples of alleged malign foreign intervention in the youth demonstrations in Bangladesh and Nepal that ousted sitting governments.

Yet the greater danger facing the Indian government is that it comes to believe its own propaganda and ignores genuine discontent. In both Bangladesh and Nepal, the depth of popular grievance was obvious for anyone willing to look—as it is in India. Despite India’s headline growth rates, young people worry about their economic future. Graduate unemployment is at 23 percent, white-collar job growth has slowed to a creeping one percent this year, and nearly a quarter of Indians between the ages of 15 and 29 are not in employment, education, or training programs. The very institutions that were meant to secure their future—elections, courts, and independent agencies—are losing their trust. The strongman is failing, even as the opposition struggles to inspire a great deal of conviction and enthusiasm. What is powerful about this moment, then, is the spectacle of young people who, lacking many good options, are still choosing to fight for their democracy the only way citizens can when it is in peril: with energy, aspiration, and the belief that they can craft their own collective future, wherever that may lead.

As the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) prepares to convene the Fifth Plenum of its 20th Central Committee, rumors about personnel changes at the top are once again sweeping through overseas Chinese-language circles. One of the most outlandish claims is that Premier Li Qiang, the formal second-in-command, has already been designated as Xi Jinping’s successor and will even replace him as party general secretary at the upcoming meeting. Before last year’s Fourth Plenum, it was Cai Qi, another member of the Politburo Standing Committee, who was rumored to be Xi’s successor.

Neither claim is credible. Yet taken together, these rumors inadvertently reveal something fundamental about the workings of Chinese elite politics: Xi relies heavily on Li and Cai, his two most important lieutenants, to sustain his rule.

Understanding Chinese elite politics requires identifying the relationships that determine how power operates, rather than obsessing over supposed clues in every meeting or photograph. One reason outside observers have repeatedly misjudged Xi’s rule is that they have become lost in the fog of power struggles, imagining Zhongnanhai, the Chinese leadership compound, as a place perpetually on the verge of a palace coup while overlooking how the system functions on a daily basis.

However much power Xi has concentrated in his own hands, he cannot personally manage the enormous machinery of the Chinese state, or the vast CCP itself. He needs lieutenants. Within the 20th Politburo, Li and Cai are his two most indispensable aides—one overseeing government affairs, the other party affairs. Together, they constitute what I call the “central gang of three” that defines elite politics in the Xi era.

By “gang of three,” I don’t mean to suggest that the three constitute a faction akin to the notorious Gang of Four of the Cultural Revolution (1966-76), the gaggle of revolutionary leaders who were later blamed for the disasters of the period. Rather, this is a framework for understanding power. Zhou Enlai, the first premier of the People’s Republic of China, once advised senior military commanders newly entering the highest levels of politics that understanding the political situation required grasping and navigating what he called “central politics”—the relationships among Mao Zedong, military leader Lin Biao, and Mao’s wife, Jiang Qing, the three-most powerful figures at the time.

Zhou’s observation captured the essence of elite politics during the Cultural Revolution. Mao occupied the center, while Lin and Jiang drew on different sources of political power to secure their exceptional positions. Both depended on Mao, yet they also competed with each other. Lin would lose out in 1971, when a still mysterious set of events resulted in his death. Jiang would fall in 1976, when the Gang of Four, which she headed, was toppled by Mao’s successor, Hua Guofeng.

Today, Xi, Li, and Cai form a similarly unequal triangle: Xi is the supreme authority over both men, while Li and Cai perform distinct functions, share common interests, and remain potential competitors.

But the historical similarities should not obscure the more important differences. Lin was not only Mao’s officially designated successor but also a military commander with wartime achievements, considerable prestige, and long-standing connections within the armed forces. Jiang enjoyed the unique status of being Mao’s wife, along with the political network she built during the Cultural Revolution. Neither could have exercised power without Mao, but neither depended entirely on his day-to-day authorization—particularly Lin.

Li and Cai are different. Both owe their political ascent to the trust they established with Xi during their careers in provincial and central government. Neither possesses the independent political capital that Lin commanded, much less the familial connection that gave Jiang her special standing. As a result, the interests binding Xi, Li, and Cai are more closely aligned than those that bound Mao, Lin, and Jiang, and neither man can establish independent centers of power outside Xi’s authority.

Xi has clearly divided responsibilities between Li and Cai while preventing either from monopolizing power within his respective domain. Xi relies on both without publicly showing a pronounced preference for either. At least so far, there is no visible evidence of the kind of acute, overt conflict that characterized relations between Lin and Jiang.

The division of responsibilities between Li and Cai since the 20th Party Congress in 2022 appears straightforward. Li serves as premier of the State Council and ranks second in the party hierarchy. Cai, meanwhile, is the highest-ranking secretary of the Secretariat, the Politburo’s executive arm. Although he ranks fifth in the party hierarchy, Cai oversees the Secretariat’s daily work and simultaneously heads the powerful General Office of the CCP Central Committee. This year, he also took charge of the Central Party School in Beijing. These positions place him almost constantly at Xi’s side.

Yet while some have seen Cai as more powerful than Li as a result, this is wrong. Frequent access to the supreme leader undoubtedly confers considerable influence over his schedule and the information reaching him. But proximity is not all in a system such as the CCP’s.

In Xi’s hierarchy of priorities, the party unquestionably takes precedence over the government. The party’s organizational apparatus, personnel system, disciplinary machinery, ideology, and security institutions directly determine whether Xi can sustain his hold on power. The economy may fluctuate, but political control over the party cannot be allowed to weaken. That is precisely why Xi entrusted the Secretariat and the General Office to Cai, whom he trusts deeply.

Yet this arrangement contains an easily overlooked paradox: It is precisely because party affairs are so important that Xi cannot fully delegate their core powers to Cai.

Cai coordinates, communicates, and supervises the implementation of central party affairs. But party affairs do not constitute a single domain that he can monopolize. Discipline inspection and anti-corruption work, personnel and organizational affairs, propaganda and ideology, political and legal affairs, and security are all overseen by other Xi loyalists, who report to the leader directly.

Moreover, last year Xi transferred Shi Taifeng, who had previously overseen united front work, to head the Central Committee Organization Department, which is responsible for evaluating and selecting party officials. Whether or not Xi intended the appointment to constrain Cai, it objectively limits Cai’s ability to turn the party apparatus into his own personal power base. The military, of course, remains under Xi’s direct control.

Nor does Cai’s responsibility for party affairs mean that Xi refrains from intervening directly. How far anti-corruption campaigns should go, what political standards officials must meet, and what ideological education campaigns the party should undertake are ultimately decided by Xi himself. Cai’s role is to translate Xi’s will into the party center’s daily operations.

Li, of course, operates under numerous constraints of his own. Xi has brought many major economic decisions under the direct authority of the party center through bodies such as the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission and the Central Financial Commission. Within the State Council, Vice Premiers Ding Xuexiang and He Lifeng, among others, oversee important portfolios and play major roles. These arrangements have dispersed some of the premier’s authority.

But the State Council differs fundamentally from the Secretariat and the General Office. It is a state institution with its own administrative system, legally defined powers, and vast implementing apparatus. The administration of the country—including economic, social, and cultural affairs, as well as foreign affairs and certain aspects of national defense—requires coordination and organization by the State Council.

Public administration, particularly economic management, involves an enormous number of concrete decisions. It requires reconciling conflicting interests among ministries and local governments and making choices as circumstances constantly change. Xi can determine broad policies and the overall direction of development, but he cannot personally run the government for Li every day. This gives Li a degree of discretion over day-to-day policy within the framework established by Xi.

With China confronting a lingering real estate crisis, local government debt, weak domestic demand, and sustained strategic pressure from the United States, Xi’s long-term ambitions cannot be realized without a functioning economy. An economic stagnation could also intensify social tensions and threaten stability. At certain stages, therefore, economic management may be no less important than political rectification. Party discipline and political loyalty can be reinforced through organizational measures, but economic growth cannot be restored by political declarations alone. Xi entrusted the State Council to Li, a longtime associate with extensive experience managing provincial economies because he needs someone who is both politically reliable and capable of getting things done.

Both men operate under Xi’s authority, but Li’s position as premier and the State Council’s distinct administrative machinery give him responsibilities for governing the country that Cai does not possess. Cai is unquestionably important to maintaining Xi’s personal grip on power, but he functions more as the chief manager of the party apparatus surrounding the supreme leader. His influence depends to a greater extent on Xi himself. Both lieutenants matter enormously, but proximity to Xi alone is no reason to reverse their relative positions.

Next year’s 21st Party Congress will provide a crucial test of whether this structure can endure. Both Li and Cai will have passed the customary retirement threshold of 68 by October 2027, but Li will be only three months beyond it, while Cai will be nearly four years over the limit. Under the long-standing informal retirement convention known as “seven up, eight down,” both would normally be expected to retire. Yet this convention is not an unbreakable rule.

Keeping both men on the Standing Committee, however, appears unlikely, since Xi must also make room for other loyalists to move up. My assessment is that Li has a stronger prospect of remaining on the Standing Committee—not simply because Cai is older but because the two men perform different political functions.

Cai, however, is unlikely to retire completely. He could follow the example of Chen Xi, who remained president of the Central Party School after leaving the Politburo. Cai might even simultaneously assume the largely ceremonial position of state vice president.

The question is whether Xi needs an aide who can help him govern the country or one who can help him control the party. As the challenges of governing the country grow more complex, his dependence on a lieutenant such as Li, who can actually manage those challenges, may only deepen. Next year’s party congress may be when this difference becomes clearest.

In a nod to banking industry concerns over the potential loss of deposits to stablecoin accounts, the law barred stablecoin issuers like Circle and Tether from offering interest payments or rewards as an enticement. However, the law left the door open to third-party intermediaries, primarily crypto exchanges, offering yield-bearing options.

The final rule from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency makes clear that exchanges like Coinbase and Kraken couldn’t offer interest or rewards simply for holding, using or retaining stablecoins for transactions. But other activities involving stablecoins would be eligible on a case-by-case basis, the OCC said, without providing a specific list of allowable use cases.

One potentially eligible activity, according to experts, might be participation in “liquidity pools” offered on platforms like Uniswap, where users deposit two or more stablecoins that can be easily swapped, supporting liquidity and limiting volatile price spikes or declines. In return for contributing to the pool, users receive trading fees and other incentives.

Since such an activity doesn’t fall within the prohibited categories, it’s more likely the OCC wouldn’t flag it for compliance violations under the final rule.

That’s a major, crypto industry-backed shift from the earlier proposed rule, which maintained a general ban on arrangements paying interest or rewards while allowing a “rebuttable presumption” opportunity to explain why an activity would not violate the law.

Within hours of digesting the 721-page rule, the American Bankers Association had seen enough.

“While the rule includes several positive steps, we disagree with the OCC’s decision to move away from its original position on stablecoin yield,” ABA President and CEO Rob Nichols said in a statement. “The result is a final rule that will preserve the status quo for crypto platforms despite Congress’s clear intent to protect local lending.”

The Blockchain Association recommended that the OCC drop the rebuttable presumption provision, which would have placed the burden of proof on the industry to explain why issuer arrangements with third parties didn’t violate the law’s yield ban. The organization said that provision went beyond what Congress intended and that the final rule more closely aligned with the statutory text.

“We are especially grateful that the OCC listened to the industry,” Blockchain Association chief policy officer Lindsay Fraser said in a statement, adding that the final rule reflects changes the group’s members suggested during the public comment period.

The final rule should make it easier for stablecoin issuers to explain their utility that justifies the yield being indirectly offered, said Steve Gannon, a partner at Davis Wright Tremaine.

“If it’s difficult for an issuer to come up with that description, maybe they shouldn’t be paying yield,” Gannon said.

After passage of the 2025 law, the continuing legal uncertainty over stablecoin yield bled into the debate over the broader crypto industry “market structure” bill that the House passed but stalled in the Senate.

Within a year of enactment, regulators would have to issue a rule defining which “rewards or incentives based on bona fide activities or bona fide transactions that are not economically or functionally equivalent to the payment of interest or yield on an interest-bearing bank deposit” are allowed, the latest bill text says.

The measure also provides examples of permissible activities, such as “providing liquidity for market-making activity,” i.e. liquidity pools.

The bill also contains a “regulatory circuit-breaker” provision, allowing regulators to bar previously-allowed interest or yield payments if community banks — defined as institutions with assets totaling less than $10 billion — have suffered deposit flight resulting in “substantial detrimental impact” to those banks’ deposits.

That so-called kill switch provision might ease some qualms about the legislation, known as the “Clarity Act,” given the final OCC rule that appears to be less restrictive on stablecoin yield, Gannon said.

“Maybe there’s a way to get a skinny Clarity Act in place during a lame-duck session,” he said.

#5279. The Nuclear Deal That Could Save NATO

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

IVO H. DAALDER is Senior Fellow at Harvard’s Belfer Center for Science and International Affairs. He served as U.S. Ambassador to NATO from 2009 to 2013.

How Europe Can Take More Responsibility—Without Starting an Arms Race

At a meeting earlier this year, top foreign ministry officials from several European countries were asked who among them still believed that the United States would come to their defense if they were attacked. Not a single hand went up. It was a stark indication of the extent to which European governments have concluded that the United States—for so long the backbone of European and NATO security—is no longer a reliable ally. Over the past 18 months, conversations in Brussels and in other NATO capitals have revolved around how to boost the continent’s conventional defense to make up for Washington’s retrenchment. But a quieter and more urgent discussion is also taking place, focused on the status of the ultimate deterrent: the U.S. nuclear guarantee.

More than half a century ago, the Great Debate, as the French strategist Raymond Aron termed it, broke out between European countries and the United States over how to preserve European nuclear deterrence in the face of the Soviet Union’s expanding nuclear capabilities. Then, European leaders feared that Washington’s growing emphasis on the conventional defense of the continent reflected an increasing reluctance to protect Europe with nuclear weapons—especially as Moscow developed missiles that could reach the U.S. homeland. Would the United States be willing to risk Boston to defend Berlin? That debate was ultimately resolved through a new grand bargain: in return for a commitment from European countries to forgo developing nuclear weapons, the United States would invite more European participation in nuclear decision-making. That essential bargain stuck for 60 years, helping secure both the United States and Europe and preventing nuclear proliferation.

Now, however, that bargain has come undone. Washington says that it wants a new division of labor: the Europeans need to take on primary responsibility for their conventional defense while the United States continues to ensure their nuclear defense. But that hardly addresses the problem. “If you don’t believe in the United States’ Article 5 commitment,” one of the officials attending the meeting told me, “then extended deterrence is effectively dead.” Already, alternative options are being considered. In some European capitals, officials are quietly raising the possibility of acquiring their own nuclear weapons. France, meanwhile, has offered to adapt its nuclear policy to help defend its European allies.Each of these options has real logic. But none of them adequately addresses the problem of ensuring European security without risking further proliferation. A better approach would be a fourth alternative: a renewed U.S.-European nuclear bargain, this time with a real European core. Led by France and the United Kingdom, NATO’s European members need to take on a larger role in their nuclear deterrence while working to restore the transatlantic defense link that has been the foundation of U.S. and European security for so many decades.

The Soviet Union’s 1957 launch of the Sputnik satellite posed a profound dilemma for NATO. Until then, the alliance had counted on U.S. nuclear weapons deployed in Europe to deter an attack by a much larger Soviet conventional force. But once Moscow demonstrated its ability to launch intercontinental rockets, Europeans feared that the United States would no longer be willing to use nuclear weapons to prevent a Soviet invasion of Europe.

These concerns prompted a fierce debate within NATO about how the continent could ensure its nuclear defense. Some, led by France, were convinced that European countries needed their own nuclear weapons. Others sought less costly ways to restore some control over nuclear deterrence. One American idea that gained substantial traction was to create a so-called multilateral force—a fleet of vessels carrying some 200 missiles. Such a multilaterally operated and funded force could give Europeans a sense of ownership and control over the use of nuclear weapons, the thinking went, while preserving the U.S. president’s final authority over their launch.

The debate exposed a fundamental problem with extending U.S. nuclear deterrence to allies, especially if an ocean separates those allies from Washington. An adversary might be deterred from attacking an American ally by even the small possibility of a U.S. nuclear retaliation; the consequences of a miscalculation would be catastrophic. But that same small possibility would offer little comfort to the ally, who needs to believe that Washington would actually risk nuclear devastation on its behalf. Denis Healey, the United Kingdom’s defense secretary throughout much of the 1960s, postulated what became known as the Healey theorem: “It takes only a five percent credibility of American retaliation to deter an attack, but it takes a 95 percent credibility to reassure the allies.”

The debate was ultimately resolved through a bargain. Washington’s European allies agreed to forgo their right to acquire nuclear weapons in return for a greater say in U.S. nuclear weapons policy and strategy. Although the idea of a costly multilateral force was abandoned, the creation of the NATO Nuclear Planning Group (NPG) provided a vehicle for Europeans to give input into U.S. nuclear planning and for Washington to demonstrate that European security interests were integral to its nuclear strategy and force posture. Reassured, European countries (aside from France and the United Kingdom) agreed to codify their nonnuclear status in the 1968 Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty.

Many European countries retained ground- and air-based systems that could deliver U.S. nuclear weapons deployed on their territory. Today, about half a dozen European countries still deploy dual-capable aircraft that can carry U.S. B-61 nuclear bombs. These nuclear consulting and sharing arrangements proved far more crucial to reassuring allies than the hard-power measures (such as counterforce targeting, low-yield weapons, and greater precision) that U.S. strategists typically believe were essential. The latter measures focused on resolving the five-percent problem; the former addressed the 95-percent questions.

France was the only country to reject this nuclear bargain. (The United Kingdom had become a nuclear power in the 1950s, but it committed all its weapons to NATO and participated in the NPG and the dual-capable aircraft missions.) Under President Charles de Gaulle, France came to believe that it could not count on another country for its nuclear security. Paris embarked on its own nuclear program, refused to participate in the NPG or the dual-capable aircraft mission, and, in 1966, withdrew from NATO’s military command structure. The overall bargain, however, laid the foundation for what has become a highly successful deterrence, reassurance, and nonproliferation regime.

Unlike in the 1960s, today’s nuclear debate has not been prompted by a change in the global balance of power. It has been precipitated by a unilateral change in the United States’ attitude toward Europe. As I explained in Foreign Affairs last year, in Trump’s second term, the United States has rejected the two core principles of NATO’s collective defense commitments: that there is a shared understanding of the threat to NATO members and that members’ security is indivisible.

European members of NATO can no longer assume that the U.S. commitment to their security—derived from the fundamental idea, born of two devastating world wars, that American security is inextricably linked to the security of Europe—is ironclad. Instead, as the Pentagon’s policy chief, Elbridge Colby, has repeatedly noted, “under President Trump’s leadership, the simple fact is that countries can no longer take America for granted.” European countries have never taken America for granted. They have assumed that it shares their belief in the indivisibility of U.S. and European security—and they proved their own commitment to this belief when NATO invoked Article 5’s collective-defense obligation for the first and only time, in response to the September 11, 2001, attacks on the United States.

The American president and other senior officials have made it clear that the United States’ security commitment to Europe is based not on a shared understanding of the threat that both entities face but on secondary considerations, such as European countries’ level of defense spending and their support for U.S. foreign policy. In August, the Pentagon sent its 31 NATO allies a long questionnaire stressing the conditionality of Washington’s security commitment: it asked them to profess their loyalty to Trump’s foreign policy, commit to procuring defense equipment from U.S. firms, and prioritize funding the restoration of the U.S. industrial base by opposing any “buy European” initiatives.

Some European NATO leaders still refuse to admit in public that anything fundamental has changed. Privately, though, most have reached the same conclusion as senior European political officials: that the United States can no longer be counted on to come to Europe’s defense. And if that is true when it comes to conventional defense, it is even more true with respect to the nuclear guarantee. That leaves Europe facing questions it has never had to ask before. What if Russia attacked NATO territory and Washington told Europe it was on its own? How would nonnuclear allies stand up to a nuclear-armed Russia? What if Moscow used nuclear threats, implicit or explicit, to try to force Europe to end its support for Ukraine? None of these scenarios are far-fetched. Russia has issued veiled nuclear threats against Europe repeatedly since 2022, most recently in September, after NATO conducted air exercises above northeastern Poland, near Kaliningrad.

Three options have begun to emerge to fill the gap created by the change in U.S. policy. Washington favors reestablishing the nuclear bargain with a new distribution of responsibilities, which it calls NATO 3.0. “We will continue to provide the U.S. extended nuclear deterrent,” Colby told NATO defense ministers earlier this year, “with Europe taking the lead for its conventional defense.” The United States has even suggested that it might be willing to expand its nuclear umbrella by extending its nuclear sharing arrangements to other countries (Poland, for instance) and developing a next-generation nuclear bomb for potential deployment in Europe.

But this option ignores the core problem. The Trump administration has done nothing to convince its allies that the United States remains committed to NATO other than saying so. No additional weapons deployments can compensate for the central implication that U.S. security commitments to European allies are now conditional on political loyalty.

A second option would end the nuclear bargain altogether. In its place, European countries would acquire their own nuclear weapons. The clearest statement of this idea came in March 2025, when Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk told the country’s parliament that “we would be safer if we had our own nuclear arsenal, that is beyond doubt.” A year later, he told his cabinet that Poland would “strive to prepare . . . for the most autonomous actions possible” on nuclear security. And Poland isn’t the only country thinking seriously about this option. In Sweden, some politicians have invoked the country’s secret nuclear program during the 1960s to indicate their interest in pursuing a national option. Even in Germany, where discussions of defense have historically been constrained by its own history, a Bundeswehr general argued publicly earlier this year that Germany “needs its own tactical nuclear weapons,” an assertion that would have been unthinkable just a few years ago. Similar ideas are being voiced in Asia, especially in South Korea, raising the prospect of a cascade of allied proliferation.

In theory, if more European countries acquired nuclear weapons, it could resolve the extended deterrence dilemma. In practice, it isn’t a workable solution. It would take many years for Poland, Sweden, or even Germany to develop independent nuclear deterrents. European nuclearization would break the Nonproliferation Treaty architecture that the original 1960s U.S.-European nuclear bargain was built to protect, and it could set off a nuclear arms race that could make Europe less, not more, secure. A strategy designed to bolster deterrence would end up weakening the very alliance structure it aimed to strengthen.

As an alternative to these two options, France has spearheaded a third approach that would aim to replace, or at least augment, the U.S. umbrella. French President Emmanuel Macron proposed this solution in a speech in March 2026, announcing a “forward deterrence” initiative that would include European allies in nuclear training exercises, temporarily forward-deploy French nuclear-capable aircraft on allied soil, and host extensive nuclear discussions with participating countries. So far, ten European countries have joined the initiative by establishing bilateral nuclear steering groups to discuss French nuclear strategy and how its evolution could enhance their security.

The Macron proposal represents a major evolution in French thinking by explicitly extending France’s vital interests to include the security of its European partners. Paris’s willingness to engage European neighbors in direct talks about nuclear issues is welcome. But there are real drawbacks to such an initiative. In his announcement, Macron reiterated the long-standing “four nos” that underpin France’s defense doctrine: no joint decisions on nuclear use, no joint nuclear planning, no shared definition of French vital interests, and no nuclear guarantee “in the strict sense of the term.” The framework’s reliance on bilateral discussions also weakens the idea that Europe’s defense ought to be a collective NATO effort.

And crucially, France’s nuclear doctrine—which allows for a warning shot, followed by a devastating response if the warning fails to deter the adversary—gives it fewer options for managing conflict than does NATO’s more flexible strategy, which allows for gradual escalation. This narrower range of options could make its nuclear threats less credible in a conflict over an ally’s security. In the end, the French proposal would add a measure of reassurance and deterrence, but not enough to overcome the voids created by the change in U.S. policy.

None of the three options now being discussed resolve Europe’s extended nuclear deterrence dilemma. But elements of all three point to a possible solution. NATO needs to bolster both the European and the U.S. legs of its nuclear deterrence posture, bring France into its nuclear consultation and planning structures, and extend the existing nuclear sharing arrangements to incorporate additional members. These tasks will require NATO members, especially its three nuclear powers, to make significant adjustments to their existing beliefs and policies. The alternative is not the status quo—which is already unsustainable, given the nature of the Russian threat and the changes in U.S. policy—but rapid nuclear proliferation.

NATO countries will need to agree to three major adjustments in their nuclear posture: in the number and kind of weapons deployed, in how nuclear and nonnuclear NATO members consult one another, and in the alliance’s doctrine for using nuclear weapons. First, NATO nuclear powers need to modernize their nuclear forces. The United States should replace the B-61 gravity bomb, which will be difficult to use effectively against heavily defended targets, with a stand-off missile that can reach deep into Russian territory from NATO airspace. Such a modernization is long overdue. In addition, European nations should agree to develop a long-range missile that could be outfitted with a nuclear warhead developed jointly by France and the United Kingdom. The missile should be financed by European countries and be deployable on aircraft owned and operated by nonnuclear allies. Finally, these new U.S. and European nuclear capabilities need to be based in a wider range of NATO countries, expanding European participation in the nuclear deterrent mission.

Changes to hardware are necessary to restore the credibility of Europe’s nuclear deterrence. But the software of consultation needs to be at the core of the effort, as it was in the 1960s. Back then, France refused to join the NATO consensus and withdrew from the integrated military command structure. Even when it returned, in 2009, its nuclear deterrent remained fully independent, and it did not join the Nuclear Planning Group. That insistence on independence is what led Paris to pursue bilateral nuclear arrangements this year. But these parallel bilateral discussions not only create confusion and distrust about Paris’s true intentions among dialogue partners; they also fail to foster the cohesion that could come from a collective European nuclear deterrence dialogue.

Put simply, Paris needs to join the NPG. Doing so would not automatically place its nuclear capabilities under NATO command and control. But it would give European partners a better understanding of French thinking and afford France more knowledge of European requirements for deterrence and reassurance. European NPG members could agree to meet regularly without the United States to discuss how to integrate the new European nuclear missile forces into NATO’s nuclear strategy. For many years, countries hosting U.S. nuclear forces have met separately from other NATO members in the NPG; a similar, European-only caucus would build on that precedent.

The third necessary adjustment needs to be doctrinal. One element of NATO’s nuclear doctrine cannot change: that the ultimate decision to use nuclear weapons will always reside with the nuclear powers, France, the United Kingdom, and the United States. These decisions cannot be delegated. And national nuclear deterrents will continue to be guided by national doctrine, not subject to NATO control. If France wants to retain its one-warning-shot escalatory doctrine, it can do so, even after it joins the NPG. But it would need to explain how this doctrine would reassure allies and enhance deterrence. At the same time, France’s participation in the NPG and the addition of the new European nuclear missile force would expand Europe’s influence over the development of NATO nuclear strategy. And it would confront Moscow with the reality that the decision to use nuclear weapons in Europe’s defense no longer rests in Washington’s hands alone.

These are major posture adjustments that will require significant changes in policy and perspective. Washington will have to accept that the deployment of additional European nuclear forces means it can no longer dictate NATO’s nuclear policy and strategy—including, ultimately, the decision to use nuclear weapons. Paris, meanwhile, must accept more integration into NATO. London will need to extend its nuclear cooperation with the United States to France, develop a second nuclear system (in addition to its submarine force), and find the resources necessary to design and build a new warhead in collaboration with France. Nonnuclear European countries will have to agree to finance the development and procurement of new missile systems and possibly airplanes (although not nuclear warheads, which would violate their treaty commitments). They must also collectively finance security, communications, and other upgrades to enable the expanded deployment of dual-capable aircraft. Most important, these nonnuclear allies would need to reiterate their commitment to forgoing nuclear weapons of their own, reaffirming the essential bargain that was cemented 60 years ago.

None of this will happen quickly or easily. It will require London, Paris, and Washington to give up an element of control over their nuclear arsenals that each has jealously guarded for more than 60 years. But the alternatives are worse. The security of Europe and the United States is, in fact, indivisible, no matter what the current administration in Washington may believe. If Europe were left to fend for itself and nuclear weapons spread from capital to capital, Americans would be less safe, not more. Yet U.S. retrenchment is also real, and no amount of reassurance from Washington will restore the credibility of a nuclear guarantee that Europeans have come to doubt. With the United States pulling back, a new bargain in which Europe carries more of the nuclear burden and has more say in the choice to use these weapons is the only way to ensure transatlantic security.

How Battlefield Cooperation Is Binding Together America’s Adversaries

Andrea Kendall-Taylor and Richard Fontaine

On July 17, an Iranian missile struck Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan, killing three American soldiers and wounding four others. The strike was unusually precise. In the weeks that followed, U.S. officials discovered that before the attack, Tehran had received high-resolution satellite images of the base that could aid its targeting. Afterward, Iran obtained more imagery, potentially helping it assess the damage and hone future attacks. The images came from Chinese entities.

China is not alone in assisting Iran. Russia, too, has helped Tehran find and hit American and allied targets. Two days before the March 27 attack on Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia that wounded 12 American personnel, 14 Russian spy satellites passed overhead. U.S. officials later concluded that Moscow had fed Iran satellite intelligence that aided Iranian precision strikes. Moscow has also provided Iran with weapons and military expertise, including upgraded versions of Iranian drones refined on the battlefield in Ukraine, and Russian engineers are helping Iran develop supersonic cruise missiles that could evade U.S. and Israeli air defenses.

Neither Russia nor China has directly entered Iran’s war with the United States. They haven’t needed to. By providing intelligence on American targets, military technology and components, and economic and diplomatic support, they can give Iran what it needs without joining its war.

The United States entered the conflict with advantages its adversaries lacked: a vast network of formal allies, military bases, intelligence-sharing arrangements, defense partnerships, and interoperable forces spanning Europe, the Middle East, and Asia. Yet many of Washington’s traditional partners were reluctant to associate themselves with the campaign, and some denied the United States specific forms of military access, at least for a time. Others publicly resisted U.S. calls to help secure the Strait of Hormuz.

The contrast illustrates a striking shift since we first described Russia, China, Iran, and North Korea as an axis of upheaval in Foreign Affairs two years ago. The United States’ adversaries began this war with this weaker network, and Washington with the far stronger one. But after helping Russia fight its war in Ukraine over the past few years, members of the axis are now finding ways to make their network work in a new theater. They are fine-tuning their cooperation to make their partners more resilient—and more dangerous to the United States—while limiting costs and risks. Washington, for its part, is creating friction in many of its partnerships instead of deepening them, and risks squandering its network just as its adversaries are learning to make more of theirs.

Much has changed since 2024. Most obviously, the United States itself has become a source of upheaval in the international system. The Trump administration has challenged international institutions, strained traditional alliances, and adopted a more transactional approach to foreign policy. It has upended trade agreements, threatened the sovereignty of allies such as Canada and Denmark, and embraced unpredictability. It has also pursued improved relations with China, North Korea, and Russia while seeking to weaken Iran. None of this has loosened the ties among the United States’ adversaries.

Early in the Trump administration, U.S. officials expressed hope that normalizing relations with Moscow might peel Russia away from the group. Yet President Donald Trump’s oft-expressed bonhomie toward the Kremlin has opened no gaps with the other three. As seen from Moscow, the administration has been unable to deliver a deal on Ukraine, and any Trump-driven rapprochement with Washington is unlikely to endure: Congress continues to pressure Russia, and a future administration may well adopt a tougher approach.

More fundamentally, attempts to weaken the axis misunderstand what holds it together. Its members object to an international order that they see as enshrining Western dominance at their expense. But more pointedly, they oppose the United States’ extraordinary power to constrain, isolate, and punish them, and potentially to threaten their regimes. Recent U.S. actions have only rekindled those concerns. The United States’ capture of Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro and the killing of Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei reinforce long-standing fears in Beijing, Moscow, Pyongyang, and Tehran about U.S. intervention and regime security.

The axis countries may moderate their anti-American rhetoric when doing so is tactically useful, but their motivation to reduce their vulnerability to American power remains. The Trump administration’s own skepticism about international order will not ease minds in axis capitals. On the contrary, Trump’s efforts to create a more transactional and coercive world may increase the value of their partnerships. As rules and institutions constrain less and brute power matters more, countries will seek partners to help them blunt sanctions, access critical technologies, compensate for military weaknesses, expand their room for maneuver, and resist isolation. Axis cooperation is evolving to meet the moment.

When Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, cooperation among the four countries was relatively easy to map. Iran provided Russia with drones, and North Korea sent ammunition, ballistic missiles, and later, troops. China became Moscow’s most critical lifeline, buying Russian oil and supplying the machine tools, microelectronics, and other components it needed first to sustain and then to expand its military production. Russia, in turn, transferred increasingly sophisticated military technology to its partners.

These exchanges were consequential, but they could still largely be understood as a series of bilateral transactions focused on Russia’s war. That is becoming less true. Cooperation is increasingly networked and geared toward solving new problems in new regions.

Russia’s drone program illustrates the change. Iran initially sold Russia its Shahed drones and then helped Moscow establish production at home. Russia did not simply replicate what it received; it incorporated Chinese components and continually modified the drones’ navigation, communications, payloads, propulsion, and tactics in response to Ukrainian defenses. New military units fed successful modifications back into production, generating an unusually rapid cycle of battlefield adaptation. Russia’s Unmanned Systems Forces, a drone-and-robotics branch of the Russian armed forces, was established last year to help institutionalize this cycle. The combination of domestic adaptation and axis partnership has proved effective. Russia’s latest jet-powered Geran drones, for instance, are faster, carry larger payloads, and are significantly harder for Ukrainian forces to intercept than earlier variants.

Russian improvements do not stay in Russia, either. They circulate through the axis network. For Iran, the technology that originally flowed to Russia is now returning, along with tactical advice drawn from Russia’s experience in Ukraine. If Russia’s jet-powered drones also show up in the Middle East, as they likely will, Iranian attacks on U.S. forces and regional energy infrastructure will become harder and costlier to defend against.

North Korea and China are also benefiting. According to Ukrainian reports, Russia struck a deal to help North Korea expand production of its Shahed-type drones. North Korean engineers have learned from time spent working in Russia’s massive Yelabuga drone factory, and North Korean forces fighting alongside Russia have gained firsthand experience in integrating drones into modern combat. Chinese military personnel have received training in drone warfare from Russian forces, and Chinese suppliers are gaining valuable information about how their technologies perform in combat. Beijing is thus seeing gains from the evolution of drone warfare that began with Iranian technology and has been refined in Russia’s war in Ukraine.

Two years ago, it was not clear whether partnerships among the four members of the axis could outlast the war that accelerated their formation. If the Ukraine war ended and Russia no longer needed Iranian drones, North Korean ammunition, or Chinese economic and technological support, the relationships might lose momentum.

Yet the war did not only create an immediate need for cooperation. It has also provided proof of concept for what these partnerships can accomplish, beyond what any country might attain on its own. Ukrainian defenses, Western sanctions, U.S. military supplies, and diplomatic isolation have all been less effective than would be the case if Russia were operating alone. By helping one another withstand pressure, the axis countries make it harder for the United States and its allies to compel them to change course.

Iran is now showing that the model can travel. Russia’s support for Tehran has been operationally meaningful but carefully bounded. Even as it fights its own war in Ukraine, Moscow has found ways to help Iran strike American targets and sustain the fight through diplomatic cover and material support while stopping short of actions that could draw Russia into direct conflict with the United States. China has been even more cautious, but its sharing of dual-use electronics and purchases of Iranian oil have similarly enhanced Iran’s ability to withstand U.S. pressure.

Such restraint is not evidence that the axis is weakening but rather a defining feature of how these partnerships work. With the striking exception of North Korean soldiers deployed on Russia’s behalf, the axis countries have generally avoided fighting one another’s wars. Instead, they provide political backing, economic support, intelligence, technology, and limited military assistance when doing so advances their own interests at manageable risk.

The wars in Ukraine and Iran have taught the axis another lesson: supporting a fellow member does not merely strengthen the recipient. Conflict in one theater can also create opportunities for partners elsewhere. Iran’s closure of the Strait of Hormuz, for example, sent oil prices soaring, delivering a windfall to Russia and prompting Washington to temporarily ease restrictions on Russian oil exports. Iran’s war thus generated resources Russia could use to sustain its own.

Before attacking Iran, the United States redeployed key military assets, including air and missile defenses, from the Indo-Pacific to the Middle East. As the fighting continued, U.S. forces put two aircraft carriers in the region, leaving none in the Indo-Pacific. China gains operational freedom from a thinner U.S. military presence in the western Pacific and is less vulnerable when U.S. munitions stocks are depleted. If Washington settles into a prolonged strategy of containing Iran, as it did with Iraq in the 1990s, Beijing could enjoy the advantages of those diversions for years.

The experience of the axis of upheaval shows that the size and strength of a network matter less if its members cannot turn their individual capabilities into collective power. The United States and its allies start from a position of considerable advantage: greater economic and technological resources, more capable militaries, and a far larger network of allies and partners. The question is which network can make better use of its assets.

That starts with leadership. For decades, the United States favored an international order that forbade wars of conquest, upheld freedom of the seas, maintained a generally open global economy, and allowed populations to exercise democratic rights, even if autocracies remained. Washington coupled this vision with an implicit bargain: any country that wished to join the order could do so. Upholding the order’s basic principles conferred benefits, including from the United States: trade relations, diplomatic preferences, sometimes military alliances.

For countries that rejected the open international order, as the axis countries do today, the benefits were far fewer and the penalties considerable. The United States sanctioned, pressured, and sometimes went to war against rogue states, and it resisted the establishment of competing orders such as the Cold War communist bloc. U.S. foreign policy started from the seemingly uncontroversial premise that Washington should befriend like-minded countries and treat them better than rejectionists.

The Trump administration demonstrates, at least at times, something like the opposite inclination. The U.S. ambassador to China recently said that Trump offered to sell Beijing weapons, while stalling arms sales to Taiwan. The president has expressed greater interest in and affection for North Korea’s dictator, Kim Jong Un, than for South Korea’s democratically elected president, Lee Jae-myung. The administration has also intermittently pressured Ukraine to cave in its war of survival against Russian aggression. And Trump has threatened to bomb Oman, a longtime partner. Such moves both strengthen the axis countries and weaken the cohesion of the network best positioned to confront them.

Doing no further harm would be a good start in dealing with the axis. Beyond that, Washington should help its partners grow stronger. Just as the axis countries are helping one another work around sanctions, preserve access to critical technologies and components, and sustain their economies under pressure, the United States should aid its own network. That means strengthening supply chains that run through trusted partners, sharing technology, and encouraging allies to take on greater security burdens. Washington must take care, however, not to transfer defense responsibilities faster than allies can develop the capabilities to assume them—and not to do so in ways that erode trust, interoperability, and coordination. Otherwise, the United States and its allies could become individually more capable but collectively less effective.

The United States should also reverse policies that undercut its own network. Tariffs and other protectionist measures harm allied economies and their industrial bases. Expressing a desire to seize allies’ territory and attempting to undermine their political leaders do not make for a more capable international network. Washington should project vision and leadership to its friends, not jarring uncertainty and bullying transactionalism.

Even if the United States changes course, American and allied policymakers should prepare for a long struggle. As the unsuccessful regime-change effort in Iran demonstrated, there are no shortcuts to besting the axis countries, together or individually. The contest of networks will be nearly global, waged in multiple domains, with huge stakes riding on the outcome. The United States and its allies have the capacity to prevail. They should demonstrate the will.

#5261. A Better “America First”

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

CHARLES KUPCHAN is Professor of International Affairs at Georgetown University and a Senior Fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations. He is the author of Bringing Order to Anarchy: Governing the World to Come.

What Trump Gets Right—and Wrong—About Foreign Policy

Critics from across the political spectrum have disparaged U.S. President Donald Trump’s “America first” brand of statecraft—and for good reason. He has alienated allies, appeased great-power rivals, launched yet another failed war of choice in the Middle East, and blundered into trade wars that have worsened the United States’ affordability crisis.

But as debate heats up about what the next administration’s foreign policy should look like, Democrats and Republicans alike must be careful not to throw the baby out with the bathwater. Trump is right to recognize that U.S. strategy is in need of an overhaul. Most of his instincts are sound and align with fundamental changes in the international system and in American public opinion. His “America first” foreign policy is a symptom, not a cause, of global disarray and the public’s turn against the establishment; many U.S. voters have been disillusioned by the country’s chronic overreach abroad and the affordability crisis at home. Trump successfully tapped into these grievances by promising to scale back international entanglements and bring back factories and manufacturing jobs.

These are worthy goals, but Trump’s policies have badly missed their mark. He has started new wars and mostly failed to end old ones, and his tariffs have fueled inflation while doing little to resuscitate the manufacturing sector. Trump has implemented his course corrections with marked incompetence, making policy by impulse, gutting the diplomatic corps, relying on an inexperienced inner circle, and prizing loyalty over fact.

The challenge ahead is not to reflexively restore what came before; liberal internationalism long served the United States well, but during an era that no longer exists. Instead of burying “America first,” Trump’s successor should salvage its best elements and implement them more capably. The next version of U.S. foreign policy should marry the realist impulses that shape “America first” with the liberal cause that remains at the heart of the American experience. That composite strategy would see Washington pare back its commitments abroad, reach across ideological dividing lines to work with great-power rivals, operate through nimble coalitions, and repair the United States’ broken social contract and ailing democracy. Foreign policy starts at home: only by redressing the socioeconomic discontent that has cleaved the body politic into hostile camps can the country recoup the bipartisan centrism and democratic vitality needed to anchor a purposeful and steady statecraft.

Two tectonic shifts are leading to a historic turning point. Power is rapidly shifting from West to East and North to South, as Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, Nigeria, and other large developing countries rise. The United States and its democratic allies, assuming they manage to stay partners, will remain an influential bloc, but no longer a preponderant one. As the diffusion of power fuels geopolitical rivalries, Americans are growing weary of entanglement in a fragmenting world. Meanwhile, technological advances made possible by the digital age have undermined the social contract of the industrial era and hollowed out the middle class. As broadly shared prosperity has given way to economic insecurity and inequality, political cleavages have emerged along socioeconomic, educational, and rural-urban lines. Liberal democracy is in retreat worldwide, including in the United States, where the centrist, bipartisan compact that long anchored U.S. statecraft has collapsed.

Enter Trump. By the time U.S. forces withdrew from Afghanistan in 2021, more than 70 percent of Americans deemed the two-decade war a failure. Speaking to an electorate fed up with the never-ending conflicts in the Middle East, Trump pledged “no more wars” as he ran for reelection in 2024. He began his second term by using force sparingly. The single-night air campaign against Iran’s nuclear installations in June 2025 and the in-and-out raid to seize Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro in January 2026 succeeded in their narrow aims. Perhaps emboldened by these low-cost wins, Trump then proceeded to launch a full-scale war against Iran in February 2026. By planning for a short conflict and getting stuck in a long one, he fell into the very trap that he swore to avoid.

Given that the United States keeps getting mired in errant conflicts, Congress urgently needs to reassert its constitutional obligation to approve acts of war and oversee retrenchment. The bar for engaging in major conflicts outside the core strategic areas of Europe and East Asia should be quite high. It is past time for Washington to return to its traditional geopolitical vocation: playing the role of great-power balancer in order to preserve stability in the strategic heartland. American strategy toward Europe and East Asia has been a steady and cost-effective success, facilitating great-power peace since 1945. The roughly 150,000 U.S. troops still stationed in these core theaters are a wise investment in extending that track record.

A retrenchment from the Middle East and other peripheral areas does not mean a comprehensive retreat, but it does mean relying far more heavily on diplomacy and economic engagement than on the military. When force is required to counter threats to U.S. interests or allies, the United States should generally limit itself to supporting regional partners and relying on special forces and surgical strikes. As it seeks to end its addiction to military force, Washington could take a page from Beijing: China has spread its influence and maximized its leverage in the global South not through armed intervention but through investments, trade, and the Belt and Road Initiative, the biggest infrastructure project in modern history.

As the United States refocuses its attention and resources on maintaining stability in Asia and Europe, it can nonetheless lighten its load by rebalancing its alliances. Trump made headway by pushing allies to shoulder their fair share of the defense burden; NATO members and Asian allies are substantially increasing their defense budgets. But true to form, Trump has marred this success by taking actions that corrode decades of trust, such as threatening to seize Greenland and repeatedly demeaning allied leaders.

Allies are force multipliers; rather than alienating them, the United States should hold them even closer as they beef up their militaries. Washington should work with allies’ defense industries and military planners to come up with a new division of labor, set force levels, and create a realistic timetable to meet their goals. The United States’ network of defense pacts is poised to get stronger as its allies get stronger, but only if rebalancing occurs with consent, foresight, and respectful treatment of partners.

Adjusting U.S. strategy to the onset of multipolarity also entails building on Trump’s recognition that regional spheres of influence are making a comeback. As power diffuses across the international system, Washington will be neither willing nor able to oversee all the world’s main strategic theaters. Instead, major powers in each theater will play a more prominent role in guiding projects of regional integration, shaping regional order, and providing public goods.

For decades, most American policymakers have denounced spheres of influence. In 2021, Secretary of State Antony Blinken called the concept “a recipe for an international system that falls apart.” Yet since the founding era, the United States has prioritized establishing and defending its own sphere of influence in the Western Hemisphere. During the Cold War, Washington granted the Soviet Union a buffer zone in Eastern Europe while entrenching its own zones of control in Western Europe, Northeast Asia, and the Middle East.

Trump recognizes this double standard. The 2025 National Security Strategy endorses a reassertion of U.S. dominance in the Western Hemisphere and calls on other countries to “assume primary responsibility for their regions.” Trump has exhibited long overdue regard for the strategic interests of other great powers and appropriate caution about the extension of U.S. commitments into their backyards. He has precluded the possibility of Ukraine’s entry into NATO, aware that membership would run afoul of Russia’s legitimate security interests and overextend the alliance. He is also looking for ways to reduce tensions in East Asia, pausing arms sales to Taiwan and canceling military exercises with South Korea.

But even if Trump is right to herald a return of spheres of influence, he has acted with self-defeating excess. In the Western Hemisphere, he has been driving away Canada, treating Venezuela like a U.S. colony, strangling Cuba, ordering strikes of questionable legality against boats allegedly running drugs, and renaming the Gulf of Mexico and Lake Ontario. Instead of working toward a benign sphere of influence that attracts neighbors and promotes stability, Trump has been building a malign sphere of coercion that prompts other countries to run for cover.

Trump is making a similar mess in other theaters. Even if he is right to close NATO’s door to Ukraine, he has failed to convince Russia to end its war of aggression. A vision of a stable security order in Europe is nowhere to be found. And although regional integration offers the best bet for creating the stronger Europe that Trump seeks, he is backing far-right parties hostile to the European Union. Trump’s approach to East Asia is similarly muddled. He holds summits with Chinese leader Xi Jinping and hopes to do the same with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, but his administration lacks a coherent strategy for China or northeast Asia more broadly.

The diffusion of power makes a more regionalized world inevitable, and to maintain order in that world, the United States will need to work with China, Russia, and other countries that hold sway in their neighborhoods. Trump’s successors will need to salvage his defective effort to help guide this transition. Washington needs to exercise a restrained and lawful brand of hegemony in the Western Hemisphere, respecting the sovereignty of its neighbors and pursuing mutual gains. That approach will encourage other powers to do the same in their own neighborhoods. To that end, Washington needs to grant Russia and China more breathing room in return for their readiness to exercise restraint within their regions. Moscow and Beijing might well take a less coercive stance toward their neighbors if the United States were to reduce its own military presence in and designs on their rimlands.

It will be particularly important for the United States to work with China, its only peer competitor, to de-escalate tensions without encouraging Chinese expansionism. On one hand, the United States needs to make allowances for growing Chinese power and ambition. A new regional order in Asia will endure only if the reigning hegemon and rising challenger can come to terms on the distribution of power and influence within the order. On the other hand, Washington cannot allow Chinese aggression against its neighbors. The United States needs to walk a fine line between prudent accommodation and dangerous appeasement. The goal in the long term would be to establish a multilateral system of regional governance—anchored by China but accepted by its neighbors.

Trump’s 2025 National Security Strategy embraces what it calls “flexible realism,” a pragmatic foreign policy that aims to establish good relations with all countries “without imposing on them democratic or other social change that differs widely from their traditions and histories.” This readiness to work across ideological lines is an important corrective to the “democracy versus autocracy” framework that guided the Biden administration’s foreign policy. The United States must navigate the world as it is, not as it would like it to be. Trump is right to reach out to autocracies and set aside the zealous promotion of democracy.

Trump’s flexible realism also entails recognizing the need to form bespoke coalitions of the willing to augment the United Nations and other large multilateral institutions. When it comes to preventing or stopping major conflicts, the UN has largely been a bystander. Support for formal multilateralism has also evaporated in Washington; Republicans, in particular, have embraced a hostility toward institutionalized international commitments. Coalitions of the willing are already emerging as the vehicles of choice for resolving conflicts. China, France, Germany, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States negotiated the 2015 nuclear deal with Iran. Oman, Pakistan, and Qatar have led efforts to mediate between Iran and the United States. Meanwhile, a coalition of Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and the United States has been working to end Sudan’s civil war.

Yet even if Trump’s brand of realism marks a needed course correction, its implementation has been deeply flawed. Trump’s outreach to Xi and Russian President Vladimir Putin occurs in fits and starts and lacks a clear set of objectives. Washington’s unilateralism and fondness for ad hoc coalitions have also gone too far. The UN system admittedly falls woefully short on managing most security issues, but it plays an important role in humanitarian relief, global health, and climate change. By slashing humanitarian assistance and withdrawing from the World Health Organization and the Paris climate agreement, Trump has hobbled effective multilateral initiatives. And even though his signature coalition of the willing, the Board of Peace, has potential, it was thrown together haphazardly. The board’s first mission—to pacify and bring lawful governance to Gaza—has been an utter failure.

Whoever comes after Trump should continue his pragmatic effort to work with autocracies. But that effort must be guided by a clear set of strategic objectives, promote geopolitical stability, and be anchored by the sustained work of experts, not just a handful of financiers. Experienced U.S. diplomats should thoroughly prepare summits and identify concrete deliverables with their foreign counterparts. Summits between world leaders are critical opportunities to advance the cause of global stability and must be treated as such.

Greater reliance on coalitions of the willing is the right move, but they need to be fit for their purpose. The Board of Peace is certainly not. It has over 25 members, including countries that carry little diplomatic weight, such as Kosovo, Mongolia, and Paraguay. Instead, the board should function as a concert of great powers, both democratic and autocratic, tasked with managing crises and overseeing and building consensus on matters of global governance. Unlike the UN Security Council, which is often paralyzed by veto, this steering group would forgo binding rules and formal charters. It would strive for consensus through the give-and-take of diplomacy, providing a venue for sustained engagement that currently does not exist.

Even as it sets up a great-power steering group to manage core security issues, Washington should resume supporting the UN to advance human welfare. The World Food Program, the World Health Organization, and the Paris climate agreement may not be perfect, but Washington should try to make them more effective, not undermine them. And when the United States defects, others follow. France, Sweden, and the United Kingdom all slashed spending on foreign assistance after the United States gutted its own budget. Washington needs to be a leader, not a laggard, on issues of human welfare. Doing so is not only a matter of altruism; the United States itself will have to grapple with migrants fleeing failing states, pandemics, and the dangers of climate change.

Finally, although the United States should continue to practice ideological pluralism and work with autocracies, that effort must be paired with a revival of democracy at home. Americans have no choice but to learn to live comfortably in a politically diverse world. Yet they should do so while upholding the original version of U.S. exceptionalism, which was to spread republican ideals by serving as a democratic exemplar. Americans should not give up on their messianic mission to bend the arc of history toward more freedom. But instead of seeking to foist democracy on others, they should pursue this mission by refurbishing the American experiment and demonstrating that representative government can outperform the autocratic alternatives.

The antiestablishment wave that Trump rode to power in 2016 and 2024 is animated by discontent with the quality of life at home at least as much as overreach abroad. Much of Trump’s voter base feels left behind by automation and globalization, displaced by immigrants, and sidelined by progressive social and cultural trends. Trump has responded by cracking down on immigrants and attacking “DEI”—diversity, equity, and inclusion. At the center of his domestic agenda is his pledge to rebuild the middle class. As he ran for reelection in 2024, Trump promised that higher tariffs would turn the United States back into a “manufacturing powerhouse,” with plenty of factory jobs to go around.

Trump is right to want to tame inequality, scale back some of the excesses of globalization, and secure supply chains of critical goods. But his protectionism won’t revive the manufacturing sector. Automation, not free trade, is the main cause of the decades-long decline in manufacturing jobs. Despite years of tariffs and industrial policy, manufacturing jobs have continued to disappear. And the rate at which machines replace humans will pick up as firms grow reliant on artificial intelligence. Around 80 percent of the U.S. workforce already works in the service sector, and most will not be headed back to the production line. In the meantime, Trump’s tariffs, combined with the supply chain disruptions caused by the conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East, have significantly raised the cost of living—the main reason that Republicans are heading into the midterms in deep political trouble.

Instead of promising to claw back manufacturing jobs that are gone for good, the next administration should train Americans for the jobs of the future. The public sector must work with the private sector to fashion an education and employment ecosystem fit for the digital age. In the decades ahead, most Americans will work in information technology, data processing, education, finance, health care, travel, hospitality, leisure, and retail. And as AI disrupts labor markets in many different sectors, workers will need transferable skills that will allow them to adapt. Expanding worker retraining programs, investing in the ailing public school system, and improving access to community colleges will do far more than tariffs to rebuild the middle class—and are the first steps in forging a new social contract. A credible plan for getting Americans back on their feet is critical to repairing the nation’s democracy and steadying its statecraft.

Even if the Democrats take back control of one or both chambers in the November midterms—which appears likely—they will struggle to change Trump’s course. He ignores Congress on most issues, but especially on matters of foreign policy, over which the legislative branch has limited power.

Accordingly, Americans have little choice but to ride out the Trump era and try to limit the damage that he does by defending democracy at home. But getting ready for the next administration requires drawing the right lessons from his errant presidency. Trump has begun implementing a needed overhaul of U.S. grand strategy, but he is incapable of turning good instincts into sound policy. It will be up to his successor to better align U.S. grand strategy with a changing world and provide the judgment and competence missing from Trump’s time in charge.

The highlights this week: Chinese exports make their biggest monthly jump in 16 years, the Chinese Communist Party prepares for an important political meeting, and the former White House press secretary’s law career in China draws scrutiny.

China’s exports grew by an astonishing 36.3 percent year-over-year in September, their biggest monthly jump since July 2010, thanks to the artificial intelligence industry and the easing of trade tensions with the United States.

It’s only one month, but it fits a pattern of soaring exports since the COVID-19 pandemic that cemented China’s place as the world’s factory. Exports will likely exceed $4 trillion this year. Unlike the manufacturing boom of the 2000s, this one is increasingly concentrated in high-end sectors such as electric vehicles, smartphones, and power grid management.

The paradox is that this success is partly thanks to a domestic economy still staggering from the twin blows of the pandemic and the ensuing real estate crisis. The latter has devastated household finances, while the former made people even more nervous about spending, especially given how little public support most households received during that time.

Those weaknesses fueled the manufacturing surge in several ways. Deflation at home, especially at the factory gate, made Chinese products more price-competitive. But that may be coming to an end this year; producer prices have risen for seven consecutive months as the government pushes to rebalance supply and demand.

Manufacturing has also benefited from cheaper credit as the property sector has crashed. Real estate lending once formed an unhealthy loop in which property giants borrowed from banks to buy land from local governments. That loop has broken, with land sales down 30 percent this year alone and loans flowing into manufacturing instead. A tight regulatory environment at home has also made foreign markets more tempting for Chinese firms.

Other factors are at work, too, including the growing demand for AI-related products and services and China’s heavy investment in alternative energy and EVs. But domestic weakness is what allows such astonishing export growth to coexist with relatively weak GDP growth for a developing country of China’s size.

GDP growth this quarter was stronger than expected, and based on this year’s figures so far, annual growth will probably be 4.6 percent, toward the lower end of the official target range of 4.5 to 5 percent. But those figures are still far below pre-pandemic levels, and the true figure may be lower still if the more skeptical analysts are correct.

Beijing has tried to cool the boom, conscious of both an imbalanced economy and global pushback against exporting overcapacity and deflation. But it has also benefited from the rest of the world’s failure to respond effectively.

By now it’s clear that U.S. President Donald Trump’s trade policies ended up fueling Chinese export growth rather than restricting it by making Beijing appear like a better partner than Washington. Even Canada, which had locked horns with China only a few years earlier, has shifted course in Trump’s second term.

Consumers in the developing world are happy to get cheap, high-quality goods, even as those imports swamp local industries. The only real pushback to China in this area may come from an increasingly alarmed Europe, which has both the will and leverage to extract concessions from Beijing.

Proposed Ukraine summit. Russian President Vladimir Putin has proposed a trilateral summit with Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping to decide the future of Ukraine—without Ukraine’s involvement. Though this vision of dividing the world among themselves is undoubtedly meant to play to Trump’s ego, it will not result in much.

For one, Europe—and Ukraine itself—is extremely unlikely to cooperate. But China also cannot trust any grand bargain with Trump. Beijing has always had a keen nose for political weakness, and its general mistrust of the United States has made it quicker than most to realize that Trump rarely keeps his promises.

Fifth Plenum approaches. The Chinese Communist Party will hold the Fifth Plenum of its 20th Party Congress in Beijing next week. Asia Society has a useful look-ahead, as does Deng Yuwen in FP.

Plenums are held at regular intervals between party congresses, and traditionally the fifth plenum of each cycle has concentrated on the next five-year plan. But amid Xi’s purges, the focus is likely to be on party discipline and anti-corruption efforts.

Law in China. Katie Zacharia’s brief tenure as Trump’s press secretary took place almost entirely in the span since last week’s China Brief. In that time, she offered a lesson in interpreting—and overinterpreting—careers that overlap with China.

Zacharia, a lawyer, had never passed the bar exam in the United States but had worked for Zhong Lun, a major Chinese law firm, from 2011 to 2013. Foreign lawyers cannot technically practice law in China, but they can advise Chinese firms on international law.

A role like that would normally draw scrutiny in U.S. security clearance vetting, because time in China can leave people vulnerable to recruitment by foreign intelligence, but the Trump administration has ridden roughshod over that process anyway.

Moreover, Zacharia’s former job was not an impressive one, akin to the so-called white monkey jobs that proliferated in China at the time, wherein businesses hired foreigners simply to appear more international.

In general, low-level jobs in China can be useful for Americans hoping to understand how Chinese systems work. Perhaps not for Zacharia, though.

Russian satellites. China’s assistance to Russia in its war on Ukraine extends to orbit, where Chinese firms have been supplying key satellites to Russian defense-industrial start-ups.

But there’s a twist: Russian firms have been trying to pass off this technology as domestic, not to avoid foreign scrutiny but to comply with Moscow’s push to boost its own industries. Russia is slowly becoming technologically dependent on China—to an extent that even Moscow itself may not fully realize.

#5253. Europe’s Credibility Gap

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

CLAAS MERTENS is a Research Fellow at the Blavatnik School of Government and Worcester College, University of Oxford.

NGAIRE WOODS is Professor of Global Economic Governance and Founding Dean of the Blavatnik School of Government at the University of Oxford.

Dependence on America Has Left It With Little to Offer Would-Be Partners

The second Trump administration’s National Security Strategy declared that “the days of the United States propping up the entire world order like Atlas are over.” That sentiment has animated much of the administration’s foreign policy over the past two years, and its approach to Europe has been no exception. In 2025, the United States suspended military aid to Ukraine, and in May of this year, it announced it would remove 5,000 troops from Germany. President Donald Trump has threatened to seize Greenland, the territory of a fellow NATO country, and promised to levy tariffs against member states that opposed the effort. When the United States and Israel attacked Iran in February, they neither consulted nor warned European powers, which have been left to face the war’s economic consequences largely alone.

Europe has not stood idly by amid this onslaught. It stepped in to fill the gaps left by the United States’ withdrawal of financial support for Ukraine in 2025. During the Greenland episode, European countries facing tariffs convinced Trump to back down by threatening massive retaliatory trade measures against the United States. And the continent has begun to deepen its other relationships. The EU has set up a $168 billion defense-loan scheme, with Canada as its first non-European member. Earlier this year, it finalized the largest trade agreement in its history, with India, and signed a long-delayed deal with the South American economic bloc Mercosur.

These measures have been encouraging departures from the bureaucratic indecisiveness for which the EU has frequently been criticized. But despite its leaders’ newfound interest in self-sufficiency, Europe remains dependent on the United States. In fact, some of its attempts to stand on its own have made it more dependent on the United States. European defense planning, command, nuclear deterrence, and military enabling systems still rely on American capabilities. Increased defense spending buys American weapons. As of 2025, the United States supplied more than half of the EU’s liquefied natural gas imports. Many of the continent’s most promising technology firms still move to New York or San Francisco.

This continued dependence has in turn weakened Europe’s credibility with other countries seeking new security, technology, and economic ties of their own. For decades, the European offer to these countries relied on the continent’s relationship with Washington. As long as the United States underwrote European security and welcomed Europe’s integration with its economy, that offer was an attractive one. Now that Washington has begun to treat both arrangements as negotiable, however, it no longer is. The weaker the transatlantic relationship becomes, the more Europe needs other countries’ export markets, raw materials, defense industrial capacity, and solidarity against coercion—and the less able it is to convince those countries of its worth as a partner. Europe needs a better offer, grounded in its own capabilities rather than those of its mercurial ally.

European countries have long presented themselves as leaders on issues such as the environment, consumer protection, and high regulatory standards. To neighboring countries, Europe dangled membership in and association with the EU. Beyond the continent, it promised developmental and humanitarian partnerships in the form of aid through the EU and national agencies. Until recently, these were appealing terms: 13 countries have joined the EU since 2004, and more than 140 countries accepted some form of aid from EU or member state programs.

The United States upheld the underlying security and economic order that made these offers possible. EU aspirants could count on Washington to underwrite Europe’s defense through NATO, and and non-European countries could count on a U.S.-led rules-based economic order, within which they could easily access European aid, markets, and investment.

But the U.S. retrenchment has accelerated the emergence of a more bellicose “might is right” disorder in which different needs have risen to the fore. Countries are now seeking security partnerships to shore up their defense capabilities, stable trade relationships to protect against economic warfare, and financing to make up for cuts in aid and increases in the costs of government borrowing.

For example, when Ukraine turned to Europe in the early 2010s, it did so because of the prosperity and modernization that economic integration promised. But Kyiv now needs intelligence and weapons to fend off Russia, which Europe can deliver only through the United States. For countries similarly seeking security assistance and cooperation, dependence on such a fickle partner is a liability.

Partners hit by Trump’s tariffs need access to reliable markets to export goods and services once destined for the United States. But Europe has felt the need to defend its own economies instead of opening up its markets to them. For instance, the European Commission drastically increased tariffs on steel and reduced its quotas for duty-free imports, blocking India, South Korea, and Turkey from selling into the European market—precisely as those countries were searching for alternative markets to the United States.

Other countries need financing for infrastructure and the energy transition. Europe has the firepower to meet these countries’ needs, but its commitments take far too long to be realized as completed projects. And as in security, the U.S. withdrawal has left Europe adrift. Joint G-7 packages to finance clean energy buildouts in Indonesia, South Africa, and Vietnam, for example, suffered when Washington withdrew its support in 2025.

Europe has lately shown some ability to rise to the occasion. In 2025, EU member states’ combined annual military expenditure was approximately $461 billion, less than the United States’ $954 billion, but higher than China’s $336 billion and Russia’s $190 billion. When the United States wound down its aid to Ukraine in 2025, European governments made up most of the shortfall and have since committed to financing Kyiv’s war effort through 2027. The continent also has an existing defense industrial base, two permanent UN Security Council seats, and nuclear forces. Partners just beyond the core add more: Ukraine has a battle-tested army and an innovative drone industry, and Turkey has NATO’s second-largest army and access to the strategically important Black and Mediterranean Seas.

There is similar cause for optimism in trade and finance. The EU’s 2026 trade agreement with India removed nearly all tariffs for both parties, reduced nontariff barriers to trade, and made it easier for Indians and Europeans to temporarily work in each other’s markets. The EU’s Global Gateway investment program, Europe’s answer to China’s Belt and Road Initiative, passed its $335 billion mobilization target ahead of schedule.

These individual successes show that Europe has the potential to present partners with a more compelling offer. Yet much of that potential remains unfulfilled.

Europe has substantial assets across security, trade, and finance, but it lacks the internal cohesion needed to bundle them into a comprehensive package to attract other countries and bolster its own autonomy. When Europe could fall back on the United States’ leadership in institutions such as the World Trade Organization and NATO, internal disunity was less of a problem: American military commanders not only supported but organized European defense, and U.S.-backed rules governing global trade opened foreign markets to European exports, reducing the need to negotiate bilateral agreements. Without that leadership, the continent has been left to overcome its internal fragmentation on its own.

Nowhere is that fragmentation more apparent than on security. European leaders have committed to improving the continent’s defense in response to Washington’s wavering on NATO, but defense budgets, industries, and forces remain primarily national. As governments prioritize negotiating the best deals for domestic firms and other constituencies, conflicts arise: joint projects fail, purchases overlap, redundant R & D efforts waste precious resources. These frustrations have ultimately led the continent to simply purchase more U.S. systems. The EU’s Security Action for Europe loan program aims to reverse this trend by financing member states’ joint purchases of European equipment. But loans alone will neither overcome distributional conflicts nor create common command structures, standards, and logistics.

Europe does negotiate its trade deals with one voice. But many agreements need to be ratified in each country, leaving them at the mercy of domestic lobbies, from farmers to automakers, which seek to protect their markets at the expense of the continent’s collective interest. More than a decade after Canada and the EU negotiated the Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement, for example, just 17 of the organization’s 27 member states have ratified it. The 2026 trade deal with Mercosur, in the works for decades, was immediately contested, politically and legally, by individual member states and their representatives in the European Parliament.

On finance, Europe does not lack money but channels it through a byzantine set of EU instruments, member-state agencies, development banks, and changing flagship initiatives. Partners are left to navigate this morass of funding sources, each with their own procedures, timelines, and conditions. In Ghana, for example, Europe is present through EU programming, member-state development agencies, the European Investment Bank, and Global Gateway initiatives. When the Trump administration’s cuts to the U.S. Agency for International Development hit Accra, a more coordinated Europe might have been able to make its finance more visible, usable, and politically credible.

Many of these challenges predate the U.S. retrenchment. But they were less damaging to Europe’s other relationships as long as Washington upheld an imperfect but generally secure global order. Now that U.S. leadership is no longer part of the European offer, though, they loom much larger for potential partners.

The United States is not the only great power to have weaponized its chokepoints. Beijing and Moscow have also used their dominance over important industries coercively. Europe’s problem is that attempts to reduce the continent’s exposure to Russian and Chinese chokepoints too easily increase its dependence on the United States. Weaning itself off Russian pipeline gas means buying American energy. Procuring rare-earth elements outside China’s supply chains means cooperating with Washington on critical mineral exploration. When, for instance, Beijing weaponized its demand for French cognac by imposing duties on the liquor in October 2024, the industry’s best alternative was the U.S. market—until the Trump administration levied tariffs of its own. A more enticing offer to other partners must be built on economic ties and capabilities that are not beholden to American commitments.

To make such an offer, Europe must first reduce its reliance on U.S. defense commitments and equipment, moving toward becoming a more equal partner within NATO. Although Europe is unlikely to replace the United States as a global security guarantor, it needs to be able to offer third countries viable security partnerships, sustained deterrence in its neighborhood, and the ability to act with coherence when American priorities shift.

It can begin by working faster and more efficiently with its current friends. Since 2022, Poland has bought South Korean tanks, howitzers, aircraft, and rocket artillery; more recent agreements have moved beyond off-the-shelf purchases toward technology transfers that enable production, service, and repair in Poland. Japan has joined with Italy and the United Kingdom to develop a next-generation combat aircraft through the three countries’ collaborative treaty-based Global Combat Air Programme. The EU-India Security and Defense Partnership signed in January 2026 provides a framework for cooperation spanning maritime security, emerging technologies, and the defense industry. Europe should build on this approach, making use of its partners’ strengths to benefit both their and its own security.

European dependence goes well beyond security, however. Europe has the scale, capital, universities, industrial base, and regulatory capacity to be a tech power. But a lack of later-stage financing, fragmented product markets, and shortages of specialized talent and compute have too often driven European entrepreneurs, including the founders of such high-tech leaders as Hugging Face, Arm, and Wise, to start, expand, or list their companies in the United States.

These companies might be persuaded to remain in Europe if they could count on investments and a truly single market. But only a tiny percentage of European pension fund assets are currently invested in venture capital. The EU has announced a slew of initiatives to increase venture capital funding, but it still lacks anything comparable in size to the biggest U.S. funds. The harder tasks are to integrate capital markets, reduce regulatory differences between member states, and dismantle barriers to cross-border investment and commerce. A Europe-wide financial system and consumer market would make investment more attractive, keeping the continent’s most promising innovators from crossing the Atlantic in search of funding and customers. A competitive tech apparatus would give Europe not just control over the leading technologies it produces but also the ability to offer partners a viable alternative to the U.S. innovation ecosystem.

Europe must also reduce its energy and raw-material dependence. As the war in Iran has shown, exposure to volatile fossil fuel markets can harm overreliant economies. The International Energy Agency has noted that renewable energy has partly insulated European economies from truly disastrous fossil fuel price shocks. But the risk of shortages of refined petroleum products, especially diesel and jet fuel, is a daily reminder of the continent’s ongoing reliance on imports over which it has no control.

Integrated energy markets offer a way to prevent such dependence from forcing U-turns on foreign policy. After Russia’s February 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Germany, which in 2021 had imported more than half of its gas from Russia, initially opposed an embargo on Russian oil and gas before reversing course in May of that year. When Russian gas subsequently stopped flowing, Berlin was spared shortages and a more severe economic crisis largely thanks to other European countries, which provided emergency supplies. The continent seems to have learned the right lessons from the episode: the European Commission’s 2026 work program, its annual legislative agenda, explicitly commits to building a “genuine Energy Union” through electric grid upgrades and the removal of cross-border bottlenecks. The more resilient its energy system becomes, the more reliable a partner Europe will be.

In raw materials, on the other hand, it has little hope of achieving self-sufficiency. The European Commission’s planned Critical Raw Materials Centre will gather market intelligence, coordinate joint purchasing, and facilitate stockpiling. But demand-side measures alone cannot produce the raw materials the continent needs. Europe could start to build a global network of partner countries by creating the conditions for investment in mines and refineries across Africa, the Americas, and Asia. Because mines, processing plants, and the infrastructure to transport their outputs require long planning horizons and prices are volatile, joint actions could make projects more financially attractive and less risky and would ensure the minerals produced have a market.

Finally, Europe needs the ability to retaliate when China or the United States threatens to choke it. Europe’s response to U.S. tariff threats exposed how difficult it can be to get European powers to agree to retaliate. But it does have a powerful tool at its disposal. In 2023, Europe created its Anti-Coercion Instrument, a bundle of economic countermeasures, including tariffs and restrictions on imports or exports and restrictions on access to EU public procurement, foreign direct investment, and intellectual property rights, that in essence permits European countries to weaponize other countries’ dependence on economic interactions with them. A global ACI, expanded to pool non-European partners’ resources for more powerful joint retaliatory measures, would not solve every problem for Europe and its partners, but it could swiftly convert some of Europe’s strongest assets into a partnership offer with real security benefits.

Europe’s relationship with the United States was founded on shared values, mutual reliance, and reciprocity. It enabled European countries to focus on rebuilding their economies after World War II and on building institutions to avert future wars on the continent. The tumult of the last two years has revealed that Europe took the benefits of that relationship for granted. Today, the continent faces different threats than it did 80 years ago, and it can no longer count on the United States to come to its aid. Rather than mourn the loss of its partner in Washington, Europe must find new ones built on the recognition of a different kind of mutual reliance: that most countries have little hope of resisting great-power coercion if they go it alone.

Building these new relationships to reduce European dependence on the United States would not mean shutting out Washington entirely. Both sides would still benefit from security cooperation through NATO, from continued trade and investment, and from an integrated international financial system. Cooperation with the United States would remain valuable but no longer indispensable. Europe will get better deals from the Trump administration and its successors if it can negotiate from a position of strength, undergirded by more diverse partnerships.

Those partnerships will not emerge overnight. Nor will a new coalition become a unified strategic actor immediately. But an improved European offer will set that coalition on the right path. As with all important endeavors, the hardest part is getting started.
