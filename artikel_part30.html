#4950. Amazon, Google among self-funded employers joining CMS' prior auth pledge

The Trump administration revealed Wednesday that five of the country's largest employers will expand electronic prior authorization in self-funded plans.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services and Department of Labor announced that the companies have signed a statement of support to invest in the technical and other capabilities necessary to advance electronic prior auth, which was outlined in insurers' 2025 pledge on the topic.

The participating employers are Amazon, Google, Meta, Oracle Corporation and Axon Enterprise, per the announcement. CMS and the Labor Department said they have been collaborating to help bring the prior authorization pledge to the self-funded employer market.

"We’ve brought insurers, providers and technology companies to the table to modernize prior authorization, and now employers are helping us take another important step," said CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., in the announcement. "When the entire healthcare system moves together, we can spend less time on paperwork and more time getting patients the care they need."

In June 2025, insurers from across the country—including the largest firms as well as smaller payers—agreed to take steps to reform prior authorization, including embracing electronic avenues to prior authorization. A framework for electronic submissions is set to be established and in place by Jan. 1, 2027.

In April, data from major payer industry organizations suggested insurers had eliminated 11% of prior auths under the reform pledge. Payers had agreed to demonstrate tangible progress toward improvements by the midpoint of 2026.

Self-funded employers generally rely on third-party administrators to manage medical costs as well as services like prior auth. More than half (53%) of Americans are covered by self-funded employer plans, according to Census data, so unlocking improvements there is critical to reach about 180 million people.

Broader adoption of electronic prior authorization can ease administrative burdens for providers, who face a fragmented experience based on a person's individual plan, CMS said. And for patients, it would likely lead to fewer delays and gaps in care thanks to speedier decisions.

The agency urged other employers that might be interested in joining the pledge to reach out.

"By signing this statement of support for the expansion of electronic prior authorization reform, government and private industry are working together on behalf of the American workforce," said Department of Labor Assistant Secretary for the Employee Benefits Security Administration Daniel Aronowitz in the announcement.

#4949. Mysterious brain activity under anesthesia challenges what we know about consciousness

Even when a person is fully unconscious under general anesthesia, their brain may still be doing something remarkable: processing language, recognizing patterns, and even anticipating which words will come next.

Researchers at Baylor College of Medicine have discovered that the brain retains surprisingly sophisticated abilities during anesthesia, despite the absence of conscious awareness. The findings, published in the journal Nature, challenge traditional assumptions about the relationship between consciousness and thinking. They could also provide new insights into how the brain processes memories and language, potentially leading to advances in technologies that help people communicate after neurological injuries.

"Our findings show that the brain is far more active and capable during unconsciousness than previously thought," said Dr. Sameer Sheth, professor and Cullen Foundation Endowed chair of neurosurgery and a McNair Scholar at Baylor. "Even when patients are fully anesthetized, their brains continue to analyze the world around them."

Recording Brain Activity During General Anesthesia

To investigate what happens inside the unconscious brain, Sheth, who is also a neurosurgeon at Baylor St. Luke's Medical Center, and his colleagues studied patients undergoing epilepsy surgery while under general anesthesia.

These procedures offered an unusual opportunity to examine the hippocampus, a brain structure deeply involved in learning and memory. Located within the brain's temporal lobe, the hippocampus helps organize information and plays an important role in forming new memories.

The researchers used advanced recording devices called Neuropixels probes to monitor electrical signals from hundreds of individual neurons in the hippocampus. These tiny probes can detect activity from many nerve cells simultaneously, providing a detailed picture of how groups of neurons respond to information.

According to the researchers, this technology had not previously been used to investigate this particular brain region in this way. It allowed the team to examine how the hippocampus responds to sounds and language while patients are unconscious and unable to report what they experience.

The first experiment involved playing a series of repeating tones, occasionally interrupted by a sound that differed from the others.

Although the patients were under anesthesia, neurons in their hippocampus responded differently to the unexpected sounds. Even more surprisingly, the brain cells became better at distinguishing these unusual tones as the experiment continued.

This improvement suggested that the hippocampus was showing signs of neural plasticity, the brain's ability to change its responses through experience. In other words, some of the basic processes associated with learning appeared to remain active even without conscious awareness.

The Unconscious Brain Can Process Language and Predict Words

The researchers then tested whether the unconscious brain could handle something more complicated than simple sounds.

They played short stories to the anesthetized patients while continuing to record activity from neurons in the hippocampus.

The results revealed a surprising level of language processing. Distinct patterns of neural activity contained information about different categories of words, including nouns, verbs, and adjectives. This suggested that the hippocampus was responding to meaningful features of language rather than simply detecting that sounds were occurring.

But the most intriguing discovery went a step further.

The researchers found that neural activity also carried information that could be used to predict upcoming words in a sentence. Even though the patients were unconscious, their brains appeared to anticipate how the language unfolding around them might continue.

"The brain appears to anticipate what comes next in a story, even without conscious awareness," said Sheth, who is also Director of The Gordon and Mary Cain Pediatric Neurology Research Foundation Laboratories within the Duncan Neurological Research Institute at Texas Children's Hospital.

This ability is related to a process known as predictive coding. Rather than passively receiving information, the brain continually uses patterns and previous experience to anticipate what might happen next. When listening to someone speak, for example, the brain can use the words already heard to form expectations about what is likely to follow.

Scientists have typically studied this kind of sophisticated prediction in people who are awake and paying attention. Finding evidence of it during general anesthesia suggests that at least some of the underlying neural processes can operate independently of conscious awareness.

"This kind of predictive coding is something we associate with being awake and attentive, yet it's happening here in an unconscious state," said Dr. Benjamin Hayden, professor of neurosurgery at Baylor.

What Does This Discovery Mean for Consciousness?

The findings raise important questions about what consciousness actually contributes to the brain's ability to process information.

Consciousness is generally associated with subjective awareness, including the experience of hearing, thinking, and understanding the world around us. Yet the study suggests that some neural processes involved in interpreting language and predicting future information can continue even when that awareness is absent.

This does not necessarily mean that the anesthetized patients consciously understood the stories being played to them. Instead, it indicates that the brain can carry out surprisingly complex aspects of language processing without a person being aware of doing so.

The distinction could help researchers better understand how consciousness emerges.

One possibility is that awareness depends not simply on whether individual brain regions remain active, but on how information is coordinated and shared across larger networks of brain regions.

Under this view, a structure such as the hippocampus might continue analyzing information even when the broader communication between brain regions needed for conscious experience is disrupted.

A Surprising Connection Between the Brain and Artificial Intelligence

The discovery also highlights an intriguing similarity between human brain activity and artificial intelligence (AI).

Large language models, the technology behind many modern AI chatbots, generate text by using patterns learned from enormous amounts of language data to predict what should come next.

The researchers observed a related predictive ability in the hippocampus, where neural signals reflected information about upcoming words even during unconsciousness.

Although biological brains and AI models operate in fundamentally different ways, both can use patterns in language to anticipate future information. Studying these similarities may help scientists better understand how prediction works in natural and artificial systems.

The findings could also have practical implications for brain-computer interfaces, which use signals recorded from the brain to communicate with external devices.

One potential application involves speech prosthetics, technologies designed to help people communicate when injuries or neurological conditions have impaired their ability to speak.

If scientists can reliably identify meaningful language signals in the brain, those signals might eventually be used to support communication technologies for patients who have lost speech abilities.

"Can we use these signals to deploy and run a speech prosthetic for some of the parts of the brain that are damaged by stroke or injury? These are questions that we can now consider in relation to this part of the brain," said Dr. Vigi Katlowitz, first author and a neurosurgery resident with Baylor.

Scientists Are Rethinking the Limits of the Unconscious Brain

Despite the striking results, the researchers emphasize that several questions remain unanswered.

The experiments were conducted under one particular type of general anesthesia, so the findings cannot automatically be extended to other anesthetic methods or states of unconsciousness.

It is also unclear whether similar language processing occurs during sleep or coma, two conditions that differ significantly from medically induced unconsciousness.

Another important limitation is that the researchers focused on the hippocampus. Further studies will be needed to determine whether comparable abilities persist in other brain regions and how these areas interact when consciousness is absent.

Nevertheless, the results provide compelling evidence that unconsciousness does not necessarily mean the brain has stopped performing sophisticated information processing.

Instead, the brain may continue analyzing patterns, responding to unexpected events, and anticipating language even when a person has no conscious awareness of the surrounding world.

"This work pushes us to rethink what it means to be conscious," said Sheth. "The brain is doing much more behind the scenes than we fully understand."

#4936. Scientists discover how exercise reverses muscle aging

Scientists have uncovered a biological mechanism that helps explain how exercise protects aging muscles and restores their ability to repair themselves. The discovery reveals why physical activity can counteract some of the changes that weaken muscles with age and points toward potential new treatments for age-related muscle loss.

The research, led by Duke-NUS Medical School in collaboration with Singapore General Hospital and Cardiff University, identified a molecular system that becomes disrupted as muscles grow older. Exercise can help bring this system back into balance, allowing muscle cells to remove damaged proteins and maintain healthy tissue.

Published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), the findings provide new insight into the biology of muscle aging. They also help explain why exercise may be more effective for some older individuals than others.

Although the experiments were conducted in fruit flies and mice, the researchers believe the underlying mechanism could eventually help scientists develop new approaches to preserving muscle strength and mobility in humans.

Muscle loss is one of the most significant physical changes associated with aging. Beginning around midlife, muscles gradually lose strength and function, making everyday activities more difficult and increasing the likelihood of falls and broken bones.

Weaker muscles can also complicate recovery from injuries and illnesses and make it harder for the body to regulate blood sugar. Because muscles play an important role in metabolism, their deterioration can affect far more than physical strength.

For aging populations, including Singapore's, maintaining muscle health is especially important. Preventing muscle decline can help older adults remain independent while reducing the demands placed on caregivers and healthcare systems.

At the cellular level, keeping muscles healthy requires a careful balance between producing new proteins and disposing of old or damaged ones. Proteins form much of the machinery and structure within muscle cells, so this ongoing maintenance is essential for normal function.

One of the main systems responsible for regulating this process is a signaling pathway called mTORC1. It acts as a growth regulator, helping cells determine when to manufacture proteins and support muscle maintenance.

However, this system can become less balanced with age.

When mTORC1 becomes excessively active, muscle cells may devote too much attention to producing proteins while failing to clear away damaged ones. Over time, these defective proteins accumulate, placing stress on cells and interfering with their ability to function properly.

Scientists have long recognized this imbalance as a contributor to muscle deterioration, but the mechanism responsible for pushing the system out of balance remained unclear.

Scientists Discover a Key Gene Behind Muscle Aging

The researchers traced the problem to a gene called DEAF1, which appears to play an important role in controlling how muscles age.

As muscles grow older, DEAF1 levels can increase. The team discovered that this rise drives excessive mTORC1 activity, disrupting the normal cycle of protein production and removal.

The result is a breakdown in muscle maintenance. Instead of efficiently replacing damaged components and preserving healthy tissue, aging muscle cells become increasingly burdened by proteins that should have been cleared away.

Under normal circumstances, DEAF1 is regulated by a family of proteins known as FOXOs.

These proteins help control gene activity and are involved in important cellular maintenance processes. In healthy muscles, they prevent DEAF1 from becoming excessively active.

But FOXO activity tends to decline with age. As this protective regulation weakens, DEAF1 can increase, driving mTORC1 activity higher and further disrupting the processes needed to maintain muscle health.

The researchers found that this chain of events helps explain why aging muscles gradually lose their capacity to repair themselves.

Crucially, they also discovered that exercise can interrupt the process.

How Exercise Helps Aging Muscles Repair Themselves

Exercise does more than stimulate muscles to grow stronger. According to the findings, physical activity can also help restore the cellular maintenance system that becomes disrupted during aging.

The researchers found that exercise activates regulatory proteins that reduce DEAF1 levels. This helps return mTORC1 activity to a healthier balance, allowing muscle cells to remove damaged proteins while continuing to produce the new proteins they need.

In effect, exercise helps muscles perform an important internal cleanup, making it easier for them to maintain healthy tissue and recover from damage.

Assistant Professor Tang Hong-Wen, the study's lead author from the Cancer and Stem Cell Biology Program at Duke-NUS, explained:

"Exercise can reverse this process, correcting the imbalance. Physical activity activates certain proteins which lower DEAF1 levels, bringing the growth pathway back into balance. This allows aging muscles to clear out damaged proteins, rebuild themselves properly, and help them stay stronger and more resilient."

However, the researchers discovered an important limitation.

When DEAF1 levels become extremely elevated or FOXO activity falls too low, exercise may no longer be sufficient to fully restore normal muscle repair.

This suggests that the biological condition of muscle cells can influence how effectively they respond to physical activity. It also offers a possible explanation for why some older adults experience greater improvements from exercise than others.

The findings do not suggest that exercise loses its value with age. Rather, they indicate that understanding the biological changes occurring inside aging muscles may be necessary to develop more effective strategies for preventing muscle decline.

Experiments Reveal a Way to Restore Muscle Strength

To investigate the role of DEAF1, the researchers performed experiments using fruit flies and older mice.

These two animal models allowed the team to examine whether the same biological mechanism influences muscle health across very different species.

The results revealed a consistent pattern.

When the researchers increased DEAF1 levels, muscles weakened rapidly. But when they reduced DEAF1, the balance between protein production and removal improved, and muscle strength was restored.

Finding similar effects in both fruit flies and mice suggests that DEAF1 participates in an evolutionarily conserved system, meaning the underlying biological function has been maintained across species over long periods of evolution.

These experiments also provided evidence that DEAF1 is not simply associated with muscle deterioration. Changing its levels directly affected muscle function, strengthening the case that it plays an important role in the aging process.

Whether targeting this system can produce comparable benefits in humans remains to be established.

Could Scientists Mimic the Benefits of Exercise?

The implications of the discovery may extend beyond ordinary muscle aging.

DEAF1 also influences muscle stem cells, which are specialized cells that help repair and regenerate muscle tissue after injury or damage.

As people age, these cells become less effective, and their numbers or activity can decline, making recovery more difficult. Disruptions involving DEAF1 may further impair the body's ability to regenerate healthy muscle.

By identifying DEAF1 as an important regulator, researchers have uncovered a potential target for therapies designed to improve muscle maintenance and recovery.

Such treatments could eventually benefit people who have difficulty exercising, including those recovering from major surgery, serious illness, or chronic conditions such as cancer.

One possibility is that adjusting DEAF1 activity could reproduce some of the molecular changes normally triggered by physical activity. This might help preserve muscle strength even when a person's ability to exercise is limited.

However, this remains a potential future application rather than an established treatment. Further research will be needed to determine whether manipulating DEAF1 is both safe and effective in humans.

Priscillia Choy Sze Mun, the study's first author and a research assistant in the Cancer and Stem Cell Biology Program at Duke-NUS, described the discovery:

"Exercise tells muscles to 'clean up and reset.' Lowering DEAF1 helps older muscles regain strength and balance, almost like hitting the rewind button. With millions of older adults at risk of muscle decline, understanding DEAF1 could lead to new ways to protect muscles and improve quality of life."

A New Direction for Fighting Age-Related Muscle Loss

The findings highlight an important distinction between simply stimulating muscle growth and maintaining the cellular systems that keep muscles healthy.

Producing more proteins is not always beneficial if damaged proteins are allowed to accumulate. Effective muscle maintenance requires both processes to work together.

By showing how exercise helps restore this balance, the researchers have identified a potential biological explanation for some of its protective effects against muscle aging.

Professor Patrick Tan, Senior Vice-Dean for Research at Duke-NUS, emphasized the broader significance:

"This study helps explain, at a molecular level, why aging muscles lose their ability to repair themselves and why exercise can restore that balance in some individuals. By identifying DEAF1 as a key regulator in this process, these findings may lead to new ways in which the benefits of exercise can be brought to societies with rapidly aging populations."

The discovery may ultimately help researchers design interventions that complement physical activity, particularly for people whose muscles no longer respond as effectively to exercise.

For now, the research reinforces the importance of exercise while revealing that its benefits depend on an intricate network of molecular processes inside muscle cells.

As populations around the world grow older, understanding these processes could become increasingly valuable for protecting mobility, supporting recovery, and helping people maintain their independence later in life.

#4916. U.S. Is Missing 4.3 Million Households as Priced-Out Young Adults Stay Home

More American adults are living with family or housemates today than at any time in decades, leaving a deficit of 4.3 million households compared to 2010 levels—a gap linked to housing market constraints.

There are currently 135 million households across the U.S. instead of the 139.3 million that would be expected if 2010 living patterns remained unchanged, according to a new report from John Burns Research and Consulting (JBRC).

A key driver of this trend is the growing number of adult children living at home instead of moving out.

As recent research from Realtor.com® revealed, a record 25.2 million adults under age 35 lived with their aging parents in 2025, representing nearly 1 in 3 young adults.

Another notable development in household composition is that more married couples are staying together, due to declining divorce rates and longer life expectancies.

Between 2010 and 2024, the overall U.S. divorce rate declined from about 3.6 to 2.4 per 1,000 people, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. During roughly the same period, life expectancy rose from approximately 78.1 years to 79.3 years.

Even as adult cohabitation increased, the average household size has shrunk from 2.54 to 2.42 people since 2010. The decline is largely driven by falling birth rates, with the number of children dropping from 63 to 51 per 100 households.

Based on 2010 metrics, an influx of empty nesters and seniors living alone after the death of a partner was supposed to lower the average American household size from 1.91 to 1.85 adults by 2025.

However, surging rates of cohabitating adults held that figure steady at 1.91.

"That means 4.3 million fewer households formed over 2010–2025 than would have formed without the shift," write JBRC CEO John Burns and VP of Demographic Research Eric Finnigan in the report.

The shortfall is not just due to youngsters living with their parents. Data shows that every age group is missing between 600,000 to 900,000 households, with the largest deficit recorded among 45- to 54-year-olds.

However, relative to their population size, Gen Zers bear the brunt of the deficit, with 15- to 24-year-olds missing 9% of their households, compared with just 2% to 4% for older groups.

These trends have crucial implications for a housing market dependent on household formation.

Typically, post-college 20-somethings move out of their parents’ homes, form independent households, and eventually purchase homes.

But high borrowing costs, heavy student debt, and inadequate entry-level wages have priced many young adults out of independent living.

A Realtor.com analysis from March estimates that housing supply scarcity and unaffordability have kept 1.8 million young households made up of 18- to 44-year-olds from forming.

"Demand isn’t just outpacing supply," says Realtor.com senior economist Hannah Jones. "It is artificially suppressed because suitable, affordable homes aren’t available."

Notably, according to the annual profile of homebuyers and home sellers released last year by the National Association of Realtors®, the median age of first-time buyers rose to 40 in 2025 from 38 the year before and from 33 in 2020, reflecting the persistent affordability challenges constraining the market.

Burns and Finnigan argue that the current 4.3 million household shortfall represents substantial pent-up demand. Unlocking it requires developers to build smaller properties that young people and seniors—many of them currently living with family—could actually afford to buy, ultimately driving new household formation.

As the report authors sum up: "We encourage cities and builders everywhere to zone and build more small homes that future households want to live in and can afford."

Jones agrees with that conclusion, stressing the importance of introducing to the market more starter homes with limited square footage to fit today's smaller families.

"Households will form faster when entry-level homes, both rentals and starter homes, become available at prices younger and lower-income people can afford," says the economist. "That takes more construction in that segment, along with zoning and permitting changes that allow smaller, lower-cost product."

Jones stresses, however, that small homes improve affordability only if they are actually priced lower, which has not always been the case.

A recent report on starter homes from Realtor.com shows that nationally, the share of entry-level properties has shrunk from 38.1% to 36.2% of active inventory over the past seven years, even as the median price rose from $260,000 to $340,000.

Simply put, there are 21,000 fewer starter homes available now than there were in 2019, and they are more than 30% more expensive than before.

To jump-start household formation, according to Jones, builders must offer smaller, well-priced homes across various housing types that cater to the distinct needs of young adults, empty nesters, and seniors.
