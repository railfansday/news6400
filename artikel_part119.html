#499. Brutal, revolutionary empire builder: Genghis Khan's legacy

Berlin is host to a major exhibition on the founder of the Mongol Empire and his successors. Here's how Genghis Khan disrupted the course of history.

Genghis Khan is one of history's most radical figures. A blood-thirsty invader whose conquests caused the deaths of an estimated 40 million people, he was also a visionary political thinker who established the largest contiguous land empire in history.

The founder of the Mongolian Empire died in 1227. Ahead of the 800th anniversary of his death, Berlin's James-Simon-Galerie explores his legacy and the "Mongol Century" (1250–1350) in the exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols."

The ruler remains "incredibly relevant" to this day, says Matthias Wemhoff, the director of the exhibition project.

"What the era of Genghis Khan — the 'Mongol century,' if you will — vividly demonstrates is how quickly established structures can collapse, how rapidly vast empires can vanish from the map and how swiftly the entire landscape can be utterly transformed," the archaeologist tells DW.

The charismatic leader's unprecedented impact on the world also evokes present-day disruptive individuals who are triggering sudden, unpredictable societal shifts, says Wemhoff: "There's always been this question: Are world politics shaped by individuals or rather by groups and sociological processes?"

In this case, the answer is clear, says the museum director, who also co-wrote a 2026 book on the nomadic leader.

"If Genghis Khan hadn't existed, the course of world history would truly have been entirely different — so much of it is inextricably linked to his person," Wemhoff says.

Genghis Khan was born as Temujin around the year 1162. His father, a Mongol chieftain called Yesugei, died when he was still a child. His family was abandoned by its tribe. To secure his familial position, Temujin killed his older half-brother, Bekter, when he was still a young teenager.

Thanks to his charismatic personality, he built alliances and found followers who helped him become the sole ruler on the Mongolian steppe. He formally adopted the title of Genghis Khan in 1206.

At its peak, the Mongol Empire covered approximately 24 million square kilometers (about 9.3 million square miles), stretching from China and Korea to parts of Eastern Europe and the Middle East, and accounting for over 16% of the Earth's total land area.

Wemhoff lists various innovations under his rule: For one, Genghis Khan transformed warfare. Commanders were promoted for skill, discipline and loyalty rather than based on aristocratic rank or tribal loyalties. Soldiers were organized into decimal units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000.

Despite being illiterate by modern standards, he also pushed for the introduction of the first written script for the Mongolian language, which allowed to build a state bureaucracy, track population and tax registers, record decrees, issue legal decisions and handle diplomacy.

A set of laws were established that replaced fragmented tribal customs with a unified legal system.

Trade was simplified through his adoption of a paper currency.

A policy of religious freedom was also established across the Mongol Empire, allowing all individuals to practice their chosen faith without state coercion.

Before the Mongol Empire, the Silk Road was fragmented by local conflicts and heavy taxes. Mongol rule placed the entire route under a single authority, making long-distance travel vastly safer.

The Mongol Empire is known as a period of relative political stability, economic growth and cultural exchange, an era historians call the "Pax Mongolica."

But "it all came with incredible levels of cruelty. The millions of people who fell victim to the Mongols — the mass executions, the abuse of women — this happened on a scale previously unheard of," notes Wemhoff.

The exhibition opens with a raid that happened after Genghis Khan's death, but took place just 260 kilometers (around 160 miles) away from Berlin: the 1241 Battle of Legnica, in Silesia, Poland.

It embodies the brutality and intensity of the Mongols' methods.

There came the "highly mobile, agile Mongols on small horses — lightly armored in lamellar gear, wearing helmets that left the face exposed, and wielding bows capable of firing 20 or more arrows in a single minute," points out Wemhoff.

European knights, heavily armored and mounted on large, massive warhorses, didn't stand a chance against their enemies.

"When a hail of arrows rained down on them, the horses eventually went down, and the knights ended up lying on the ground, stuck on their back, much like a June bug," he adds. "The two styles of combat were simply mismatched."

The Mongols' western campaigns shifted historic power centers in a way that still impacts world politics today.

For example, after the Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus', which was the first East Slavic state, its thriving capital Kyiv was reduced to a depopulated ruin. Moscow became a key regional power, partly because it chose to cooperate with the Mongols rather than pursue resistance.

"In our Western European collective memory, the Mongol invasion plays hardly any role. It's something of an anecdote," says Wemhoff, noting that the era of Genghis Khan and his immediate successors is barely mentioned in standard Western European school curricula.

Germans are mainly familiar with the name of the empire's founder through the 1979 Euro-disco pop band Dschingis Khan, which represented West Germany at the Eurovision Song Contest with a campy song of the same name that reduced complex history to extreme tropes.

But in eastern Europe, the Mongol invasions "changed everything fundamentally," says Wemhoff. "And so fundamentally that we still feel the consequences of it today."

The exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols" runs from October 21, 2026, to April 18, 2027.

#497. Shola Ameobi: ‘I thought: how can I be most useful to Newcastle? Then the cog started turning’

From experiencing racism growing up in Newcastle to becoming a derby hero and now a member of the executive team, the former striker has been on a remarkable journey

“There were times where I feared for my life as a kid, being harassed and chased by different groups of people,” Shola Ameobi says. “There were situations where I had to sprint to get away from groups of lads who didn’t like me because of what I looked like.”

Ameobi is recalling growing up in Newcastle during the late 1980s and 1990s, having moved from Nigeria aged six. There were lots of moments like that.

“It was almost as if I had to hide myself to be able to do what I love to do,” he says. “It wasn’t a very nice feeling, having to worry about getting from A to B. It was just part of my world. I had to be more aware of my surroundings and what I was doing.”

Ameobi’s “A” was often the family home, situated within a few pitch lengths of Newcastle’s St James’ Park, and “B” was frequently the club’s base at the time in Benwell, where he would head for academy training.

“It built incredible resilience in me,” he says. “That really helped me in a sporting context in my career, but also as a person, understanding that, irrespective of what people thought of me, what really mattered was the value I put on myself.”

That young boy running in fear became the scorer of 79 goals in 397 appearances for Newcastle including seven against their local rivals, Sunderland. The likelihood is that some of those he fled from while wearing a club tracksuit subsequently cheered him.

Since July, Ameobi has been Newcastle’s head of corporate affairs, after seven years as the loans coordinator. His role is to ensure “we’re doing things that are valuable not only to us as a football club but to the region”.

He adds: “We want to make this club the heartbeat of this city. But by being the heartbeat of the city, it’s making sure we integrate well into everything the city is about, whether that’s civically, whether that’s through our sport or through our community engagement.”

Ameobi’s job switch followed the 100-day review conducted by the CEO, David Hopkinson. At the end of a preliminary discussion, Hopkinson told Ameobi to take the weekend to consider things. But Ameobi, while grateful for the “level of trust and commitment”, pushed back, wanting to consider what the role would look like in practice. Six months and plentiful discussions later, he accepted.

For Ameobi, the leap to an executive position was not as huge as it might appear. He started his previous role in the Mike Ashley days and built that department “from nothing”, he says. “That’s really helped me understand how to business-plan and build a team.”

Ameobi has been part of the Premier League’s Black participants advisory group since it formed in 2019. Its successes include implementing the No Room for Racism action plan in 2021 – this weekend marks the second of two match rounds this month dedicated to the initiative – and the pilot launch of the player to executive pathways scheme in 2022. That is designed to help those from underrepresented groups into executive roles and Ameobi was part of the initial seven-strong cohort.

“It gave me a really good framework,” he says. “It took us through, at a very high level, what the different streams of executive work look like, whether that’s finance, brand, the future of working, how organisations work. That gave me a little bit of confidence, if I’m honest, more than anything, to go: ‘Actually, a lot of the things I’m already doing translate into that world.’

“One of the biggest things was working in a pressurised environment, which is what I’ve done for 20 years, and learning how that can really help you in those moments when things get a little squirrelly, when they aren’t going as planned.”

Completing the programme fuelled a fire in Ameobi that had flickered for two decades. In November 2006, having struggled with a hip injury for several years, he had career-saving surgery. The resulting time out was “the start of my thought process beyond the football pitch”. Whether he would return to playing at his previous level, or at all, was uncertain. So, Ameobi’s thoughts turned to: “How can I best be useful to my teammates and this football club? That’s when the cog started turning.

#491. Rejuvenation breakthrough: Scientists reverse 60 years of aging in human cells

Scientists in Germany have found a way to dramatically reverse the molecular age of human cells, transforming blood cells from an 80-year-old person into stem cells with an age signature of less than 20 years.

Researchers at the University Hospital of Bonn and the University of Bonn achieved this remarkable transformation by reprogramming immature red blood cells into neural stem cells, which can eventually develop into brain cells. During the process, molecular markers associated with aging were effectively reset, making the cells more than 60 years younger at the molecular level.

Unlike earlier approaches, the rejuvenation happened gradually, allowing scientists to follow the changes over an extended period. This slower process could provide important clues about how cellular aging can be reversed and potentially help researchers investigate treatments for diseases associated with aging.

The findings were published in the journal Aging Cell.

How Scientists Turn Blood Cells Into Brain Stem Cells

The human body contains hundreds of specialized cell types, all of which originate from a single fertilized egg and carry essentially the same genetic instructions. As cells develop, however, they take on specific roles. Under normal circumstances, a skin cell cannot transform into a liver cell, and a blood cell cannot become a nerve cell.

Modern laboratory techniques have made it possible to overcome these biological restrictions. Scientists can now reprogram certain mature cells to adopt entirely different identities. For example, skin cells can be converted into cells capable of producing nerve tissue, an approach that could eventually contribute to treatments for neurodegenerative diseases.

The process relies on a combination of proteins called transcription factors, which regulate how genes are activated. By changing which genetic instructions a cell follows, these proteins can redirect its development and give it a new cellular identity.

"Using this method, we have directly converted red blood cell precursors into neural stem cells," explains Prof. Dr. Oliver Brüstle, director of the Institute of Reconstructive Neurobiology at the University Hospital of Bonn.

Earlier experiments by the Bonn team had already demonstrated that nerve cells generated through this approach could successfully connect with existing neurons after being transplanted into the brains of mice.

Scientists Reverse More Than 60 Years of Cellular Aging

The latest investigation focused on an especially intriguing consequence of cellular reprogramming: the dramatic reversal of molecular aging.

"In our current study, however, we focused on a different phenomenon: namely, the observation that cells become significantly rejuvenated during reprogramming," emphasizes Brüstle, who is also a member of the Transdisciplinary Research Area (TRA) Life & Health at the University of Bonn.

To determine how much the cells had rejuvenated, the researchers examined their molecular clocks, particularly biological markers known as epigenetic clocks. These clocks estimate a cell's biological age by analyzing chemical modifications to DNA that tend to accumulate or change as organisms grow older.

Importantly, these modifications do not alter the DNA sequence itself. Instead, they affect how frequently certain genetic instructions are used (epigenetics). Because these patterns change predictably with age, scientists can use them to estimate how old a cell appears at the molecular level.

The results were striking. After reprogramming, cells taken from an 80-year-old individual displayed molecular age signatures corresponding to someone younger than 20. In other words, the process had reversed more than six decades of cellular aging.

"We also know that this epigenetic rejuvenation ensures that the cells actually behave like young cells," says the scientist.

A Slower Rejuvenation Process Reveals New Clues

Scientists have previously observed that reprogramming cells can reset their biological age. However, earlier demonstrations of this effect involved a more complicated process requiring two distinct stages.

First, researchers would convert an existing cell, such as a blood cell, into a pluripotent stem cell. These versatile cells have nearly unlimited developmental "career paths" because they can give rise to many different cell types throughout the body.

The pluripotent stem cell would then be guided into becoming a neural stem cell, which is more specialized and can produce cells found in the nervous system.

This conventional approach produces rejuvenation relatively quickly, making it difficult to examine all the changes taking place.

The Bonn researchers instead bypassed the pluripotent stage entirely.

"In contrast, we converted blood cells directly into neural stem cells -- that is, without first passing through the pluripotent cell stage," said Brüstle. "In our approach, rejuvenation occurred gradually and could be tracked for over 100 days."

The researchers observed a dramatic reduction in molecular age, with the study highlighting rejuvenation of approximately 60 years after 50 days. Because the process continued over a much longer period, scientists could examine the biological changes as they unfolded rather than observing only the final result.

What Cellular Rejuvenation Could Mean for Alzheimer's Research

The ability to watch cells gradually become younger could be particularly valuable for scientists trying to understand the mechanisms that control biological aging.

By studying this extended transformation, researchers can investigate which molecular pathways are involved and test whether particular substances can speed up or slow down the rejuvenation process.

"Rejuvenation that extends over such a long period of time is ideal as an experimental model: Since the epigenetic clocks are slowly reset over several weeks, we can use this model to investigate which factors and active substances accelerate or slow down the rejuvenation process."

The findings could be especially relevant to neuroscience, where aging is closely connected to the development of serious neurological conditions. Although the work does not establish a treatment capable of reversing aging in people, it offers a laboratory model for investigating how cells regain youthful characteristics.

This could eventually help scientists better understand the cellular changes associated with conditions such as Alzheimer's disease.

"After all, age is the most important risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's!"

The research involved teams from the University Hospital of Bonn, the University of Bonn, and RWTH Aachen University. Funding was provided by the EU's Horizon 2020 program, the German Research Foundation (DFG), and the Federal Ministry of Research, Technology, and Space (BMFRT).

#484. Why US investors are snapping up Germany's famed Mittelstand

Increasing amounts of US capital are flowing into Germany's traditional industrial firms and family-owned businesses. As many business owners struggle to find successors, more companies are becoming acquisition targets.

What do the long-established German companies Birkenstock, Viessmann, Linde and ebm-papst have in common? All have fallen into American hands.

Traditional German family-owned businesses and small-to-medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) have become increasingly attractive to US companies and investors.

Last year, US investors were involved in 76 acquisitions and investments in German industrial and manufacturing companies, according to an analysis by financial data provider PitchBook. The transactions were valued at about $29.5 billion (€26.3 billion) in 2025, nearly double the 2022 total. As of August 19, 2026, the figure stood at $23.9 billion.

"From what we are seeing in the market, the interest from US investors in German companies is definitely still there," said Samar-Ahmad Albrecht-Mahmood of Veracity Capital Investments, which advises companies looking to invest in Germany.

German companies, particularly those in the industrial and manufacturing sector, remain attractive, "because many of them are highly specialized, have strong customer relationships and very good technical know-how," he told DW. "In many cases, these are businesses that have built up a strong position in their niche over decades."

At the same time, many German business owners are reaching an age where they are thinking about selling their business, Albrecht-Mahmood underscored, pointing out that "there is often no successor within the family."

"This brings companies onto the market that might not have been available for sale a few years ago," he said.

Notable takeovers this year include ebm-papst, the Mulfingen-based manufacturer of electric motors and fans, which was bought by US company Madison Air for $5.4 billion in August.

In June, the long-established Augsburg-based company Everllence, formerly MAN Energy Solutions, was sold to Bain Capital, with the US investment firm acquiring a 51% stake in the company.

According to Die Deutsche Wirtschaft (DDW), a German business information network focused on the Mittelstand (medium-sized and family-owned companies), no country owns more German firms than the United States. Of the total 7,861 companies identified in the database "German Companies Under Foreign Ownership," 1,750 are currently held by US investors.

#482. Astronauts’ hearts just passed a surprising test for Mars

A journey to Mars may be less challenging for the human heart than scientists once feared. A new study suggests that astronauts can adapt remarkably well to months of weightlessness, offering encouraging evidence that humans could endure the long trip to the Red Planet and function under its weaker gravity.

Researchers from Northwestern Medicine, UT Southwestern, and UT Houston monitored the hearts of 13 astronauts who spent approximately six months aboard the International Space Station. Their findings showed that although spaceflight initially affected heart performance, the astronauts gradually adapted, with intensive daily exercise playing an important role in maintaining their cardiovascular health.

One of NASA's major concerns about sending humans to Mars is how astronauts' hearts and circulatory systems will respond when they leave the weightless conditions of spaceflight (known as microgravity) and encounter Martian gravity, which is only about one-third as strong as Earth's.

Encouragingly, the researchers found that the astronauts showed no significant signs of cardiovascular strain when exposed to simulated Martian gravity after returning to Earth.

"With our findings, there is now reason to think that we would be able to traverse to Mars, get there and actually work on the surface of Mars," said study senior author Dr. James Thomas, professor of medicine in the division of cardiology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine.

The study, published last month in the journal Circulation, is the first to investigate how the heart responds to simulated Martian gravity following an extended period in space.

How Daily Exercise Protects Astronauts' Hearts in Space

Living without gravity creates unusual challenges for the human body. On Earth, the heart constantly works against gravity to circulate blood, while muscles must support the body's weight. In space, those demands decrease substantially, allowing muscles and even the heart itself to shrink over time.

Returning to a gravitational environment can also be difficult. Astronauts on previous missions have frequently experienced dizziness or lightheadedness when standing after landing. This happens because gravity pulls blood toward the lower body, sometimes causing blood pressure to drop suddenly. The condition is called orthostatic hypotension. (If you've seen Sandra Bullock crawling after returning to Earth in "Gravity," you get the idea.)

However, the astronauts involved in the new study generally handled this transition well, potentially because they had maintained demanding exercise schedules throughout their missions.

As part of NASA's Exercise Countermeasures program, astronauts exercised for up to two hours each day. Their routines included 30 to 45 minutes of endurance training, which strengthens the cardiovascular system, along with 60 to 75 minutes of resistance training to help preserve muscle mass and strength.

"You lose muscle mass and cardiac mass when you're in a weightless environment because you are working less up there," explained Thomas, who also is a Northwestern Medicine cardiologist. "You're floating around in the air, and you give a little tap with your foot, and you can sail over to say hello to your buddy."

"To counter that, exercise is crucial when you're in space," Thomas added.

Heart Scans Reveal How the Body Adapts to Weightlessness

To understand what happens to the heart during prolonged spaceflight, the researchers collected echocardiograms before, during, and after each astronaut's mission. These ultrasound scans use sound waves to produce moving images of the heart, allowing doctors to examine its structure and measure how effectively it pumps blood.

While aboard the International Space Station, astronauts were trained to perform these scans on one another every few weeks. The recordings were transmitted to Earth, where researchers could monitor changes in heart function throughout the missions.

Thomas worked alongside Dr. Benjamin Levine of UT Southwestern Medical Center and Dr. Michael Bungo of the McGovern Medical School at UTHealth Houston.

"It would be typically about 3 a.m. for me because the space station runs on Greenwich Mean Time," Thomas said. "I would watch the recordings come in live and coach the astronauts in real time to improve the images."

The scans revealed that the astronauts' hearts initially struggled to adjust to their new environment. During the first two weeks in space, researchers observed declines in cardiac filling, stroke volume, and several other measures of cardiovascular performance.

Cardiac filling refers to how much blood enters the heart between beats, while stroke volume describes the amount of blood pumped out with each contraction. Both measurements decreased early in the missions, indicating that the astronauts' hearts were temporarily moving less blood with each beat.

As the astronauts spent more time in space, their cardiovascular systems adapted. Cardiac filling and stroke volume gradually improved, approaching the levels recorded before launch.

These results suggest that the heart can adjust to prolonged weightlessness more effectively than early measurements might indicate, particularly when astronauts maintain regular physical activity.

Astronauts' Hearts Pass a Simulated Mars Gravity Test

The researchers also wanted to answer a particularly important question for future Mars missions: What happens when an astronaut who has spent months in weightlessness suddenly encounters gravity again?

Mars has roughly 38% of Earth's surface gravity. Although this is considerably weaker than what people experience on Earth, it would still force astronauts' hearts to work differently after months of floating in space.

To reproduce some of these effects, the researchers conducted a simulated Martian gravity test after the astronauts returned to Earth.

Each astronaut was tilted upward by 22 degrees from a lying position. This posture caused blood to shift toward the lower body, approximating the circulatory effects of Martian gravity.

The results were encouraging. After spending about six months in space, the astronauts' hearts performed just as well under simulated Martian gravity as they had when standing upright on Earth before their missions.

This suggests that astronauts who maintain their cardiovascular fitness during spaceflight may be capable of transitioning to the weaker gravity of Mars without experiencing major cardiovascular difficulties.

Although the findings come from a relatively small group of astronauts and a simulation rather than an actual Mars landing, they provide valuable evidence for planning future missions.

Combined with the benefits of daily exercise, the heart's ability to adapt to weightlessness and respond well to simulated Martian gravity offers another reason to be optimistic about sending humans to the Red Planet.

The study is titled "Impact of Prolonged Spaceflight on Cardiac Structure and Function: An Exploratory Longitudinal Observational Study." #472. People are taking an unapproved weight-loss drug. AI found something surprising

An experimental weight-loss drug that has not yet received regulatory approval is already attracting users willing to obtain unapproved versions online. Now, scientists are using artificial intelligence to investigate their experiences, uncovering unexpected symptom reports that differ from those documented in clinical trials.

The drug, retatrutide, belongs to a new generation of GLP-1 medications designed to help regulate appetite and body weight. Although it remains under investigation and is not approved for use, people are purchasing unapproved formulations that are not intended for human consumption and discussing their experiences on internet forums.

Researchers at the University of Pennsylvania analyzed nearly 150,000 Reddit posts and comments from approximately 40,000 users to investigate these accounts. Their study, published in JAMA Network Open, provides the first systematic examination of potential side effects reported by people claiming to use unapproved retatrutide.

One finding stood out. Among users whose reported symptoms could be classified, increased appetite was the most common complaint. That was particularly unexpected because retatrutide is designed to suppress hunger, and its Phase 2 clinical trials primarily identified digestive problems as the most frequent side effects.

AI Uncovers Unexpected Weight-Loss Drug Reports

"Our goal is to identify hypotheses in online conversations that we could test. AI helps us find recurring patterns in people's self-reports, including experiences that may be missed by conventional drug-safety monitoring," says Sharath Chandra Guntuku, Research Associate Professor in Computer and Information Science (CIS), Director of Penn's Computational Social Listening Lab and senior author of the study.

However, the researchers emphasize that the findings should not be interpreted as proof of previously unknown side effects. Reddit users represent a very different population from clinical trial participants, and their accounts cannot establish whether the drug caused the symptoms they described.

There is also considerable uncertainty about what these individuals are actually taking. Products sold outside regulated medical channels may differ from the genuine experimental medication in their ingredients, purity, and dosage.

"We can't say that retatrutide is causing these symptoms, or even that Reddit users are actually taking genuine retatrutide, since the drug is not legally available, and gray market formulations vary," says Lyle Ungar, Professor in CIS and a co-author of the study.

Rather than confirming new risks, the research offers scientists an opportunity to identify questions that warrant more rigorous investigation.

"This is the first glimpse into the experiences of a population that is otherwise outside conventional drug safety monitoring because they are taking unapproved versions of a drug that has yet to finish clinical trials," says Neil Sehgal, the study's first author and a doctoral student in CIS.

How Researchers Analyzed 150,000 Reddit Posts

The project builds on previous research by the University of Pennsylvania team. In that earlier investigation, scientists used large language models (LLMs), the type of artificial intelligence that powers many modern chatbots, to examine more than 400,000 Reddit posts discussing semaglutide and tirzepatide.

That analysis revealed reports of possible side effects, including menstrual irregularities, that may not have been fully documented in clinical trials or official drug safety records.

"We wanted to apply the same methodology to gray-market retatrutide," says Sehgal.

Retatrutide is particularly interesting because it works differently from existing GLP-1 medications. Semaglutide acts on one hormone receptor involved in blood sugar control and appetite regulation, while tirzepatide targets two. Retatrutide activates three receptors, potentially producing a different combination of effects throughout the body.

To investigate what people were experiencing, the researchers used AI to examine nearly 150,000 Reddit posts and comments. They identified approximately 14,000 users who claimed to have personally taken an unapproved version of retatrutide.

The AI systems then extracted descriptions of symptoms that users associated with the medication and translated those descriptions into standardized medical terminology. This allowed researchers to compare reports even when different people used different words to describe similar experiences.

After excluding expected effects such as weight loss and reduced appetite, the team identified 7,823 users who reported at least one symptom that could be medically classified.

"A researcher might be able to examine a few thousand Reddit posts by hand, but looking at nearly 150,000 would be an enormous undertaking," says Sehgal. "AI makes it possible to analyze these conversations at scale, identifying patterns in what people are sharing that would otherwise be difficult to recognize."

The most striking difference involved hunger.

In retatrutide's Phase 2 clinical trials (the drug is currently in Phase 3 trials, the final step before FDA approval), participants most commonly experienced gastrointestinal complaints, including nausea. These effects are familiar concerns with medications that influence appetite and digestion.

But among Reddit users who reported taking unapproved retatrutide, the pattern looked different. Increased appetite emerged as the most frequently described classifiable symptom, despite the substantial weight-loss effects demonstrated in clinical testing.

The researchers also compared the new findings with their earlier analysis of semaglutide and tirzepatide.

Compared with those earlier Reddit reports, discussions involving unapproved retatrutide contained proportionally more mentions of appetite-related changes, sleep disturbances, increased heart rate, and muscle-related symptoms. Reports of nausea and other digestive complaints were proportionally less common.

These differences could reflect how retatrutide interacts with the body. However, they could also result from differences between the groups of people discussing the medications, their other drug use, or the contents of the unapproved products.

"Retatrutide works differently from existing GLP-1 drugs, so it's possible that its effects on the body are different, too," says Jena Shaw Tronieri, Senior Research Investigator at Penn's Center for Weight and Eating Disorders and a co-author of the study.

"But the people discussing it on Reddit also may be different," she notes. "Users willing to obtain an unapproved medication on the gray market likely differ from other clinical populations. Some may be taking multiple experimental peptides, and we can't verify exactly what they're taking. We need more research to understand what, if anything, these differences in self-reported symptoms mean."

Can AI Detect Drug Safety Concerns Earlier?

Beyond the findings about retatrutide, the study demonstrates how AI could potentially improve the early detection of drug safety concerns.

Traditional monitoring systems depend heavily on clinical trials, reports submitted to regulators, and information collected by healthcare professionals. People obtaining experimental medications through unapproved channels may not be represented in those systems.

Social media offers another source of information, allowing researchers to identify recurring experiences that might otherwise escape attention. However, these reports must be independently investigated before they can be considered reliable evidence of a drug-related problem.

The Penn team has already seen an example of how this approach can help guide further research.

In their previous investigation of semaglutide and tirzepatide, researchers identified online reports of menstrual irregularities. Those findings subsequently encouraged another scientific team to examine similar symptoms using the FDA's database of reported adverse drug events.

The follow-up study, published in Obstetrics & Gynecology, also detected disproportionate reports of certain menstrual changes. Although those findings did not establish causation, they provided additional reasons to investigate the potential connection.

The researchers hope their retatrutide findings will lead to similar independent studies, particularly as the drug advances through clinical development.

"That's what we hope will happen with this study," says Sehgal. "Other researchers can take what we find on Reddit and look for the same patterns in other datasets, using entirely different methods. Whether those signals hold up or not, the goal is to build a better understanding of what people are actually experiencing."

#461. A train was delayed by a solar storm. But something didn't add up.

A mysterious train delay nearly 180 years ago has led scientists to uncover a surprising mistake in the history of space weather. An international research team has solved a 178-year-old mystery involving one of the earliest documented cases of solar activity interfering with electrical technology.

The incident dates back to the 1840s, when electric telegraph networks were beginning to transform long-distance communication. As these early systems expanded, operators occasionally encountered strange electrical disturbances that could disrupt their equipment. Scientists now understand that some of these problems were caused by changes in Earth's magnetic field triggered by activity on the Sun.

Researchers led by Lancaster University have revisited a historical account of a train delayed in Exeter, England, when solar activity interfered with the railway's telegraph system. The investigation involved scientists from RMIT University, the British Geological Survey, Natural Resources Canada, Baylor University, and the UK's national space laboratory, RAL Space.

By piecing together historical documents and scientific observations, the team uncovered an error that changes the timeline of the earliest known technological disruptions caused by space weather.

How Solar Storms First Disrupted Technology

One of history's most dramatic examples of space weather affecting technology occurred in 1859. Known as the Carrington Event, the powerful solar storm involved a massive solar flare and a coronal mass ejection, an enormous eruption of charged particles and magnetic fields from the Sun.

The resulting disturbances produced spectacular auroras, commonly known as the northern lights, that appeared as far south as Hawaii and Central America. In some locations, the sky glowed brightly enough for people to read newspapers at night. The unusual illumination even fooled gold miners into thinking morning had arrived, prompting them to prepare breakfast shortly after midnight.

The storm also wreaked havoc on telegraph networks around the world. Electrical surges caused equipment failures, sent sparks flying from wires, shocked operators, and even started fires. In some cases, the electrical currents generated by the disturbance were so powerful that telegraph systems continued operating without batteries.

Although the Carrington Event remains the most famous example from this period, evidence of solar activity interfering with electrical systems stretches back even further.

In 1871, an anonymous author published an account in the scientific journal Nature describing a "very intense magnetic disturbance" that supposedly occurred on October 18, 1841. According to the report, the disturbance interfered with railway signals in Exeter and delayed the scheduled 10:05 p.m. train departure by 16 minutes.

If the date had been correct, the event would have represented the earliest known instance of space weather disrupting human technology.

However, a new investigation published in the American Geophysical Union journal Space Weather has revealed that the original account contained a significant historical error.

The Railway Mystery That Changed Solar Storm History

Associate Professor Brett Carter, a space weather researcher at RMIT University, helped investigate the historical records. During the investigation, the team noticed a problem that immediately called the original timeline into question.

The Exeter-to-Starcross railway line mentioned in the report did not open until 1846, almost five years after the event supposedly occurred.

"In this study, we effectively investigated what had to be a typo in a Nature paper from 1871. We know it was a typo because the Exeter-to-Starcross train line mentioned in that paper didn't exist until 1846, which is 5 years afterwards," he said.

To establish when the train delay actually happened, the researchers examined a wide range of historical evidence. They compared railway timetables and newspaper reports with observations of solar activity, accounts of auroras, and digitized measurements of Earth's magnetic field.

Together, these records pointed to a different date: October 18, 1848, exactly seven years later than originally reported.

The revised timeline resolves the apparent contradiction and places the incident during an important period in technological history, when electrical communication systems were becoming increasingly widespread.

Carter explained that the 1840s marked a turning point in humanity's relationship with space weather. Solar disturbances had always occurred, but the emergence of electrical technology meant that their effects could now interfere directly with everyday human activities.

"Understanding this historical event is important because the 1840s mark the intersection between the rise of our technological age and space weather, which has always been around," Carter says.

"Since this line was crossed, humans have not looked back."

The findings also change the incident's place in the historical record. Although the Exeter disruption remains one of the earliest credible examples of space weather affecting technology, it can no longer be considered the first.

That distinction now belongs to an earlier documented incident involving telegraph interference on Britain's Midland Railway in March 1847.

Professor Jim Wild of Lancaster University, who led the study, said the findings demonstrate that the dangers associated with space weather began affecting society long before the development of modern power grids, satellites, and electronic communication networks.

"What this research highlights is that space weather is not a new threat but a long-standing natural hazard. Society has been experiencing the effects of space weather on technology for almost as long as electrical technologies have existed," Wild said.

The Exeter incident is especially significant because it occurred when electrical technology was still in its infancy. Early railway operators were beginning to rely on telegraph systems, creating new opportunities for solar disturbances to interfere with essential infrastructure.

"The Exeter train delay is a fascinating story because it sits right at the point where emerging technologies first began to encounter the realities of the space environment. By combining historical archives with scientific observations, we've been able to show that the event almost certainly happened in 1848 rather than 1841.

"Although this means it is not the earliest recorded space weather impact, it remains one of the first clear examples of solar activity disrupting critical infrastructure," he said.

The investigation also shows how historical scientific records can be used alongside newspapers and other archival materials to reconstruct events that occurred long before modern monitoring instruments became available.

"It also demonstrates the value of combining scientific records with contemporary newspaper reports and archival documents when reconstructing historic space weather events."

Dr. Mike Hapgood, a Visiting Scientist and space weather expert at the UK's national space laboratory, RAL Space, described the research process as something resembling a detective investigation.

"It highlights the importance of preserving these older records, which give us the evidence base we need to interpret past events and strengthen future predictions," he said.

Modern Technology Still Faces the Threat of Solar Storms

Almost two centuries after the Exeter train delay, solar storms continue to pose risks to railways and other essential infrastructure. The technologies involved have changed dramatically, but they remain susceptible to disturbances originating from the Sun.

Today, space weather can interfere with electrical power systems, railway signaling equipment, satellite navigation, and communication networks. Powerful solar events can disturb Earth's magnetic field, potentially generating unwanted electrical currents in infrastructure and affecting systems that depend on reliable satellite signals.

Scientists now have far more sophisticated instruments and forecasting methods than were available in the 19th century. However, Hapgood emphasized that modern society's growing dependence on interconnected electrical and space technologies also creates new vulnerabilities.

Understanding how past solar storms affected infrastructure can help researchers assess the dangers posed by future events and develop better ways to reduce potential disruptions.

"Deepening our understanding of these events is essential for preparing for, and mitigating, the impacts of space weather - especially as we look forward to a decade of exciting space developments that will face the challenge of a new solar cycle in the 2030s," he said.

#453. Too much RNA can starve cells of energy, scientists discover

Scientists have uncovered a surprising way that viruses can interfere with the body's cells. When too much RNA accumulates inside a cell, it can damage the structures that produce energy, potentially leaving the cell struggling to function normally.

A new study led by researchers at the Texas A&M College of Veterinary Medicine and Biomedical Sciences (VMBS) found that excess RNA produced during poxvirus infections can impair mitochondria, the tiny structures responsible for generating most of the energy cells need to survive.

Published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, the research reveals an unexpected connection between RNA buildup and cellular energy production. The findings could have implications far beyond viral infections, potentially helping scientists better understand aging-related diseases and improve RNA-based medical treatments, including mRNA vaccines.

"Scientists have long known that RNA degradation helps control protein production and remove defective RNA," said Dr. Zhilong Yang, a professor in VMBS' Department of Veterinary Pathobiology. "Our study reveals another important role: it helps cells maintain the energy they need to function properly."

Cells constantly produce and break down RNA as part of their normal activities. This cleanup process, known as RNA degradation, helps regulate protein production, eliminate faulty RNA, and control immune responses. The new findings suggest it also performs another essential function by protecting the cell's energy supply.

RNA plays several important roles in keeping cells functioning. Messenger RNA (mRNA), for example, carries genetic instructions that tell cells which proteins to produce. These proteins are needed for nearly everything cells do, from maintaining their structure to carrying out essential chemical reactions.

Another type, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), frequently appears during viral infections. Its presence can serve as a warning signal, alerting the immune system that a virus may have entered the cell.

Viruses often generate large quantities of RNA as they reproduce inside infected cells. Ordinarily, cellular cleanup systems help prevent RNA from accumulating to harmful levels. However, when RNA production exceeds the cell's ability to break it down, the excess material can interfere with mitochondria and reduce energy production.

Scientists already understood that excessive double-stranded RNA could provoke potentially harmful immune responses. What surprised the researchers was discovering that too much ordinary messenger RNA could also damage mitochondria.

"MRNA is a normal component of our cells, but it seems like with many good things, if we get too much, that can become a problem for the cell," Yang said. "When too much RNA builds up, including mRNA, it can damage the mitochondria and interfere with the cell's ability to produce energy."

This creates an unusual challenge for viruses. Although viruses depend entirely on the cells they infect to supply energy and manufacture proteins, producing excessive amounts of viral RNA can damage the very machinery they need to reproduce.

According to Yang, poxviruses, a group that includes the viruses responsible for smallpox and mpox, appear to have ways of managing this problem. They use RNA cleanup mechanisms to remove excess RNA, helping infected cells remain functional long enough for viral replication to continue.

"If the cell is not healthy, the virus would not replicate well," Yang said. "The virus needs to keep RNA levels balanced inside the cell."

Scientists Discover RNA Can Directly Damage Mitochondria

One of the study's most unexpected findings emerged when researchers investigated exactly how excess RNA affects mitochondria.

Initially, the team suspected that RNA buildup was damaging these cellular powerhouses indirectly by activating the immune system. Certain types of RNA are known to trigger defensive responses, which can place additional stress on infected cells.

However, experiments revealed that the damage did not require an immune response.

"When we isolated only the mitochondria and added RNA, we still saw mitochondrial damage," said Dr. Djamal Brahim Belhaouari, the study's first author. "That was the moment we realized this could be a previously unknown cellular response to RNA."

The researchers also observed that mitochondrial problems developed before substantial immune activation occurred. Furthermore, the damage did not depend on the immune pathways normally activated during infection.

Together, these results indicate that excess RNA itself can interfere with mitochondrial function, rather than simply causing damage through the body's response to a virus.

The precise mechanism remains unclear, but the researchers have proposed a possible explanation involving electrical charges.

Mitochondria produce energy by maintaining an electrical and chemical difference across their inner membranes. This difference helps drive the production of ATP, the molecule cells use as their primary energy source.

RNA carries a negative electrical charge. The researchers suspect that when unusually large amounts accumulate near mitochondria, they may disturb the electrical conditions required for efficient energy production.

"We think the negatively charged RNA may accumulate around mitochondria and disrupt the electrical balance needed for energy production, although how it happens is unknown," Yang said. "This is a new concept that we are continuing to explore."

What the Discovery Could Mean for Aging and Disease

Although the experiments focused on viral infections, the findings raise questions about whether RNA buildup might contribute to other health problems.

Abnormal RNA accumulation has been observed in several conditions, including certain cancers, neurodegenerative diseases, and disorders associated with aging. Neurodegenerative diseases involve the progressive deterioration of nerve cells, as occurs in conditions such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease.

Because mitochondria are essential for maintaining healthy cells, disruptions to their energy production could potentially contribute to cellular dysfunction in some of these conditions.

The researchers suggest that excess RNA may represent one previously unrecognized source of mitochondrial stress. However, further studies are needed to determine how broadly this mechanism operates and whether it directly contributes to these diseases.

"We think this has quite broad translational implications," Brahim Belhaouari said. "Too much double-stranded RNA or mRNA that cannot be degraded effectively may contribute to disease processes in some contexts."

Potential Implications for mRNA Vaccines and Future Treatments

The discovery could also provide useful information for scientists developing medicines that rely on RNA.

RNA-based therapies, including mRNA vaccines, work by introducing RNA instructions into cells to produce specific proteins. Understanding how cells respond to RNA, process it, and eventually break it down is important for designing these treatments.

By identifying another way excessive RNA can affect cellular health, the findings may help researchers optimize future RNA-based therapies and vaccines. The study does not establish that existing mRNA vaccines cause mitochondrial damage in people.

"Understanding this mechanism could help researchers design and optimize future RNA-based therapeutics and vaccines by better understanding how cells respond to large amounts of RNA," Yang said.

More broadly, the research suggests that controlling RNA levels is important for more than maintaining accurate protein production or managing immune activity. It may also be essential for keeping mitochondria functioning and ensuring cells have enough energy.

For the scientists, the most striking result was that even a molecule cells depend on every day can become harmful when it accumulates beyond normal levels.

"Scientists already knew that too much double-stranded RNA can be harmful," Yang said. "What surprised us was finding out that too much mRNA -- something cells normally need every day -- can also damage mitochondria and interfere with the cell's ability to produce energy."
