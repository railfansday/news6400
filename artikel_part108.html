#1047. 'Ragebait' is the 2026 German Youth Word of the Year

Already named the 2025 Oxford Word of the Year, "rage bait" has also been widely adopted in German slang.

The 2026 Youth Word of the Year, "Ragebait," was announced on Saturday at the Frankfurt Book Fair by publisher PONS Langenscheidt.

The "Jugendwort des Jahres," or German Youth Word of the Year, is an annual campaign that documents the popularity of new slang expressions.

"Ragebait" (in English: rage bait) refers to social media content that is deliberately designed to make people angry, frustrated or morally outraged.

It aims to drive engagement, since the algorithms of platforms like Instagram, TikTok and X reward high-energy interaction. A post that generates a heated debate through angry comments, or that is widely shared due to the emotional reaction it provokes, counts as high engagement, which leads the platforms to push the content to more feeds.

"Ragebait" was on a shortlist of three words for the 2026 Youth Word of the Year, alongside "Peak" and "Crashout."

All three terms are borrowed from English, demonstrating once again the language's significant influence on German youth culture.

Around 1 million young people, aged 11 to 20, have voted since August 28 for their favorite term.

"Ragebait" obtained 36.5% of the votes, followed by 33.8% for "Peak" and 29.7% for "Crashout."

"The close result shows that all three terms have a firm place in the vocabulary of generations Z and Alpha," said Artur Raiser, marketing manager at PONS Langenscheidt, in a press statement. "'Ragebait' held a slight lead from the start," but only by a narrow margin throughout the voting period, he added.

"It remained an exciting race for us at the publishing house, too, until the very last moment," said Raiser

"Crashout" (used by young Germans as "crashout gehen," or "going crash out") originally comes from the English "to crash out," which traditionally means to fall asleep very quickly due to exhaustion.

But in newer internet slang, popularized on gaming and social media platforms, "crashing out" means having an intense emotional breakdown, losing control of one's temper or reacting with sudden, uncontrolled anger or stress. It is often used with a touch of humor, to emphasize that the reaction might be somewhat exaggerated.

As for the other shortlisted word, "Peak," it refers to something that is the absolute best. In German youth slang, it functions like an adjective: "That was peak football" or "that is peak comedy."

PONS Langenscheidt, a publishing company that specializes in language reference works, has been selecting the German Youth Word of the Year since 2008 — originally via a jury, and since 2020 through an online vote. Offensive, racist or discriminatory terms are not permitted. The multi-stage public nomination and voting process for next year's word will start in the summer of 2027.

"Das crazy" was selected last year, while "Aura" was the Youth Word of the Year 2024.

#1041. SpaceX just delivered some very strange cargo to the International Space Station

SpaceX's Dragon spacecraft arrived at the International Space Station at 10:27 a.m. EDT on October 15, docking at the space-facing port of the station's Harmony module. The spacecraft delivered more than 6,300 pounds of food, essential supplies, and scientific equipment for the Expedition 75 crew.

The arrival marks SpaceX's 35th commercial resupply mission to the orbiting laboratory under its contract with NASA. Along with everyday necessities for the astronauts, Dragon brought an unusual collection of scientific experiments that could help researchers develop new medical treatments and better understand how materials behave in space.

Artificial Retinas, Brain Research, and New Solar Arrays

Among the most important items aboard Dragon is the final set of International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Arrays, known as IROSAs. These solar panels are designed to expand the station's ability to generate electricity, providing additional power for its operations and scientific research. Installation is planned for later this fall.

The spacecraft also delivered equipment for manufacturing artificial retinas in microgravity, the condition of near weightlessness experienced aboard the space station. Researchers hope that producing these structures in space could eventually lead to technologies that help restore vision for people on Earth.

Other experiments include 3D heart tissue models, which recreate important features of human heart tissue. Scientists plan to use these models to advance methods for testing large numbers of potential drugs during future space missions.

Dragon also transported materials for investigating how a new type of glass forms in microgravity. Studying the process without the usual influence of Earth's gravity could provide insights into how the material develops and behaves.

Another experiment involves brain organoids, tiny three-dimensional models of brain tissue grown from cells. These miniature models allow scientists to investigate aspects of brain biology that can be difficult to study directly in humans. Researchers hope the findings will reveal potential targets for treating neurological conditions, including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and multiple sclerosis.

Dragon is expected to remain docked at the space station until mid-November. After completing its stay, the spacecraft will carry time-sensitive research materials and other cargo back to Earth, ending its journey with a splashdown in the Pacific Ocean off the California coast.

The mission began on October 13 at 6:33 a.m. EDT, when a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket lifted off from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida.

#1034. Germany's population is shrinking, but not everywhere

By 2045, Germany will have more older people, fewer workers and a smaller population overall. Yet some regions will defy this trend. Their success depends on how much they have to offer.

Germany's population is expected to decline from 83.5 million in 2025 to around 81.8 million by 2045, according to a new study from the Berlin Institute for Population and Development (Berlin-Institut für Bevölkerung und Entwicklung), in collaboration with the Wüstenrot Foundation, a nonprofit that supports research on housing, urban development and social change.

The decline in population alone is not the central challenge. More significant is the continuing rise in the number of retirees alongside a steadily shrinking workforce. Across Germany, the working-age population is projected to decrease by 4.7% by 2045.

"The baby boomer generation is retiring, while significantly smaller cohorts are following. Birth rates are falling, and immigration is not sufficient to offset the growing surplus of deaths over births," said institute President Catherina Hinz when presenting the study in Berlin.

The term "baby boomers" refers to those born in the 1960s, when annual births exceeded 1 million. Thereafter, the birth rate fell sharply, effectively halving.

Germany's population may be shrinking, but according to the study, this trend is far from uniform across the country. In the years ahead, a region's economic attractiveness, and the financial strength that comes with it, will become even more important than it is today.

"Some regions will lose substantial numbers of residents and workers in the years ahead, while others continue to grow. Even within eastern Germany, southern Germany and rural areas, the demographic and economic divide is widening," said Hinz.

"Demographic change is unfolding before our very eyes, and its effects are being felt across the country," Hinz added. "Its consequences can still be mitigated, but they can no longer be avoided. And the longer we wait, the narrower our scope for action will become."

Hinz's warning is echoed by Frederick Sixtus of the Berlin Institute. Almost every region now records more deaths than births, he noted, a consequence of decades of low birth rates. The implication is clear: Migration, both inward and outward, now shapes a region's demographic trajectory. Over recent decades, immigration from abroad has repeatedly helped stabilize population trends. Yet migration alone is no longer enough to halt the aging of society.

According to the study, several factors are critical if a region is to remain attractive to people relocating from abroad or from other parts of Germany: High-quality jobs offering good pay and working conditions, a sufficient supply of skilled workers for local businesses, financially robust municipalities, reliable childcare, and efficient transport and mobility infrastructure.

Many major cities are expected to continue to grow in the years ahead, according to Manuel Slupina of the Wüstenrot Foundation. The study projects that Berlin will lead the way, with the capital's population increasing by almost 10% by 2045. Other cities expected to attract people include Hamburg, Frankfurt am Main, Munich and Düsseldorf in western Germany, as well as Leipzig, Dresden and Potsdam in the east.

The study's authors argue that the familiar distinction between urban and rural areas does not hold true everywhere. The hinterlands of many major cities are also expected to grow. While young people are increasingly drawn to metropolitan centers, families with children tend to settle in the surrounding areas.

Among the places likely to lose population are several large cities in western Germany, including Bremen, Duisburg, Dortmund and Kaiserslautern, whose economic appeal has gradually diminished. At the bottom of the ranking are many sparsely populated regions, a large number of them in eastern Germany.

Twelve of the 20 districts and independent cities with the weakest overall ratings are now located in eastern Germany, up from five in the previous assessment in 2019. According to the study, these regions are also expected to see the steepest declines in their working-age populations, with losses of up to 18% by 2045. The authors conclude that competition for workers is therefore set to become one of the key demographic challenges of the decades ahead.

In this competition, the southern German states of Bavariaand Baden-Württemberg are regarded as having the strongest prospects. Bavaria ranks particularly highly thanks to its robust economy, which is thriving across much of the state, and its strong labor market. Women, older people and people without German citizenship are also more likely to be employed in Bavaria than elsewhere. The ranking shows that economically successful regions are more effective at integrating different population groups into the labor market than weaker ones.

Under Germany's constitution, the country is committed to ensuring that people enjoy comparable opportunities, and with them the prospect of a good life, regardless of where they live.

The researchers offer clear recommendations to policymakers. They argue that the focus must be on addressing structural challenges and regional disparities.

"What may be the right response for a growing major city is not necessarily the right one for a shrinking rural district," said Slupina. Tailor-made solutions are needed, but they require the right framework conditions. "There needs to be less rigid regulation, more scope for local initiative, and greater financial resources, especially for municipalities and those actively engaged at the local level." Slupina's message is clear: Regions should be trusted to shape their own futures.

Local authorities would no doubt welcome that. For years, mayors and district administrators have been calling for greater financial autonomy. They are currently seeking a larger share of overall tax revenues for municipalities. The federal government and the states, however, argue that they face budgetary constraints of their own.

Yet both sides recognize that, sooner or later, Germany will have little choice but to reform its system of fiscal federalism.

The achievement marks an important step toward the telescope's ambitious mission to investigate dark matter, dark energy, and other mysteries of the universe. Even the smallest improvements in optical precision could help Roman capture clearer images during its sweeping surveys of the cosmos.

"All of Roman's sweeping surveys will rely on the Wide Field Instrument," said Dave Hickey, a Wide Field Instrument systems engineer at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland. "Many of the mission's primary goals, like studying dark matter and dark energy, wouldn't be possible without it, so it's both exciting and a huge relief to see that it's working well!"

NASA Puts Roman's Powerful Camera System to the Test

Following the activation of the Wide Field Instrument in September, engineers began a comprehensive testing campaign to confirm that each component worked properly on its own and as part of the complete system.

Their first priority was examining the instrument's 18 detectors, each roughly the size of a sticky note, along with the electronics that operate them. The team then evaluated the element wheel, a rotating mechanism equipped with filters and other optical components. These allow selected wavelengths of light to reach the detectors and can separate incoming light into its individual colors.

Next came the instrument's internal calibration system. Engineers activated its light-emitting diodes, or LEDs, which function as adjustable light sources that can produce different colors and brightness levels under commands from Earth. By measuring how the detectors respond to these controlled signals, scientists can identify differences in sensitivity and make the corrections needed for accurate observations.

Engineers also tested the instrument's alignment mechanism and moved it from the deliberately unfocused position used during launch to the position expected to provide the sharpest images. They finished by checking the thermal control system, which maintains the temperatures required for the instrument's various components to function correctly.

"Calibrating an instrument is like tuning a piano or guitar before a concert," said Maxime Rizzo, a Wide Field Instrument systems engineer at NASA Goddard. "You adjust everything until it's just how you want it and then leave the settings alone so it performs consistently while you use it."

A Space Telescope Already Close to Perfect Alignment

While one group concentrated on preparing the Wide Field Instrument, another worked to refine the telescope's overall optical alignment.

The results were encouraging from the beginning. Roman's mirrors and other optical components were already positioned with remarkable accuracy, meaning engineers needed to make only minor corrections.

"The telescope's performance was excellent even before we began adjusting the alignment of all the mirrors and other optics, thanks to exceptional preparatory work done on the ground prior to launch," said Matthew Bolcar, Roman's optical systems lead at NASA Goddard. "All of the testing and modeling work done at Goddard and by contractors at L3Harris and BAE Space and Mission Systems left only small corrections for us to make to bring everything into near-perfect alignment."

Achieving that level of precision is especially challenging for a spacecraft. The forces experienced during launch can cause tiny shifts in sensitive equipment, and conditions in orbit differ significantly from those on Earth. Roman launched on August 30, carrying optical systems that had been meticulously tested before leaving the ground.

"Our ground testing couldn't exactly reproduce the environment Roman would experience in space, such as the absence of gravity," Bolcar said. "And there are also slight differences in temperature variations and moisture content. Despite those challenges, the recent assessments showed that our ground testing was excellent, with both instruments either matching or exceeding the performance we had predicted."

A Mirror Adjustment Smaller Than a Human Hair

Roman relies on an intricate arrangement of mirrors and optical components to collect light from distant cosmic objects and direct it toward its two scientific instruments. For the resulting images to remain sharp, the light must follow an extremely precise path.

Even a microscopic misalignment can affect how accurately the telescope brings light into focus.

Fortunately, Roman's optical system was already so well aligned that only incredibly small movements were necessary to improve its performance.

"We moved the secondary mirror by about one-sixteenth of the diameter of a human hair," said Alden Jurling, a senior optical engineer working on Roman at NASA Goddard. "This adjustment nudged the height of the secondary mirror above the primary. If we scale that height up to the height of the Empire State Building, the change in height is a little bit less than you would get by stacking a single U.S. penny on top of the tip of the tower."

Although nearly impossible to appreciate at a human scale, such a small adjustment can make an important difference when a telescope is designed to study objects across vast cosmic distances.

The team initially concentrated on Roman's Coronagraph Instrument, which had been producing images that were slightly out of focus. The problem was less severe than engineers had anticipated, making it relatively straightforward to correct.

The coronagraph now meets the team's demanding performance expectations. Engineers will continue refining its camera settings and determining the optimal configurations for its deformable mirrors, which can change shape to make extremely precise corrections to incoming light.

In the coming weeks, the team plans to test the coronagraph's ability to block unwanted light, an essential capability for observing faint objects near much brighter ones.

Once the coronagraph's focus had been optimized, engineers returned to the Wide Field Instrument for another round of adjustments.

Roman's Image Quality Exceeds Expectations by Nearly 30%

The final measurements revealed an especially impressive result. Roman's Wide Field Instrument achieved optical performance nearly 30% better than the team had predicted.

Before launch, engineers established an image quality requirement of approximately 90 nanometers RMS (Root Mean Square). This measurement describes the amount of optical error in the system, or how far the incoming light deviates from an ideal, perfectly focused wavefront.

To put that scale into perspective, 90 nanometers is roughly 1,000 times thinner than a human hair. At these tiny dimensions, even seemingly insignificant imperfections can influence the sharpness of astronomical images.

After the latest corrections, however, engineers measured an optical error of just 63 nanometers RMS, substantially better than the original requirement.

That improvement means Roman should be capable of producing even sharper images than its designers initially expected, strengthening its ability to carry out detailed observations across enormous regions of the sky.

"All of the images from Roman's cosmic surveys will be even sharper than we expected," Jurling said. "It's a great outcome from years of careful modeling, precision metrology, and testing."

With both scientific instruments meeting or surpassing their expected performance, Roman has cleared another important milestone in its preparation for routine observations. The results highlight the extraordinary precision of the telescope's design and offer a promising outlook for its upcoming investigations into the hidden structure and evolution of the universe.

One of her guests, Federal Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt, of the conservative Christian Social Union (CSU), responded immediately: "Na doch" (or, "well, yes she is"). #1001. Scientists predicted doomsday for November 13, 2026. Should we be worried?

In 1960, Science, one of the world’s leading science journals, published an article with an unambiguous title: Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026. It predicted that on this future date — less than a month from today — the human population would approach infinity and effectively annihilate itself.

The outlandishness of the claim landed the article widespread public attention. More surprisingly — because its underlying research and math seemed sound — it also received careful and sustained scientific attention.

The authors — Heinz von Foerster, an Austrian-American professor and cybernetics expert and two of his students, Patricia M. Mora and Lawrence W. Amiot — argued that human populations are exceptional and might grow beyond an exponential rate, at an ever-increasing one.

“Our great-great-grandchildren will not starve,” they argued. “They will be squeezed to death.”

The article was regularly cited, and its predictions appeared remarkably accurate — until around 1993, halfway to the predicted “doomsday,” when the global population began falling behind its forecasts.

Today, the global birth rate is slightly less than half what it was in 1960. Hopefully, we will all wake up on Nov. 13 to an ordinary day. Nonetheless, this article remains exemplary for blending rigorous science with the art of stagecraft to staggering effect.

The mid-20th century saw increasing societal concern about global population growth and its potential to lead to resource scarcity and geopolitical conflict. Underlying much of this concern was implicit or explicit acceptance of the Malthusian idea that whereas population has the potential to grow exponentially, it is kept constantly under check by its capacity to feed itself.

In their article, however, von Foerster and his co-authors argued that as far as populations go, humans may be exceptional.

Given our capacity to communicate and form “coalitions” that help us resolve problems, rising population density might simply mean more opportunities for collaboration, so that our numbers might grow not only beyond an exponential rate but at an ever-increasing one.

They then used 24 estimates of the world population stretching across the past 2,000 years to show that human numbers have in fact continually outpaced expectations. All this was presented in calm, scientific prose and aided by dense mathematical equations.

Von Foerster and his co-authors then projected these historical figures into the future, calculating that thanks to ever-increasing growth, in the year 2026 our numbers would near infinity.

Thankfully, the same capacity for collaboration that assisted our species’ development throughout history could now avert this catastrophe. Since we are unlikely to want to reduce our life spans, they concluded, we should work together to reduce the global birth rate by half.

This publication caused an immediate stir. There were stories in Time and The New York Times. It became the subject of a series of Pogo comic strips and many commentators took the article as strictly a tongue-in-cheek comment on population fears.

Rather than the story dying out, a funny thing happened. When Science published letters to the editor from mathematicians and demographers attacking von Foerster and his colleagues’ work — calling it “ridiculous” and “manifestly absurd” — the authors responded with spirited defenses that doubled down on the underlying math and its conclusions.

Throughout the 1960s through to the 1980s, the “doomsday” equation became regularly cited — usually at face value — in textbooks on genetics, ecology and demography.

It appeared in stranger places, too. In a 1963 Canadian Parliamentary committee, for example, a witness from the chemical industry prominently cited it as reason chemical pesticides were required: to feed the approaching population explosion. In a 1971 U.S. Supreme Court abortion case leading up to Roe v. Wade, it was cited as why there was no compelling reason for the government to favor increased population.

The doomsday idea persisted in part because its predictions for future populations turned out to be highly accurate — at least for a while. Its core equation yielded projections that were better than those of most demographers throughout the 1970s and 1980s.

Only around 1993 did that accuracy begin to falter and the global population began falling behind the forecasts — because the world began to do what von Foerster and his colleagues recommended it do. Today, the global birth rate is slightly less than half the 1960 rate.

We will probably not all wake up on Friday, Nov. 13 “squeezed to death.” But before simply moving on — prepping for the next doomsday scenario — it is worth asking what we can learn from this one.

For me, the lesson is in how we present our beliefs to the world. Heinz von Foerster was, at one point in his life, a stage magician. Borrowing the classic language of stagecraft, an effective illusion relies on three parts: the setup, the extraordinary turn and the final reveal that returns the situation to normal.

Von Foerster and his co-authors’ article gained attention because it was an ordinary scientific article wrapped in an extraordinary title and conclusion. It retained significance because it was firmly grounded in research and argument.

To draw the world’s attention to a serious matter by way of absurdity — that’s a good trick.
