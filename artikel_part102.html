#1346. Backlash over data centers tests Europe's AI ambitions

Massive data centers needed to run AI are triggering protests across Europe. Their operators counter that they boost digital sovereignty and energy independence.

Like coal-fired power plants and wind turbines, few people actually want a data center in their backyard. But they do want to use their services. As a global debate rages about the potential dangers of artificial intelligence, there are also many local discussions about where exactly the data centers needed to train and run AI should be built.

This is currently a major issue in Europe, where many governments and companies have decided that they would prefer to store data in Europe rather than in the United States, which is the global leader in computing. But more digital sovereignty in Europe will mean more consumption of the continent's resources.

According to the German Ministry for Economic Affairs, for every kilowatt-hour of electricity consumed, an average of between 0.4 and 4.4 liters (0.8 and 9.3 pints) of water is used to cool data centers, with the water either evaporating or being diverted back to tanks in a warmed state.

The summer that just ended was the hottest on record in Europe — in some areas, water use was restricted for residents, agriculture and even nuclear power plants. The summers are expected to get warmer, so many are wondering about the potential water consumption of data centers in the future.

They are also worried about the high electricity consumption of the centers. Fingrid, Finland's national grid operator, recently forecast that electricity consumption would rise by almost 40% if all projects with signed connection agreements were implemented in full.

This week, the European Commission announced a new scheme for data centers to increase transparency on their energy and water use. Those with a capacity of more than 500 kW will receive an efficiency grade that will also take into account their contribution to the grid, such as the reuse of waste heat. "Tripling our data center capacity cannot mean tripling the pressure on our grids, our water and our energy bills," said  Teresa Ribera, the Executive Vice-President of the European Commission for a Clean, Just and Competitive Transition.

Europe's most powerful data centers have primarily been built near the major cities of Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin (FLAP-D) because of high connectivity, proximity to users and businesses, and well-established ecosystems with specialized engineers and secure supply chains.

However, according to a report by the investment group CBRE, the situation is changing as these markets face power and grid infrastructure constraints. New data centers are being built in the Dutch city of Groningen, Barcelona in Spain or the Belgian capital Brussels, as well as in more rural areas.

And as this happens, there are more and more protests taking place, both on and offline. Earlier this month, hundreds turned up for a protest in front of tech giant Google's offices in the Austrian city of Linz, demonstrating against its already advanced plans to build a data center in the small town of Kronstorf.

Earlier this month, bestselling Irish author Sally Rooney successfully led a campaign to overturn planning permission for a proposed data center in the village of Killala, arguing that it would breach national climate policy.

In central France, meanwhile, several elected officials in the village of Etrechet have resigned over Google's plans to build a massive data center, which, at full capacity, would require between 880 megawatts and 1.1 gigawatts of electricity or the equivalent of the total output of a nuclear power plant. Etrechet mayor Florence Laurent told the French daily Le Monde that she was resigning out of a "sense of powerlessness, since the village has no decision-making power" regarding the project.

Some operators of data centers argue that the facilities can be built in such a way that they save energy. A large data center currently being built in Marseille will reportedly use a cooling system that requires 30% less power and consumes no water.

Other data centers, in Scandinavia, for example, use waste heat from the servers and route it into district heating networks via heat exchangers and pumps.

This spring, the German energy company E.ON published the results of a survey that found nearly three-quarters of people would be more likely to welcome data centers near residential areas if they fed their waste heat into the heating network.

For proponents of building more data centers in Europe, the facilities not only contribute to the continent's digital sovereignty but also help to strengthen energy independence.

#1338. What would Francis of Assisi make of his fame today?

The Catholic saint renounced wealth and fame to become a mendicant friar. Yet throughout history, his teachings and life have inspired popes, Dante and even Mussolini.

One could safely say that Francis of Assisi's life — and afterlife — is defined by paradox.

He embraced poverty, yet lies entombed today in a grand basilica in his native Assisi, Italy, with frescoes by renowned medieval artists.

He advocated meekness, yet hundreds of thousands venerated his remains when they went on public display earlier this year to commemorate 800 years since his death on October 3, 1226.

So, why has this Catholic saint inspired papal encyclicals, ecological activism, global pet‑blessing rituals and even the propaganda of former Italian fascist leader Benito Mussolini? And what might Francis make of this fame that he'd shunned while alive?

Born in Assisi, a hill town in central Italy, in 1181 or 1182, he was first named Giovanni di Pietro di Bernardone. His father, a cloth merchant with trade links to France, renamed him Francesco.

Early Franciscan hagiographers, that is medieval writers who recorded his life and shaped how later generations understood him, describe him as a lively, sociable young man who enjoyed singing, parties and the privileges of wealth.

However, he didn't want to join his father's trade; he dreamed of becoming a knight.

In 1202, Francis joined Assisi's forces in a battle against neighboring Perugia. He was captured, spent a year in prison and returned home shaken and ill. This, and a series of subsequent events, precipitated a slow shift in his outlook.

One occurred at the small, ruined Church of San Damiano outside Assisi, where Francis believed he heard Jesus Christ calling on him to "rebuild my church." Taking it literally, he began repairing local chapels. When he ran out of money, he sold cloth from his father's shop, which enraged the latter and led to a dramatic episode in Assisi's town square.

Before the bishop and gathered townspeople, Francis stripped off his fine clothes, renouncing his father, his inheritance and all material comforts. He committed himself to a life of poverty, humility, care for the poor and sick and a deep kinship with nature.

As a wandering preacher, he drew followers and eventually founded the Order of Friars Minor, known today as the Franciscans.

Widely considered the first saint to have miraculously received the stigmata — physical wounds resembling those on the crucified body of Jesus Christ — Francis was canonized by Pope Gregory IX on July 16, 1228. This unusually swift recognition amplified his fame beyond Assisi.

In the following centuries, Francis became one of the most frequently depicted saints in Western art, inspiring works from Dante's "Divine Comedy" to films, children's books and even a 1980 Marvel comic.

"Most saints are seen as unapproachable or unrelatable, but Francis' story of lifelong conversion and the desire to be in a positive relationship with others, God and the rest of creation speaks to people across time and cultures," theologian and former Franciscan friar Daniel P. Horan tells DW.

According to cultural historian Amanda Minervini, this broad appeal is also what made Francis so adaptable across eras.

"In Francis, there seems to be something for everyone: the pacifist, the ascetic, the mason, the environmentalist, the minimalist, the lover of animals also loved by animals, the diplomat, the mystic, the radical, the performer, and even the model of a different kind of masculinity," they tell DW.

Minervini points out that his image has been reconstructed throughout history. Their research highlights one particularly jarring example: Francis' political appropriation by Benito Mussolini's fascist regime.

In their recent book, "St. Francis and Mussolini: Fascist Appropriations of a Beloved Italian Saint" (2026), they explain how fascist rhetoric manipulated Francis' stigmata, reframing them as symbols of heroic suffering and sacrifice. "The wounds of the saint were thus invoked to glorify sacrifice and inspire Italian youth to go to war."

Noting how Mussolini chose to be symbolically baptized in 1927 to strengthen his alliance with the Vatican, they add, "St. Francis offered a particularly useful means of conferring religious legitimacy upon political power."

Minervini's research shows that Francis' pacifism, "likely because he had known what war meant," was deliberately played down. Their book reproduces a confidential note addressed to military chaplains in 1929, which expressed concern that they might preach ideals of peace to the troops.

"This document provides clear evidence that peace was an aspect of Francesco's legacy that fascist groups sought to suppress rather than promote."

Furthermore, after Italy — a former Axis power along with Nazi Germany — switched sides and joined the Allies during World War II, the latter were often willing to condone or overlook Italy's crimes rather than investigate its fascist past.

"In this climate, many fascists remained in place, and the politically compromised image of Francis was quietly set aside," explains Minervini.

Francis has, however, remained associated with peace-making.

In 1219, during the Fifth Crusade, he traveled with the crusading army to Damietta, a port city in northern Egypt and obtained permission to cross into the Muslim camp and meet personally with the sultan, Malik al-Kamil. He aimed to convert the sultan and end the bloody conflict.

Describing his action as "an extraordinarily audacious thing to do," Minervini says the encounter did not produce the conversion or political breakthrough Francis may have hoped for, but his unscathed return ensured early sources took notice of him.

Horan, the former Franciscan friar, adds that despite Christians viewing Muslims as the enemy back then, St. Francis "would not go along with that dehumanizing perspective" and instead celebrated "the shared humanity and brotherhood he recognized in the Sultan."

But he is most widely known as an animal lover and an early figure of environmental consciousness.

"He was ahead of his time both theologically and in terms of modern natural sciences, recognizing the complexity of the cosmos and the fact that human beings are also creatures in relationship with the world around us," says Horan, author of "Francis of Assisi and the Future of Faith" (2012).

In 1979, Pope John Paul II declared Francis the patron saint of ecology, while the Argentinian Jorge Mario Bergoglio chose the saint as his papal namesake when he became Pope Francis in 2013.

This pope would later publish "Laudato Si'" in 2015 — based on the saint's 1225 hymn "Canticle of the Creatures," in which he addresses natural elements as "Brother Sun," "Sister Moon" and "Sister Mother Earth."

The pope's encyclical connected environmental destruction directly to human suffering, besides critiquing consumerism and the "throwaway culture," and urging humanity to undergo an "ecological conversion."

"I also believe that in a time of deep polarization and economic inequality that St Francis models another way of being in the world, one that prioritizes relationship and community over individual gain and power," says Horan.

"It's always tricky to try and guess what St. Francis, who died 800 years ago this October, would think about anything today. Our world is so unlike his own. But we do know that in his own time he was very uncomfortable with the positive reception he and his brother friars were receiving across Europe," says Horan.

Minervini suggests that he might have been more comfortable with representations of himself as a pacifist saint.

"I suspect he would be profoundly troubled, perhaps even furious, as it is known he could get upset, by the condition of our late-capitalist, technocratic world and by the ways in which we continue to understand ourselves as separate from, and superior to, the other creatures."

#1323. Diesel prices are surging putting further pressure on Europe

Trucks, farms and industry are powered with diesel. Now prices are soaring due to refinery disruptions, supply shortages and geopolitical tensions. A potential US export ban could make Europe's diesel crunch even worse.

Even if you don't fill your own vehicle with diesel, its price has a big impact on your life.

While gasoline is mainly a consumer fuel for cars, diesel powers much of the wider economy, from freight transport and farming equipment to construction and industry.

That is why diesel shortages or price shocks can have a bigger economic impact than higher gasoline prices by raising the cost of moving goods and operating heavy machinery.

As businesses become strained, these higher energy costs will be passed on to consumers and add to a growing cost-of-living crisis and inflation. So what's driving global diesel prices higher?

The United States and China are among the world's largest diesel producers. China keeps much of it for domestic use, leaving the US as the world's biggest diesel exporter.

Still, the weekly average price of a gallon of diesel is now $6.52 (€5.73) in the country, according to the US Energy Information Administration, a big increase from a year ago when it averaged $3.74 per gallon or two years ago when it averaged $3.53 a gallon.

President Donald Trump recently called on Ukraine's president to stop targeting oil refineries in Russia "because he's causing a shortage of diesel."

Russia is one of the world's other biggest diesel suppliers. But since 2022, some of its oil refineries have been attacked as many as 15 times, according to the International Energy Agency.

The agency estimates that diesel production in Russia is down by nearly 30% from 2025 levels. Moscow has now restricted diesel exports to protect domestic supplies.

As Russian production declined, the US went into high gear to help make up the difference, but even this was not enough. And on September 22, Trump announced that his administration was also considering a diesel export ban.

Such a move is likely an attempt to bring down domestic prices ahead of the November midterm elections since energy prices and affordability have become important campaign issues.

Leading energy lobby groups have come out against such a plan, and many industry experts think a ban would be slow to bring down prices or could backfire by pushing US refiners to reduce production, push up prices, or both.

For Europe, more expensive diesel could have dramatic consequences.

"Decades of policy incentives, such as tax advantages, left Europe's vehicle fleet far more diesel-heavy than regions like the US," said Georg Zachmann, a senior fellow at Brussels-based Bruegel think tank.

"As a result, the EU is structurally long on gasoline, which it exports, and short on diesel, which it must import," added Zachmann, who specializes in energy and climate policies.

In the EU, just over 38% of passenger cars are diesel and road transport, including freight, makes up 77% of total diesel and gas oil consumption, according to a briefing published by Transport & Environment, a Brussels-based advocacy group.

This reliance on diesel-powered transport "leaves drivers and businesses particularly exposed to oil and fuel price shocks," wrote Juliette Egal, the briefing's lead author.

The NGO calculated that EU drivers are paying €30 more for a 50-liter tank of diesel on average since the start of the war in Iran. An average German long-haul truck driver is paying an extra €236 a week.

Increased fuel costs for EU road transport add up to €270 million a day — of that, €203 million is for diesel, according to the briefing. Overall, the EU economy has seen additional costs of €40 billion from road diesel since the beginning of the war.

In the near term, Russia's crude oil processing volumes are the single fastest-moving lever, said Skip York, a nonresident fellow at Rice University's Center for Energy Studies in Houston, Texas.

"Each new wave of Ukrainian strikes shows up in diesel prices within days, unlike Hormuz, which has been running at a depressed but relatively stable flow rate for months," he added.

#1310. Spain grows fruit and vegetables for Europe, but it's running out of water

Spain's southern coast produces up to 25% of Europe's fruit and vegetables. But amid intensive farming, climate change and illegal wells, the water it takes to grow them is drying up.

Donana National Park in southwestern Spain — one of Europe's most important wetlands, home to millions of migratory birds and threatened species like the Iberian lynx — is running dry. But rising temperatures and drought caused by climate change are only part of the problem.

The UNESCO-protected region on the Atlantic coast relies on an underground aquifer that's been tapped by hundreds of illegal wells. The reason: Europe's fruit and vegetable supply. Spain provides one-quarter of Europe's fresh produce, supplying colder parts of the continent with fruit and vegetables year-round.

The scale of water extraction in Spain is staggering: In 2016, WWF Spain estimated that the Donana wetland was home to over 1,000 illegal wells and more than 1,700 ponds illegally irrigating an area as big as 4,200 football fields.

"It's gotten hotter every year. And the water dries up so quickly," one local resident in the town of El Rocio, just north of the wetlands, told DW.

"For me, day to day, the main issue is my animals," said another, hitching his horse up to a tourist carriage. "I have to draw water for them, but the well doesn't have enough water to provide it all at once."

The Donana area used to be royal hunting grounds, consisting of marshes and ponds. In the early 20th century, parts began to be used for dryland farming, agriculture in arid regions that relies heavily on rainwater.

But in 1971, Spain — still under the dictatorship of General Francisco Franco — declared the region's irrigation to be of national interest. The Almonte-Marismas Plan, named after the aquifer it tapped into, was part of a massive, state-sponsored effort to increase food production.

Authorities decided to bet on strawberries, which they called "red gold." According to calculations based on FAO data, strawberries can yield up to €150,000 (about $170,000) in gross revenue per hectare per year, compared to roughly €20,000 for potatoes or €15,000 for grapes.

But growing strawberries at scale also meant using a lot more water. Authorities handed out generous quotas and allotments, seemingly without considering how much extraction the aquifer could handle. Even those legal quotas weren't enough for the ever-expanding fields.

Farmers turned to illegal wells and reservoirs to make up the difference. They built them to stockpile water, draining supplies from local residents and the UNESCO World Heritage Site next door.

"There was extreme drought and lack of attention from the administration," said Pedro Banez, of the Condado Water Users' Association. "And we found ourselves with a number of farms — or parts of farms — that fell outside the land classified as irrigable agricultural land."

The turning point came in 2019 when, following numerous reports of unlawful wells, a 2-year-old fell into deep, reportedly illegal borehole on farmland near Malaga and died. In the aftermath, Spain's Civil Guard police force created dedicated units focused on water theft.

Two years after that, in 2021, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that Spain had failed to protect the Donana aquifer, finding the country in violation of EU law.

Today, police and NGOs like WWF Spain track illegal extraction — near Donana, and other overtaxed agricultural regions across the country — using a mix of old and new tools. They start with satellite images of the region, comparing them against land rights maps to flag areas that appear greener than their legal status allows. Drones follow, giving investigators a closer look at where the water is actually coming from.

Some wells are completely illegal. Others are legal but extract more than they're allowed, which requires closer, on-site inspection to prove.

Investigators check the flow meters that measure how much water a well is pulling. Tampering is common, often using a powerful magnet which disrupts the internal mechanism of the flow meter, allowing water to pass through unrecorded.

Farmers caught extracting illegally can be charged with crimes against the environment and fraud. Most face fines, but sentences can run up to five years in prison.

In a bid to undo decades of overuse, local and national authorities have negotiated a €1.4 billion joint plan. The government has continued shutting down illegal wells, sealing almost 300 in the Donana region alone.

Officials have also started buying up farmland, rewilding it and turning it into protected areas, while setting up funds to create jobs in other sectors like tourism and renewable energy.

Farmers, meanwhile, have had to change how they water their crops.

Many have switched to drip irrigation systems, where each plant gets its own individual dripper, paired with moisture sensors that report exactly how much water a plant needs. That cuts down on the overuse of both water and fertilizer.

Farmers have also begun planting less water-intensive crops and shifting their growing season to colder months.

Spain isn't alone in confronting this problem. As economies mature, fruit and vegetable production starts being outsourced to places with cheaper labor, fewer restrictions and more sun.

Mexico, Brazil and Colombia grow food for parts of the US, while countries in southeast Asia export a lot of produce to China or Japan. Some countries in northern Africa, including Morocco and Egypt, also supply fresh fruits and vegetables to their European neighbors to the north. But, as in Spain, intensive agriculture has strained local water supplies.

And in California, the US state has paid farmers to halt certain types of irrigation and introduced legislation to monitor and allocate water more sustainably.

Securing water, no longer seen as an unlimited resource, will increasingly be a challenge as the effects of climate change intensify. Addressing overextraction will take a coordinated effort from governments, farmers and the industries that depend on their water.

#1306. Global trade weathers Iran, Ukraine and Trump's tariffs

Over the past six years, COVID, two wars and Donald Trump's tariffs have shaken world trade. With globalization already stalling, is this a temporary shock or the start of a great reversal?

From COVID to Donald Trump's tariffs — with wars in Ukraine and the Middle East in between — world trade policy is enduring the most unpredictable period of the postwar era.

The pandemic shut factories worldwide and accelerated a shift away from China, the world’s largest manufacturer. The two conflicts then disrupted global supplies of oil and gas, food and fertilizer.

US President Donald Trump’s tariffs, meanwhile, pushed US duties to their highest level in decades, forcing firms to find new suppliers and markets.

Robert Staiger, chief economist at the World Trade Organization (WTO), calls the turmoil "the most serious and sustained disruptions since the [world trading] system was created 80 years ago."

Despite these shocks, global trade has remained surprisingly resilient.

According to the WTO's Global Trade Outlook published Thursday, the growth in goods trade is now projected to reach 3.9% this year, a major upwards revision from the March guidance of 1.9%.

The WTO estimated that growth could even reach 4.1% in 2027, lifted by strong demand for the advanced technology behind the artificial intelligence (AI) build-out.

The report said "global supply chains adapted to disruptions in energy and fertilizer markets [from the Iran war], while strong investment in AI-related infrastructure boosted trade in AI-enabling goods.”

In the first six months, AI-related tech accounted for nearly half (47%) of goods trade growth globally.

Since returning to office last year, Trump has continued to pivot from WTO rules on global trade.

The US president has set country-specific duties, breaking the WTO’s equal-treatment rule. He has since negotiated exemptions and "reciprocal" tariffs with individual countries.

Despite this, globally, about 72% of goods are still traded on the Geneva-based agency's core terms, down from roughly 80% in 2022.

On a longer horizon, though, globalization appears to have stalled. WTO data shows that since about 2015, world trade has grown only as fast as the global economy. Despite the recent geopolitical upheavals, globalization has yet to shift into reverse.

Policymakers and economists say it is too soon to know whether the wars, alongside a pivot toward protectionism in the US, China, the EU and post-Brexit Britain, mark the end of an ever-interconnected world.

"The world is certainly not becoming more globalized," Thomas Sampson, an associate professor at the London School of Economics, told DW. "But it's still an open question whether we're actually at deglobalization or just shifting around who we trade with."

Since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the EU has sharply cut its reliance on Russian pipeline gas from 37% in late 2021 to around 10%, according to Eurostat, the bloc's statistics agency. EU oil and coal imports from Russia are now negligible.

These moves spurred new energy deals with the US, Norway, Australia and Kazakhstan, with liquefied natural gas (LNG) replacing Russian pipeline gas.

Moscow did find new energy buyers — notably China and India — which took Russian oil at a large discount to global benchmark prices.

Toby Morton, who wrote several episodes of the animated comedy series South Park, launched a website seeking accountability in connection with an alleged 2024 sexual assault case involving seven fraternity members at Cornell University in the US. The site names seven men accused of sexually assaulting a 20-year-old woman who was allegedly unconscious after being given drugs and alcohol at a Cornell fraternity house, and collects personal data and photos of the members involved.

"I've received messages reminding me I'm not a journalist, I'm 'just a satirist,'" Morton wrote in a September 27 Instagram post regarding the creation of his website TheCornell7.org. "This is true, but it makes me more dangerous," he added, saying he has "nobody to tell me I've gone too far."

The case has recently drawn a surge in public attention after the accuser, identified as Jane Doe to protect her identity, filed a lawsuit in mid-September. Her complaint alleges that she was brutally sexually assaulted in 2024 by seven members of the Chi Phi fraternity while unconscious after being pressured to take ketamine, smoke marijuana and drink alcohol at the fraternity house on Cornell's campus in Ithaca, New York. The university, she said in the lawsuit, failed to protect her. Cornell conducted an internal investigation after the incident, but no one was charged.

Since the lawsuit was filed on September 16, the case has generated widespread commentary on social media. Some have criticized Cornell and the district attorney's office, while others have questioned the allegations.

Tompkins County District Attorney Matthew Van Houten, who handled the original case, said his office has reopened it and plans to present evidence to a grand jury amid public questions about why no criminal charges were initially filed.

Van Houten said in a statement that his office is considering bringing criminal charges related to the incident, though the allegations in the civil complaint are "dramatically different" from the woman's statement to police two years ago — in her statement to authorities, she did not allege she was drugged without her knowledge or that she did not consent to engage in sexual acts.

Cornell's Chi Phi chapter was barred from campus after the alleged incident in 2024. The woman who accused the men of sexual assault dropped out of Cornell University, according to her attorney.

One of the most chilling allegations of the lawsuit is that a message was sent to a Chi Phi Snapchat group at about 1:42 a.m., inviting members to the room to abuse Doe, who was heavily under the influence of drugs and alcohol. The lawsuit alleges that more men then entered the room and that the assault continued for several hours until Doe lost consciousness.

"There were so many people that could have prevented it and they didn't. I've never seen anything like it," an attorney for Jane Doe, Thomas Giuffra, told CBS.

Giuffra pointed out that two of the seven accused fraternity members were expelled, while the others received lesser disciplinary measures.

"Not one of these guys was arrested," Giuffra told the network.

Many people, from US politicians to Hollywood actors, have spoken out about the case in recent days. US Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, a New York Democrat, condemned what she described as a "culture of rape" protected by elite institutions.

"When I read what happened at Cornell, those men posted in a group chat. That means it was normal to them. That means it was acceptable to them. This is a culture, and what we must do is understand that we cannot protect those spaces anymore," she said Sunday at a forum near Cornell's campus.

British actress Florence Pugh described the allegations as "horrific" on social media and expressed her support for the student involved.

Pugh, who is currently on a promotional tour for her upcoming Netflix series "East of Eden," wrote a lengthy statement on Instagram, saying that "Colleges/schools/universities repeatedly fail women around the world. You build men that get taught through time and time again that they can do extreme harm and that they won't be harmed in return."

"Those who protect and impose light sentences on men who rape and destroy lives should be punished and closely scrutinized. Why are you protecting someone so fiercely?" she wrote.

American actor Josh Gad, best known as the voice of Olaf in Disney's "Frozen" films, also commented on the Cornell case, criticizing what he described as insufficient disciplinary consequences for the students accused of sexual assault. In an Instagram post, Gad said sexual violence should not be treated as a mistake or addressed with limited disciplinary action by a university.

He argued that the alleged conduct had "destroyed a life" and called for greater accountability. Edited by: Joshua Lang
