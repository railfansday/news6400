#5650. Tesla driver says he was asleep on FSD when he hit CHP car, 2 officers hurt

Two California Highway Patrol officers are recovering from serious injuries after a Tesla drove into their patrol car at a crash scene on Interstate 80 in Fairfield. The 22-year-old driver told investigators he was asleep with Tesla’s “Full Self-Driving” engaged.

CHP released dashcam video of the impact on Thursday. It’s the latest Tesla to plow into an emergency scene, a failure federal regulators started investigating five years ago.

The crash happened just before midnight on Tuesday, October 13, on eastbound I-80 west of Air Base Parkway. Officers were there for an earlier fatal crash: about an hour before, a 19-year-old from San Pablo lost control of his Honda Civic and was hit by a Chevrolet Tahoe. He didn’t survive.

Four lanes were already shut down behind flares and cones when the Tesla showed up at freeway speed, per CHP. It went through the closure and into a Dodge Charger patrol car with both officers sitting in it. Cameras on the Charger and on another vehicle at the scene caught the impact.

The Tesla driver, from Sacramento, walked away with minor injuries. Both crashes are still under investigation.

In its statement, CHP stressed that FSD is a driver-assistance feature that requires the person behind the wheel to stay alert and in control: “Technology does not relieve drivers of their responsibility to safely operate their vehicles.”

CHP hasn’t released the Tesla model or the software version. And for now, the driver’s word is the only evidence FSD was on. Tesla’s vehicle logs will settle that.

We’ve been here before. In August 2021, NHTSA opened an investigation after a series of Teslas on Autopilot crashed into stopped police cars and fire trucks, many of them at night with flares, cones, and flashing lights on scene. That probe ended with Tesla’s December 2023 recall of more than 2 million vehicles, fixed with an over-the-air update that added more driver-attention alerts.

FSD hasn’t solved it either. In June 2024, a Tesla on FSD hit a parked Fullerton police car while officers were handling, again, a fatal crash. That driver admitted to being on his phone.

A nighttime lane closure lit up with flares is about as clear a “stop or move over” signal as a road can give. If FSD was engaged in Fairfield, it drove straight into one at highway speed.

FSD (Supervised) uses the cabin camera to make sure the driver is watching the road. It’s supposed to nag, then disengage, when someone isn’t paying attention. A driver who’s actually asleep shouldn’t get very far.

So either he hadn’t been asleep for long, or the camera didn’t catch it. We don’t know which yet. But Tesla has been relaxing that monitoring, not tightening it. We recently reported that Tesla drivers can easily bypass the monitoring system, even a simple dollar bill placed in front of the camera can nullify it.

Last December, Elon Musk claimed FSD drivers can now text and drive “depending on context of surrounding traffic.” Texting while driving is illegal in California.

The driver is responsible. If he fell asleep at the wheel at freeway speed, he nearly killed two cops who were already cleaning up after a fatal crash, and he should face the consequences. CHP is right about that.

But as usual, I have to emphasize Tesla’s responsibility here. Tesla doesn’t get to wash its hands of this. It sells a product called “Full Self-Driving” in the US, it agreed to drop that name in Europe this month after German regulators called it misleading, and its CEO tells owners they can text with it. When you tell people the car drives itself, some of them believe you. A 22-year-old driving home at midnight is exactly who believes you.

It's an established principle of design that the company making a product must make it safe, and that includes ways that users might easily and readily work around safeguards or written instructions. Its obvious that Tesla has a legal responsibility to detect sleeping drivers, and they have the means to do so. In fact, it's not that hard or costly for them.

It’s the complacency issue I have been talking about for years. FSD gets more dangerous as it gets better. You go on social media and there are legions of Tesla fans who believe that the system is reliable for this. It clearly isn’t.

Juries are starting to see it that way too. Florida’s $243 million Autopilot verdict put a third of the blame on Tesla, and the Katy, Texas FSD crash earlier this year showed the same complacency trap from another angle. Driver fault and Tesla fault aren’t mutually exclusive.

And there’s the technical failure. Emergency scenes are the exact scenario NHTSA flagged in 2021. Five years and a 2-million-vehicle recall later, a Tesla on Tesla’s most advanced driver-assist software allegedly drove into one anyway. Musk keeps promising unsupervised FSD on customer cars. If Tesla wants to take the driver out of the loop, will it take the liability for crashes like this one? Until it does, it shouldn’t be telling anyone it’s OK to look away.

#5649. There Should Be Black Holes in our Neighbourhood. Can We Find Them?

Scientific estimates show that there should be between about 100 million and 1 billion stellar-mass black holes in the Milky Way. But they aren't easy to locate. They're not like supermassive black holes (SMBH) at the center of galaxies. Those are usually easy to spot for different reasons.

When a SMBH is actively accreting material, the material gathers in a rotating accretion ring, where it heats up and emits energetic radiation. These are called active galactic nuclei (AGN). The brightest of them are called quasars, and they can be seen from vast distances. AGN and quasars emit a pair of powerful astrophysical jets opposite thei axis of rotations, and in some cases these are pointed straight at Earth. We call these blazars, and they announce their presence with a lighthouse-like beam of energy that can't be missed.

Other SMBH are found by their powerful gravity and the effect it has on their surroundings. Sgr A*, the Milky Way's SMBH, is a quiescent black hole that feeds on only a trickle of matter. It emits almost no radiation. It was detected by the way its gravity accelerates the stars that are closest to it, and how that shapes their orbits.

Stellar-mass black holes are much more plentiful, but they're also more subdued than SMBH and more difficult to detect. Still, they must be out there, and some should be in our neighbourhood. Modelling predicts them, and also predicts that they should be isolated.

Research in The Astrophysical Journal presented an effort to find them in Gaia data. It's titled "Searching for Isolated Black Hole Candidates within 15 pc of the Solar System in Gaia DR3," and the lead author is Abdurakhmon Nosirov. Nosirov is from the Center for Astronomy and Astrophysics, Department of Physics, in Fudan University, Shanghai, China.

The Local Interstellar Cloud (LIC) is a cloud of partially ionized gas. The Solar System is moving through the LIC, near its edge. Other interstellar clouds, like the G-Cloud, are nearby, and collectively they're referred to as Local Interstellar Clouds (LICs).

But predicting the electromagnetic emissions of these black holes is challenging because of "large uncertainties in the expected accretion spectra," according to the authors. The situation is different for black holes within the LICs and those outside of it.

Outside of the LIC, there's very little for black holes to feed on. "Outside these clouds, the accretion rate would be too low; according to our models, the resulting electromagnetic flux is well below the detection thresholds of current and near-future observational facilities," the researchers explain. But inside the LICs, the interstellar medium should be dense enough for black hole accretion to be detectable.

Electromagnetic flux is only one way to detect a black hole. The other way is through their gravitational effect on nearby stars, which could be measured with astrometry. Sadly, that won't work in our neighbourhood. "While astrometric detection via gravitational perturbation of nearby stars is conceivable, the local stellar density is too low for this method to be realistically successful," the authors write.

The researchers turned to Gaia and its Data Release 3 to find potential isolated black holes that are accreting from the ISM, and they found five. (Gaia found six, but one is excluded as a brown dwarf.) Again, astrometry is ruled out as a solution. "All candidates lie close to the Galactic plane, making them likely spurious astrometric solutions, for instance caused by unmodeled background sources (crowding) and/or unmodeled binarity," the researchers explain.

So it came down to their modelled infrared and radio emissions.

Gaia gathered enough spectroscopic data to detect black hole candidates, but not enough to confirm them. But there are multiple catalogues of emission data covering the 15 parsecs surrounding the Solar System. CatWISE is one of them, a catalogue of infrared sources from NASA's Wide-field Infrared Survey.

RACS, and VLASS are radio catalogues of the sky. RACS is Rapid ASKAP Continuum Survey, and VLASS is the Very Large Array Sky Survey. RACS has observed the radio sky repeatedly with the entire 36-dish ASKAP radio telescope, and VLASS catalogued 10 million individual radio sources.

"Although the detection thresholds of the radio catalogs are close to the fluxes predicted by our models, both models agree that, if these sources were black holes accreting from the interstellar medium, they should be detectable in CatWISE," the researchers write.

Sadly for black hole aficionadoes, no sources were found that matched the Gaia candidates.

"Our search for infrared and radio emission from these sources further suggests that they are unlikely to be black holes accreting from the interstellar medium," the authors write.

This effort wasn't fruitful, but that doesn't mean the search will end. Other research shows that any isolated black hole that was accreting from the ISM would be very faint, and would exhibit a fairly bland spectrum without many features. But that doesn't mean they're undetectable. It just shows that they're likely undectable with observations with single instruments.

"Multiwavelength follow-up campaigns, covering radio, optical, and X-ray bands, are necessary to rule out alternative interpretations and confirm the nature of any candidate," the authors write.

"Future efforts will focus on searching for isolated black holes accreting from the warm, partially ionized interstellar medium in radio and X-ray catalogs," the authors write in their conclusion.

In early 2027, the Square Kilometer Array will be releasing another tranche of data. Will it be the data that allows black hole hunters to be successful?

That data "appears especially promising for this endeavor," the researchers conclude.

#5647. All the Ways to Book a Private Jet—Or At Least Snag a Seat on One

I've often heard it said that once you've flown first class, you're ruined for any lesser form of travel. But once you've flown private, you've got a whole new lens through which to view air travel. Primarily, one in which TSA lines are nonexistent, security checks are not your problem, and rather than mill around a gate waiting for your boarding group to be called, you can saunter on up to the plane at your leisure. It's an entirely different ballgame.

I learned this firsthand when I flew with Flexjet from New Jersey's Teterboro Airport to Paris this summer. It was my first time flying private, and I couldn't get over how seamless the experience was. While a commercial transatlantic flight would have left me feeling tired and ragged, my body barely registered the six-hour journey. I could get up from my plush leather seat whenever I wanted to stretch my legs, freshen up in the spacious bathroom with seemingly endless amenities (no really, options ranged from Burt's Bees lip balm to Neutrogena makeup remover wipes, tissue packets, hand sanitizers and lotions…even playing cards!), or chat with fellow passengers over a glass of Champagne. Don't even get me started on the meals—pastries, quiche, and yogurt parfaits for breakfast; tuna niçoise salads for lunch—all made fresh by our personal chef Francesco, who was the bar manager at Villa d'Este before coming to Flexjet. For the first time, I found myself wishing the flight was longer.

Indulgent as it was, my trip with Flexjet also demonstrated that private jet travel no longer requires access to your own personal G5. In response to the pandemic's private jet boom, new booking options have emerged for travelers looking to take to the skies in seclusion—and many of them with ticket prices that deliver less sticker shock than you might imagine.

Even though social distancing may not be the term du jour any longer, the state of commercial aviation certainly has travelers keen to fly on their own time. “Private aviation has experienced remarkable growth over the past several years, reflecting a fundamental shift in what travelers value most,” says D.J. Hanlon, Flexjet's EVP of global sales. “Time has become the ultimate luxury, and people are looking for greater flexibility, efficiency, and a more personalized travel experience.”

However, for those who, like myself, are a newly initiated high-flier, navigating the multitude of companies and different categories of private air travel can feel overwhelming. Below is a primer on some of the various ways to score a seat on a private aircraft—from fly-share services and traditional charters to fractional ownership, and more.

Flyjets enables users to sort results by preferred aircraft size and desired departure time.

These days, all it takes is a strong Wi-Fi connection (and a handy app or two) to get yourself onto a private plane. A number of services connect fliers directly with charter operators, allowing you to browse, book, and buy within minutes.

FlyHouse—which acquired JetASAP in 2024 and Sun Air Jets in 2025, allowing it to expand its tech and operational capabilities as well as its geographic reach—allows fliers to secure and book private flights directly from their smartphones. It has access to more than 2,300 aircraft across the US, with a variety of midsize to ultra-long-range jets. Within the app, travelers can coordinate not just private flights but hotels, ground transportation, dining, and curated experiences too. Fliers are never required to pay a commission—just punch in a flight request, which includes your airport of departure and preferred flight times, and live operators will respond with quotes and can answer any questions. Private charters typically cost between $3,000 to $12,000 per in-flight hour.

The FlyJets platform debuted in January 2020, and operates like a travel search engine, allowing customers to filter results via their preferred aircraft size, location, and desired departure time and book via the website or app. Prices can be as much as 50% lower than the costs of an average charter, and booking options include full charters, seats on shared charters, and group-purchases. Travelers can even bid on flights that lack a fixed price. They can also earn rewards for flying green, with carbon offsets delivered directly to their user dashboard.

Jettly was designed to simplify the act of chartering a private plane. It has a network of more than 23,000 aircrafts, and lets user search for one-way flights, round-trip tickets, and multi-city travels, flying out of popular airports like Van Nuys in LA, Teterboro in New Jersey, and Miami International Airport. Flights can be arranged within a few hours, but the platform recommends booking several days in advance to ensure the widest aircraft availability and best pricing. While a membership is not required, Jettly does offers memberships that of course come with perks—lower hourly rates, waived booking fees, and access to things like empty leg alerts.

Founded in 2010 and based in London, Victor is another digital marketplace that prides itself of transparent, real-time pricing (including the aircraft tail number and photos of the aircraft, so you know exactly what to expect) and carbon-offset initiatives. An account is required, but registering is free; once you're in, you're assigned a Personal Account Manager to handle all your travels.

XO, a private jet marketplace that was acquired by Vista in 2018, has access to thousands of safety-vetted aircraft with real-time pricing. The mobile app and website offers both “buy as you fly” and membership options, with featured flight deals ranging from $3,000 to $90,000.

Wheels Up offers a Signature Membership as well as on-demand charter options.

If you want to reserve an entire plane for a one-time flight, private jet charters are the most straightforward way to book. Charter companies work with dozens of private air operators to book on-demand flights and manage door-to-door logistics, with no long-term commitment required. Many also offer the option to purchase a jet card, which you can load up either with a fixed number of flight hours or with money you spend like a debit card.

Founded in 2013, Wheels Up provides access to more than 7,000 aircrafts, including its own controlled fleet of Phenom 300 and Challenger 300 series jets. Initially a membership-only offering, the Wheels Up app launched in 2021 widened the pool so that nonmembers could browse and book one-off flights.

In the years since, the brand has continued to iterate on its membership tiers, including launching a Signature Membership program in 2025, giving anyone willing to fork over $200,000 guaranteed nationwide availability and 365-days-a-year access to its fleet; oh, and Delta perks too—namely, Delta SkyMiles Diamond Medallion status (based on qualifying Wheels Up spend). Members can also use Wheels Up funds to purchase Delta flights and receive a 20% discount on Delta flights booked through the Wheels Up platform.

One of the biggest global aircraft charter brokers in the world, ACS charters private jets (for both business and leisure), group aircrafts, and even cargo planes. Users fill out a simple form to get a quote back quickly, and then a dedicated account manager will suggest aircrafts that might suit your needs—considering they have access to 50,000 private airplanes and 130 different aircraft types, this is quite helpful. While costs vary, estimates provided say a 40-minute flight in a small plane could cost around $2,800, while a 1.5-hour flight in a midsize jet will be closer to $34,500.

Fly-share companies are the easiest on the wallet. Companies like Surf Air and JSX enable travelers to snag a coveted seat on a private jet charter—sometimes for as little as $249 each way—sharing space with only a handful of other passengers and enjoying perks like no security lines and departure from private terminals.

Started in 2019 by Uber cofounder Garrett Camp, Aero offers white-glove service on a fleet of sleek 16-seater black jets. The company flies to private terminals in Aspen, Colorado; Los Cabos, Mexico; Los Angeles (Van Nuys); Maui; Miami; New York (Westchester and NYC); and West Palm Beach, Florida. Tickets on these semi-private flights (unless your whole crew books one together) range from around $1,000 to $3,000 per leg, and pets are welcome on board.

This summer, Aero launched an exciting new seasonal route on a Gulfstream IV-SP taking passengers from from Van Nuys in LA into New York City, with a helicopter transfer via Blade out to East Hampton. The weekend flights went out on Fridays and returned on Sundays, with seats costing $7,570.

Blade is a popular choice for city-based travelers, with dozens and dozens of established flight routes. It connects fliers not just with jets but also turboprops, seaplanes, and helicopters in their contracted fleet via a user-friendly app. This year, its BladeOne planes are back to offering seasonal service from NYC to Miami and Palm Beach (on planes powered by Aero) from November 2026 through May 2027. There's no membership required and fliers can purchase multipacks of flight passes on certain routes (the New York to South Florida packs start at $2,300 per seat).

A Traveler reader favorite, Tradewind Aviation operates semi-private scheduled routes and private charters in the northeast (US) and the Caribbean. Specifically, its aircraft bases are in Westchester, New York, and Bedford, Massachusetts (heading to Nantucket and Martha's Vineyard); Fort Lauderdale and Stuart, Florida (with a new route from FLL to Nassau coming this December); and San Juan, Puerto Rico, and St. Barts; though charter flights are available throughout the regions.

Dog lovers may already know about Bark Air, a semi-private flight carrier designed around luxury travel for pups and their owners, with planes typically carrying around nine human guests. Read more about the airline here.

JSX, a semi-private carrier, offers flights on 30-seat jets, out of private terminals, starting at $249 each way. The company currently serves 18 markets including Austin, Texas; Los Angeles; Miami; and Westchester County, New York. Thanks to a partnership with United Airlines, travelers can earn United MileagePlus miles for their flights.

Flexjet operates a fleet of more than 300 aircrafts.

#5646. New smart glasses harvest energy every time you blink or smile

Engineers built glasses with sensors that turn blinks, jaw clenching, and a heartbeat into electrical signals while using almost no power.

To do any good, a fitness tracker has to be on someone’s wrist, and most trackers have to come off to charge. Glasses are different. People who need them put them on in the morning and wear them all day.

Engineers have now built glasses with soft sensors that turn the small push of a blink, a clenched jaw, or a heartbeat into an electrical signal.

In tests, the frames told six face and jaw movements apart about 94 percent of the time, while the sensing circuit used only a few millionths of a watt. That’s a step toward health-tracking glasses that wouldn’t need a big battery to keep running.

Nivedita Arora, an assistant professor of electrical and computer engineering and computer science at Northwestern University, led the work. She is an expert in self-powered electronics and leads the university’s VAK Embodied Systems Lab.

“The human face is a goldmine for health data, making everyday glasses the perfect wearable,” she said.

“Our idea is based on the fact that half the population already wears glasses,” she added. “We can turn those glasses into wearables without asking people to change their habits and behaviors.”

Her team presented the glasses, called GlassTENG, on October 15 at UbiComp/ISWC 2026, a wearable computing conference in Shanghai. The device is still a lab prototype, that has, thus far, been tested on 20 volunteers.

Anyone who has rubbed a balloon on their hair has felt the triboelectric effect, a form of static electricity. When two different materials touch and then pull apart, electrons can move from one to the other and leave a small electrical charge behind.

Each sensor on the glasses is a paper-thin stack of two films, one made of soft, rubbery silicone and the other of thin plastic. Both carry a coating of silver about 150 nanometers thick, hundreds of times thinner than a human hair.

When a muscle moves or an artery pulses under the sensor, the films press together, and when the push eases, they separate and produce a small voltage.

“As the layers touch and separate, they generate a tiny voltage,” Arora said. “That means the very movement being measured supplies the mechanical energy needed to power its detection. In other words, a blink helps power the sensor that detects the blink.”

The team placed three sensors on the frame. One sits on the nose pad, over an artery near the inner corner of the eye. It picks up the pulse along with forceful blinks and eyebrow raises.

A second sensor is mounted on a small extra arm that reaches down from the frame to rest against the temple. It catches clenching, teeth tapping, and chewing.

The third is built into the arm of the glasses just above the ear, over a jaw muscle, where the frame’s natural grip keeps it pressed against the skin.

A pulse produced about 50 to 100 millivolts. Blinks and eyebrow raises produced 200 to 500 millivolts, and hard jaw movements reached as high as 1.5 volts.

“These differences give various facial activities their own recognizable electrical signatures,” Arora said.

Eleven women and nine men took part. Half of them wore glasses every day. Each person wore GlassTENG for a 30-minute session.

During it, they blinked hard, raised their eyebrows, clenched their teeth, tapped their teeth together, ate, and talked.

A computer program learned these signal patterns from 19 people and then guessed the activities of the person it hadn’t seen, a test that was repeated for every volunteer.

Its overall accuracy was 93.8 percent. The program got blinks and teeth tapping right 99 percent of the time and clenching 96 percent of the time.

Talking was the hardest activity to recognize. The program identified it correctly only 55 percent of the time and mistook it for eating in 37 percent of cases, because the jaw moves in similar ways for both.

The glasses also tracked heart rate. Ten volunteers sat still for five minutes while wearing a chest strap that measures heart rate accurately. On average, the glasses’ reading landed within 1.82 beats per minute of the strap’s.

Many health sensors in smart glasses use light or electrodes, and those draw milliwatts, or thousands of microwatts. The circuit that reads each GlassTENG sensor uses 1.36 microwatts. All three together use about 4.1 microwatts.

The team ran the numbers on a small battery running a basic low-power chip. Adding a common light-based heart rate sensor would cut that battery’s life by 1.6 to 9.8 hours. All three GlassTENG sensors would cut it by less than half a minute.

Study coauthor Nabil Alshurafa is an associate professor of preventive medicine at Northwestern, with appointments at both its Feinberg School of Medicine and McCormick School of Engineering.

“One of the most exciting possibilities is using this technology to turn unconscious behaviors into opportunities for meaningful behavior,” he said.

The tested glasses weren’t battery-free. A separate circuit board read the signals and saved them to a memory card, and the team analyzed the data afterward.

Pairing the sensors with an ultra-low-power chip, and running everything on harvested energy are still to come.

“If it detected a physiological event, it could have on-device intelligence in a battery-free way that could give immediate feedback. For example, if it detected jaw clenching, it could immediately alert the user to relax their jaw,” Arora said.

All of the testing happened in a lab, one activity at a time. The team wrote that a large study of people going about their normal day would help separate talking from eating. They also want to drop the extra arm at the temple, since the sensor above the ear catches many of the same signals.

Volunteers who already wore glasses rated their chances of using GlassTENG daily at 5.4 on a 7-point scale. The others rated theirs at 3.5.

Whether people would wear GlassTENG every day is still open, and the next version, built on a printed circuit board, will have to answer that.

#5644. A Single Gene Could Hold the Key to Taming One of the Deadliest Cancers

Blocking one gene’s activity shifted aggressive pancreatic cancer cells into a less aggressive form in experiments, pointing to a possible way to disrupt tumor evolution.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma kills nearly 85 percent of people within five years of diagnosis. Part of the challenge is that its cancer cells can change, shifting between less aggressive and more aggressive forms in ways that earlier studies could not explain.

A study led by researchers at NYU Langone Health and its Perlmutter Cancer Center now shows how genetic reshaping within individual tumors enables those transitions. Published in Nature Genetics, the findings suggest that ongoing changes to cancer cells’ DNA help fuel both resistance to treatment and metastasis, the spread of cancer to other parts of the body.

More than half a dozen genes have been implicated in this complex disease. Earlier research generally measured increases and decreases in gene activity across groups of cells analyzed together, separating pancreatic cancer cells into two broad subtypes: classical, which is less aggressive, and basal, which is more aggressive. Those bulk measurements could not explain how cells switched between the two.

To examine the differences among individual cells, the researchers used transcriptomics, which measures gene activity. They analyzed 443,451 single cells from 62 tumors in 56 men and women with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Six patients provided tumor samples both before and after chemotherapy, allowing the team to examine changes across treatment.

“Our study provides the most granular view to date of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, revealing how ongoing genetic reshaping drives tumor evolution over the course of disease,” said study lead investigator Despoina Kalfakakou, PhD, a postdoctoral research fellow at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and Perlmutter Cancer Center.

Within the tumors, the team identified clones, groups of cells with similar genetic makeup. Individual tumors contained between two and 11 clones on average, with mixtures of basal and classical cells. These groups behaved differently as they evolved, with some developing resistance to therapy and others acquiring the ability to spread rapidly.

“Our research also suggests that single-cell clonal analysis offers a better approach to understanding complex cancers, like pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, as it develops over time, by mapping how tumors evolve and adapt at each stage of disease progression,” said study co-senior investigator Aristotelis Tsirigos, PhD, a professor in the Departments of Medicine and Pathology at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and a member of Perlmutter Cancer Center.

Using patterns of gene activity to trace the clones’ genetic origins, the researchers linked variations in DNA copy number to the mix of cells making up each tumor. This helped them identify which genes were most active at different stages of disease progression. Among the changes associated with tumor evolution was increased activity of a gene called MYC in aggressive tumor cell populations.

In further experiments with basal pancreatic tumor cells, blocking MYC activity returned the cells to the less aggressive classical form. It also exposed them to more immune cells, potentially making them more vulnerable to immune attack. “These findings suggest that targeting the mechanisms underlying tumor evolution, including MYC, may help restrain the cancer’s rapid progression,” Kalfakakou said.

Other differences among clones offered clues to why this cancer resists immunotherapy. In some clones, the TIGIT gene was more active, producing proteins that help cancer evade immune detection. In those same cells, activity of the PD1 gene was suppressed, which may help explain why some immunotherapies targeting PD1 are rarely effective against the disease.

“More broadly, our study results highlight the critical role of clonal diversity, genetic plasticity, and interactions with the tumor’s microenvironment in shaping human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma biology,” said Dr. Tsirigos, who also serves as co-director of the Division of Precision Medicine and director of NYU Langone’s Applied Bioinformatics Laboratories.

Clinical trials of MYC inhibitors or TIGIT inhibitors for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and other cancers are underway, Tsirigos said. He added that the findings should encourage larger investigations into the different states cancer cells can occupy and how treatments might target them.

For 17 years, Arturo Casadevall has watched an evolutionary crisis of his own forecasting unfold in slow motion across the globe.

Driven by a warming planet and shifting human physiology, the fungal kingdom—home to yeasts and molds—is breaching our species’ biological defenses. Cases of deadly fungal infections are surging, and modern medicine is woefully ill-equipped to stop them.

“Humanity is not prepared for a fungal pandemic,” warns Casadevall, MD, PhD, who is chair of molecular microbiology and immunology at Johns Hopkins University. We are on perilous terrain, he says, and asleep at the wheel.

The warning signs began in 2009, when an unassuming yeast was isolated from the ear canal of a patient in Japan. It arrived already armed with resistance to one of our three primary classes of antifungal drugs.

“Nobody thinks anything of it at first, because you report new fungal diseases every year,” Casadevall explains. “But what was amazing was what happened over the next few years: Candida auris (named for the Latin word for ear) emerged simultaneously as a human pathogen across South America, South Africa, and the Indian subcontinent. And the isolates were completely unrelated.” Unconnected strains soon surfaced in Iran, Russia, and Singapore. Today, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) ranks Candida auris as an “urgent threat”—its highest alert level for a microbe.

The clinical portrait of a Candida auris infection reads like a medical thriller. It doesn’t confine itself to the ear. It can persist for weeks on all surfaces, including dry hospital furniture, and if it hitches a ride on an intravenous catheter, it quickly slips into the bloodstream. Once inside, it colonizes internal organs, coating the liver, lungs, and heart. The body attempts to mount a defense, but in up to 60 percent of invasive cases, the host’s immune system fails to contain it, leading to death.

According to Casadevall, what is driving this global rise in fungal pathogen spread is that the difference between the temperatures of mammals and the temperatures of their ambient environments has been narrowing. Most fungi thrive in cooler environments, and for millennia humans have been protected by a biological “thermal shield”—our internal body heat is simply too warm for most fungal species to replicate. But a warming climate is forcing environmental fungi to adapt to higher heat, suddenly making the human body a far more hospitable environment.

This thermal convergence is further compounded by a quiet physiological shift: Modern human bodies are getting cooler. Studies have found that thanks to cleaner living environments, widespread hygiene, and modern medicine, our immune systems face less chronic inflammation than our ancestors did, resulting in a measurable drop in baseline human body temperature over the last century.

Ana Traven, PhD, a professor of medical mycology at Monash University in Melbourne, stresses that public awareness and research investment lag dangerously behind the evolving fungal threat. But her primary concern isn’t just climate change—it is our alarming lack of medical defenses. Unlike bacterial infections, for which doctors can choose from dozens of antibiotic classes, modern medicine relies on a meager three major classes of antifungals to treat invasive diseases. To make matters worse, fungal resistance against this thin line of defense is climbing fast, leaving clinicians with dangerously few options when treatments fail.

“Overspraying crops is 100% the problem,” Traven says. “We are spraying crops and using the same classes of drugs that we use in the clinic on these crops.” Industrial agriculture routinely douses fields with triazoles to protect food supplies from crop rot. This practice creates environmental pressure that selects for resistant fungal mutations long before the fungi ever encounter a human patient. As a result, emerging environmental yeasts enter hospital intensive care units already armed against our frontline drugs.

Designing effective antifungal therapeutics is an exceptionally tough evolutionary nut to crack. Unlike bacteria, which are prokaryotes with vastly different cell structures, fungi are eukaryotes—meaning they share our complex cellular architecture and biochemical pathways.

“With bacteria, you have a better chance to kill the bacterium without killing the human,” Traven says. “With fungi, the cell biology, the biochemistry, everything is conserved. So it’s really hard to find targets that you can hit that would be fungal-specific.”

To bypass the challenge of finding fungus-specific drug targets, Traven turned her focus to the human immune system. Her team discovered that when Candida yeasts invade, they outcompete host immune cells for sugar, triggering a metabolic stress response that activates NINJ1, a macrophage-rupturing protein. These immune cells—which fight fungi by engulfing them whole—burst under elevated levels of NINJ1, allowing the fungus to break free from the host immune defenses and spread through tissue. Traven’s team determined that administering the amino acid alanine to the host blocks this NINJ1-driven rupture.

While promising, Traven stresses that this strategy is nowhere near enough. One major issue is that the field of medical mycology remains far too small globally. To solve complex fungal threats, science needs a “critical mass” of researchers approaching the problem from diverse, creative angles, because “you never know where the big breakthrough is going to come from.”

As deadly as Candida auris is, it lacks the machinery to spark a true global pandemic—its reach remains largely confined to direct contact among the most vulnerable, particularly the elderly and immunocompromised. But Casadevall warns that a far more contagious pathogen could eventually emerge.

“How would it work? You need a fungus to be airborne,” Casadevall says. “And it can happen if the fungus sporulates in the lung and you cough it up.”

For anyone who believes an airborne fungal pandemic feels comfortably remote, Casadevall offers a sobering reality check. “They say—go ask the Spartans. I say—go ask the frogs, which have lost 100 species already in the greatest cataclysm that has happened to amphibians,” he says. “Frogs have been killed the world over in a fungal pandemic. Bats have been killed in North America. Salamanders in Europe are being killed right now.”

The fungal kingdom has already proven its capacity for destroying species; humanity’s only choice is whether we wake up before we become its next target.

#5637. Scientists Develop a Remarkable New Coating for the Powerhouses of Our Cells

Scientists have given the tiny powerhouses of our cells a remarkable high-tech makeover that preserves their energy production and could pave the way for future treatments.

Scientists have developed a way to coat mitochondria, the tiny structures responsible for producing energy inside cells, with advanced materials without interfering with their ability to function. The breakthrough could eventually help researchers develop new approaches to treating diseases caused by malfunctioning mitochondria.

The discovery comes from a collaboration between IRCCS Fondazione Istituto Neurologico Carlo Besta (FINCB) and Politecnico di Milano. The work was conducted through the BraiNs joint laboratory and published in the Journal of the American Chemical Society (JACS).

The project combines the expertise of two research teams. One is led by Dr. Valeria Tiranti (FINCB), who works in the Medical Genetics and Neurogenetics Complex Unit (SC) and heads the Fondazione Mariani Center for Pediatric Mitochondrial Diseases. The other is led by Professor Pierangelo Metrangolo (Politecnico di Milano), whose team specializes in chemistry and materials engineering.

Often described as the powerhouses of cells, mitochondria generate the energy needed to keep essential cellular processes running. When they malfunction, they can contribute to a range of medical conditions, including rare mitochondrial diseases.

These disorders are particularly harmful to tissues that require large amounts of energy, such as the brain and muscles. Treatment options remain limited, making new strategies for working with mitochondria especially valuable.

The researchers focused on a class of advanced materials known as Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOF). These materials can be customized for different purposes by adjusting their properties. Their scientific importance was recognized with the 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Susumu Kitagawa, Richard Robson and Omar Yaghi.

Applying these materials to mitochondria presents a considerable challenge because the cellular structures are extremely delicate. Even small changes to their surroundings could potentially interfere with their essential functions.

To overcome this difficulty, the researchers developed a technique that allows them to apply very small quantities of the materials directly to the mitochondrial surface.

Most importantly, the mitochondria continued producing energy after receiving the coating. This demonstrates that their surfaces can be modified with these materials without compromising their energy generating function.

The ability to customize the coating could make the technique useful for a variety of future applications. By changing the material’s properties, researchers may eventually be able to create coatings designed to carry out specific tasks.

“The possibility of modifying the coating now opens up new research prospects: in the future, it will be possible to develop MOF with different characteristics depending on the objective, creating coatings capable of performing specific functions,” observes Prof. Metrangolo.

One potential application involves mitochondrial transplantation, an experimental strategy that seeks to introduce healthy mitochondria into cells whose existing mitochondria are not functioning properly.

“A specifically designed coating could, in the future, facilitate this transfer and the interaction between mitochondria and cells,” emphasizes Dr. Tiranti.

Although the findings offer a promising direction for further research, the potential benefits for mitochondrial transplantation have not yet been demonstrated. Additional experiments will be necessary to establish whether coated mitochondria perform better during transplantation than those without coatings.

In the late 1800s, Westminster Abbey clergy stumbled upon a mysterious sarcophagus. The lid was decorated with an 11th-century Anglo-Saxon cross, but the base had a fourth-century Roman funeral inscription. For nearly 150 years, the tomb has baffled experts, but the abbey's consulting archaeologist may have finally figured out who was buried there, according to a new study.

Westminster's chief mason unearthed the stone sarcophagus in 1869, but when experts investigated it, they discovered that the burial had been looted earlier that century. Experts also noticed that the carved decoration on the side of the sarcophagus was stylistically Roman, with a Latin inscription that can be translated as "To the memory of Valerius Amandinus. Valerius Superventor and Marcellus made this for their father." But an enormous medieval cross had been carved into the lid of the sarcophagus, suggesting that the tomb had been reused to bury an important religious figure hundreds of years later.

The mystery of the medieval individual buried in a Roman sarcophagus remained unsolved for 150 years, as no one thought that any bones were left inside. But when the stone coffin was opened for conservation in 2016 and a full skeleton was discovered, clues to the identity of the deceased finally came to light, Warwick Rodwell, the consultant archaeologist for Westminster Abbey, wrote in a study published Wednesday (Oct. 21) in the Journal of the British Archaeological Association.

Although the skeleton had been disturbed over the years, with a hole accidentally made in the skull and arm and leg bones mixed up, it was clear that the person was male, middle-aged, and of average height, bioarchaeologist James Langthorne of Pre-Construct Archaeology wrote in a 2016 report. The man also had some evidence of osteoarthritis, two healed rib injuries and gout. The bones were carbon-dated to between 987 and 1052, confirming that the Roman-era sarcophagus was reused in the Middle Ages.

"This brings us to the final question of importance: who was he?" Rodwell wrote in the study. Despite the lack of grave goods, which may have been stolen in the early 19th century, "he was almost certainly an abbot" who was given a dignified burial in a stone sarcophagus with a large cross, Rodwell wrote.

Based on the birth and death dates of the abbots of Westminster and their tenure as clergy, Rodwell narrowed the candidates down to Wulfsige (abbot from 988 to 993), Ælfwig (abbot from 993 to 1020) and Wulfnoth (abbot from 1020 to 1049). Although the final resting place of Ælfwig may have been found after a millennium, some questions remain unanswered.

For example, it is unclear why an 11th-century abbot was buried in a recycled fourth-century Roman sarcophagus. The reuse of Roman coffins is extremely unusual, Rodwell noted in the study, and nothing comparable to this sarcophagus has ever been found. #5633. Vultures have a secret world of sounds, even before they hatch

Scientists discovered that Rüppell's vultures use 16 different calls during feeding, conflicts, and breeding, revealing a surprisingly complex social life.

Nobody enjoys watching another diner reach over and take food from their plate. Add a few dozen hungry neighbors, leave little room to move, and even a good meal can turn into an argument.

For a Rüppell’s vulture, that crowded gathering happens around a dead animal.

Getting a bite means dealing with other birds that want the same food, and the exchange can get surprisingly noisy.

That noise challenges a familiar image of vultures as nearly silent scavengers.

Researchers recorded 35 Rüppell’s vultures in two Dutch zoos and identified 16 kinds of calls.

The birds even start calling before they hatch. Those early sounds have left researchers wondering whether helping parents hear their chicks inside the egg could improve breeding success.

Rüppell’s vultures spend much of their lives close to other vultures. In the wild, they nest on cliffs in colonies that can hold hundreds or even thousands of closely packed pairs.

Connor Proudfoot studied their calls for his master’s thesis at Utrecht University‘s Vulture Cognition Lab. He suspected that all those neighbors might help explain why the birds need a varied voice.

“And when they find a carcass, they often encounter dozens of other vultures and have to compete for a place,” Proudfoot said.

“You would expect a species living in such a complex social environment to have sophisticated ways of communicating, so we decided to look into that.”

Proudfoot labeled 2,500 calls recorded during everyday encounters, feeding, and breeding. Among the calls the team could match to a clear situation, most came during conflicts.

Food disputes brought out nine kinds of calls, more than any other activity. Five appeared only during those disputes.

A bird guarding meat might hiss while spreading its wings, stretching its neck, or stamping its feet. Some jabbed their necks toward a rival.

The bird hoping to get some food often brayed instead, repeating its call until it got a bite or someone chased it off.

Ignoring that bird did not necessarily make it quiet: its calls could grow more intense, and some vultures combined a bray with a hiss as an attack drew closer.

A bird on the receiving end of a threat or attack could respond with what researchers call a metallic groan. It turned up more often than any other call, making up almost three in ten recordings.

One groan could contain a single note or stretch into a string of up to 43.

Birds mostly made these sounds when another vulture threatened or attacked them over food or space.

The researchers think the groan might express protest or submission. Its many variations could let a bird signal how worked up it feels or that it wants the fight to stop.

That interpretation still needs testing, so nobody can yet translate a groan into a definite message.

But it gives researchers a useful starting point for asking how these birds manage disputes without making every encounter a fight.

The sounds did not always fit neatly into 16 separate categories. Some calls sounded distinct, while others varied and blended together.

A calm request and an angry demand can use the same words, yet sound very different. Proudfoot thinks changes in a vulture’s call might likewise reveal how strongly it feels.

“That blending of calls shows there is flexibility in how the birds communicate. For example, they can convey not just that they are excited, but also how excited they are,” Proudfoot said.

The team has not tested what those differences mean to a listening vulture.

Still, the variety itself stands out: the authors place the species above the average bird in its number of call types, alongside some highly social primates.

Some of the most intriguing recordings came from birds that had never seen another vulture.

Chicks made soft, tuneful “hmm” calls after breaking into the air pocket inside the egg and starting to breathe.

The team heard those sounds because Diergaarde Blijdorp, one of the zoos in the study, hatches its vulture eggs in incubators.

Parents sit on model eggs while staff care for the real ones elsewhere.

After hatching, the same kind of call appeared to continue for months, becoming more varied along the way.

The researchers suggest this may resemble babbling, when young animals practice sounds they will use later.

Microphones inside three nest boxes also let the team listen to parents and chicks without disturbing them. That let them hear family life as well as the arguments around food.

At Blijdorp, staff hand-rear chicks for three to seven days before returning them to a nest. By then, the parents have missed the sounds their chicks made inside the egg.

“In zoos, eggs are often incubated artificially, so the parents can’t hear the chicks calling from inside the eggs,” Proudfoot said.

“As a result, they may be less prepared for the chicks’ arrival. Perhaps more chicks could be successfully reared if their parents could hear those sounds.”

Calls from inside eggs affect parents’ responses in animals including gulls, terns, and crocodiles. Researchers still need to find out what they do in vultures and whether playing them to parents would help them raise chicks.

The 35 zoo birds could only reveal part of the story.

Wild vultures travel together and watch one another while searching for food, so they may use calls that the researchers never had a chance to hear.

The study also cannot establish that social life caused the birds to develop so many calls.

To investigate that idea, researchers want to listen to species that spend less time together.

Lappet-faced and white-headed vultures, for example, mostly forage alone.

If they have fewer call types, that would support the idea that a crowded life creates a greater need for varied communication.

The Rüppell’s vulture population has declined by an estimated 97% over the past three generations.

That makes the possibility of helping parents raise more chicks worth investigating, even though researchers still need to test whether hearing egg calls would help.

The birds’ disappearance also matters beyond their colonies. The authors warn that widespread vulture losses could contribute to the spread of disease.

The recordings capture a critically endangered bird getting on with daily life: finding food, dealing with neighbors, and raising chicks.

The study is published in the journal PLOS One.

#5630. How Flare Ribbons Reveal Hidden Solar Explosions on the Sun

Many topics in astronomy and astrophysics are esoteric. There's not much of a relationship between something like the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way and the life of a regular person going about their business. But the Sun is different. Its presence in our lives is inescapable. Its behaviour, especially its flaring, has an impact.

Solar flaring makes itself known on Earth when their accompanying coronal mass ejections (CME) strike Earth and create mesmerizing aurorae. But they can be more than beautiful manifestations of light and magnetic fields. They can pack a punch, especially when they're really powerful.

Astrophysicists classify solar flares by their X-ray brightness. From least energetic to most energetic they're placed in categories A, B, C, M, and X. Each letter indicates a ten-fold increase in energy output.

A solar flare is actually a kind of explosion, and they're the most powerful explosions in the Solar System. They release an enormous amount of energy, equivalent to the simultaneous detonation of millions or even billions of hydrogen bombs. They dwarf supervolcano eruptions on Earth, and even the huge asteroid that ended the dinosaurs.

They're caused by the Sun's powerful magnetic fields, which are constantly twisted and rotated. Their specific trigger is reconnection, when the magnetic fields can no longer put up with the twisting and rotating and finally snap. They reconnect into a more straightforward and stable configuration. When that happens, some of the Sun's energy is released from its magnetic restraints and emitted as a solar flare.

Solar flares can cause radio blackouts when they strike the ionosphere, disrupting aviation and maritime communications. They can introduce inaccuracies in GPS systems by altering the altitude of satellites. They can sometimes create malfunctions in electronics on spacecraft. Their accompanying CMEs can also disrupt our power grids.

There's an ongoing effort to understand solar flares, and research published in The Astrophysical Journal is part of the effort. It's titled "Evolution of Spatial Complexity in Flare Ribbon Substructure and Its Relationship to Magnetic Reconnection Dynamics." The lead author is Marcel Corchado Albelo from the U.S. National Science Foundation and an ambassador for the Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope.

"Itâs a beautiful example of theory, observation, and collaboration coming together..." - Maria Kazachenko, co-author, National Solar Observatory.

Flares originate in the Sun's outermost layer, the corona, which is hidden by the Sun's glare, and they're difficult to observe there. But in this research, the authors focused on flare ribbons. These are kind of like a flare's footprints, made of plasma and high-energy particles that slam back into the Sun during reconnection. They follow magnetic field lines, and when they collide with the Sun's chromosphere, they light up brilliantly.

"Recent three-dimensional flare models suggest that flare ribbon substructure is linked to the fragmentation of the reconnecting current sheet in the corona," the authors write. "Flare ribbon substructure can therefore potentially serve as a unique diagnostic tool for physical processes in the flare current sheet."

The researchers figured out how to quantify the evolution of these flare ribbons and their substructure by looking at small details of the flare ribbons where they deposit their energy in the chromosphere. These details provided clues to what's happening far above.

They created a model and tested it against synthetic observations. Then they applied it to a real flare. "We then apply it to an M6.5-class solar flare on 2015 June 22 observed by the Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph (IRIS)," the authors write. IRIS is a solar observation satellite launched in 2013.

They found that when the flares deposit their energy, it's not a smooth or uniform process. Instead of one big energetic event, the flare ribbon breaks apart into different sized fragments and deposits its energy in bursts.

"For the first time we have experimentally shown a physical relationship between the irregular shapes we observe, and the structure of the energy conversion region," lead author Corchado Albelo said in a press release.

#5629. Scientists Discover “D-Rex,” a Giant Fungus Unlike Anything Seen Before

A newly discovered giant fungus belongs to a previously unknown family that may have emerged around 65 million years ago, shortly after the mass extinction that killed the non-avian dinosaurs.

A fungus discovered in China’s Yunnan forests produces whitish, spore-bearing structures up to 50 centimeters (about 20 inches) tall and weighing as much as 2.2 kilograms (4.9 pounds). The newly described species, Dianjunus rex, has the largest known fruiting bodies in the fungal phylum Ascomycota, surpassing previous record holders that stood 20 to 30 centimeters (about 8 to 12 inches) tall.

Ascomycota is the largest group of fungi, with more than 64,000 species. Its members, called ascomycetes, do not grow true mushrooms. Instead, they release spores from tiny, sac-like structures inside or on the surfaces of their fruiting bodies. For D. rex, producing such large reproductive structures could offer an advantage in a crowded forest, although the researchers have not established why it grows so big.

“A fruiting body which towers above surrounding vegetation and debris could have better dispersal of spores by wind in dense forests. However, such a large structure requires substantial energy investment, suggesting that this strategy likely evolved only where the ecological benefits outweighed the costs,” hypothesized Dr. Indunil C Senanayake, the study’s corresponding author and an associate professor at the Germplasm Bank of Wild Species of the Kunming Institute of Botany in China.

Scientists from the institute encountered the fungus during fieldwork in temperate coniferous forests near Damaidi and Yipinglang and in tropical rainforest near Nanbang. Its upright fruiting bodies, known as stromata, grew on the woody roots of Chinese cork oak, emerging from the rhizosphere, the soil immediately surrounding the roots. Senanayake said, “The discovery illustrates how much fungus biodiversity remains to be discovered in East Asian forests.”

To investigate what they had found, the researchers collected fruiting bodies and deposited voucher specimens in the Kunming Institute of Botany’s collections. They grew spores on laboratory media to study the fungus’s form and structure and sequenced its genome, including its full set of protein-coding genes.

The genetic results, supported by the detailed examination of its morphology, established a new species and genus within the order Xylariales. The findings, published in Frontiers in Fungal Biology, also warranted recognition at a broader level of classification.

“This remarkable species represents a new fungal family, Dianjunaceae, and provides a rare window into the evolution of extreme fungal body forms,” said Senanayake.

The researchers chose the name Dianjunus rex for the fungus’s regal size and the ancient Dian kingdom. Its closest known relative, Graphostroma platystoma, grows on dead hardwood across the Northern Hemisphere and is itself the sole member of another family, Graphostromataceae.

Other fungi in Xylariales are known for producing secondary compounds, chemicals beyond those essential for basic growth, as well as enzymes that break down complex plant molecules. Similar enzymes might help “D-rex” digest dead wood, a possibility that gives context to the researchers’ investigation of its evolutionary origins.

Using a molecular clock analysis, which estimates evolutionary timing from genetic differences, the team compared the new species’ DNA with genomes from hundreds of fungi with known evolutionary ages. The results suggested that the Dianjunaceae family arose around 65 million years ago, roughly one million years after the Cretaceous-Paleogene mass extinction. That event wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs and countless other animals and plants.

“This timing raises an intriguing possibility. After the mass extinction, forests experienced dramatic changes, including the sudden availability of large amounts of dead plant material,” said Senanayake. “For wood-associated decomposing fungi, such environmental disruption may have created new ecological opportunities.”

The researchers expect more fungal discoveries in subtropical China, where conditions favor diversification but finding and documenting species remains difficult.

“This region combines exceptional habitat heterogeneity, climatic stability, and complex biotic interactions that promote fungal diversification. Historically, however, fungal diversity in these ecosystems has been underestimated due to logistical difficulties in field sampling, the seasonal and cryptic nature of fungal fruiting, and a historical emphasis on economically important fungi,” said Senanayake.

Now, a new large-scale analysis of that archive, published in Psychology of Consciousness: Theory, Research, and Practice, has uncovered a statistical oddity that’s puzzling researchers.

The team, led by cognitive neuroscience researcher Saeedeh Sadeghi, analyzed 1,678 cases in which investigators had identified a named “Past Person” whose biographical details matched a child’s statements. Each case had been vetted by investigators interviewing the child, parents, relatives, and neighbors, then cross-checking the claims against verifiable records.

The variable of interest was “intermission time,” which means the time gap between the Past Person’s death and the child’s birth. Across the 1,319 cases with usable timing data, the median intermission was 16 months, although the longest observed intermission time in the dataset was a staggering 150 years.

Completely unexpected deaths were linked to a median gap of just 12 months, compared to 21 months for the most anticipated deaths. That’s a roughly 43 percent reduction, and the more unexpected the death, the shorter the interval.

The obvious counterargument is cultural contamination, since it seems intuitive that families who believe in reincarnation will tend to foster such stories. But the researchers carefully ran moderation analyses for verification quality, memory contamination, family association, social status, and reincarnation belief. Remarkably, they found that the pattern of shortened intermission time was stronger in families whose religions don’t endorse reincarnation at all.

“These results do not support the idea that social beliefs are driving this pattern,” Philip Cozzolino, a social psychologist at UVA, says in a press release.

An interesting detail from the research is that unnatural deaths accounted for 57% of the analyzed cases, while 68% of the deaths were rated as completely unexpected. The median age of the “Past Person” at the time of their death was also remarkably young, at just 30 years old

Death unexpectedness explained only about 2 to 3 percent of the variance in intermission time, and the data are purely observational. So it’s possible that subjective factors lie behind this strange data anomaly, and the results don’t prove that consciousness survives death.

What makes the data noteworthy, the researchers argue, is that any link exists at all, since no conventional mechanism connects how someone died to when an unrelated child is born. Intriguingly, the pattern echoes beliefs found in Hindu, Buddhist, and Indigenous North American traditions, where sudden death is thought to leave unfinished business that hastens rebirth.

“Is this evidence of anything metaphysical? The data are observational and we’re careful to stay agnostic about mechanism,” Sadeghi says in the press release. “What I can say is that the pattern is real in the data ... which, to me, is a reason to keep looking, not to look away.”

Quantum computers capable of doing a million reliable operations could bring about a new stage of quantum calculation. That “megaquop” regime — as it’s called — isn’t here yet. But scientists just took a significant step toward it.

A quantum computer built by the company Quantinuum has error rates so low that it could perform a million operations with only about five errors, researchers report in a paper posted October 19 on the company’s website and arXiv.org.

“These statistics are consistent with this being a good building block for that megaquop machine,” says quantum physicist David Hayes of Quantinuum, who is based in Broomfield, Colo. In order to reach that regime, however, such computers will need to be scaled up.

Quantinuum’s computer, known as Helios, is made of electrically charged atoms, called ions, hovering over a chip. Those ions serve as the 98 quantum bits, or qubits, of the computer. But, like all qubits, those ions are finicky. Their states are delicate and can flip unintentionally and mess up calculations, like a 0 randomly switching to a 1.

A technique called quantum error correction tackles this by combining multiple error-prone qubits into one, forming a supercharged unit called a logical qubit that is less prone to mistakes. (For those who grew up on 1990s TV, it’s a bit like how Captain Planet is born from the combined powers of the gang of Planeteers.)

Logical qubits come in different sizes, integrating a given number of physical qubits — the ions, in this case. Each time a logical qubit is scaled up, going, say, from seven physical qubits to 19, the error rate drops by a factor called lambda. A previous demonstration of error correction by Google, reported in Nature in 2024, had a lambda of about 2, meaning errors were cut in half each time the logical qubits were scaled up.

Quantinuum’s computer has a lambda of about 10. So errors dropped quickly when the researchers made larger logical qubits. A logical qubit composed of 37 physical qubits had just 5 errors in a million operations — close to what would be needed for the megaquop regime. However, that experiment involved only a single logical qubit. Reaching the megaquop regime could possibly involve around 50 to 100 logical qubits.

The large value of lambda means that achieving a desired error rate takes fewer physical qubits, says Ken Brown, a quantum engineer at Duke University and a scientific adviser for the company Logiqal who was not involved with the research. “That’s a huge savings in the overall number of qubits you would need. So that’s what’s exciting.”

#5625. These Mysterious Underwater Reefs Could Help Save Marine Life As Coral Reefs Decline

Submerged ancient forest floors off Mozambique now shelter young reef fish, suggesting overlooked peat reefs could offer some species refuge as coral reefs decline.

Much of the seabed along Mozambique’s Bazaruto coastline is shifting sand, leaving marine life few solid places to settle and shelter. Exposed layers of ancient peat provide that foothold, supporting fish communities comparable in diversity to those on a nearby coral reef.

These submerged “peat reefs” began as coastal swamps and forests. Their plant remains accumulated into peat, a compressed, carbon-rich material. As sea levels rose over the past few thousand years, the sea flooded these landscapes. Waves later stripped away the overlying sand, exposing firm platforms that marine life could colonize.

A study published in Scientific Reports documents these habitats in the shallow waters of the Bazaruto Archipelago in the Western Indian Ocean. Researchers from the Bazaruto Center for Scientific Studies (BCSS) Ocean Observatory worked with collaborators at universities and research institutions in the United Kingdom, United States, and Canada. The work is believed to be the first ecological study of tropical peat reefs and the first anywhere to examine peat reefs from a biodiversity perspective.

The team surveyed three peat reefs and a nearby coral reef, comparing seabed cover, invertebrates, small fish, and larger animals such as sharks, rays, and turtles. No living coral grew on the peat reefs. Instead, seagrass and seaweeds grew directly on the peat, while crevices and ledges sheltered spiny lobsters, oysters, barnacles, and juvenile fish. Young snappers and grunts, usually associated with coral reefs, likely use these spaces as refuges from predators.

Each peat reef supported 26 to 36 small fish species, with species diversity comparable to the coral reef. The two reef types shared much of their fish life, even though their communities of invertebrates attached to the seabed were entirely different. Sharks, rays, and turtles were also recorded on both reef types. Green turtles and dugongs occur in the area and could graze the seagrass patches, although that potential role remains distinct from the animals documented during the surveys.

“What was once a forest floor is now a nursery for reef fish. We were surprised by how much life these ancient peat deposits support, and how many species move between them and the coral reefs nearby. This coastline has very little natural hard seabed, mostly shifting sand. When rising seas expose these old peat layers, they create rare, solid habitat where marine life can settle, hide and feed,” said Dr. Mario Lebrato, lead author and co-founder of the BCSS Ocean Observatory.

The research was enabled by the observatory’s monitoring capabilities, described in two earlier papers in Frontiers in Marine Science. Established in Mozambique’s Bazaruto Archipelago in 2017, BCSS is Africa’s first permanent Ocean Observatory. It has generated more than 22 million measurements across over 30 chemical, environmental, and biodiversity variables.

Its network spans six interconnected ecosystems and 14 long-term monitoring stations across approximately 6,850 square kilometers, or 2,645 square miles, of the greater Bazaruto seascape. The habitats monitored range from mangroves and seagrass meadows to peat and coral reefs, lagoons, seamounts, and the open ocean.

The peat reefs occupy a particularly changeable part of that seascape. Sitting just 2–4 meters deep, or about 7–13 feet, they are regularly exposed by the tides and receive much more sunlight than the deeper coral reef. Temperatures can shift between 24.5 and 28.6°C, or 76.1 and 83.5°F, within a day. Storms and cyclones bury, uncover, and break apart the peat, and many reefs disappear through erosion and sand movement as sea levels continue to rise.

“The existence of tropical peat reefs underscores the resilience and adaptive reinvention potential of coastal ecosystems under pressure from sea-level rise and shoreline change. Their contribution to biodiversity, fisheries habitat, and potential legacy-carbon preservation should be recognized in coastal management and conservation strategies, particularly in regions where coral reef health is declining. The ability to demonstrate the vitality of these habitats as a result of our continuous observation excites me for the prospect of implementing conservation policy that protects similar regions around the world,” Lebrato said.

Radiocarbon, or carbon-14, dating places the formation of the peat between roughly 300 and 5,000 years ago. Chemical analysis confirms that it came mostly from coastal land plants and trees, and some samples contained up to 40% organic carbon by dry weight. Ancient plant carbon has persisted beneath the sea for thousands of years, but the amount held across these reefs remains unknown.

For comparison, peat swamps on land can hold 1,000–2,000 metric tons of carbon per hectare below ground. That does not establish how much carbon the submerged reefs contain, or whether they are storing or releasing carbon today.

Resolving those questions requires measurements of peat thickness and bulk density, which describes how much material occupies a given volume. Researchers also need to determine carbon stocks per unit area, the rates at which peat is buried or eroded, and the movement of greenhouse gases into and out of these systems. Without those measurements, the reefs’ present-day role in carbon cycling remains unresolved.

Scientists have spent decades examining how warming and disturbance affect ancient carbon in tundra, peatlands, and mangroves. Tropical peat reefs are at a much earlier stage of understanding. Their discovery raises related questions beneath the sea about how stable the stored carbon is and what happens when the deposits erode or are disturbed.

The authors call for research to establish whether these habitats primarily preserve carbon inherited from ancient forests and swamps, whether their living communities contribute to new carbon burial, and how those processes interact as environmental conditions change. The answers would help determine whether peat reefs can be classified as “blue carbon” habitats alongside mangroves and seagrass meadows.

Coastal planners and conservationists rarely map or protect peat reefs, despite their potential to provide backup refuges for some species as coral reefs decline. The authors recommend including them in coastal habitat maps, conservation plans, and assessments of legacy coastal carbon. They also see the study as a starting point for considering these habitats within broader blue economy policies governing the use and protection of ocean resources. Similar peat reefs may lie undiscovered along the coasts of Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, and West Africa.

#5623. Supernova Explosions First Aided, Then Later Impeded, the Epoch of Reionization

The Universe has gone through many defining changes over its 13+ billion year history. One of its phases was the Dark Ages, which started after Recombination and lasted until somewhere between about 150,000 and one billion years after the Big Bang. It ended with the Epoch of Reionization (EoR), when neutral hydrogen atoms in the early cosmos were reionized. At that time, light was able to travel freely through the Universe, and the Dark Ages came to an end.

Standard thinking says that the Dark Ages ended because stars, galaxies, and quasars/active galactic nuclei formed. They were responsible for reionizing the hydrogen with their UV radiation. How it all worked in the EoR is a subject of deep study in cosmology. Cosmologists know it happened, but the relative roles that different ionizing sources played is not clear. For more than two millennia, Archimedean screws have been used for water pumping and, more recently, as turbines in hydroelectric power stations[1]. Astronomers have discovered the lightest known pair of neutron stars — two ultradense stellar remnants that circle each other every 2 hours, 22 minutes, veering toward an inevitable collision about 82 million years from now. By timing radio pulses from one of the stars, the researchers detected three subtle effects predicted by Albert Einstein's general theory of relativity and found that the theory describes the pair's motion to within about 1% — proving the visionary physicist right yet again.

The researchers published their findings Sept. 15 in the journal Physical Review Letters.

Neutron stars are the collapsed cores of massive stars that exploded as supernovas. Each packs more mass than the sun into a ball roughly 12 miles (20 kilometers) in diameter. Some, called pulsars, spin rapidly and sweep beams of radio waves across the sky like cosmic lighthouses. Their rotation is so steady that their pulses work as extraordinarily precise clocks.

Very few pulsars orbit another neutron star. "There are only 30 such double neutron star systems, and the relativistic effects are very strong if they are in compact orbits," study co-author JinLin Han, an astronomer at the National Astronomical Observatories of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, told Live Science via email.

The first such pair, discovered in 1974 and known as the Hulse-Taylor binary, provided the first evidence for gravitational waves — ripples in space-time predicted by Einstein. Such waves carry energy away from an orbiting pair, so its orbit should gradually shrink, and the Hulse-Taylor orbit shrank at just the rate Einstein's theory predicted.

Later, the "double pulsar," which completes an orbit every 2.45 hours, matched the predicted rate to within about 0.01%. Eventually, this shrinking forces such pairs to merge, unleashing gravitational waves like those the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory and Virgo interferometer picked up in 2017.

The new system, designated PSR J1856-0039, lies roughly 4,200 to 6,500 light-years from Earth. In 2020, it turned up in a survey of the Milky Way's disk by China's Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical Radio Telescope (FAST), the world's largest single-dish radio telescope.

"We have discovered about 900 pulsars," Han said of his team's work. "After a few follow-up observations, this one is very extraordinary."

The pulsar spins about 43 times per second, and the two stars are separated by roughly twice the distance between Earth and the moon. But the "most attractive feature" is the short orbital period of about 2.36 hours, Han said. "This is the second-shortest one, and it must show stronger relativistic effects than the famous double pulsar and the Hulse-Taylor system."

The team observed the pair with FAST 17 times between 2020 and 2025, sometimes long enough to cover a whole orbit. The researchers measured the arrival times of the system's pulses and then fit that data to orbital models that accounted for relativistic effects, like subtle changes to the pulse arrival times at different parts of the stars' orbits.

"With so many measurements by FAST, which would be hard to match in accuracy with other telescopes, we got not only the basic parameters of the pulsar and its orbit but also three relativistic effects," Han said.

First, the orbit is shrinking as the stars radiate gravitational waves, drawing them about 9 feet (2.7 meters) closer each year. Second, the oval orbit turns slowly, by 17.6 degrees per year — some 150,000 times faster than the relativistic drift of Mercury's orbit. Third, because both gravity and speed slow time, the pulsar's clock runs at a varying pace along the oval orbit, shifting its pulses by up to about half a millisecond.

An illustration of two neutron stars generating gravitational waves (purple) as they spiral toward each other in a tightening binary orbit. The rate of this orbital shrinkage offers a natural test of Einstein's theory or relativity.

"From these measurements, we determined the pulsar mass and the companion mass," Han explained. "The total mass is the lowest among all known double neutron star systems." The pulsar weighs about 1.3 times as much as the sun, and its companion is just 1.19 solar masses — one of the lowest-mass neutron stars known. Simulations suggest that supernovas can't forge neutron stars much lighter than that.

The stars will merge in about 82 million years. That merger will probably leave behind a heavier neutron star of about 2.26 solar masses, though the remnant could instead collapse into a black hole, the researchers estimate.

Any two of the three effects are enough to calculate both stars' masses. The third then serves as an independent check, because if general relativity is correct, all three must point to the same masses — and they do.

"We found that general relativity is needed to explain the data, and the consistency is about 1%," Han said. The double pulsar test is more precise, but it drew on 16 years of data, compared with about five for the new pair. "Unfortunately, we could not beat this description of gravity," Han added, referring to physicists' hope of finding cracks in Einstein's theory that might reveal new physics.

Next, the team hopes to detect "frame dragging," an effect in which a spinning mass twists space-time around it, making nearby orbits wobble. "In the future, we will monitor this pulsar, probably over decades, to detect the frame dragging induced by the neutron star's spin," Han said.

Measuring the effect would reveal the pulsar's moment of inertia, which depends on how its mass is spread out inside. That would provide "a novel constraint" on how matter behaves at the crushing densities in neutron star cores, Han said — one of the biggest open questions in physics.

Left unchecked though, a few bad actors can trigger social breakdown, researchers report October 21 in Proceedings of the Royal Society B. The truly deviant among us “basically take off the guardrails of the group,” says Lucas Molleman, a psychologist at the University of Amsterdam.

Molleman and his colleagues designed a thought experiment to show what it takes to sway people to bend or break the rules. They picked a contentious time: A week before the 2024 U.S. presidential election. The team recruited 750 U.S. participants, with roughly half identifying as Democrats and half as Republicans. The online participants watched an animated shooting star streak across a row of 100 squares and then selected which square shone the brightest.

Though the brightest square always appeared around the row’s middle, participants received more money for selecting squares farther to the right (the farthest-right square netted participants roughly the equivalent of $1.30). Dedicated rule followers, or those who always followed the rules, and rule breakers, existed in roughly equal numbers, with each comprising about a fifth of the larger group, initial tests showed.

The researchers then stirred the pot. Participants repeated the simple experiment several times while receiving information about a previous respondent, including their political affiliation and level of adherence to the rule. Rule followers rarely begot more rule followers, even when respondents belonged to the same political party, the team found.

The reverse held true for rule breakers, though. Receiving information that someone with the same political leanings had just disregarded the rules shifted selections rightward on average. Absent social cues, participants selected the farthest-right square roughly 23 percent of the time. That increased to almost 40 percent of the time after participants learned that a political comrade had made the most lucrative choice. Bad behavior, in other words, was contagious.

Most individuals’ rule-breaking levels mirrored or stayed below that of others. But just over 4 percent of individuals escalated deviant behavior beyond that of the previous respondent.

The researchers then created a computer simulation to see how those rule-breaking behaviors might play out across time. They designed a digital twin for each of the 750 participants and had the doppelgängers interact in groups of 100. In some scenarios, those few escalation-prone individuals could push everyone in the group toward wild rule breaking. But if the 100-twin selection had the same proportion of dedicated rule followers as the human experiment, overall deviance settled at a more intermediate level.

This study provides some keen insights into how humans might behave absent consequences, says behavioral economist Eugen Dimant of the University of Pennsylvania. “But that’s not how the world works.” His work suggests that to truly understand social contagion and the emergence of mobs, researchers must consider societal forces, such as punishments and social norms, that tend to keep behavior in check.

To Molleman, though, the study’s insights into individual behaviors are key. This pared-down experiment shows how just a few bad actors can cause entire groups to spiral out of control. Such findings could underpin everything from collective speeding to peaceful protests that turn violent, he says. “This is fundamental science … Obviously reality is way more complex.”

My recent discovery of a Roman fort and its extraordinary connection with medieval folklore was the fruition of many hours of painstaking research.

For several reasons, his reliability has been questioned, but mostly because of the perception that the Romans did not reach west Wales, which was rife in the Regency period and persists to this day.

However, the archaeologist Sir Mortimer Wheeler dug a similar site on the same latitude in 1922, and I reasoned that both sites could lay close to a Roman road. This began a quest to trace a network of routes through west Wales.

A stretch of Roman road was in fact identified by Fenton in the vale of Whitland. This was excavated in 1995, in advance of the construction of a bypass, but I felt that there was more of a network to be discovered. I made a breakthrough in 2022. Several other Romano-British sites have been discovered since Wheeler’s day, including a fort at Wiston near Haverfordwest in 2012–2013, and several villas.

The site lies adjacent to the A478, which runs straight in that area, and many sections further south are similar, but the line is lost in places, which is often the case with Roman roads.

It is my belief that it is part of a previously unidentified major arterial Roman road connecting modern Pembrokeshire with Ceredigion to the north.

My hypothesis gained traction with the results of a limited geophysical survey in October 2025, which placed two rectilinear (straight-lined and right-angled) buildings in the right place. Trial excavations followed in March and July 2025, and a steady trickle of finds followed, including coins, a gaming counter, some Roman pottery, and a spindle whorl for spinning wool. A more comprehensive evaluation excavation was needed, however, to establish for certain whether the site was a Roman auxiliary fort.

He’s mentioned in the Mabinogion (a collection of medieval Welsh stories) in The Tale of Emperor Maxen, also known as The Dream of Macsen Wledig.

This places the late 4th-century usurping emperor on the Frenni Fawr, the easternmost peak of the Preseli Hills, which dominates the site a little over three kilometers to the northeast. In the first and second trenches, we identified the bases of floor structures built of clay and packed with the remnants of thin stone blocks. Many of the latter had been disturbed by intermittent plowing over many decades.

In the second trench there was a diagonal alignment of stones, which indicated a later phase of civilian occupation. We recovered many pottery sherds, particularly in the second trench, and found a kiln, which yielded many fragments of Roman ceramic tile.

The third trench had a clay floor consistent with Roman granaries. In the fourth trench we unearthed the remnants of the rampart base and a road surface below plow level with rut marks on their expected orientation. The fifth trench did indeed reveal a track. Its date has yet to be established, but we did recover a small number of pottery sherds that are seemingly Roman.

It does indeed suggest a connection between the camp of Magnus Maximus mentioned in the Mabinogion and the site I first identified in 2024. Magnus Maximus will feature in my forthcoming book, Lives of the Roman Emperors: From Augustus to Romulus Augustulus.

The earthworks are still visible today, almost certainly adjacent to a major Roman road, and would have been more conspicuous in the 4th century and listed on official itineraries. The discovery represents an extraordinary fusion between myth and reality and demonstrates that folklore is often underpinned by fact.

The team used those movements to estimate which landscapes jaguars would most likely cross. The goal was to find the routes between key populations, from Mexico to Argentina.

“Connectivity is fundamental to the long-term survival of jaguars,” said Karandikar.

“Using GPS movement data from jaguars across their range allowed us to identify areas that are most likely to support movement between populations,” he added.

Some of the corridors identified by the research align with previous conservation priorities, while others are new and could be useful opportunities to maintain connectivity.

The researchers also compared their results with corridors proposed in earlier work. Their findings suggest that some of those older routes may not exist anymore. In those places, a corridor drawn on paper may no longer match the land as it is today.

Robin Naidoo, a co-author and lead scientist at WWF-US, said fieldwork has to come next.

“Connectivity maps show us where jaguars can move and what barriers may stand in their way, but the real test is what happens on the ground,” Naidoo said.

“By combining range-wide mapping with field research, we can see which corridors are being used, where connections are breaking down, and where restoring habitat could help put those connections back together.”

In South America, one of the biggest results was a jaguar corridor that starts in northern Bolivia. It runs through the Pantanal and the Paraguay River region, then into Paraguay and northern Argentina.

The Pantanal is the world’s largest tropical wetland. According to the World Wildlife Fund, it holds the second-largest concentration of jaguars anywhere.

Allison Devlin, a co-author who directs the jaguar program at Panthera, a wild cat conservation group, called the study “a future-forward blueprint for the species’ conservation efforts.”

Some places on the new map still need ground-truthing, which means checking a predicted route against what is actually there.

“Encouragingly, this research highlights the importance of connections like the Bi-National Corridor in Honduras and Guatemala, the Amazon-Pantanal Corridor in Brazil where Panthera works, and key areas to ground-truth – including Colombia’s A2O Corridor – that warrant greater conservation efforts,” Devlin said.

Many of the corridors the team identified lie outside national parks and other protected areas. In the authors’ view, keeping those corridors open will take more than parks. Careful planning of how land gets used matters, and so does restoring damaged habitat.

The researchers see the map as guidance for governments and conservation groups. They also see it as a tool for Indigenous Peoples and local communities, along with the people who manage land.

Luke Hunter is executive director of the WCS Big Cats Program. The program coordinates the group’s big cat conservation work in more than 25 countries.

“Jaguars are telling us something important about the condition of the landscapes they inhabit,” Hunter said.

“When connectivity is lost, populations become isolated and more vulnerable to decline. Protecting corridors is not just about conserving jaguars – it is about maintaining the ecological processes that sustain entire ecosystems across the Americas.”

The new corridors are predictions, not confirmed routes. In the researchers’ view, each one still needs to be checked in the field.

They listed camera traps and genetic tests among the methods. Surveys for traces of genetic material that animals leave behind in the environment are on the list too, along with the knowledge of people who live along these routes.

Until that checking is done, it isn’t clear which of the possible new links jaguars use today. It also isn’t clear how many of the older ones have been lost.

The authors expect the map to guide international efforts to secure and reconnect jaguar populations across the species’ range.

Private companies are getting better and better at launching more and more mass into space with larger and larger rockets. But, there is still a major constraint on the size of objects we can get into orbit - fairing size. But a new paper from researchers at Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics and the Beijing Institute of Technology offers a solution - a self-shaping, deployable module that can fine-tune both its positioning and its own dimensions.

To understand how that offers an advantage, itâs best to look at how engineers currently solve this problem. The problem is, these systems almost never deploy perfectly in real environments, and when talking about snapping together multiple modules, even a few millimeters of error can cause the entire mission to fail.

Fixing this requires taking steps after that initial unfolding to correct whatever errors might have been introduced. The researchers built the system as a set of three hexagonal corner pods connected by carbon-fiber rods. Maneuvering the modular assembly into place is where the thrusters come in. Each corner pod is equipped with a cold gas thruster, all connected to a central control system that uses them to nudge the module forward, backward, or spin it to match the rotational speed of its intended target. #5607. Cannabis May Be Sabotaging Your Sleep Without You Even Knowing It, Study Finds

Nearly 35 years of observational data link greater lifetime cannabis use to poorer sleep in middle age, challenging its reputation as a sleep aid.

More than eight hours of sleep might sound like a good night’s rest, but a new Northwestern Medicine study linked recent cannabis use to both a greater likelihood of sleeping that long and more disrupted nights. The fragmented sleep and longer stretches awake recorded by wrist-worn monitors were not reflected in participants’ own sleep ratings, suggesting that people may not notice these disturbances.

The study, published October 9 in JAMA Network Open, analyzed data from 1,266 participants in the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) Study. This community-based study began in 1985–86 and follows Black and white adults across the U.S.

Researchers repeatedly tracked participants’ cannabis use over nearly 35 years, from young adulthood into middle age. In middle age, participants wore a wrist-based sleep monitor for a week and completed sleep surveys, allowing the team to compare recorded sleep patterns with people’s impressions of their own sleep.

Millions of Americans use cannabis as a sleep aid. “This study challenges the common belief that cannabis helps people sleep better,” said corresponding author Dr. Thanh-Huyen T. Vu, research associate professor of preventive medicine (epidemiology) at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine. “Our findings suggest cannabis may actually be associated with poorer sleep quality, even if people don’t perceive those changes.”

Greater cumulative cannabis use was associated with lower sleep efficiency, meaning participants spent a smaller percentage of the night asleep. Their sleep was also more fragmented, and they spent more time awake after initially falling asleep. Compared with people who had never used cannabis, recent users showed similar patterns and were significantly more likely to sleep longer than eight hours a night.

“This was one of the most interesting findings,” Vu said of the comparison between the monitor readings and participants’ sleep surveys. “The wearable device detected more nighttime disruption among participants with long-term cannabis exposure, but the participants themselves generally did not report worse sleep.”

The associations with poorer objectively measured sleep remained after researchers accounted for numerous factors known to affect sleep, including age, sex, race, cigarette smoking, alcohol use, physical activity, depression, chronic health conditions, and use of other substances. The study was not designed to determine whether cannabis directly causes sleep problems, so the findings establish an association rather than a causal effect.

“Our data do not support the belief that cannabis is helpful as a sleep aid,” said senior author Mercedes R. Carnethon, chair of the department of preventive medicine at Feinberg. “As cannabis becomes increasingly available and accepted, it’s important to better understand its long-term associations with health behaviors such as sleep.”

The analysis of long-term recreational cannabis use did not distinguish between consumption methods such as smoking and eating edibles. Patterns of cannabis use likely changed over the decades participants were followed, making consumption methods an especially important question for future studies, the authors said.

For people seeking better sleep, well-established strategies can improve sleep quality, said study co-author Kristen L. Knutson, associate professor of neurology (sleep medicine) and preventive medicine (epidemiology) at Feinberg.

“Keep a regular bedtime, make your bedroom cool, dark and quiet, and avoid looking at phones and other screens right before bed,” Knutson said. “It can also help to establish a calming routine before sleep, such as reading, meditating or taking a shower. If sleep problems persist, or if you’re a loud snorer, talk with your health care provider to rule out an underlying sleep disorder.”

The Nazis were far from completing a working reactor for their atomic bomb program, despite claims by the project's leader that it was almost ready, a new study finds.

The German physicist Werner Heisenberg wrote in 1947 that the reactor for the "Nazi bomb" project ‪— which culminated in Germany's B8 nuclear reactor ‪— was almost finished at the end of World War II.

Heisenberg, who led the reactor's development, claimed the reactor was "just insufficient" to create a self-sustaining fission reaction and that it lacked only a "relatively small amount of uranium" to work.

If it had worked, the B8 reactor would have confirmed that a fission chain reaction was possible, marking a key step toward the German development of an atomic bomb. It could have also spurred the development of specialized fission reactors used to create weapons-grade plutonium from uranium. Instead, the atomic bomb was developed first by the United States and was used to force the surrender of Japan in August 1945. (Germany had already surrendered in May 1945.)

The new study, published Sept. 29 in the journal PNAS Nexus, used historical documents and forensic investigations of two of the project's surviving uranium cubes to refute Heisenberg's claim. The new research indicates that the German B8 reactor had less than half the amount of "heavy water" and about half the amount of uranium it would have needed for a continuous chain reaction.

"My results argue that Heisenberg was off by roughly a factor of two," said first author Patrick Park, a doctoral student at Princeton University who co-wrote the study while at Columbia University.

Park said Heisenberg's mistake was understandable: His calculations were only approximate, and the project was relocated to a rock cellar in the town of Haigerloch, Germany, after Berlin was bombed.

"People were dying or starving and so these reactor calculations were not his first priority," while many details of neutron physics were unknown at that time, Park told Live Science in an email. "Given these circumstances, I think he did the best he could."

To answer the question of how far the reactor for the Nazi bomb project got, the scientists began by tracing archival records of hundreds of uranium cubes that had been used by German scientists. The B8 reactor consisted of five concentric circles of uranium cubes suspended on chains within the heavy water tank. The German scientists planned to place a radium-beryllium neutron source into the reactor's core to kick-start the chain reaction with a flood of neutrons that would split the uranium nuclei and release more neutrons, furthering the chain reaction.

Heavy water is a form of water that contains the neutron-heavy hydrogen isotope deuterium. Like the mainly-graphite blocks and rods used in many reactors, it can be used to "moderate" or slow down fast neutrons created in a fission reaction so they trigger fission — and more neutrons — in nearby uranium atoms. The Germans also used small amounts of graphite in their reactor, but as a coating to reflect neutrons back into the chamber.

A cube of natural uranium from the German atomic bomb project.

By combining the records of the cubes, heavy water and graphite used by the German scientists with measurements of the two surviving cubes, the study authors created a mathematical model of the reactor that agreed closely with historical measurements. This enabled them to establish that the heavy water reactor was far from achieving a stable reaction. The findings also helped them examine the suggestion that the Germans should have instead built a graphite-based reactor like the nuclear pile developed at the University of Chicago to build the American bomb.

But the study authors determined that a graphite-moderated reactor was unfeasible in wartime Germany because the local supply of coal available to create graphite contained high levels of neutron-absorbing boron, making it useless.

Instead, the heavy water used in the B8 reactor was produced at a single factory in Norway, which was occupied by Germany from April 1940 until May 1945. However, the reactor contained only about 370 gallons (1,400 liters) of heavy water, instead of the more than 780 gallons (roughly 3,000 liters) it would have needed, the researchers determined.

Heavy water makes up a tiny fraction of a percent of ordinary water, and extracting it by electrolysis in the Norwegian factory took extraordinary effort. The factory was producing as much heavy water as it could, but the B8 reactor would have needed more than twice the amount it produced during the entire war, the researchers wrote.

Another finding is that the B8 reactor had about half the amount of uranium it would have needed to sustain a fission chain reaction. The reactor contained a total of 664 cubes, each weighing about 5.11 pounds (2.32 kilograms), for a total of 3,390 pounds (1,540 kilograms) of uranium. Similar cubes were distributed among other experimental groups, giving a total inventory in wartime Germany of about 1,100 cubes of uranium.

But the researchers determined that the B8 reactor would have needed at least 1,200 cubes to work. Germany, therefore, was not about to develop a reactor for an atomic bomb in 1945. Contrary to Heisenberg's claims, the heavy-water reactor was not nearly ready, and a working graphite reactor was impossible.

"We conclude Germany could not have built heavy water nor graphite reactors during World War II," the researchers wrote.

Mark Walker, a historian at Union College in Schenectady, New York, who wasn't involved in the study, said the findings explain Germany's decision to develop a heavy-water reactor, since a graphite reactor was not feasible and they didn't know at the time how much heavy water would be needed.

Walker is the author of "Hitler's Atomic Bomb: History, Legend, and the Twin Legacies of Auschwitz and Hiroshima" (Cambridge University Press, 2024) and a noted expert on Germany's wartime nuclear efforts. He agreed that Germany could not have made a graphite reactor during World War II.

"This paper argues that if the Germans had done something they did not do, then this would have failed," he told Live Science in an email. "While this is counterfactual history, it is an interesting engineering analysis."
