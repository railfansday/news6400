#5099. New Jersey may finally reverse its draconian e-bike law after massive backlash

New Jersey lawmakers are already looking to walk back one of the country’s most controversial e-bike laws, just months after the sweeping new regulations took effect and sparked widespread backlash from riders, bicycle shops, and advocacy groups.

A newly introduced bill, S4830, sponsored by State Senator Raj Mukherji, would remove licensing, registration, and insurance requirements for low-speed electric bicycles with motors rated at 750W or less and a maximum motor-powered speed of 20 mph (32 km/h). This includes Class 1 and Class 2 electric bicycles.

The proposal would essentially restore bicycle-like treatment for these two classes of e-bikes, importantly including throttle-equipped models that make up the majority of e-bikes sold in the state and more broadly in the US, while maintaining stricter requirements for faster and more powerful electric bicycles.

The move comes after New Jersey’s previous governor, Phil Murphy, signed a sweeping e-bike law in January following several tragic crashes involving young riders.

That legislation, which took full effect in July, eliminated the state’s previous three-class e-bike framework and introduced licensing and registration requirements for all electric bicycles, including ordinary low-speed commuter models. Throttle-equipped and faster e-bikes also faced insurance requirements.

As we previously reported, New Jersey bicycle shops experienced devastating sales declines after the law took effect, with some reporting drops of 20% to 90%. Several businesses were forced to lay off employees, downsize, or even close entirely as e-bike sales and usage plummeted.

Meanwhile, riders faced confusing new requirements that made e-bikes considerably less accessible, which was particularly impactful for lower-income residents, people with disabilities, and those who rely on electric bicycles for daily transportation.

The original law was intended to improve safety, but critics argued that it largely punished responsible riders instead of addressing the growing problem of high-powered electric motorcycles masquerading as bicycles. Only responsible riders actually registered their bikes as required by law, while others were deterred from cycling, and the hooligan riders who often gave e-bikes a bad name continued to ride illegally.

After all, requiring someone riding a 20 mph (32 km/h) commuter e-bike to obtain a driver’s license and register their bicycle does little to address reckless riding on machines capable of highway speeds.

The newly proposed legislation appears to acknowledge that distinction, offering a more targeted approach that could preserve reasonable safety regulations without making ordinary e-bike ownership unnecessarily complicated.

S4830 still needs to work its way through the legislature before becoming law, but for New Jersey’s struggling e-bike industry, it’s a promising sign that lawmakers may finally be listening. It is still widely argued that requiring electric bicycles from 21-28 mph (32-45 km/h) to require a driver’s license and registration is overly burdensome, but at least the state appears to be moving back toward the right direction.

An Interior Department auction for roughly 187,000 acres generated less per acre than a similar sale in the same state in 2025.

The U.S. Bureau of Land Management sold geothermal leases on 60 parcels of federal land in Nevada on Tuesday, netting nearly $4.83 million for 186,641 acres. The sale included two parcels that overlap the site for NextEra Energy’s proposed 1-GW Sawtooth Energy Center solar project.

The sale netted less than previous geothermal lease auctions, including the 2025 Nevada lease sale, which brought in total receipts around $9.4 million, and June’s geothermal lease auction in New Mexico, which brought in $16.5 million — the second-highest-grossing sale in BLM history.

In 2025, BLM sold roughly 280,000 lease acres in Nevada, while the 2026 auction sold nearly 187,000 acres.

Parcels were located in Lyon, Churchill, Mineral, Humboldt, Esmerelda, Lander, Nye, Eureka, Lincoln and White Pine counties in Nevada, BLM said. Ormat Technologies was the biggest bidder in Tuesday’s auction, spending around $2.3 million to win eight parcels covering about 20,263 acres.

BLM originally marked 83 parcels for sale in this auction. Zero parcels were removed from the bidding process due to protests; 10 were removed “for other reasons,” according to BLM. The remaining 13 parcels drew no bids.

The two parcels that overlap NextEra’s Sawtooth project were sold to United Minerals and Mining for about $15,400 each. NextEra has a pending right-of-way grant application for Sawtooth’s proposed location in Nye County, Nevada.

NextEra had filed a comment on the proposed sale, asking that BLM “remove, defer, or reconfigure” three overlapping parcels during final parcel review “to avoid creating a potentially incompatible leasehold interest before BLM completes its review” of the Sawtooth application.

BLM rejected that request in its response to the comment, saying it “manages the land for multiple use,” but one of the three overlapping parcels didn’t end up listed in Tuesday’s auction.

BLM told NextEra in its response to its comments that “subsequent authorizations for other uses, including right of ways, must not unreasonably interfere with geothermal operations undertaken by the lease holder.”

“Because the parcel in question would be leased prior to authorization of NextEra’s [right of way], NextEra must collaborate with any potential lessee and adjust its operations accordingly to avoid land use conflicts,” BLM said.

NextEra did not respond to a request for comment.

Generate Capital and TurningPoint Energy celebrated the ribbon-cutting of a new 3.5-MWAC community solar project in Sandwich, Illinois, marking another community solar project in Kendall County developed through Illinois’ Adjustable Block Program, now known as Illinois Shines. The project is part of an eight-project, 34.5-MWac portfolio developed by TPE and financed, built, owned and operated by Generate.

The single-axis tracking system spans approximately 23 acres, and the bifacial solar panels are expected to generate more than 8 million kWh of clean electricity annually.

The project incorporates agrivoltaic and pollinator-friendly land management practices, using sheep grazing to naturally maintain vegetation and native flowering plants to support pollinator populations and enhance biodiversity. These practices help improve soil health and demonstrate how renewable energy can work hand-in-hand with agriculture and responsible land stewardship.

#5092. Tesla pushes Optimus deliveries to late 2027, says hands still a problem

Elon Musk now says Tesla will start delivering Optimus humanoid robots to outside customers around the end of 2027. On Tesla’s Q3 2026 earnings call, he said the hands, the robot’s durability and its AI all still need work before then.

Those are the same three problems every humanoid robot company is fighting. And despite Musk’s claim that nobody is close to Tesla, XPeng and Hyundai’s Boston Dynamics look at least as far along.

Musk said Tesla has started early production of Optimus on its high-volume line in Fremont, where Model S and Model X used to be built. Most of a line designed for a million robots a year is installed, but he said it will take at least a year, possibly longer, to reach that rate of about 20,000 units a week, because parts of the design are still changing.

He listed what has to be solved first: the AI, the electromechanical design, and reliability, including making sure the robot doesn’t wear out after a month of use. He expects those issues to be solved around the end of next year. That’s when Tesla expects to deliver Optimus to companies outside Tesla, starting with industrial jobs that are dangerous, repetitive or need to run around the clock.

When an analyst asked where the design was still changing, a Tesla Optimus engineer pointed to the hand as one of the hardest hardware areas, along with custom gears that still carry reliability concerns. Musk agreed the hand is the main issue, explaining that fingers need to be soft enough to handle delicate objects while surviving constant wear, and robots can’t heal like human skin can.

On the AI side, Musk said Tesla is still working through problems with the AI architecture. Tesla doesn’t have a fleet of robots collecting data the way its cars do for FSD, so it plans to build 10,000 to 20,000 robots and have them practice tasks in what it calls an Optimus Academy. Tesla also plans to put its AI5 chip in Optimus around April 2027.

Musk also promised an Optimus video within a month or two showing the robot doing tasks that aren’t pre-programmed. He argued that many humanoid demos look impressive but only run scripted routines that wouldn’t hold up in the real world.

None of this is new. Last month, we reported that Tesla had ramped Optimus to hundreds of units a week, but that the robots couldn’t generalize to new tasks. According to The Information, the hand and forearm had over 100 small parts assembled by hand, touch sensors had reliability issues, and the AI could take days to learn basic tasks.

Tesla’s own Q3 shareholder letter now calls the bots coming off the Fremont line “the first production-quality bots that we have manufactured.” So every Optimus before this quarter wasn’t production quality.

And the timeline keeps moving. In 2024, Musk said Tesla would build Optimus for internal use in 2025 and hopefully in high volume for other companies in 2026. In January 2025, he said Tesla would build about 10,000 robots that year, with “several thousand” doing useful work by year-end. He later admitted none were. Now external deliveries are a late-2027 target.

Musk said on the call that, to the best of his knowledge, “there is no one that is even close to Tesla” on a general-purpose humanoid robot. That’s hard to square with what competitors have shown.

XPeng turned on an automated IRON production line in Guangzhou in September, with more than 80% of core processes automated. IRON has 21 degrees of freedom in each hand and runs XPeng’s physical AI model on three in-house Turing chips. XPeng plans mass production by the end of 2026, deliveries in the first half of 2027 starting with stores and malls, and a European rollout in the second half of 2027. That’s a similar timeline to Tesla’s, if not slightly ahead.

XPeng is dealing with the same reality. It hasn’t shipped IRON to a paying customer, and its timelines have slipped like everyone else’s.

Boston Dynamics started producing its electric Atlas in January and says all of its 2026 Atlas deployments are already committed, including to Google DeepMind, whose AI models Boston Dynamics is integrating. In September, it opened a robot training center at Hyundai’s Metaplant in Georgia, where Atlas is learning to sequence car parts for the assembly line. Hyundai plans to deploy 25,000 robots across its plants over the next few years. Boston Dynamics also hired Milan Kovac, Tesla’s former head of Optimus, in January.

Boston Dynamics also seems to have taken a more pragmatic approach to hands, prioritizing sturdy grippers for industrial parts over human-like dexterity. That’s less impressive in a demo, but it’s a way around the problem Tesla says is holding it back.

My prediction is that Optimus will forever be delayed. It's like FSD in Tesla cars -- never quite good enough to really take off and always one or two releases away from fixing everything. At least a Tesla car with a broken FSD is still a good car. Optimus with a bad brain is an interesting-looking boat anchor.

Tesla is facing the exact same problems as everyone else: hands that survive real work, robots reliable enough to pay for themselves, and AI that can handle tasks it wasn’t trained for. Nobody has cracked generalization yet, Tesla included. The difference is that Tesla’s competitors aren’t telling investors they have no competition.

Based on what’s public, XPeng has a more automated production line and a similar delivery date, and Boston Dynamics is already deploying Atlas in a car factory with committed customers. Tesla may well have the most robots built, but building robots isn’t the hard part. Making them useful is.

Tesla has promised a video in a month or two. If it shows Optimus doing real, unscripted work, great. But we’ve heard about Optimus timelines before, and they’ve slipped every year. Why should late 2027 be different?

#5090. The secret to easier exercise as an adult may start in childhood

How much energy your body needs to move as an adult could be influenced by how active you were during childhood. A new study led by researchers at Penn State suggests that physical activity early in life may have lasting effects on movement efficiency. Using birds as a model, the scientists provided the first direct evidence that experiences during development can change how much energy animals expend when moving as adults.

According to Jonas Rubenson, a professor of kinesiology at Penn State, the ability to move efficiently could play an important role in animal evolution. Animals that spend less energy getting around may have more resources available for essential activities such as finding food, hunting, and reproducing. The findings, published in Proceedings of the Royal Society B, Biological Sciences, suggest that the energy demands of movement are more adaptable than scientists previously believed.

To investigate, the researchers attached small weights to one leg of young guinea fowl, birds closely related to chickens that walk on two legs like humans. They wanted to determine whether carrying extra weight during development would change how efficiently the birds moved once they matured.

The results revealed a striking difference. Birds that grew up carrying the weights could walk with the added load without expending additional energy. By comparison, birds raised without weights required 23% more energy when the same load was attached to their legs.

"This study showed how limb loading -- the application of mechanical stress on arms or legs, typically through weights or exercise -- during development can help an animal save energy during movement throughout its lifespan," said Rubenson, senior author of the study. "This is one of the first studies to indicate that animals' behaviors and environments in youth can affect how effortful moving is as adults."

Why Childhood Exercise Could Matter for Lifelong Health

Although the experiment involved birds, the findings raise important questions about human physical activity. One widely accepted scientific hypothesis proposes that people tend to avoid movement when it feels physically demanding or requires substantial energy.

If early activity influences movement efficiency in humans as it does in these birds, people who were less active during childhood might need to expend more energy to perform the same movements as adults. That additional effort could make them less inclined to exercise, potentially reinforcing a pattern of inactivity.

Rubenson believes the discovery could have broad implications for public health, particularly as children today are generally less physically active than those in earlier generations.

"Overall, children in the U.S. today are much less active than previous generations," Rubenson said. "This study suggests that more exercise through play and exercise during childhood could make it easier for people to remain or become more active as adults. If people do not need to exert as much energy to move as adults, then exercise -- and all its health benefits -- will be more accessible to them. This could have implications for many aspects of our nation's well-being, from heart health to mental health."

How Researchers Tested the Effects of Early Exercise

The experiment followed 12 guinea fowl from two weeks of age until they reached 16 weeks, when the birds were approaching their full adult size.

Half of the birds wore lead bands on one leg, with each band weighing 4% of the bird's body weight. The remaining six birds developed without any added weight. Apart from this difference, all 12 animals experienced the same living conditions and exercise routines.

When the birds reached nine weeks of age, the researchers began training them to walk on a treadmill.

At 16 weeks, the team assessed their energy expenditure using a flow-through metabolic chamber fitted to the treadmill. This equipment measured the oxygen each bird consumed, along with the carbon dioxide and water vapor it released. According to the researchers, this approach is considered the gold standard for determining how much energy an animal uses during a particular activity.

Each bird was tested under three conditions: standing still, walking on the treadmill without added weight, and walking while wearing a weight band on one leg. By comparing oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production across these conditions, the scientists could determine how the additional load affected energy use.

Early Training Produced a Surprising Energy-Saving Effect

The researchers found no differences in energy consumption when the birds were standing still. However, their responses changed considerably once they began walking with weights.

Birds that had not carried weights while growing up experienced a 23% increase in energy expenditure when walking with the added load compared with walking without it.

The birds that had worn weights throughout development showed a remarkably different response. Their bodies had adapted so effectively that walking with the extra load required slightly less energy than walking without it. This unexpected result surprised the research team.

"The birds became so efficient that they were not using any more energy to carry the weight than they needed to walk without the weight," Rubenson said.

Another unexpected finding emerged when the researchers compared the two groups. On average, birds accustomed to carrying weights throughout development used no more energy while walking with their bands than ordinary birds used when walking without any added weight.

Together, these observations suggest that experiences early in life can substantially influence how efficiently animals move as adults.

"This shows that animals -- and this probably includes humans -- can become more efficient based on their life experiences. So, when humans exercise regularly throughout our childhood and adolescence, it could build our capacity for exercise throughout our lives."

The findings provide new evidence that physical activity during development can have lasting effects on the energy required for movement. Whether the same adaptations occur in humans remains to be established, but the research suggests a potential mechanism through which childhood exercise could help support physical activity later in life.

Stephen Piazza, a professor of kinesiology at Penn State, also participated in the research, along with several current and former graduate students working with Piazza and Rubenson.

The study's two co-first authors were Talayah Johnson and Kavya Katugam-Dechene. Johnson completed her master's degree at Penn State and is now pursuing a doctorate at the University of Pennsylvania. Katugam-Dechene received her doctorate from Penn State and currently serves as a research assistant professor of biomedical and rehabilitation engineering at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

Other contributors included Ian Dechene, who worked as research staff on the project and is now a software engineer, and Roberto Castro, a Penn State doctoral student jointly advised by Piazza and Rubenson.

#5088. Gen Z candidates in both parties aim to tackle high housing costs

Left: Democrat Olaleye Onikuyide, 28, is running for state representative in Connecticut. Right: Republican Lukas Schubert, 21, is campaigning for reelection as a state representative in Montana. Both candidates are focusing on a lack of affordable housing in their states. Raquel C. Zaldívar and Sarah Mosquera for NPR hide caption

WEST HARTFORD, Conn. — "My bus is running slightly behind," Olaleye Onikuyide texted ahead of our meeting at a coffee shop on West Hartford's main street. The 28-year-old Democrat running for Connecticut state representative still made it on time and said he loved riding public transit.

Onikuyide also lives with his parents, though he said that's by choice to save money, unlike the many young adults who can't afford to live on their own.

Still, as part of a generation that faces record-high housing unaffordability, he started obsessing years ago over how to make it cheaper. Now that the cripplingly high cost of both rent and homes has become an urgent national conversation, he likens it to being a fan of a niche music group before everyone else caught on.

"All of a sudden, it's blown up and everybody's at the concerts and you're like, 'OK, this is a bit of a crowd here now,'" he said with a broad smile.

Onikuyide and his campaign manager, Karo Shakhnazarov, canvass in West Hartford, Connecticut. Raquel C. Zaldívar for NPR hide caption

Nationwide, housing costs are a top issue for voters in a midterms season laser focused on affordability. A chronic housing shortage has pushed up prices, and the fallout cuts across generations.

Younger people are renting with roommates into their 30s. They're struggling to save for a down payment and wondering whether it even makes sense to buy, given today's sky-high prices. Empty nesters looking to downsize can't find an affordable place to go, and longtime homeowners are reluctant to move at all, given current high mortgage rates.

Generation Z candidates from both major parties said their age group has a lot at stake and is uniquely positioned to help create solutions for everyone.

"We're in the thick of it," Onikuyide said, citing not only high housing and gas prices but also student loan debt and a tough labor market. "We might be the generation that is more educated and yet worse off than our parents."

Onikuyide leaves information about his campaign at a voter's home in West Hartford. He appreciates the national debate on high housing costs but says the action to address it is happening at the state level. Raquel C. Zaldívar for NPR hide caption

His policy goals include creating more mixed-income housing and walkable cities. Adding density to urban areas can be controversial, as longtime residents worry about more traffic or strained infrastructure. But Onikuyide and others also see an opportunity for bipartisanship. And if elected, he would feel well-placed to make life less expensive for his constituents.

"I keep one eye on the national conversation about it," he said, "but the action is happening at the state level."

About an hour away in New Haven, Conn., 26-year-old Eli Sabin found a quiet room for talking inside the ornate City Hall. He's also a Democrat running for state representative, and he has worked in this building.

When he was a sophomore in college, he started serving on the Board of Alders. That's "our old-fashioned term for the city council," he explained. "We changed some of the zoning rules to allow for denser development and more affordable units."

With over 40 years in energy markets, policy and security, including as administrator of the U.S. Energy Information Administration, I have learned a key lesson: When electricity prices rise, policymakers should rely on data, not slogans.

I have devoted my career to prioritizing credible data for energy and electricity policymaking. I’ve come to this conclusion after examining the objective data on these matters.

Jigar Shah is correct that data centers alone did not “jack up” every family’s electric bill. However, his broader claim in an August Newsweek column that “utility greed” is to blame misdiagnoses the issue and misguides policymakers.

Utilities cannot build projects without public oversight. They earn returns only on investments state regulators deem prudent, useful and in customers’ interest. Major capital projects undergo public rate case reviews, where consumer advocates, regulators and intervenors can challenge costs. This is regulated investment, not profiteering.

A recent Alliance for Innovation and Infrastructure report, Ratemaking for a Reliable Grid, makes clear what Shah’s piece blurs: Both requested and authorized utility returns on equity have trended downward over the past two decades, not upward. And authorized ROE is an opportunity to earn a regulated return that has been carefully calibrated for customers and is subject to oversight — it is not a guaranteed profit. Understanding these points is central.

The data simply does not support a national “utility greed” narrative. A Charles River Associates analysis earlier this year also found that recent residential rate increases are concentrated in specific regions, especially California, the Northeast and parts of the Mid-Atlantic, while most states have seen stable rates or even rates that are below the national average. In parts of the PJM Interconnection, where states like Pennsylvania and New Jersey rely on forward-looking capacity auctions and unregulated suppliers rather than utility-owned generation, customers face volatile capacity prices and ongoing supply shortages.

This is the larger issue Shah overlooks. PJM customers already face the costs of volatile wholesale markets, while new generation has struggled to come online. Allowing regulated utilities to build generation would maintain oversight, as state regulators would still determine whether projects are prudent and if cost recovery is justified.

This approach also provides a hedge for customers. If a utility-owned plant earns revenue in the PJM market, those earnings offset customer costs. This differs from relying solely on independent power producers, who reap profits from wholesale market outcomes and scarcity pricing, unconstrained by regulated returns or an obligation to serve customers.

Shah encourages readers to question utility incentives, which is reasonable. However, he does not address whether today’s market gives the unregulated power producers those utilities buy their power from an incentive to benefit from prolonged supply shortages and higher wholesale prices. The data shows that publicly traded energy corporations — exempt from the same regulation as utilities — have made record profits over the last few years without building the new power needed to keep up with demand. Companies that can, have waited until these supply shortages became apparent before announcing “press releases” about build new generation.

Furthermore, utilities already use demand response, energy efficiency, advanced grid management, time-of-use rates and other tools to maximize existing infrastructure. Ultimately, meeting unprecedented demand requires building new infrastructure, including generation, transmission, substations and distribution upgrades.

Families need lower bills, but they also need reliable power. While blaming utilities may be politically convenient, ensuring adequate electricity supply is the real solution to the affordability crisis.

#5076. Rare study reveals a striking divide inside an Alzheimer’s patient’s brain

An unusually detailed examination of an Alzheimer's patient's brain has revealed striking differences between regions where treatment successfully removed harmful amyloid plaques and areas where those plaques remained. The regions cleared of amyloid showed little or no buildup of tau tangles, another protein abnormality strongly associated with brain cell damage and declining memory. Nearby areas that still contained amyloid, however, showed much higher levels of tau and evidence of continued neurodegeneration.

The discovery offers rare evidence from a human brain that removing amyloid plaques may provide protection against other damaging processes involved in Alzheimer's disease (AD). The results also suggest that clearing amyloid thoroughly, particularly early in the disease, could help limit tau accumulation and slow the progression of brain damage.

Researchers from the Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania presented the findings at the 2026 Alzheimer's Association International Conference. The study was also published in JAMA.

"Seeing both disease patterns side-by-side in the same brain gave us a rare opportunity to understand how amyloid removal affects other proteins that contribute to Alzheimer's disease," said co-senior author David Wolk, MD, co-director of the Penn Memory Center and director of the Penn Alzheimer's Disease Research Center. "The findings provide some of the clearest human evidence to date that anti-amyloid therapies may limit the accumulation of tau and slow the brain changes that lead to memory loss and cognitive decline."

How Amyloid and Tau Contribute to Alzheimer's Disease

Two proteins play major roles in the development of Alzheimer's disease. Beta-amyloid collects outside nerve cells, forming sticky deposits known as plaques. Tau, meanwhile, accumulates inside neurons and forms abnormal tangles. Both are associated with the progressive damage to brain cells that contributes to memory problems, changes in personality, and difficulties with thinking.

Treatments designed to eliminate amyloid plaques have significantly changed how Alzheimer's is managed in recent years. Aducanumab became the first drug of this type to receive accelerated approval from the FDA, although it is no longer sold. Since then, newer and more effective medications that target amyloid through a similar approach, donanemab (Kisunla) and lecanemab (Leqembi), have received full FDA approval.

Researchers have long believed that amyloid buildup helps create the conditions in which harmful tau tangles develop. What has been much harder to establish is whether eliminating amyloid can also limit tau accumulation in the human brain.

Direct evidence has been scarce because relatively few people who received amyloid-clearing drugs have undergone brain examinations after death.

A Rare Brain Examination Reveals a Striking Pattern

The researchers were able to investigate this question using brain tissue donated by a former Alzheimer's clinical trial participant.

The man was in his fifties when he was diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment caused by Alzheimer's disease. He subsequently joined a clinical trial testing aducanumab and received 30 doses of the medication over four and a half years.

He died approximately four years after his last dose, more than a decade after his first symptoms appeared. Before his death, he expressed a wish to donate his brain to scientific research. The tissue was provided to the Center for Neurodegenerative Disease Research in the Department of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine.

When specialists examined the donated brain, they discovered an unusual pattern in which neighboring regions showed dramatically different amounts of Alzheimer's-related damage.

The raised ridges of the brain's folded surface, known as gyri, had lost most of their amyloid plaques and contained very little accumulated tau. In contrast, the neighboring grooves between these ridges, called sulci, still contained considerable amyloid buildup. These regions also exhibited substantially more tau tangles, inflammation, and degeneration of brain tissue.

This patchwork distribution was consistent with scans taken while the patient was alive, which had already indicated that amyloid removal varied considerably across different parts of his brain.

"Many previous reports have shown either extensive amyloid clearance or limited clearance," said co-senior author Edward B. Lee, MD, PhD, the John Q. Trojanowski, MD, PhD Professor for Neurodegenerative Disease Research in Pathology and Laboratory Medicine. "This case represented a unique 'Goldilocks' scenario in which some regions cleared amyloid and others did not. It allowed us to directly compare what happened next in neighboring brain regions and better understand the relationship between amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration."

Could Alzheimer's Treatments Protect the Brain for Years?

The findings could have important implications for the latest generation of Alzheimer's medications, which remove amyloid using mechanisms similar to the therapy received by this patient.

Although the investigation involved only one individual, the contrast between different brain regions suggests that substantial amyloid removal may influence other disease processes long after treatment has stopped.

The results also raise an important question about how the benefits of Alzheimer's drugs are measured. Clinical trials commonly evaluate patients over approximately 18 months, but some protective effects might take considerably longer to become apparent.

However, the study cannot establish how much amyloid must be removed to achieve lasting protection or prove that plaque clearance was directly responsible for the differences in tau accumulation and brain damage.

Researchers now hope to understand why certain parts of the brain respond more effectively to amyloid-clearing treatments than others. Identifying and potentially strengthening the biological mechanisms responsible for plaque removal could lead to better therapies.

Another major question is whether beginning treatment before extensive tau tangles develop could substantially delay Alzheimer's or even prevent the disease from emerging.

Why Earlier Alzheimer's Treatment Could Matter

Because amyloid can accumulate silently in the brain for many years before memory problems begin, researchers are increasingly interested in treating people during the earliest stages of the disease, potentially before noticeable symptoms appear.

"Patients may accumulate amyloid for years before symptoms appear," said first author Christopher A. Brown, MD, PhD, an assistant professor of Neurology. "This case suggests that removing amyloid early may help limit the changes that ultimately damage brain cells. Ongoing trials will help determine whether starting treatment before symptoms begin can provide even greater benefit."

The United States is expected to add more than 7 GW of wind installations in 2025, a 36% increase over the previous year, and the five-year outlook remains unchanged quarter-over-quarter from previous forecast, according to the “U.S. Wind Energy Monitor” report released by Wood Mackenzie and the American Clean Power Association (ACP).

Amid a challenging market environment, the report shows that the United States is on track to add 46 GW of new wind capacity from 2025 to 2029, with total projected volumes unchanged quarter-on-quarter from previous forecasts. However, timing has shifted, as 2026 and 2027 will deliver significant gains, at 10.7 GW and 12.7 GW, respectively, as more assets advance through the development pipeline.

While Q3 installations came in 23% below forecast at 932 MW, the market has shown progress with 3.8 GW queued for Q4 2025 — representing 52% of the year’s total expected capacity. This back-loaded installation pattern is consistent with typical project-commissioning timelines.

U.S. turbine order intake has rebounded to pre-One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) levels quarter-over-quarter, supported by 2+ GW of firm commitments in Q3, the biggest intake in the region in the last nine months and a 79% quarter-over-quarter increase. However, true visibility remains limited as OEMs increasingly withhold project details and much qualifying “start-of-construction” activity occurs through off-site component manufacturing.

The market will then see a noticeable drop off, as 2029 weakens meaningfully quarter-over-quarter following project cancellations and inactive designations for late-decade capacity, driven by permitting and broader development challenges.

“The U.S. power market is facing mounting strain after a decade of flat demand, with utilities committing to 160 GW of large-load additions. This represents a significant opportunity for wind energy, which benefits from strengthened economic fundamentals and a compelling business case driven by its competitively low LCOE,” said Leila Garcia da Fonseca, director of research at Wood Mackenzie. “However, turbine costs remain elevated due to tariffs and mid-term wind growth will depend on resolving permitting and policy uncertainty.”

Power demand growth through 2029 is expected to average around 3% compared to just 0.7% over the previous decade, with data centers accounting for approximately 59 GW of the 90 GW total peak demand growth. This surge in baseload demand positions wind as a natural fit to meet rising power needs.

The five-year capacity outlook remains unchanged at 39.8 GW of added capacity quarter-over-quarter. The 2025-2027 pipeline is fully committed with all projects having turbine orders in place. More than 60% of the three-year capacity outlook has been commissioned or is under construction.

Activity is led by western states, such as Wyoming, New Mexico and others, that will account for 34% of activity in this time period. Major projects driving the outlook include Pattern’s 3.5-GW SunZia project in New Mexico, which will position the developer as 2026’s top installer, and Invenergy’s 998-MW Towner Energy Center in Colorado, the largest single project expected in 2027.

The market continues expanding geographically, with Arkansas bringing its first utility-scale onshore wind project online through Cordelio’s Crossover Wind.

The repowering market remains strong, as Wood Mackenzie projects that 18 projects will drive 2.5 GW of capacity additions in the next three years.

Wood Mackenzie expects the offshore installation pace to slow in Q4 2025 due to harsh winter weather conditions, pushing remaining capacity into 2026. Despite these near-term adjustments, Vineyard Wind has demonstrated strong execution, connecting 15 turbines in Q3 and delivering 200 GWh through nine months.

“U.S. offshore wind shows diverging momentum,” said Garcia Da Fonseca. “Projects under construction with COD estimated for 2026 continue to hit key milestones, but post-2027 developments face potential delays amid constrained wind turbine installation vessel capacity, driving delays and contract terminations.”

The offshore sector is also experiencing significant financial pressure.

The report highlights that uncertainty around tariffs is a threat to projections. Wood Mackenzie projects that tariffs will drive up turbine costs in 2026, before moderating in subsequent years. In total, U.S. onshore wind capex is projected to increase by 5% through 2029.

“U.S. wind turbine pricing is experiencing unprecedented uncertainty as conflicting market and regulatory forces interact,” said Garcia Da Fonseca. “Domestic manufacturing overcapacity relative to permitted project volumes, particularly after 2028, would normally place downward pressure on prices. Despite this, onshore wind costs are expected to continue rising due to tariff exposure on raw material inputs and subcomponents.”

#5065. Scientists discover a surprising danger in smaller cancer cells

Some cancers grow faster, spread more aggressively, and respond less effectively to treatment than others. Researchers at Virginia Tech are uncovering surprising reasons behind these differences, including how abnormal cancer cells recruit healthy tissue to support tumor growth and why the size of certain cancer cells could help predict their danger.

The discoveries emerged from years of painstaking laboratory work, including the careful examination of tumors grown in mice.

Graduate student Megan Sweet, who studies biological sciences at Virginia Tech, spends much of her time preparing extremely thin slices of tumor tissue. Working inside a refrigerated metal chamber, she carefully adjusts a mounted tumor, roughly the size of a pinkie fingernail, toward a sharp cutting blade.

"It's all about fine-tuning and making sure it's going to be an even slice," said Sweet, who studies biological sciences.

As the blade moves through the tissue, it produces a steady, rhythmic chopping sound.

"This is the hardest and most time-consuming part," Sweet said. "But it's also kind of meditative."

Each section is cut so thin that light can pass through it. Sweet carefully transfers the delicate tissue onto a glass slide, where selected cellular structures are stained to make them easier to distinguish under a powerful microscope.

The process involves repeated rounds of cutting, staining, examining, and comparing samples. Although painstaking, this work is helping scientists piece together why certain tumors become much more dangerous than others.

The Virginia Tech team has published findings that shed new light on how cancer develops and progresses. Their research was published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, with additional findings published earlier this year in Cancer Research.

Most healthy cells in the human body are diploid, meaning they contain two complete sets of chromosomes, with one inherited from each parent.

Under normal conditions, these cells divide to produce new cells with the same chromosome arrangement. Occasionally, however, something goes wrong during division, leaving cells with an abnormal number of chromosomes.

When these errors are passed on during subsequent cell divisions, they can accumulate over time. Such changes are one of the ways cancer can develop.

For the past five years, Sweet and fellow graduate student Mat Bloomfield have worked with cell biologist Daniela Cimini to investigate cells carrying abnormal numbers of chromosomes, a common characteristic of cancer.

To study the consequences of these abnormalities, Cimini's team manipulated diploid cancer cells so they would duplicate their chromosomes without completing cell division.

The result was a type of cell known as a tetraploid cell, which contains four complete sets of chromosomes rather than the usual two.

These cells are not limited to laboratory experiments. Tetraploid cells can also emerge during the development of human tumors, and their presence has been associated with cancer progression and poorer outcomes for patients.

Cancer Cells Recruit Healthy Cells to Fuel Tumor Growth

Scientists have long recognized that tetraploid cells can make tumors more aggressive. What remained unclear was exactly how they contributed to cancer growth.

To investigate, Sweet and Bloomfield compared tumors that developed from ordinary diploid cancer cells with those formed from tetraploid cancer cells.

The experiments produced an unexpected result.

As tumors grew in mice, the proportion of tetraploid cells actually decreased. Despite this decline, the tumors continued expanding rapidly and reached substantial sizes.

The researchers discovered that tetraploid cancer cells were encouraging the recruitment of stromal cells, which are noncancerous connective tissue cells that help provide structural support.

These additional cells appeared to create an environment that favored further tumor development.

"The presence of even a small fraction of these tetraploid cells can promote the recruitment of extra non-cancerous cells that support further tumor progression," Sweet said.

The finding revealed a previously unrecognized mechanism through which abnormal cancer cells can promote tumor growth, even when they account for only a small portion of the tumor.

Smaller Cancer Cells Reveal a Surprising Danger

A separate investigation uncovered another unexpected connection between tetraploid cells and cancer aggressiveness.

Bloomfield was initially examining the physical characteristics of tetraploid cells. After producing tetraploid cells from human cancer cells and establishing colonies derived from individual cells, he noticed that the resulting groups were not all the same size.

Because tetraploid cells contain twice the usual number of chromosome sets, the researchers expected them to be approximately twice as large as diploid cells.

Instead, some of the cells were 25 to 30 percent smaller than anticipated.

Even more surprisingly, those smaller cells proved to be more capable of forming aggressive tumors than their larger counterparts.

"The smaller clones are more aggressive," Bloomfield said. "They grow faster, are more invasive, and more tolerant of common anti-cancer and stress-inducing drugs."

Further experiments in mice supported these observations. Tumors containing smaller tetraploid cells frequently grew more quickly than those containing larger ones.

The pattern was not restricted to a single type of cancer. Researchers observed similar results in both colorectal and breast cancer cells, suggesting that the relationship between tetraploid cell size and tumor aggressiveness may extend across different cancers.

Cancer Cell Size May Help Predict Patient Survival

The researchers next examined whether the same relationship could be found in human cancers.

They analyzed information from the Cancer Genome Atlas, a large collection of detailed data from thousands of cancer patient samples.

The results revealed that smaller tetraploid cells were associated with worse prognoses and lower survival rates across several cancer types.

This suggests that measuring the size of these abnormal cells could provide valuable information about how aggressively a tumor might behave.

"We already knew that tetraploidy can make cells more tumorigenic, but now we know that if you incorporate the size of the cells, it can be more predictive of tumorigenic potential," Cimini said.

The findings add an important dimension to scientists' understanding of cancer. Abnormal chromosome numbers have long been linked to dangerous tumor behavior, but the new research indicates that cell size may offer additional clues about a cancer's potential to grow and spread.

The team now plans to investigate the biological mechanisms responsible for these effects in greater detail while continuing to examine human cancer data.

For Sweet, that means more hours at the laboratory bench, carefully cutting and analyzing tiny sections of tumor tissue in the search for answers to one of cancer biology's most important questions.

AI is supporting utility demand forecasting and maintenance prioritization, but uneven outputs, or those that can’t be audited, constrain its broader deployment.

The utility industry is a long way from allowing large language models to take action affecting physical infrastructure, executives say. For now, most AI deployments in the sector are niche tools designed to support human decision-making on topics like load forecasting and vegetation management.

Barriers to broader utilization include uncertainty over what to do with AI outputs that are uneven or that can’t be audited in a highly regulated sector with strict safety and reliability standards.

“Most utilities could not reconstruct what an AI system read, what it did and why,” said Guardrail CEO T.J. Many utility AI projects remain in development or pilot stages, Tagare said, as utilities are still working out what cybersecurity risks they pose as information technology and operational technology networks become more interconnected.

“[For] anything that touches the physical assets directly, we haven't seen widespread deployment of agentic architecture yet,” he said.

#5055. Cancer is rising in younger adults, and scientists can't fully explain why

Cancer rates are increasing among adults of different ages in England, but a new study has uncovered a particularly concerning pattern among younger people. Rates of bowel and ovarian cancer have risen among younger adults (under 50), while the same increases have not been observed in older age groups.

The findings, published in the open-access journal BMJ Oncology, also show that several other cancers are becoming more common at a faster pace among younger adults than among those aged 50 and older.

Although excess body weight appears to play an important role, researchers say obesity alone cannot account for the increases. The findings suggest that additional factors may be contributing to the growing burden of cancer in younger generations.

Similar increases in cancer among people under 50 have been reported outside England. However, scientists still do not fully understand whether changing lifestyle habits and other established risk factors can explain these trends.

Cancer Rates Rise Across Multiple Age Groups

To investigate, researchers examined cancer incidence records collected by England's National Disease Registry Service between 2001 and 2019. They compared trends in more than 20 cancer types among men and women aged 20 to 49 and adults aged 50 or older.

The team also analyzed national health survey data to track changes in established cancer risk factors. These included smoking, alcohol consumption, diet (high red/processed meat, low fiber intake), excess body weight (BMI), and insufficient physical activity. The researchers assessed how these factors changed across age groups and sexes and estimated how much they contributed to cancer cases.

The results revealed widespread increases in cancer incidence. Among younger women, 16 of the 22 cancer types examined showed statistically significant increases. Among younger men, rates rose significantly for 11 of 21 cancers.

Of particular concern were 11 cancers associated with known behavioral risk factors that became significantly more common among adults under 50. These included cancers of the thyroid, liver, kidney, gallbladder, bowel, pancreas, mouth, breast, and ovary, along with multiple myeloma and cancer of the uterine lining (endometrial).

Most of these cancers also increased significantly among adults over 50. However, bowel and ovarian cancers stood out because their incidence rose significantly among younger adults but not among older people.

The researchers also identified several cancers whose incidence increased more rapidly in younger women. These included endometrial, kidney, pancreatic, multiple myeloma, and thyroid cancers. Among men, multiple myeloma was the only cancer found to be increasing significantly faster in the younger age group.

Obesity Is a Major Factor, but It Doesn't Explain Everything

The researchers examined how established lifestyle risks might help explain the increases. Of the 11 cancers of particular interest, 10 were associated with obesity, with mouth cancer being the exception.

Six cancers (liver, bowel, mouth, pancreas, kidney, and ovary) were linked to smoking, while four (liver, bowel, mouth, and breast) were associated with alcohol consumption. Three (bowel, breast, and endometrial) were connected to physical inactivity, and one (bowel) was linked to dietary factors.

Yet the findings revealed an important contradiction. Apart from excess weight, most of these established risk factors either remained relatively stable or improved among younger adults over the previous one to two decades.

Red meat consumption, for example, showed some of the largest reported improvements, with reductions of around 7% in the survey trends examined. Among younger men, average daily consumption fell from 38 grams in 2008 to 17 grams in 2018. Among younger women, it dropped from 22 grams to 10 grams over the same period.

Younger women also consumed substantially less processed meat than younger men, averaging about 10 grams per day compared with approximately 20 grams.

Fiber intake remained a concern, with more than 90% of younger adults failing to consume enough fiber in 2018. Nevertheless, average intake was generally stable or improved slightly among both sexes between 2009 and 2019. Older adults showed broadly similar dietary trends.

These findings raise questions about why cancer incidence continued to increase even as several recognized lifestyle risks were moving in a more favorable direction.

How Much Cancer Is Linked to Known Risk Factors?

Despite these improvements, established behavioral risk factors were still associated with a substantial proportion of cancer cases.

For men in 2019, the researchers estimated that these factors accounted for 68% of mouth cancer cases among younger adults and 65% among older adults. The corresponding figures were 42% and 48% for liver cancer, 49% and 53% for bowel cancer, 29% and 33% for kidney cancer, and 36% and 34% for pancreatic cancer.

Among women, the estimated proportions attributable to these risks were 52% for younger adults and 45% for older adults for mouth cancer. For endometrial cancer, the figures were 35% and 42%, while liver cancer accounted for 44% and 46%.

The corresponding estimates were 38% and 42% for bowel cancer, 33% and 37% for kidney cancer, 31% and 28% for pancreatic cancer, and 19% and 24% for gallbladder cancer.

Excess body weight was associated with more of the cancer types examined than any other individual risk factor. In 2019, the estimated proportion of cases attributable to excess weight varied considerably, from 5% for ovarian cancer to 37% for endometrial cancer.

Even so, the researchers found that increases in obesity could not fully account for the broader cancer trends.

"These patterns suggest that while similar risk factors across ages are likely, some cancers may have age-specific exposures, susceptibilities, or differences in screening and detection practices," write the researchers.

The study was observational, meaning it could identify associations and trends but could not determine whether particular risk factors directly caused the increases in cancer incidence.

The researchers also acknowledged several limitations. Reliable national data covering long periods were unavailable for some risk factors, and the analysis focused exclusively on England rather than the entire United Kingdom.

In addition, their calculations assumed a 10-year delay between exposure to a risk factor and the development of cancer.

"The observed increasing cancer incidence despite declining trends in several behavioral risk factors may reflect the net effect of multiple influences operating in different directions. Other contributing factors not evaluated here, for example, reproductive history, early-life or prenatal risk factors, and changes in cancer diagnosis and detection practices, may also play a role," they explain.

The researchers emphasized that obesity remains important, but increases in cancer cases cannot be attributed to weight alone.

"Although overweight and obesity are linked to 10 of the 11 cancers evaluated and account for a substantial proportion of cancer cases, both BMI-attributable and BMI-non-attributable incidence rates have increased -- though the latter more slowly -- suggesting other contributors," they add.

Other potential explanations have been proposed for the increase in cancers among younger adults. These include greater consumption of ultra-processed foods and sugary drinks, childhood obesity, physical inactivity/sedentary behavior, antibiotic exposure, and air pollution.

However, many of these possible contributors have not shown the increases that might be expected if they were solely responsible for rising cancer rates.

"While these factors are common in England, most have shown stable or declining trends in the last decade," the researchers note.

One possibility that deserves closer examination is disruption of the gut microbiome, the community of microorganisms living in the digestive system. Changes in these microbial populations have been suggested as a potential influence on cancer risk, although further research is needed to establish their role.

Cancer Remains More Common in Older Adults

While the rise in cancer among younger people is concerning, the researchers cautioned against overlooking the much greater number of cases that still occur among older adults.

The findings highlight the need to investigate cancer risk throughout life, including the possibility that exposures during childhood or even before birth may influence disease decades later.

"Although increases in cancer in younger adults are concerning, the absolute burden remains far higher in older adults, underscoring the public health and clinical importance of studying risk factors across all ages," they conclude.
