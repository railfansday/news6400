Few names in the science fiction literary world carry as much weight and respect as author Alan Dean Foster. His award-winning 55-year career as a grandmaster of the genre continues with his latest novel from Blackstone Publishing, "Lost on Paradise," and we have an exclusive excerpt to share with our readers ahead of its release this week.

Foster famously ghost-wrote the first "Star Wars" novelization for George Lucas in 1976 and "Splinter of the Mind’s Eye" in 1978, which was intended to be a low-budget "Star Wars" sequel should the space opera not become a hit. He’s been a mainstay for go-to fantastic adventures featuring engaging characters, dynamic plotlines, and imaginative worlds for six decades.

In addition to crafting the screen story for "Star Trek: The Motion Picture," Foster also wrote the novelizations for the first three "Alien" films, Disney's "The Black Hole," John Carpenter's "The Thing," "The Last Starfighter," "Star Trek," "Transformers," "Terminator: Salvation", and many more.

The near-future, off-world escapades of "Lost On Paradise" are the latest addition to Foster’s acclaimed bounty of bestselling standalone novels and series, including the popular "Spellsinger" series.

"Earth is crowded, polluted, and dismal," states the novel's synopsis. "Like millions of others, Jonos and Liina scrape by on her poorly paying job and what little money he gets selling items scavenged from rich people’s trash. Like millions of others, Jonos and Liina dream of Paradise.

"Paradise: a beautiful, clean, verdant world where three races live in harmony—at least, according to the videos sent back to Earth. There are the Eiquan, masters of astonishing technology; the Lusanata, hunters and farmers; and humans, who may be restricted to a few scattered settlements, but still live a life ten times better than those on Earth.

"On the run after a criminal act, Jonos and Liina finally escape to Paradise. It's everything they hoped for. Until tragedy strikes."

The genesis of Foster's "Lost on Paradise" is something that’s been percolating in his fertile mind for many years.

"I had this notion of a planet called Paradise where everything is perfect, and everything on Earth is seriously messed up," Foster tells Space. "There’s this couple who have problems; they’re poor like everybody else.

"And you have a unique male protagonist because he recognizes that his female partner is smarter than him, so she’s the brains of the outfit, and he supplies the muscle. Although he's not stupid. Too often you get a big, hulking guy with no brains. But she has a potentially fatal disease, and they’re trying to get away like a lot of poor people in the world today. So here's a place called Paradise. How do you get there? It’s expensive and difficult. And what happens if they do? Maybe Paradise isn’t always Paradise."

In crafting a deteriorating New York some 150 years or so down the calendar, it was important to stay sharp regarding developments that pop up in the news to avoid any internal logic conflicts.

"You just don’t want to get caught out by a scientific development that you postulate is near future when it's actually contemporary," explains Foster. "So I was very careful with that. But as you’re building out an alien environment and one that’s supposed to be perfect and beautiful, and then there’s maybe some things about it that aren’t, what do you introduce to show that?

"You don’t want to contradict yourself when you’re worldbuilding. That's a large problem that’s unfortunately common in science fiction," notes Foster. "Where somebody is breathing air, and suddenly they’re breathing methane, and it just doesn’t work. There’s a lot of different variables that come together that prove that Paradise is Paradise in some ways and not in others. A good example would be when the first Europeans reached Tahiti. The problem was that the people who entered this Paradise brought venereal disease and smallpox, to which the Polynesians had no resistance. So it's Paradise until you get there, and then maybe you mess it up."

#3798. Ubisoft closing Trials developer RedLynx and downsizing Assassin's Creed: Black Flag Resynced team in Singapore

"We deeply value the contributions of both teams."

The video game layoffs continue. Ubisoft has announced it's closing Finnish studio RedLynx, creator of the Trials series of games, and that there's to be significant layoffs at Assassin's Creed: Black Flag Resynced studio, Ubisoft Singapore.

"Today, we informed our teams of a proposal to close RedLynx in Finland, subject to the local change negotiation process, which would affect up to 52 positions," Ubisoft said in a statement seen by Eurogamer. "At Ubisoft Singapore, 70 positions have been impacted to align the studio with upcoming production needs. The studio remains an important part of Ubisoft's global production organisation."

Ubisoft added: "We deeply value the contributions of both teams and recognise the impact this may have on our colleagues. We are committed to supporting our teams throughout this process and conducting it with care and respect."

Ubisoft didn't expand on its reasons for closing RedLynx, but the studio's last major standalone release was 2019's Trials Rising, suggesting it might have played more of a support role since then. Ubisoft has also been undergoing a top-to-bottom reorganisation of its business, shuffling a number of major franchises under new subsidiary company Vantage, while stretching the Ubisoft Massive label to cover more of its brands.

Ubisoft acquired RedLynx back in 2011, after the breakout success of Trials HD - a motorbike platform game with strong puzzle elements. Eurogamer loved Trials HD and I visited RedLynx shortly after its release to find out more about where the idea came from. The studio's leaders then, Tero Virtala and Antti Ilvessuo, have since moved on. Virtala notably became CEO of Remedy Entertainment (a post he recently left), and Ilvessuo - the creator of Trials - established new studio Channel 37 to make watery crafting survival game The Last Caretaker. That game appears to have done well in early access on Steam, by the way, and now approaches a spring 2027 1.0 release, on both PC and Xbox.

Ilvessuo, in response to today's news, said this to Eurogamer: "I'm proud of what we built at RedLynx, but my part in that story ended years ago. In 2012, Trials Evolution's riddle started, and its final chapter is set in 2113. So even something we made back then still has most of its future ahead of it.

"I don't know who - or what - will open that box. But someone has to care enough to carry the key all that way. That's what matters. Making something that will still mean something, something that has weight and history in a world that may be totally different in the future. Onwards!"

Assassin's Creed: Black Flag Resynced, meanwhile, seemed popular, coasting past 3.5 million sales in only a fortnight, and a Switch 2 version is reportedly on the way. Dom scored the game three out of five stars in our Assassin's Creed: Black Flag Resynced review.

#3792. I Tried the At-Home Flu Vaccine, and It Was Great

If I ever skip my flu shot, or fail to get one for my kids, it's because I didn't find time to get to the pharmacy during flu shot season. But that changed with FluMist at Home, a mail order flu vaccine (covered by insurance!) that I've used two years running. The vaccine comes in the form of a nasal spray, so you don't have to inject anything.

The at-home version of the vaccine was approved in 2024, and became available for the first time in 2025. I used it then, and the process was so painless I did it again this year. I placed an order online, and one day a refrigerated box of vaccines showed up at my doorstep, one for each member of the family. We administered them in minutes, and could check "get flu shot" off our to-do lists.

The nasal spray vaccine, called FluMist, still needs to be prescribed by a medical provider. But since nearly everyone aged 2 through 49 is eligible, FluMist’s maker (AstraZeneca) has a created a telehealth website that bundles the provider's approval with an order to an online pharmacy. The process takes about five minutes on average, the company says, and I found that to be accurate.

The first time I ordered, last year, I had already gotten a flu shot, so I ordered just for my kids. The website allowed me to add multiple people to the same order, so I filled out the questionnaire for each child, giving their names, ages, allergies, and so on.

The website then reached into some big data cloud out there and told me it already knew their insurance information (existentially frightening, yet convenient!) and presented me with my bill: an $8.99 fee, which included $2.50 for physician evaluation and $6.49 for shipping and processing. Insurance covers the cost of the vaccine itself.

This year was similar, except that I ordered the vaccine for five people in total, one of whom had a different insurance plan than the rest. It took some extra time to manually enter that person's info, but otherwise the process was the same. The $8.99 fee only covers up to four vaccines, so in total I had to pay $17.98 for all five.

The at-home vaccine is the same as regular FluMist, which is approved for ages 2 through 49. That means babies and older adults will still need to visit a provider for the regular, injectable flu vaccine. Children can get flu shots starting at the age of six months.

FluMist uses a live, weakened virus. It can't give you the actual flu, but it can cause some respiratory symptoms in people with weakened immune systems. So it's not recommended for people who are pregnant, have certain medical conditions, or are immunocompromised or have close contact with an immunocompromised person. If that's you, talk to a provider about your best options. You may be advised to get the regular injectable type, which doesn't include any live virus.

When I placed the first order, I had to choose a delivery date (to be sure I'd be home and ready to receive it). This year, there was no option to choose the date, but perhaps that's because I ordered it in mid-September, a few weeks before it actually shipped. Each time the box arrived via FedEx, with multiple texts ahead of time letting me know it was on its way. The vaccine needs to stay at refrigerator temperature, so you don't want it sitting out on your porch all weekend. I thought I would have to refrigerate it immediately—the instructions say as much—but it can actually be unrefrigerated for up to 12 hours before administration.

So the box arrives, insulated and with the vaccine packages surrounded by cold packs. There is a "TiveTag" sticker on the interior box, so when I received my first order I followed the instructions to download the TiveTag app from a QR code on the package. (This year, I had the app installed already.) It installed quickly, and then I just had to hold the top of my phone up to the RFID sticker on the box while my daughter said "now kith." I felt a buzz and a green checkmark appeared, telling me that the box had stayed at an appropriate temperature the whole time. We were good to go.

Giving the vaccine to yourself or a child takes seconds, with no special preparation. All you have to do is remove the rubber cap, squirt the vaccine into one nostril, then take off the little spacer around the plunger and squirt into the second nostril. (The spacer makes sure that you get half the vaccine into each nostril.)

I hate having things in my nose, but this wasn't bad even for me. It isn't much liquid, and you don't have to inhale or do anything special. Just squirt and you're done. If you've gotten a FluMist at a pharmacy, it's the same idea.

Adults can administer it to themselves, and children will need an adult to do it for them. Instructions are, of course, provided. After I gave it to my kids, I clicked a link in a text message to "report vaccination," which supposedly updates their health records in, again, some big ol' data cloud. You weren’t given the ability to set your glaive on fire – you figured it out yourself. And that’s a good feeling.

Echo Weaver’s unique approach to the Metroid structure is admirable, unique, and well executed. Developer Moonlight Kids puts a lot of trust in the player to figure out how it all works, and the reward is palpable. That trust leads to some frustrated, aimless wandering, and the platforming challenges occasionally venture into feeling unfair, but by the end you will feel like a genius. That’s a good feeling to leave a game with.

There are rudiments of a class system, which factors into optional terrain puzzles: some penguins are heavy enough to press floor switches, while baby penguins can't swim but are able to fit through tunnels, revealing new areas or shortcuts.

While the game recreates the calls of different species, I'm not sure Penguin Colony does much justice to the inner life of penguins. They're just a fun, disposable dramatic device, riffing on Pingu and Happy Feet as much as they do the six-foot dreadsquawkers of Lovecraft's story. I'm also not sure Penguin Colony is the best penguin platformer I've played, which isn't exactly a huge tent, because penguins, bless 'em, are not obvious ground-based acrobats. The penguins in Penguin Colony are, to be frank, janky as hell. They boop and stutter up inclines, often allowing you to 'cheat' the geography. They 'slide' like their bottoms are misfiring turbojets. In general, the game's presentation is a bit of a penguin right now. Tutorial tips reappear erratically, and there's a stamina circle that takes up far too much of the screen.

The penguins are overall performant in getting you from A to B, but this ain't sand-surfing in Journey. Still, one benefit of being a janky-ass penguin is that you often have to move quite ponderously, which enforces a lengthier appreciation of the game's environments - especially, the bleeding helix catacombs and blue-glared "downtown" areas of later chapters. There's some terrific scenery in Penguin Colony, beyond the opening dreary ice sheet: one enormous ruin houses a lumpy, rippled structure that could be either a brain coral or a god's petrified brain, defying both comprehension and articulation in the maze-folded contours of its squamous and primordial immensity. Or as my penguin at the time put it: HOOONK.

While I was expecting Penguin Colony to put the boot into settlers and Lovecraft, after trying the demo, I was startled to find that on some level, it's a Soulslike. Or maybe an anti-Soulslike. Later on, there's a "boss battle" featuring a giant, jerky Shoggowotsit that pays open homage to FromSoftware, complete with harrowing orchestral score and AOE gravity attacks. It's like somebody tried to rebuild the Gaping Dragon out of mops and pieces of accordion. Being a penguin, I merely flopped on my belly once again and scooched through the horror's ankles, onto the next region. I have done likewise with many Souls enemies, unless restrained by fog gates. Is there a Penguin Knight in Dark Souls? There should be.

In the wake of that encounter, I thought anew about all the non-penguin life I'd met during my four-hour Penguin Colony playthrough – all the near-dead fascists in foetal positions or kneeling before idols, their bodies mutilated, bleating in despair. You see hollow folk like that all over Lordran, the difference here being that as one in a million local penguins, I am not perceived as an intruder. No corner ambushes for me. The same likely isn't true of the Māori characters, who are essentially playing a 'real' Soulslike behind the scenes, navigating a more hostile incarnation of the same world.

The giant, jerky Shoggowotsit also features in one of the Māori adventurer sequences. The game dangles the carrot of a 'proper' bossfight, here, but fades mockingly to white when you take the first swing. Hence, 'anti-Soulslike': Penguin Colony invokes certain genre expectations in order to frustrate them. Or perhaps, to redirect them.

In lieu of combat, the FromSofty parallels appear most strongly in the torturous, Blighttown-worthy route design, and especially, the framing of pick-ups. There are collectible glowballs that bestow not gear and XP, but English and Te reo Māori words that often chime with or invite commentary on their surroundings – a really intriguing revising of the poetic distribution of item lore in Dark Souls. There are also photographs, taken by a Nazi propagandist, that self-combust to release snatches of flashback dialogue.

After the credits roll, you're shown a Zodiac constellation of all the lore you've found, a web of secrets and lies the devs offer in-game as a basis for recapturing "pre-social media secret finding and community storytelling". It's an incentive to replay, though Origame Digital's Tali Faulkner has elsewhere commented that the team would rather people don't try to 100% the lore – it's more about swapping notes with other players, like doom-sodden Miskatonic boffins trying to work out which bit of Cthulhu goes where.

Origame Digital plan to make more Lovecraftian games - they do, indeed, have their sights on Cthulhu, having begun their adventure with a Lovecraft story that is simultaneously well-known and less-explored, by virtue of starring a different faction of cosmic deities. I hope they also continue their experiments with Soulslike design, because aside from putting the H.P. in Penguin, their latest game has shed a strange new light on a genre I thought I knew all too well.

#3781. New drug reverses severe fatty liver disease by repairing the gut

An experimental drug developed at Michigan Medicine has reversed a serious form of fatty liver disease in animal studies by targeting an unexpected source of liver damage: the gut.

The compound, known as DT-109, works by reducing harmful gut bacteria, protecting the intestinal lining, and preventing inflammatory substances from reaching the liver. According to findings published in The Journal of Clinical Investigation, this approach successfully reversed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) in animal models, offering a promising new strategy for treating a disease that remains difficult to manage.

MASH affects approximately 7% of the world's population. It is a severe form of fatty liver disease in which excess fat accumulates in the liver, causing inflammation and cellular damage. Over time, the condition can progress to cirrhosis, which involves extensive liver scarring, as well as liver cancer and liver failure. Although treatment options have improved in recent years, effective therapies remain limited.

"We see clear evidence that DT-109 protects the gut epithelial barrier, reducing the systemic influx of harmful microbial products that are thought to contribute to MASH development and progression," said Eugene Chen, M.D., Ph.D., senior author of the study and Frederick G. L. Huetwell Professor of Cardiovascular Medicine at the University of Michigan Medical School.

"This compound shows benefits to the gastrointestinal system and has great potential as a treatment for MASH."

How Harmful Gut Bacteria Can Trigger Liver Damage

Chen's research team had previously demonstrated that DT-109, a glycine-based tripeptide, could improve MASH in animal studies. A tripeptide is a small molecule made from three amino acids, which are the building blocks of proteins. What remained unclear was exactly how the compound produced its protective effects.

The latest research provides an explanation, revealing how changes in gut bacteria can set off a chain reaction that damages the liver and how DT-109 can interrupt that process.

Before examining the drug's effects, the scientists confirmed an important contributor to MASH: excessive growth of Clostridium perfringens, a bacterium that produces ammonia inside the intestines.

When ammonia levels rise, they can damage the gut's protective inner lining. Normally, this intestinal barrier helps prevent potentially harmful substances from escaping the digestive tract and entering the bloodstream.

As the barrier weakens, however, toxins and other products generated by gut microbes can travel to the liver. Once there, they can provoke inflammation and activate immune defenses in ways that worsen liver damage.

One response involves the excessive activation of CD8+ T cells, immune cells that normally help the body destroy infected or abnormal cells. When these cells become overactive, they can contribute to the inflammatory processes associated with MASH.

The findings help explain why a disease centered in the liver can be strongly influenced by events occurring elsewhere in the digestive system.

Experimental Drug Repairs the Gut and Protects the Liver

Through a series of experiments, researchers discovered that DT-109 disrupts this damaging cycle by improving the intestinal environment and reinforcing the gut's protective barrier.

In both mice and nonhuman primates, the compound reduced levels of Clostridium perfringens and lowered ammonia production in the intestines. These changes helped strengthen the intestinal lining, limiting the movement of harmful microbial substances into the body and reducing their potential to damage the liver.

The results were particularly encouraging in nonhuman primates, whose liver biology and gut microbial communities more closely resemble those of humans than those of mice. In these animals, DT-109 reduced liver inflammation and lessened the severity of MASH.

Together, the findings show how targeting harmful processes in the gut can produce beneficial effects in the liver, even though the drug acts primarily within the gastrointestinal system.

"DT-109 connects microbiota modulation with liver protection by restoring gut barrier integrity and limiting the systemic translocation of ammonia and other pro-inflammatory microbial products within the gut-liver axis," said Jifeng Zhang, Ph.D., co-author and research professor of cardiovascular medicine at U-M Medical School.

"We also found that DT-109 primarily acts in the gastrointestinal tract, but its reach stretches much further."

The gut-liver axis refers to the close biological relationship between the digestive tract and the liver. Substances absorbed from the intestines travel to the liver through the bloodstream, making liver health particularly sensitive to changes in gut bacteria and intestinal barrier function.

By targeting this connection, DT-109 may offer a way to address some of the processes driving liver inflammation rather than focusing exclusively on damage that has already occurred.

Potential Benefits Beyond Fatty Liver Disease

Researchers believe the compound's ability to protect the intestinal barrier could make it useful against other conditions involving inflammation and metabolic dysfunction.

Previous research has shown that DT-109 can reduce the formation of atherosclerotic plaques and prevent vascular calcification in nonhuman primates.

Atherosclerotic plaques are deposits of fat and other substances that accumulate inside artery walls, potentially restricting blood flow and increasing the risk of cardiovascular problems. Vascular calcification occurs when calcium builds up in blood vessel walls, making them less flexible and contributing to cardiovascular disease.

These findings suggest that DT-109 could eventually have applications in treating both liver and cardiovascular conditions, an important possibility because metabolic disorders often affect multiple organs.

The researchers are also interested in whether the compound could help people with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), a group of chronic conditions that includes Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis.

Because problems with the intestinal barrier are associated with several gastrointestinal disorders, a treatment that strengthens this barrier might have benefits beyond protecting the liver. However, additional research will be needed to determine whether DT-109 is effective against these conditions.

A Promising Treatment That Still Needs Human Trials

Despite the encouraging results, DT-109 remains an experimental treatment. Its ability to reverse MASH has been demonstrated in animal models, but researchers have not yet established whether it can safely and effectively produce similar benefits in humans.

The next phase of research will involve further evaluation of the compound, with the goal of advancing it toward clinical trials. Those studies will be essential for determining its safety, appropriate use, and potential effectiveness in patients.

"This study presents novel evidence about the pathogenesis of MASH and provides excitement about a therapeutic avenue to explore for a condition that remains difficult to treat," said Elliot Tapper, M.D., Academic Director of Hepatology at Michigan Medicine.

"What patients with MASH need is a safe and effective therapy capable of improving their liver and heart health - of course we are excited about these developments."

The discovery highlights an important shift in how scientists are approaching fatty liver disease. By addressing harmful gut bacteria and restoring the intestinal barrier, researchers may be able to protect the liver through a treatment that acts primarily outside the organ itself.

If future human studies confirm the benefits seen in animals, DT-109 could offer a new therapeutic option for patients with severe fatty liver disease while potentially addressing cardiovascular complications as well.

#3772. Scientists tested 39 sweeteners and uncovered surprising gut effects

Common sweeteners may be doing much more inside our bodies than simply replacing sugar. Scientists at the University of Cambridge have discovered that many widely used sweeteners can directly interfere with the growth of gut bacteria, and their effects can become even more pronounced when combined with medications or other everyday substances.

In laboratory experiments involving 39 sweeteners and 25 species of gut bacteria, researchers found that roughly three-quarters of the sweeteners altered the growth of at least one bacterial species. Some slowed or completely stopped the growth of microbes associated with a healthy digestive system.

One combination stood out in particular. When the sweetener isosteviol was paired with the antidepressant duloxetine, it strongly suppressed two important gut bacteria involved in maintaining digestive health and regulating blood sugar. The combination also disrupted the balance of a simulated gut microbiome and produced changes that could have implications for inflammation and immune responses.

The findings, published in Molecular Systems Biology, raise new questions about how sugar substitutes interact with the body. However, the researchers emphasize that their experiments were conducted in the laboratory, not in humans, and further studies are needed to determine whether these effects have real-world health consequences.

Are Artificial Sweeteners Really Harmless to Gut Bacteria?

Sweeteners have become a familiar ingredient in modern diets. They appear in everything from soft drinks and candy to desserts, packaged snacks, and breakfast cereals, often providing sweetness with fewer calories than traditional sugar.

These ingredients are frequently promoted as healthier alternatives to sugar. Yet growing research has identified associations between sweetener consumption and health conditions such as type 2 diabetes, obesity, and cancer. Such associations do not necessarily mean that sweeteners cause these diseases, and scientists are still working to understand the biological mechanisms that might explain the findings.

One area of particular interest is the gut microbiome, the enormous collection of bacteria and other microorganisms that inhabit the digestive tract. These microscopic residents help break down food, produce useful compounds, support the immune system, and contribute to metabolic health.

Although sweeteners are consumed around the world, surprisingly little research has directly examined how they affect individual gut bacteria. Most previous investigations have relied on animal experiments or studies comparing dietary habits and health outcomes across large groups of people.

Professor Kiran Patil from the Medical Research Council (MRC) Toxicology Unit at the University of Cambridge said: "Most of what we know about the potential impact of sweeteners on our health comes from animal research or from population studies. While these studies have indicated involvement of the microbiome in mediating the effect of sweeteners, it's difficult to know how sweeteners act in the body - is it through direct interactions with our gut bacteria?"

An additional challenge is that people rarely consume sweeteners on their own. They are typically mixed with other ingredients, and those combinations may change how they behave.

"Answering this is further complicated by the fact that we rarely ever take sweeteners by themselves - we take them with drinks, in snacks, or even in medication to mask bitterness," added Dr. Sonja Blasche, a lead author of the study, also the MRC Toxicology Unit.

To investigate these interactions, Blasche and her colleagues designed a series of laboratory experiments examining both artificial and naturally derived low-calorie sweeteners.

The team cultivated 25 different species of gut bacteria, including microorganisms considered beneficial, neutral, or potentially harmful. Each species was grown separately so researchers could measure how it responded to specific sweeteners without interference from other bacteria.

They then exposed these bacterial cultures to 39 commercially available sweeteners and monitored whether the microorganisms continued multiplying normally.

The results revealed a surprisingly widespread effect. Approximately three out of every four sweeteners changed the growth of at least one bacterial species.

In some cases, bacterial populations grew more slowly. In others, growth stopped entirely. Several affected species are associated with maintaining a healthy gut environment, suggesting that certain sweeteners can directly influence microorganisms that contribute to digestive health.

However, the effects were not uniform. Different sweeteners influenced different bacteria, showing that these substances cannot necessarily be treated as a single group when evaluating their biological activity.

More Than 100 Unexpected Interactions With Everyday Substances

The researchers then explored a question that previous studies had largely overlooked: What happens when sweeteners are combined with other ingredients or medications?

To find out, they tested sweeteners alongside several commonly encountered substances, including caffeine, vanillin (vanilla extract), advantame (an artificial sweetener), and eight widely used drugs.

The combinations produced more than 100 instances in which a sweetener's effect on bacterial growth changed in the presence of another substance.

In 34 cases, the combinations intensified the effects on bacteria. In another 68 cases, the effects became weaker.

These findings suggest that the way a sweetener influences gut microbes may depend not only on the sweetener itself but also on what accompanies it.

A compound that has a relatively modest effect when tested alone could behave differently when combined with an ingredient in a beverage, a food additive, or a medication.

A Sweetener and Antidepressant Combination Stands Out

Among all the combinations investigated, one produced especially striking results.

The researchers found that isosteviol, a sweetener used in the food and beverage industry, strongly inhibited two important gut bacteria when paired with the antidepressant duloxetine.

The affected species were Roseburia intestinalis and Parabacteroides merdae, both associated with important functions in the digestive system, including processes related to gut health and blood sugar regulation.

Duloxetine is a widely prescribed medication. In the United States alone, more than 4.2 million patients received prescriptions for the drug in 2023.

The laboratory findings suggest that certain sweeteners and medications can interact in ways that substantially alter the growth of particular gut microbes.

However, this does not establish that people taking duloxetine experience these effects or that consuming isosteviol alongside the medication is harmful. Understanding whether the same interactions occur in the human body will require additional research.

The Combination Also Disrupted Gut Microbiome Diversity

Studying individual bacterial species provided valuable information, but the human gut is far more complicated than a collection of isolated microbes.

Inside the digestive system, bacteria coexist in large, interconnected communities. Different species compete for nutrients, exchange chemical compounds, and influence one another's growth.

To better approximate this environment, the researchers created a simplified artificial gut community containing all 25 bacterial species from their initial experiments.

After allowing the community to develop, they introduced different combinations of sweeteners and drugs. They then tracked changes in bacterial populations and measured the overall diversity of the microbial community.

Microbial diversity refers to the variety of microorganisms present and how they are distributed within a community. A diverse gut microbiome is generally considered important for maintaining digestive health and supporting the stability of the intestinal environment.

The results showed that combining isosteviol with duloxetine reduced bacterial diversity and changed the relative abundance of different species.

In other words, the combination did more than affect a few bacteria individually. It altered the makeup of the broader microbial community.

Additional experiments revealed another potentially important effect. The bacterial community exposed to the isosteviol and duloxetine combination showed increased toxicity toward certain host cells and altered the behavior of other cells involved in inflammation and immune responses.

These observations suggest that interactions between sweeteners, medications, and gut bacteria could have consequences beyond bacterial growth alone, although whether similar effects occur in people remains unknown.

Scientists Challenge a Common Assumption About Sweeteners

The results raise questions about the widespread assumption that low-calorie sweeteners have little direct biological activity.

A substance described as metabolically neutral is generally understood to have limited effects on the body's metabolic processes. The new experiments suggest that, at least under laboratory conditions, some sweeteners can interact directly with gut microorganisms.

Dr. Blasche said: "Sweeteners are often marketed as metabolically neutral, but our study challenges this idea. We found that they can directly affect gut bacteria, particularly when mixed with other compounds such as medication and food additives. These common combinations could have unintended effects on our gut microbiome."

The findings also highlight why examining individual food ingredients in isolation may not provide a complete picture of how they behave.

A typical meal or beverage contains multiple compounds, and people may also be taking medications that interact with those substances. The Cambridge experiments demonstrate that such combinations can sometimes produce substantially different effects than the ingredients do separately.

Despite the striking results, the scientists caution against drawing immediate conclusions about the safety of sweeteners or their interactions with prescription medications.

All experiments were carried out in controlled laboratory settings using isolated bacterial cultures or simplified microbial communities. The human digestive system contains a much larger and more complex population of microorganisms, along with many additional biological factors that could influence the outcome.

It remains unclear whether the changes observed in the laboratory would occur under normal dietary conditions or whether they would translate into measurable health effects.

Nevertheless, the research provides a foundation for investigating how common sweeteners, food additives, and medications might interact inside the digestive system.

Professor Patil, the study's senior author, added: "Our study suggests that artificial sweeteners don't just pass through the body passively -- they can interact with gut microbes, and these effects can be amplified or altered by other substances like medications. These findings can help guide new studies towards understanding how sweeteners might influence health in unexpected ways."

Rather than establishing that sweeteners are harmful, the findings point to an important area of research that has received relatively little attention: how everyday combinations of food ingredients and medications may influence the microscopic communities that help keep us healthy.

#3763. Scientists link a strange sugar in red meat to a 63% higher diabetes risk

A sugar naturally present in red meat may help explain why eating these foods has been associated with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes. Preliminary findings from a large observational study suggest that people who consume the most of this sugar face a substantially greater risk of developing the disease, potentially because of its effects on the immune system and chronic inflammation.

The sugar, known as N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc), is particularly common in beef and lamb. Researchers found that participants with the highest intake of meat-derived Neu5Gc had a 63% higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes (T2D) compared with those who consumed the least.

The association remained statistically significant after researchers accounted for several established diabetes risk factors, including physical activity, overall diet, smoking, and existing health conditions. It became weaker after body mass index (BMI) was also considered, but remained significant.

The findings were presented at the Annual Meeting of The European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) in Milan, Italy.

“Our findings support chronic inflammation as a specific mechanism linking red meat consumption with T2D, partly beyond the amount of red meat a person eats and partly independent of body fat,” said lead author Dr. Leopold Fezeu from the Université Sorbonne Paris Nord, Bobigny, France.

The researchers also observed that diabetes risk generally increased with greater consumption of meat-derived Neu5Gc, particularly among those with the highest exposure.

“Although we do not know exactly what levels of Neu5Gc trigger an immune response, our data suggest that higher intakes of meat-derived Neu5Gc are associated with a progressively higher risk of T2D.”

Neu5Gc is produced naturally by most mammals, but humans lost the ability to make it following an ancient mutation in the CMAH gene. Despite this evolutionary change, the sugar can still enter the human body through food.

It occurs in red meat (particularly beef and lamb), with smaller amounts also found in dairy products.

After Neu5Gc is consumed, the body absorbs it and can incorporate it into human tissues. Because humans do not naturally produce the molecule, the immune system may recognize it as foreign. Scientists classify it as a xeno-autoantigen, a foreign substance that becomes incorporated into the body's own tissues and can provoke an immune reaction.

In response, the immune system produces antibodies against Neu5Gc. The resulting prolonged immune activity, called xenosialitis (xenosialitis), has previously been associated with cardiovascular disease and cancer.

Researchers suspect that this persistent inflammatory response could also contribute to type 2 diabetes. However, before this investigation, no large prospective study had examined whether dietary Neu5Gc might promote diabetes through chronic inflammation.

To investigate the possible connection, the scientists analyzed dietary and health information from 89,953 adults participating in NutriNet-Santé, a large French observational study that follows people over time to examine how nutrition and lifestyle influence health.

Participants were 42 years old on average, and 79% were women. None had type 2 diabetes or cancer when they entered the study.

Each person completed three 24-hour dietary records. The research team then used a validated food database to calculate how much Neu5Gc participants were likely consuming based on the measured amounts of the sugar in different foods.

The analysis included a broad range of red meats, such as beef, lamb, pork, rabbit, goat, and game. Processed meats (such as bacon, sausages and cured meats) were evaluated as a separate category, while dairy products were analyzed independently.

Using the dietary information, the researchers estimated total Neu5Gc intake from all reported foods. They then ranked participants separately by sex according to their intake of meat-derived Neu5Gc and divided them into five equally sized groups (quintiles).

Diabetes risk was compared across these groups, using those with the lowest intake as the reference.

During a median follow-up period of 7.2 years, researchers recorded 966 new cases of type 2 diabetes.

The results showed a significant relationship between meat-derived Neu5Gc consumption and the likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes. The association became particularly pronounced among participants with the highest estimated intake.

Compared with the lowest intake group, individuals in the highest group had a 63% greater risk of developing the disease. Those in the second-highest group had a 41% greater risk (men and women combined).

These estimates accounted for potentially influential factors, including age, sex, exercise habits, overall dietary quality, smoking, and existing medical conditions.

The researchers also examined how much meat participants typically consumed.

Among men in the highest Neu5Gc intake group, median red meat consumption was 97 grams daily (136 g including processed meat). For women, the corresponding amount was 73 grams (96 g).

By comparison, men in the lowest intake group consumed a median of just 5 grams of red meat per day (21 g), while women consumed 0 grams (9 g).

The relationship between Neu5Gc and diabetes was observed in both sexes, with no statistically significant evidence that it differed between men and women (see tables in notes to editors).

The findings were also consistent when differences in total calorie intake were considered.

Researchers noticed that the increase in diabetes risk was especially steep at higher levels of Neu5Gc exposure. One possible explanation is that the immune system begins to respond more strongly after enough of the foreign sugar has accumulated in body tissues, although this proposed threshold mechanism has not been confirmed.

Importantly, when the analysis additionally accounted for the total amount of red meat consumed, the relationship between Neu5Gc and diabetes weakened and was no longer statistically significant. The direction of the association remained the same, however.

This means the researchers cannot determine whether Neu5Gc itself (rather than other components of red meat) is responsible for the increased risk. The findings suggest it could contribute to the relationship, but they do not establish that the sugar acts independently of red meat consumption.

Another notable finding was that obesity appeared to explain only a relatively small portion of the association.

According to the researchers' estimates, obesity accounted for approximately 13% of the relationship between meat-derived Neu5Gc consumption and type 2 diabetes.

This suggests that factors unrelated to excess body weight may contribute substantially to the observed connection.

One possibility is that immune reactions to Neu5Gc produce chronic inflammation that interferes with insulin-producing cells or tissues that normally respond to insulin. Such effects could potentially increase the likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes even without substantial weight gain.

However, additional studies are necessary to establish whether these inflammatory processes actually explain the association.

Although Neu5Gc occurs in both red meat and dairy products, the researchers found an important difference between these dietary sources.

Higher consumption of Neu5Gc from dairy products was not associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes.

This finding may reflect the relatively small amounts of Neu5Gc in cow's milk compared with red meats such as beef and lamb. The typically smaller amounts of Neu5Gc consumed through dairy servings may also contribute to the difference.

When the scientists considered Neu5Gc intake from all dietary sources combined, participants in the highest intake group had a 37% greater risk of developing type 2 diabetes compared with those in the lowest group.

That association appeared to be driven primarily by Neu5Gc obtained from red meat.

“Together, these findings suggest that the association between Neu5Gc and T2D may depend on its dietary source, with meat-derived Neu5Gc showing a stronger association with T2D risk than total dietary Neu5Gc,” said Dr. Fezeu. “There may be differences between sources, or other components of red meat may contribute.”

The findings raise the possibility that reducing dietary exposure to meat-derived Neu5Gc could eventually become one approach to lowering diabetes risk.

However, researchers emphasize that studies are still needed to determine whether replacing foods rich in Neu5Gc with other protein sources would actually produce measurable health benefits.

“Given how common red meat is in Western diets, the strong association between meat-derived Neu5Gc and T2D risk is an important finding. Future research should explore whether reducing exposure to meat-derived Neu5Gc, for example by favoring poultry or plant-based proteins over beef and lamb, which have the highest Neu5Gc content, could help prevent T2D. Studies directly measuring anti-Neu5Gc antibodies in large cohorts would also help to directly test the xenosialitis mechanism in humans.”

The research team plans to investigate how Neu5Gc interacts with other substances found in red meat, including heme iron (a form of dietary iron), and how these components may collectively relate to diabetes risk.

They also intend to measure antibodies against Neu5Gc directly. Doing so could help determine whether the proposed immune response is an important biological mechanism connecting red meat consumption with type 2 diabetes.

The researchers caution that their findings are preliminary and have not yet been validated. The reported estimates could change once the final analysis is completed.

Because the study was observational, it cannot establish that Neu5Gc causes type 2 diabetes or identify precisely which factors are responsible for the higher risk.

Although the analysis accounted for several established diabetes risk factors, unmeasured lifestyle and social influences could still have affected the results.

There were also limitations in how dietary exposure was assessed. Neu5Gc consumption was estimated using participants' food records rather than measured directly, and the researchers did not account for possible dietary changes during the follow-up period. Both factors could have introduced errors into the exposure estimates.

The findings therefore offer a potential new explanation for the established connection between red meat consumption and diabetes, but further investigation is needed to determine whether Neu5Gc directly contributes to the disease.

#3753. Scientists discover why some people still can’t taste food after COVID-19

Scientists have uncovered changes inside taste buds that may help explain why some people struggle to taste food more than a year after recovering from COVID-19. The findings point to disruptions in the molecular signals responsible for detecting certain flavors, along with physical abnormalities in the structure of taste buds.

The study, published in Chemical Senses, offers the first direct biological evidence connecting persistent taste problems reported by patients to measurable changes within their taste cells.

Why Does Taste Loss Linger After COVID-19?

To investigate the lasting effects of COVID-19 on taste, scientists from the University of Colorado Anschutz and two Swedish universities examined 28 people who had experienced taste disturbances for more than a year following infection. None of the participants had been hospitalized for COVID-19.

Testing revealed that eight of the 28 participants had clearly abnormal taste scores. Eleven reported difficulty detecting specific flavors, particularly sweet, bitter, and umami, the savory taste associated with foods such as mushrooms and broth. In contrast, their ability to detect salty and sour flavors remained largely unaffected.

Seeking an explanation for this unusual pattern, the researchers collected small tissue samples from the taste buds of 20 participants and examined them for biological abnormalities.

Scientists Identify a Molecular Defect in Taste Cells

The research team, organized by Göran Hellekant, PhD, of the University of Wisconsin and the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, identified reduced amounts of messenger RNA (mRNA) involved in producing a protein known as PLCβ2.

This protein plays an essential role in how taste receptor cells communicate with the brain. Specifically, it amplifies the signals generated when the tongue detects sweet, bitter, and umami flavors. Without sufficient levels of the protein, those signals may become too weak to register normally.

"PLCβ2 acts like a molecular amplifier inside taste cells," said Thomas Finger, PhD, professor of cell and developmental biology at the University of Colorado Anschutz and corresponding author of the study. "It strengthens the signal before it's transmitted to the brain. When levels are reduced, the taste signal weakens."

The discovery may also explain why some flavors are affected more than others. Cells responsible for detecting salty and sour tastes use different signaling mechanisms that do not depend on PLCβ2, potentially allowing those sensations to remain intact even when other taste signals are disrupted.

Hidden Structural Changes Inside Taste Buds

The researchers also discovered that the abnormalities were not limited to molecular signaling. When they examined taste bud tissue under a microscope, they found that some participants had changes in the physical arrangement of their taste cells.

"Some subjects had normal-looking taste buds, while others showed structural disorganization," Finger said. "This suggests that both molecular and architectural changes may contribute to persistent taste dysfunction."

Together, these findings suggest that lingering taste problems after COVID-19 may involve multiple biological disruptions, affecting both the way taste cells are organized and how effectively they transmit information.

Why Can COVID-19 Taste Loss Last More Than a Year?

One of the most intriguing aspects of persistent taste loss is how long it can continue despite the body's natural ability to replace taste cells.

Under normal circumstances, cells in the taste buds are renewed approximately every two to four weeks. Yet the researchers found signs that the molecular processes needed for normal taste signaling may remain disrupted for much longer in some people.

Most individuals who experience taste changes during COVID-19 regain their normal sense of taste within weeks or months. However, a small group continues to experience symptoms long after the infection has resolved. The new findings provide measurable biological evidence that could help explain why recovery takes so much longer for these patients.

"Our findings offer measurable evidence of long-term taste disruption in some post-COVID patients long after the virus has been cleared," the authors write.

Although the discovery sheds light on a previously unexplained symptom, important questions remain. Scientists still need to determine whether these molecular abnormalities can eventually return to normal and whether treatments designed to restore healthy taste signaling could help people recover their sense of taste.
