On Oct. 17, 2016, Orbital ATK (now Northrop Grumman Space Systems) returned to flight, successfully launching a cargo shipment to the International Space Station (ISS) after its previous cargo mission failed to launch.

The private spaceflight company was contracted by NASA to fly cargo to the ISS, and stakes were high for this launch. Orbital ATK's last attempt nearly two years earlier ended in a catastrophic explosion a few seconds after liftoff.

Despite some delays, the newly upgraded Antares rocket flawlessly blasted off into the night sky from NASA's Wallops Flight Facility in Virginia. Six days later, the Cygnus cargo spacecraft arrived at the ISS to deliver food, supplies and science experiments to the crew.

For the International Space Station to be able to sustain a continuous human occupancy, periodic cargo deliveries are needed. Russia fulfills part of this requirement with its Progress spacecraft, and both ESA (European Space Agency) and JAXA (Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency) have contributed with their Automated Transfer Vehicle (ATV) and H-II Transfer Vehicle (HTV), respectively.For its part, NASA contracted with Orbital (today, Northrop Grumman) and SpaceX to fly commercial resupply service missions. Both were needed to keep the U.S. operating segment of the station well supplied and with the science and research materials needed for the crew's work.

Though adjustments can be made to go without one or more of these vehicles for a short period of time, the Progress, Cygnus and Dragon are key components of the ISS's continuous operation. So returning a vehicle to flight after a catastrophic exploration is important to well-being of the International Space Station program.

As it happened, Antares would fly another 12 times before having to stand down again to be redesigned, but that is for a different This Day in Space entry.

A temporary technical glitch in the XRP Ledger network has postponed the activation of the fixCleanup3_4_0 amendment package until November. Strict decentralized security algorithms automatically reset the two-week waiting timer because of a one-minute desynchronization of validating nodes.

The entire cycle has been restarted from scratch, and the amendment will now take effect on November 4 at 14:46 UTC.

The XRPL network is governed by 35 main servers (validators). Strict conditions apply to adopting any changes: at least 29 of the 35 validators must vote continuously for the update for 14 consecutive days, and the automatic vote check runs every 15 minutes.

The amendment package was on the home stretch, but during another routine check a brief glitch occurred: several servers momentarily lost connection, causing the number of votes to drop to 26. The nodes recovered immediately, and the project is now steadily approved by 31 of the 35 participants.

However, because of the temporary break in the chain, the timer was reset. Developers are taking this calmly: the strict filters guarantee that the network would rather wait than accept code amid an unstable connection.

The update itself is not related to hacker attacks or breaches. This bug-fix amendment is a planned closure of theoretical vulnerabilities in the new protocols:

Issuance control: An account with a token-burning function (PaymentBurn) could, under rare conditions, bypass the code logic and launch the issuance of new assets. The loophole has been completely eliminated.

Foundation for DeFi and lending: The patch improves the rounding precision of numbers for deposits into future native vaults (Single Asset Vaults) and changes the logic of loan defaults: a delinquency is now recorded strictly after the deadline has passed.

Code optimization: Bugs in the operation of AMM (erroneous burning of LP tokens at a zero refund balance), NFTs, and escrow accounts have been fixed.

For ordinary XRP holders, the postponement changes nothing. Balances, transaction speed, and fees remain the same.

Institutional players also ignored the hiccup. Amid news that Franklin Templeton continues to buy up millions of XRP for its ETF clients, the token's price is steadily holding around $1.35. Large capital is satisfied with the uncompromising strictness of XRPL's automation.

TENERIFE, CANARY ISLANDS — The Artemis II astronauts may have traveled hundreds of thousands of miles away from Earth to the moon, but they made sure the rest of us didn't miss out on the adventure.

Now, their efforts to bring the wonders of space exploration back down to Earth have earned them one of science communication's most prestigious honors: the Stephen Hawking Medal.

NASA astronauts Reid Wiseman and Victor Glover, alongside Canadian Space Agency astronaut Jeremy Hansen, accepted the award on Saturday (Oct. 17), during the opening ceremony of the Starmus VIII festival. The honor recognizes all four crew members, including NASA astronaut Christina Koch, who wasn't in attendance.

For Wiseman, the experience of seeing our home planet from the vicinity of the moon carried a powerful message.

"If we could take each of you 250,000 miles away from this planet, 400,000 kilometers away from planet Earth, and you would see Earth the size of your thumb. It's tiny," Wiseman told the audience.

"When you're there looking through the moon, back at this tiny Earth, the only thing you feel is love, peace, hope, unity", he continued. "Those aren't the right words, but that is all you feel."

During their historic Artemis II mission, the astronauts did far more than put NASA's Orion spacecraft through its paces. Through live communications, interviews and glimpses into life aboard the spacecraft, not to mention the jaw-dropping photos that just keep on coming, they helped turn an extraordinarily complex mission into something people worldwide could experience and connect with.

And that's exactly what the Stephen Hawking Medal is designed to celebrate.

Established in 2016 by physicist Stephen Hawking and Starmus, the award honors those who have gone above and beyond to make science accessible, engaging and inspiring.

The Artemis II crew joins some pretty impressive company. Previous recipients include Apollo 11 moonwalker Buzz Aldrin, naturalist David Attenborough, primatologist Jane Goodall and astrophysicist Neil DeGrasse Tyson.

This year's ceremony marks the medal's 10th anniversary, with filmmaker Ridley Scott also among the 2026 honourees.

"It's a special thrill for us at Starmus to be able to honor the intrepid Artemis II crew members in the same way that we honored the Apollo astronauts who paved the way to the moon over 50 years ago," Starmus co-founder and Queen guitarist Brian May said in a statement ahead of the ceremony.

Wiseman left the audience with one final thought.

"When you come home to this beautiful planet, you realise that it's in every single one of us to choose to make tomorrow better," he said. "So let's all choose to make tomorrow better".

Starmus VIII runs from Oct. 17-22 across Tenerife and La Palma in the Canary Islands. This year's festival, themed "The Search for Truth", brings together leading scientists, astronauts, musicians and artists to celebrate scientific discovery and explore the importance of evidence and scientific thinking.

Counting macros—a.k.a. macronutrients, including carbohydrates, protein, and fats—can help paint a clear picture of what you’re putting in your body.

In fact, macro counting can beneficial for people with all kinds of goals: body recomp, weight loss, as well as athletes, bodybuilders, and those who thrive on structured eating plans, says Diala Alatassi, MD, a board-certified internal and obesity medicine physician at Endeavor Health Medical Group.

A huge perk of this eating method is that no foods are off-limits. It’s somewhat flexible compared to other restrictive diets like keto and certain intermittent fasting protocols, says registered dietitian and diabetes educator Jessica Crandall Snyder, RDN.

Repeat after me: Carbs are not the enemy—even if you're trying to lose weight. “Carbohydrates are the most important energy source for almost all human cells,” says Mascha Davis, RDN, a spokesperson for the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics.

"This stored fuel powers high-intensity exercise, which is key for building lean muscle and revving up your metabolism," she adds.

Carbs also support brain function and concentration, and they help regulate your mood by boosting levels of serotonin, a feel-good neurotransmitter, Barmmer says.

Everyone’s macro goals vary slightly, but for general health, Dr. Alatassi recommends that adults get 45 to 65 percent of their calories from carbs, 25 to 35 percent from protein, and 20 to 35 percent from fat.

When counting macros for weight loss, prioritizing protein is often a key strategy supported by research, says Dr. Alatassi. "Higher protein levels help preserve muscle mass, enhance metabolism, and improve satiety, making it easier to maintain a calorie deficit," she says, adding that you may choose to aim for the upper range of 35 percent protein, or even slightly higher, up to 40 percent. You'd then adjust your carbs and fats accordingly to stay within your target calorie range.

That said, personal preferences, dietary restrictions, goals, lifestyle, activity level, and existing health conditions can influence your ideal macronutrient balance, so consulting with a doctor or registered dietitian can help you nail down your exact numbers, Dr. Alatassi says.

As you’ve probably already guessed, counting macros involves a good amount of planning and math. “It’s a bit cumbersome to count macros unless you eat the same thing every day,” Angelone says. “But then your diet becomes monotonous and probably not nutritionally adequate.” Still, counting macros gets easier once you get the hang of it, she says.

Setting macro goals too low, for example, might cause energy fatigue, brain fog, and nutrient deficiency, while setting them too high might not give you the health benefits you’re looking for (weight loss, improved workout performance, etc.), says Barmmer.

Counting macros can become rigid or even obsessive when taken to extremes, disconnecting you from your natural hunger cues, Barmmer says, so you'll want to keep an eye out for that along the way. It also might not be beneficial for anyone who finds meticulous tracking stressful or anxiety-inducing, Dr. Alatassi adds.

“For these individuals, focusing on intuitive eating, portion control, or seeking guidance from a nutrition professional might be more beneficial and sustainable,” she adds. Remember: Your macro goals should act as guidelines, but you shouldn't feel obligated to hit them Every. Single. Day.

If you want to test it out before upending your current eating plan, Angelone recommends keeping a food diary to get an idea of what you eat on a regular basis. You can also try incorporating macro-counting in spurts, starting for a week, Snyder says. That way, you’ll "have a really good idea of the kind of foods you’re typically eating and where you need to adjust, and that may give you some really good insights," Snyder says.

Once you calculate and track your macros (per the instructions above), look at your food choices to see what changes you need to make based on those numbers. “Be sure to choose less processed foods, choose whole grains and high-fiber foods, and healthy fats, limit refined sugar, and drink plenty of water,” Angelone says.

While Snyder doesn’t recommend tracking macros forever, checking in with yourself once in a while can be helpful to make sure you’re generally still eating your proper ratio. But if you're looking to count macros for a longer period of time and have some serious weight loss or performance goals you're looking to achieve, consult a dietitian to personalize your plan, says Snyder.

MoonPay and South Korean exchange operator Dunamu are examining how their infrastructure could support cross-border payments and corporate finance using digital assets.

The companies outlined the collaboration in a MoonPay-issued announcement distributed late Tuesday in New York, at midnight entering October 21 in London.

Three areas are on the agenda: stablecoin-based payments and fund transfers, corporate finance and treasury operations, and international expansion of Dunamu’s GIWA blockchain ecosystem.

The proposed work combines MoonPay’s financial-service network with Dunamu’s technology and business capabilities. It includes assessing commercial opportunities and ways to broaden access to GIWA-based services and assets.

The wording is deliberately preliminary. The release does not announce a jointly launched payment product, a settlement-volume commitment or a date when corporate customers can begin using a new service.

That matters because the business problem spans more than transferring a token. A company making an international payment needs to know who receives it, which currency the recipient gets, what records its finance team can reconcile and what happens if a payment fails.

Treasury management adds another layer. Businesses must decide when balances are available, which counterparties hold them and how transactions fit into their existing controls. A working blockchain connection answers only some of those questions.

MoonPay and Dunamu’s announcement identifies these corporate uses as areas to investigate. It does not show that the practical, commercial and regulatory arrangements have all been resolved.

GIWA’s overseas reach is a separate part of the proposed collaboration. Access to a distribution network can make services easier to reach, but it should not be confused with evidence that those services have already attracted customers in new markets.

For now, the announcement establishes the scope of the relationship. The next substantive step would be a named product or deployment with defined users, availability and operating terms.

Bitwise is pressing the Securities and Exchange Commission to answer a question that matters to fund managers: when can a firm rely on somebody else’s determination that a crypto asset has separated from an investment contract?

The asset manager’s October 21 supplemental letter focuses on that reliance problem in the SEC’s proposed Regulation Crypto Assets framework.

It asks the commission to distinguish an issuer’s filing of Form TR from the point at which a separate market participant can rely on an unchallenged filing. It also seeks protection for reasonable, good-faith determinations if a later challenge arises, and asks for the treatment to carry through relevant investment-company and investment-adviser definitions.

Bitwise’s original October 19 response explains its position as a company that builds investment products using crypto assets rather than issuing those assets itself. Whether an asset could be treated as a security affects decisions about which products can hold it.

The practical concern is that a manager may need to make those decisions using public information, without access to all the records held by an issuer or development team.

A reliance framework would define when public filings can support that decision and what knowledge would prevent a firm from relying on them. That is distinct from declaring that any particular token is outside securities law.

The supplement also argues that filing Form TR should not itself be treated as an admission about the asset’s previous legal status. Its requests concern the consequences of using the proposed process, not simply the form’s availability.

The document is hosted in the SEC’s comment file, but its arguments belong to Bitwise. Publication there is not an endorsement by the commission.

The distinction is especially relevant to headlines about regulatory certainty. An industry participant can identify a problem and propose a detailed solution while the outcome remains undecided.

For fund managers, the issue to watch in the eventual rules is how responsibility is divided between the party making a token-status filing and the firms using it to construct financial products. Bitwise’s latest submission makes that division the center of its case.

Tezos’s co-founder Arthur Breitman has been blunt about the stakes. Previously, he has called Bitcoiners’ quantum theories “half-baked crank theories about quantum mechanics.” However, in a recent conversation with AMBCrypto, he clarified,

The crank theories represent a small group of people, though there’s a broader group that sees no urgency, or believes Bitcoin can handle post quantum without any soft or hard fork.

What exacerbated the debate on crypto and quantum computers? Well, in March 2026, Google published a research paper that compressed the timeline of the quantum threat from a “2040s problem” to a preparation window for “2030–2035.”

The paper cut the estimated physical qubits needed to break Bitcoin’s elliptic curve cryptography by roughly 20x — From millions to fewer than 500,000.

That paper triggered a scramble in the industry. What was even more notable was the “9-minute problem.” For the uninitiated, public keys are exposed in Bitcoin’s mempool while the network confirms it.

Google’s paper also claimed a powerful enough quantum computer could potentially extract a key within approximately 9 minutes under specific assumptions. And Bitcoin’s average block time is 10 minutes.

Google puts the odds of a successful hijack at 41% — not a guarantee, but much too close for comfort.

However, Maksym Sakharov, CEO & Co-Founder of WeFi, told AMBCrypto,

I would be careful about treating the 41% figure as a prediction of what will happen in practice.

Google’s paper models a specific attack scenario under a defined set of assumptions, including a sufficiently capable fault-tolerant quantum computer, a public key becoming available almost immediately, and conditions that can favor either the attacker or the legitimate user depending on network behavior.

According to Google’s paper, the attack doesn’t need to crack your cold wallet or steal your seed phrase. It just needs to catch your transaction in that brief window when your key is visible.

Sakharov, talking about the long-term, hypothesized,

Preparation matters more than prediction. The objective should be to ensure that migration paths, governance, and upgrade mechanisms are in place well before advances in quantum computing become an operational concern.

For Bitcoin, however, the problem can get even more complicated. Glassnode’s May 2026 research found that over 6 million Bitcoin, or roughly 30% of BTC’s current supply, sits in addresses where the public key is already visible on-chain. That’s roughly $500–600 billion ripe for the taking.

And yet, the contrast between the exchanges is striking. About 1.66 million BTC held on exchanges falls into the exposed category — roughly 40% of all operationally exposed Bitcoin. Coinbase has 5% exposure; Fidelity at 2%.

Binance sits at 85%. Bitfinex, Robinhood, and several others are at or near 100%.

Christopher Smith, co-founder and CEO of Quantus, elaborated on Bitcoin’s exposure problem in conversation with AMBCrypto.

It’s a solvable technical problem, but the social coordination is the difficult part. For various reasons, Bitcoin has cultivated a culture of not changing, but it really is a cultural issue more than a technical issue. If we had a post-quantum address type, then most big addresses could migrate within a few months.

The bigger issues are that post-quantum addresses could take years to ship, and nobody has a politically viable solution for Satoshi’s coins, which are currently an $80B quantum canary.

Smith’s argument highlights one of Bitcoin’s unresolved questions. The harder decisions will involve coins that will never migrate, including Bitcoin founder Satoshi’s 1.1 million stash.

Coinbase’s Quantum Advisory Council understands these concerns as well and has thus outlined three options: freeze, burn, or do nothing and take the loss. However, each option here clashes with Bitcoin’s core principles in some way.

For Bitcoin’s network to become quantum-proof, starting today means completion on around 2033. This would uncomfortably overlap with Google’s 2030–2035 preparation window.

One must also remember that Bitcoin’s governance is notoriously slow. It’s an open secret. SegWit took over eight years from conception to adoption, and Taproot underwent the same journey in nearly seven years.

For casual observers on the scene, Bitcoin’s BIP-360 may seem like the solution. It is the network’s quantum-resistance proposal that introduces a new address type called P2MR (Pay-to-Merkle-Root), removes Taproot’s key-path spending, and uses hash-based commitments.

However, be warned, BIP-360 is not quantum-proof. In fact, it does not answer the Bitcoin network’s concerns. As Christopher Smith told AMBCrypto,

The quantum safety of a BIP360 address is the same as a P2PKH address. To make a fully post-quantum address type, we need post-quantum op codes, which requires a decision on which post-quantum algorithm to support, and there is no official process for this.

In a nutshell, BIP-360 has critical limits that cannot be ignored.

First of all, it doesn’t replace Bitcoin’s cryptography as ECDSA and Schnorr signatures remain in use. P2MR reduces exposure by forcing spending through script paths, but it’s merely a risk-reduction measure.

Also, BIP-360 will not migrate existing coins automatically. There is a 2-step process here. To begin with, every wallet, exchange, and custodian needs to support the new quantum-proof addresses. Next, users will have to voluntarily move funds. That coordination hasn’t started yet, and there’s no timeline as to when it will.

Offer hash-based signatures now. The chain can soft-fork very quickly in an emergency, I believe it will, and that’s a good thing. But users can be very slow moving, and they need to be given ample time, several years, to migrate.

Altcoin networks have already started their push towards making crypto future-ready, in a sense. The most notable example is the Tezos network, whose post-quantum previewnet went live on 2nd September 2026, with  tz5 accounts using ML-DSA-44 signatures.

The network also supports STARK-based consensus aggregation, a ZODA data availability layer, and TzEL private payments on testnet, making it the most comprehensive quantum-resistant network out of any major chain.

Starks have come a long way in terms of proving speed and ease of deployment, and they pair very nicely with hash-based signatures.

As far as the rest of the market is concerned, Native Falcon-1024 accounts have been live on Algorand’s mainnet since v5.0.0 in August 2026. Since then, the network has processed over 500,000+ post-quantum-signed transactions. Right now, Falcon-1024 is a NIST Level 5 standard — the highest security category.

The Quantum Resistant Ledger, also dubbed to be the “original post-quantum blockchain,” has been running XMSS since June 2018. That’s eight years of quantum-resistant operations!

The list will be incomplete without mentioning Ethereum since it has released a roadmap targeting full quantum protection by 2029. Account abstraction, also dubbed EIP-8141, will let users switch to quantum-resistant signatures before the protocol-wide migration begins.

Today, a network being ‘quantum-ready’ is more than just a talking point. For traders, the differentiator is quite simple — which chains look ready, and which are still dragging their feet?

Currently, only Tezos, Algorand, and QRL have real implementations. Though it is a start, it is concerning, as none of the major altcoin networks, with the exception of Ethereum, even have a concrete roadmap.

Bitcoin does not even have its head in the game.

The window between “we have time” and “we’re out of time” is narrowing. If the transition to post-quantum cryptography arrives sooner, the networks that began preparing early will be the victors.

The strongest ecosystems are already thinking about cryptographic migration as part of their long-term roadmap rather than as a future emergency. Good financial infrastructure is designed around the assumption that today’s strongest cryptography will eventually be replaced by something stronger.

For now, the market is watching, and the chains already in the game have a head start.

For individual users, though, the takeaway is simpler: DYOR. As Sakharov put it,

Individual users cannot realistically prepare for advances in cryptography on their own. Their long-term protection will depend far more on whether the infrastructure they rely on is designed to adapt as security standards evolve.
