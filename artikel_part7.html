#6098. Glee Is Getting a Reboot Series 11 Years Later: Everything We Know So Far

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Glee has always held a special place in Ryan Murphy’s heart, so when he heard the show was having a major resurgence on social media, it planted the seeds for a reboot in his mind.

“That show is interesting because it’s come back around,” he said in a July interview with People. “So many young people now watch it. And I’m like, ‘Maybe we should revisit that show.’ My nostalgia and love for that show is enough that I’m like, ‘Well, maybe people would like to see a new incarnation of that in some capacity.’ So yeah, I love that show, and I loved what it became and what it meant to so many people.”

Months later, Murphy delivered on his comments—on October 21, Disney+ and Hulu granted a series order for Glee, 17 years after the original show’s premiere and 11 years after the final episode. The series also announced the news on its official Instagram, writing, “It’s official. Glee is returning.”

Details about the reboot are currently sparse, but here’s everything we know so far.

Yes. In September, Murphy told Entertainment Tonight that there has been progress with writing the show. “We have a script. Brad [Falchuk], Ian [Brennan], and I, who wrote all the first two seasons, have written something,” he said.

The original cast of Glee included Lea Michele, Kevin McHale, Chris Colfer, Darren Criss, Jane Lynch, Amber Riley, Jenna Ushkowitz, Dianna Agron, Heather Morris, Matthew Morrison, Mark Salling, Jonathan Groff, Chord Overstreet, and Harry Shum Jr. Cory Monteith and Naya Rivera were also part of the original cast, but died in 2013 and 2020, respectively.

While Murphy did not state which stars he has been talking to, he told Entertainment Tonight that he’s “been in touch with around 10 of the original cast members. It’s very sweet, and they all had a lot of questions, but they all were very enthusiastic about the idea.”

Hours after the reboot news was revealed, Variety reported that Michele will reportedly join the cast of the reboot. “She’s in,” a source told the outlet, while a second source added, “It’s still early-ish days, but it feels good and we’re happy to get the band back together.” Murphy and Michele did not provide a comment.

While the post feels like a confirmation, Murphy has not confirmed any of the cast members who will return for the show.

Overstreet and McHale also hinted that they’d be returning. Overstreet wrote, “reunited and fit feels so good!” after the reboot news dropped. McHale reacted to a tweet that said Drake has surpassed the Glee cast with the most Billboard Hot 100 chart entries. “Challenge accepted,” he replied to the tweet.

“If Ryan was doing it and the right people were involved,” she told the outlet at the time. “I would consider it.” She also dispelled rumors of drama between members of the cast, adding, “We’re a family—families don’t always have to get along. #6097. The 6 Best TVs to Buy in 2026

Every product was carefully curated by an Esquire editor. We may earn a commission from these links. Here’s how we test products and why you should trust us.

After a year’s worth of releases, here’s what impressed us the most.

The TV is the centerpiece of the living room—has been for decades at this point—but I’ll be damned if I ever want to think about buying a new one. It’s just so complicated, even for those like me who speak the language of tech. If you’re not tuned in to the most minute innovations, five years go by but it feels like a lifetime. There are the model numbers that nobody can follow and seemingly meaningless choices between QLED, Mini LED, and SQD-Mini LED. There are identical smart interfaces and feature sets across the board. It’s basically impossible for a normal person to distinguish what’s worth their money. When genuine innovation gets paired with brand-specific marketing, finding the difference between what you want and what you don’t need can feel overwhelming. Thankfully for you, it’s my job to parse it.

Deep down, what most people want is a TV that looks good and sounds good out of the box; one that will stream from all the apps they like and connect to the game consoles and devices they use. That’s something every single one of these TVs delivers. Since I can’t test them all, it’s up to Esquire’s team of testers and editors to help out. Below, I’ve highlighted some of the best options from the brands my colleagues and I have loved over the past couple years of trial and error.

From $500 all the way up to more than $8,000, these are the best TVs at every price range.

Esquire recently called this the best TV of 2026 in our gadget awards, so saying anything else here would be ludicrous. Thankfully, I agree with our tester. This TV owns. With a dynamic, bright SQD-Mini LED display, and audio from Bang & Olufsen, there is nothing left to be desired—not even a soundbar.

The SQ stands for “super quantum,” which is about the dots used to create the image. Science aside, what we can say certainly is this TV blows the other TCLs we’ve tried out of the water in terms of color, contrast, and brightness. For gaming, you’re set with a 144 hertz native refresh rate and a gaming mode that turns on when it detects you have a console connected. PS5, Xbox, or Switch, the TV doesn’t discriminate—you’re getting awesome graphics and smooth performance either way.

Anyone who watches sports (or plays video games) knows the pains of a TV that can’t handle the daylight. OLED TVs have always, for me, felt like the easiest solution to glare. The viewing angles still can’t hold up, but the latest Hisense MiniLED gives that assessment the first real run for its money I’ve seen. Our tester sat this right next to a floor-to-ceiling window in a room with a skylight. No matter the time of day, the screen was clear as can be, and ultra-vibrant in color.

The support for gaming is great too, working as seamlessly as the TCL. Technically, the 165 hertz refresh rate beats out the other TV, but to most players, the difference won’t be noticeable.

When you get to real budget territory, TCL beats out Hisense by just a hair. For under $600, a 55-inch TCL Google TV is the best way to spend your money. If somehow, you’ve never watched a movie at home or played a video game in 4K, we can say with certainly this TV is worth every single penny.

From app streaming for sports and TV to console gaming, the whole experience is super user-friendly, and reliable. I can’t say the same for every manufacturer and OS (looking at you FireTV!). Sure, the contrast won’t be as great as the new TCL, the viewing angles won’t be as numerous as with the Sony OLED, and the sound can’t compete with Bang & Olufsen. But, ask yourself, does that even matter?

When you need a TV that decorates your wall like a work of art, The Frame Pro is everything. Instead of plugging your router, consoles, and Blu-ray player to the TV itself, you can hide all that away in a corner with the Connect box. Truly, it’s still one of Samsung’s biggest game-changers.

Sony’s newest Bravia TV is probably the best entry-level OLED to ever exist. At $2,000 (and that’s before the typical discounts), this 65-inch plays in the same ballpark as the excellent TCL and Hisense TVs I’ve highlighted here. Only, for real display purists, nothing quite looks as dynamic and colorful as an OLED screen. And hear it too, in full detail thanks to the bass reflex speakers in the TV.

With the regular priced TVs sorted, let’s talk about the crazy luxury stuff. No one is doing luxury TV like Bang & Olufsen at the moment. The Danish brand has deep roots in electronics but has settled into the niche of sound products. Soundbars, headphones, speakers: B&O is best in class in all of them. A recent partnership with LG has launched the brand back to the top of the television world.

LG televisions (which, full disclosure, are narrowly behind Samsung in our estimation) are paired with built-in Bang & Olufsen audio. The sound is incredible, and LG’s OLED is as good as any is going to look (sans Sony’s QD-OLED). But the real draw is the look of the TV itself. These things are beautiful, and the use of organic materials makes them a real standout in the tech world. As a piece of decoration, it is the rival to art TVs.

And yes, the price point is insane, but the brand is part of a fascinating Cradle to Cradle certification. This isn’t a lifetime warranty, but it’s a way that the company is showing it cares about its customers. Essentially, every Bang & Olufsen product with this certification is future-proofed. This TV will last years—decades with proper care—but if new designs or tech innovations happen, the products are totally modular and allow new parts to replace the old. It’s not a fix to tech’s circularity crisis, but it’s the start of an interesting solution.

To help everyone understand it all better, here are a few very brief, very general need-to-know terms. What is QLED? What is OLED? What is 4k? What is the difference between any of these TVs? Well...

LCD: Liquid Crystal Display. The most common type of TV (which includes QLED). A backlight shines through a panel of Liquid Crystal making the colors more refined and clear. It’s pretty basic, but it’s 2025 and we can do better, so no LCD screens on this list.

QLED: Invented by Samsung, the Liquid Crystal panel of LCD is replaced with a quantum-dot panel. This innovation gives TVs brighter, more lifelike color images. For many years, competitors would call what they were working with QLEDs, but you may have noticed in recent years that has changed to Mini LED and ULED. There’s a reason for that.

Mini LED: Instead of using hundreds of tiny LED lights, these are made by using thousands of the little buggers. Compared with LCD and regular LED, this offers improved local dimming and black levels.

QD-Mini LED: This is a Mini LED with a quantum dot layer applied over it. It’s brighter than a Mini LED, while still taking advantage of the black levels that format provides. Therefore, the contrast winds up being pretty stellar on the ones we’ve seen. The new TCL TVs claim to take this to the next level with “super” quantum dots, but at the end of the day SQD-Mini LED is just another branding exercise.

OLED: A distinctly different class of TV. (Organic Light Emitting Diode) Every single pixel creates its own light. There is no liquid or quantum-dot panel, only a shit ton of little lights. The contrast on these TVs is better than what came before it. But the colors are not significantly nicer, and the price is a lot higher.

4K: This is a pixel resolution, with specific reference to the width of the TV. The images on 4K TVs are 3,840 pixels wide, which is why the image is clearer than one in 1080p.

When it comes to shopping for TVs, there are a lot of choices you have to make about size, resolution, display type, the list goes on. But before all that, it’s smart to choose a brand that aligns with your needs and values. These are a few things to consider when making that choice.

Budget: First thing’s first. What’s your budget? If you’re looking for a 4K TV in the five hundred to one thousand dollar range, TCL and Hisense Google TVs are your best bet. If the sky is the limit, go with Samsung or a Sony Bravia OLED.

Resolution: Most brands offer 4K TVs as the most common option. This is the new standard in HD, and the days of selling the 1080p TV are almost entirely behind us. But if you’re looking for something with a lower (or higher) resolution, you can still pick up a 32-inch Roku TV on the cheap. That sucker will only do full 1080p, but alternatively it’s currently only $130.

Smart Features: Almost all new 4K TVs you’ll buy will come equipped with their own set of smart features. So what we are talking about here is user interface or operating system. While TVs within a brand are sometimes differentiated on an OS level (see Roku vs Google TCLs), some of these brands like Samsung and LG have their own unique operating systems. Our advice? Look up some screenshots. Between LG’s webOS, Samsung’s One UI Tizen, and the Roku, Amazon, and Google offerings you have a lot of choices.

#6095. How to Use Custom Window Treatments to Pull a Room Together

Expert advice from the designers of the 2026 Whole Home.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Window treatments are like an accessory or piece of jewelry for the home—the finishing touch that brings a look together and makes it feel complete. Without them, a room can feel underdressed, like it’s missing something.

“They help set the mood, soften the architecture, and make a space feel finished in a way that’s hard to replicate with anything else,” says interior designer Maci McGee of Ellerslie Interiors, who put this philosophy into practice at the 2026 Whole Home in Charleston, South Carolina.

Here, McGee and Kistna Conely of House Seven Design share how they approached window treatments in the Whole Home, and offer expert advice for bringing their approach into your own space.

In the bedroom designed by Ellerslie Interiors, a velvet upholstered bed holds court below a cornflower blue ceiling, and antique furniture sits against a backdrop of mustard-hued wallpaper with a large check pattern. “We had a lot of richness happening,” says McGee, “so the window treatments needed to support that without making the room feel too heavy.”

She introduced an organic element by pairing the handwoven TWOPAGES Rae Natural Bamboo Woven Shades - Series S with patterned drapes. “The drapery brings softness and character, while the woven shade is much quieter,” McGee says. “It filters the light beautifully and adds texture without competing with everything else in the room.”

Layering window treatments solves practical challenges while adding depth to a room. For the bedroom by House Seven Design, privacy was a priority because the window faces a busy urban street. The designers also wanted to maintain flexibility regarding how much daylight entered throughout the day.

Conely’s solution was to pair TWOPAGES Liz Custom Relaxed Roman Shade in sand beige and the Rae Natural Pure Bamboo Woven Shades - Series Z in reddish brown with linen drapery. “That combination brought a beautiful layer of texture to the space and helped establish the soft, organic feeling we wanted,” says Conely.

Utilizing two shades plus drapes also offers options for managing light and privacy while making a window feel more finished. “You can let daylight filter through one layer and close the other when you want the room to feel more private,” she says.

In a home with multiple distinct spaces, window treatments help establish a visual connection without making every room look alike. The trick, McGee says, is to focus on the overall aesthetic rather than repeating the same treatment throughout.

“For us, cohesion is more about sensibility than repetition,” she says. “The fabrics can change, the treatment style can change, but there’s still a common thread. It feels collected rather than matched.”

Conely suggests carrying a color or texture from one room into another to connect adjoining spaces while preserving each room’s personality. These subtle relationships help a whole-home design feel cohesive without becoming overly coordinated. With TWOPAGES’ range of colors, fabrics, and finishes, homeowners can carry a consistent design thread from room to room while varying the treatments to suit each space.

One of the most common mistakes Conely sees is choosing window treatments that are too small for the space. “Drapery that is too short or doesn’t have enough width can make the whole room feel a little off,” she says.

Before selecting a fabric, consider where the drapery will be mounted, how far the panels should extend, and how much fullness they need. McGee also recommends thinking about how the treatments relate to the window and the room as a whole. And don't overlook function: Privacy, blackout needs, and filtering light should all factor into the decision.

If you’re aiming to achieve a more custom, high-end look with window treatments, it’s the small decisions that will have the biggest impact.

“Hanging drapery higher, giving it enough fullness, choosing a fabric with the right weight and hand, and making sure the proportions feel right all make a huge difference,” says McGee. Custom trim also offers another opportunity to add personality. “None of those choices are necessarily loud,” she says, “but together they’re what make a space feel elevated.”

#6079. At the Shanghai Grand Opera House, the Gravity-Defying Staircase Was Designed to Look Like a Hand Fan

Along the banks of the Huangpu River in Shanghai, a sweeping spiral staircase winds along the side of a white building, flowing upward toward the roof like a puff of smoke hanging in the air. But journey below the gyratory geometry, and a new image appears: The steps and railing become the canvas and sticks of a hand fan unfurled in the wind. This is the recently unveiled Shanghai Grand Opera House, where the site’s sinuous pièce de résistance can be enjoyed well before the curtain rises.

“Our initial thought wasn’t about what the building should look like, but rather: How can an important cultural institution be integrated into everyday public life in one of the world’s most vibrant cities?” says Kjetil Trædal Thorsen, founding partner of Snøhetta, the firm behind the project. “We wanted to explore how to transform it into a public landscape—a place where people can stop, experience, and enjoy themselves, whether they are watching a performance or not.”

The Shanghai Grand Opera House is located on the banks of the Huangpu River, with a total construction area of ​​approximately 1.6 million square feet. It officially opened to the public on October 17, 2026.

The Grand Opera House’s overall architecture responds to the rhythm of the Huangpu River’s tides with a fluid and dynamic form. The continuously rising spiral roof integrates diverse functions such as dining spaces, exhibition spaces, education centers, and the theater into a cohesive whole.

Overlooking the Shanghai Grand Opera House, this double-track grand staircase, where one can sit and stroll, has become the stage and theater for the entire city of Shanghai.

A decade in the making, the completed project is one of the most significant in contemporary architecture. Spanning roughly 1.6 million square feet, the building encompasses three auditoriums that are equipped for diverse musical performances, including traditional opera, classical music, and even experimental productions. In addition to the venues, the building includes rehearsal, production, education, exhibition, and hospitality spaces, creating an all-encompassing cultural experience.

The double helix staircase is the core and main body of the entire building, with independent two-way circulation routes that cleverly separate the flow of people. From below, it resembles a hand fan.

When conceiving the project, Snøhetta sought inspiration from the building’s site. Located in the Expo Back Beach area, the adjacent Huangpu River with its flowing stream, passing ships, and winding bends prompted the building’s most important design concept: movement. “While the Shanghai Grand Opera House certainly has distinctive visual characteristics, its form stems from considerations of circulation, participation, and public accessibility. The building’s geometry is closely linked to how people enter, ascend, explore, and experience the space,” Kjetil says.

Take the striking steps. While they’re the most visually captivating aspect of the design, they’re also rooted in function, allowing patrons and passersby alike to ascend them and experience the structure in new ways. Once you reach the roof, the views of the Puxi skyline and the river further connect visitors to the environment.

This idea continues throughout the rest of the complex, but as Kjetil explains, “the experience unfolds gradually.” Inside, large glass-curtain walls bring natural light into the sweeping lobby; as patrons move closer to the theaters, the design becomes more intimate, while the colors and textures become increasingly rich. “People move between open public landscapes and more intimate indoor environments; natural light changes with the alternation of day and night and the changing seasons,” he adds.

The indoor plaza of the Shanghai Grand Opera House comprises three theaters with a total seating capacity of 4,200.

Each of the three theaters also possesses a distinct spatial character. The Red Hall, with 2,000 seats, is the heart of the building. A deep burgundy color envelops the audience seating, and its compact five-tiered layout minimizes the distance between the audience and the stage. The meticulously designed balconies, boxes, and walls not only create a visual rhythm but also serve to reflect and propagate sound, turning the theater into its own expertly tuned instrument.

The main theater, the Red Hall, has 2,000 seats and is the core performance space of the entire building. The seating is divided into five levels, including floor seating, orchestra seating, and the three-tier balcony seating.

Shen Hall, with 1,200 seats, draws inspiration from the spatial form of classical opera houses and can accommodate various performance types, including Baroque opera, classical opera, local operas, and dramas. The Hall of Mirrors, with 1,000 seats, offers greater flexibility; the stage and seating can be adjusted to meet different production needs, creating more possibilities for experimental performances. The Hall of Mirrors also connects to the external landscape through a large glass-curtain wall, allowing light and the scenery beyond to become part of the performance.

Shen Hall has 1,200 seats, providing a more immersive viewing environment for smaller productions.

But great design isn’t just about what’s on the surface. At the Shanghai Grand Opera House, a network of interconnected tunnels and paths support behind-the-scenes movement necessary for a building program such as this. Audiences, actors, orchestras, stage crew, and backstage staff each have their own distinct movement paths, and multiple theaters must be able to operate simultaneously without interfering with one another. For example, the symphony orchestra’s rehearsal space and orchestra pit are located on corresponding underground floors, allowing for convenient horizontal transport of large instruments.

The Hall of Mirrors uses the outdoor landscape as a backdrop for the stage. It features 1,000 seats and a flexible stage and seating layout. More like an open art laboratory, it allows for diverse performance possibilities.

As noted, the Shanghai Grand Opera House is more than just a performance venue; it’s also meant to be a touchpoint for anyone in the city, audience member or not. “For us, ‘publicness’ is not just an ideological vision; it must be translated into a spatial reality,” Kjetil says. “People can connect with this space in many ways: watch performances, stroll on the rooftop, meet friends, visit exhibitions, admire the Huangpu River view, and even relive past memories here.”

The Shanghai Grand Opera House uses prefabricated components and partial cast-in-place technology to create the striking curved stairs.

In an era where architectural images spread rapidly through social media, this building, resembling a white folding fan, undoubtedly has the potential to become a popular landmark. But Kjetil is more concerned with whether people will want to return. “Photographs can spark curiosity, but truly meaningful architecture evokes emotional attachment,” he says. “If I return here in 50 years, I hope to see this building still deeply integrated into the daily life of Shanghai; in people’s eyes, it will no longer be just a ‘new landmark,’ but a familiar part of the city.”

#6076. What to Know About Cotchford Farm, the English Country House Behind Winnie-the-Pooh

The beloved children’s classic turns 100 in 2026.

This year officially marks a century since Winnie the Pooh, Piglet, Eeyore, and the rest of the beloved crew from the Hundred Acre Wood captured the public imagination. Dreamed up by English author A.A. Milne to amuse his young son Christopher Robin, the original Winnie-the-Pooh book was published in 1926 and spawned countless books, TV shows, movies, stories, and paraphernalia that continue to delight children—and adults—around the world. By some reports, more than 50 million copies of the books have been sold over the past 100 years.

Lesser known is that the fictional enchanted forest that the characters inhabit is actually based on a real-life place: Cotchford Farm, the landmark estate where Milne and his family would relax and unwind away from their London home base. The bucolic compound even had a famous (or infamous) life after the Milnes moved out. Brian Jones, founder of legendary rock band The Rolling Stones, also once called it home in the 1960s.

Read on for more details about the property—including how you can even spend the night.

Cotchford Farm is a Grade II-listed estate dating to the 17th century, per Historic England. The timber-framed residence follows an L-shaped plan and has three stories, with original Tudor beams still preserved throughout the interiors. The house is located in the southern English village of Hartfield, surrounded by Ashdown Forest in East Sussex, England. It’s a roughly 1.5-hour drive from London.

The six-bedroom home spans roughly 3,400 square feet and features many of the details from Milne’s time as owner, such as inglenook fireplaces, leaded-glass windows, and wide-plank oak flooring. It sits on roughly two acres that include a heated pool, manicured gardens, tennis court, and wildflower meadows.

Milne bought Cotchford Farm as a country home in 1925, when he was already an established poet, screenwriter, and novelist. It’s set on one of England’s most awe-inspiring landscapes, which captivated Milne’s young son, Christopher Robin. Inspired by the enchanting environment, Milne wrote the first Winnie-the-Pooh book at Cotchford Farm, plus all of the subsequent stories about the characters.

As Christopher Robin once lovingly recalled: “Cotchford was different. Cotchford was ours, and on an autumn morning we drove down to take possession. No, I have got it wrong. It was Cotchford that took possession of us.”

Among the elements still intact are Christopher Robin’s former bedroom as well as Milne’s original study, where he was known to write. Milne retired in 1952, following a stroke, and spent the remainder of his life at Cotchford Farm, where he passed away in 1956 at age 74.

After Milne’s time as owner, an American couple purchased Cotchford Farm and installed a pool. They then sold the estate to Brian Jones, one of the founders of rock ’n’ roll group The Rolling Stones. Jones drowned in the pool in the summer of 1969, when he was just 27.

In July, the historic estate hit the market as a rental property for roughly $13,470 per month, with short-term stays also permitted. It will cease those offerings and return to being a private residence in February 2027. A limited number of bookings are available on the official website.

Carbonylation is an important process used to produce aldehydes, carboxylic acids and esters1,2,3,4,5 that is usually catalysed by homogeneous metal complexes with ligands and iodide promoters6,7,8,9,10,11,12. Koch has reported that protons of mineral acids can also catalyse carbonylation13. Previous studies have demonstrated that Brønsted acidic zeolites can act as a heterogeneous, Koch-type catalyst for carbonylation of alcohols and olefins, but the products in this case are limited to tertiary carboxylic acids14,15,16,17,18. Here we show that targeted use of spatial confinement in a zeolite together with a high CO/olefin ratio can help break this limitation. We report that, with FER zeolite and a CO/olefin ratio of 360, olefin carbonylation can produce primary and secondary carboxylic acids as the main products; ethylene carbonylation to propanoic acid (PA) is achieved with 80% selectivity over 100 h and propylene carbonylation to isobutyric acid (IBA) is achieved with 95% selectivity over 750 h. The propylene and ethylene carbonylation selectivities decrease to 61% and 57%, respectively, as the CO/olefin ratio decreases to 24 and 90, respectively. Experiments in combination with theoretical calculations suggest that T2-O5 in eight-membered ring (8-MR) cavities of FER zeolite are the primary active sites for ethylene carbonylation and T3-O1 in ten-membered rings (10-MRs) are the primary active sites for propylene carbonylation. Our findings open new directions for olefin carbonylation and provide critical insights into the prerequisites for developing new zeolite-based carbonylation processes.

#6071. You Can Make Your Stairs Safer Without Sacrificing Style. Here Are 4 Tips Designers Always Recommend.

Form and function can share the same staircase.

Pictured above: In this historic Pittsburgh home, designer Betsy Wentz installed a custom ombré stair runner.

A well-designed stairwell is more than just a pretty sight; it should be just as safe and dependable as it is beautiful.

You might not think twice about going up and down the stairs when your eyes and joints are in their prime. However, it's important to remember that aging adults, young children, and small pets are extra susceptible to falling down a hazardous set of stairs.

We asked a handful of designers to share a few stylish additions they recommend for any staircase to improve safety.

Terri Fiori is the founder of Fiori + Co. and a Certified Aging in Place (CAPS) specialist. When designing stairs in any home, one of the first changes she recommends is adding additional lighting. The reason is that as your eyes age, your retinas receive less light, which can make navigating dimly lit stairs a challenge.

But you don't need to opt for bright and harsh overhead lighting. In a recent renovation project, Fiori added recessed lighting to the stairwell and paired it with touch-activated LED lighting under the handrails. The end result ended up enhancing the stairwell's safety for family members of all ages.

"Recessed lighting plus handrail lighting ensured that shadows on the steps were eliminated and a trip to the ER avoided," she says. "Small children who may not be able to reach the light switch also benefit from the touch-sensitive illuminated handrail."

Caroline Aycock, the owner of Koukla Collective, often works on historic home projects. When dealing with narrow and steep original staircases, she always adds a stair runner to prevent feet from slipping. Not only do runners improve a staircase's safety, but they also offer a great chance to make a statement.

"I think of a patterned staircase runner similarly to wallpaper in a powder room," she says. "It's a place to let your hair down a little and make a small statement. I love stripes in particular. There is something so satisfying to the eye to see those vertical lines running up a staircase."

Fiori also notes that the installation style matters when it comes to safety. She recommends avoiding the "waterfall style," which is where the runner simply falls down to the next tread. Since the runner is not completely attached to the underside of the nose of the step, it increases the chances of slipping.

"To prevent this, we always specify a runner that has a 'Hollywood' wrap, meaning that the runner follows the contour of the step and is wrapped and stapled tightly underneath the nosing as well as secured to the riser below," Fiori says. "This method of installation eliminates movement and stretching, which means adding years to the life of your runner.”

One particularly dangerous section of a stairwell is the landing where two sets of stairs meet, because this is often where there's a small break in the handrail. Designer Prudence Bailey says this problem can be solved by adding a second continuous handrail.

As long as it's sturdy and easy to hold onto, she says there's no reason you can't have fun with the design and make it stand out from the other handrail.

"I think of handrails as one of the jewelry moments in a home," she says. "I love painting them with a high gloss, changing the metal hardware to something pretty or even using Lucite handrails with stairs that have wallpaper."

Large gaps between your stairs' balusters could be a dangerous situation for small children and pets. While exact building codes vary depending on where you live, a good rule of thumb is that your balusters should not be spaced more than four inches apart.

When designer Shannon Crain notices balusters that are spaced too far apart, she uses it as a design opportunity. "I love an oil-rubbed bronze stair rail with closely spaced vertical balusters," she says. "They are clean and architecturally interesting, and the finish adds beautiful depth."

#6062. The Tree of Heaven Is One of the Most Notorious Invasive Plants—Experts Say You Should Do THIS If You Have One on Your Property

This fast-growing tree is tougher to remove than it looks.

The tree of heaven (Ailanthus altissima) may be one of the most ironically named plants in the entire plant kingdom. Far from being a heavenly entity, its presence on your property could signal pests, ecological distress, and even damage to pipes, if it’s close enough to a building.

As with many other invasive plants, you’ll want to be careful with how you get rid of it—cutting this tree down will only make things worse. For that reason, we tapped two experts to explain exactly how to deal with a tree of heaven in your backyard and (hopefully) prevent it from returning.

The tree of heaven is native to China and is considered to be the most invasive tree in America. In fact, as Logan Foltz, certified arborist at Urban Arborists, says, the tree “outcompetes” many of our North American trees in their natural habitats and is “notoriously challenging” to control. It’s also extremely fast-growing (up to 15 feet in the first year) and very resilient.

“The tree of heaven’s ability to thrive in many different climates and locations is what led it to rapidly spread from the East Coast gardens of Philadelphia to the California coast,” explains horticulturist and organic farmer Nathan Heinrich.

The plant can be identified by its slim but sturdy trunk and branches, and its uniform, fern-like fronds that are distinguished by having “glandular teeth” at the base of each leaf. Female varieties of the plant will have red, yellow, or green seed clusters. Note that native, non-threatening black walnut trees and some varieties of sumac also possess similar physical characteristics to trees of heaven, so always be sure you know which plant you’re dealing with.

The tree of heaven has been labeled as invasive because of its ability to produce up to thousands of seeds every year. These seeds can germinate in almost any condition, from sidewalk cracks to gutters to your very own garden. The tree’s root system, which can spread out as much as 50 feet from the trunk, can even infiltrate and crack hardscaping and pipes as it seeks out sources of water. And it’ll choke out neighboring plants.

“If the root system is damaged or the trunk is cut, the tree will respond by sending up suckers from the roots that will grow into new full-size trees,” Foltz says.

They’re considered very nearly “unkillable” because, as Heinrich explains, the tree of heaven has “virtually no natural enemies, drought tolerance, rapid reproduction, and the ability to resurrect by resprouting after being cut down.”

If that weren’t bad enough, trees of heaven are also a host plant for the equally invasive and destructive spotted lanternfly, according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture. Lanternflies, another species without a natural predator in the U.S., suck the sap out of trees and vulnerable plants and can spread plantborne diseases.

Although they’re virtually indestructible, there are some ways experts approach these tough-to-kill trees. Calling this an “old-school approach,” Foltz believes that the best way to get rid of a tree of heaven is to cut the tree down to its base, drill the remaining stump full of holes, then fill those holes with rock salt.

“This method slowly desiccates the stump and connected root system, stopping the tree of heaven from resprouting,” Foltz says. “The drawback here is that the rock salt will make the soil inhospitable for new plantings.” So, this method may be better used for areas of your property where you’re not trying to grow anything.

Heinrich agrees with this method, saying that it’s best to do this in July and August. As an alternative to using rock salt, he recommends injecting multiple rounds of herbicide into the drilled holes and slits in the stump.

He adds that another way to potentially eradicate a tree of heaven is to exhaust its energy reserves by repeatedly cutting it back until its roots can no longer sustain growth.

Trees of heaven are a menacing presence, but is it necessary to get rid of them? Foltz says that it depends.

“These trees are not inherently evil—they are simply incredibly successful at what they do, which is to make more trees,” he notes. “While I do typically recommend removing the new trees as you see them sprout up, some mature specimens may be worth retaining. Consider the overall ecological impact of your tree, and assess whether a native planting would better serve the community.”

This casserole is all about ease. After preheating the oven to 375º, melt butter in a large skillet over medium-high heat. Add your diced carrots, celery, onion, thyme, and salt to the skillet, and cook until softened. This should take around 5 to 7 minutes—make sure you are stirring often! Once softened, add the flour and stir to fully coat the vegetables.

After cooking for a couple of minutes more, add the milk, broth, mustard, garlic powder, and even more salt. Whisk to combine, then increase the heat to bring the mixture to a boil. Once boiling, immediately reduce the heat to medium so the mixture can simmer. Let simmer, stirring often, until it has visibly thickened. This should take around 3 to 5 minutes. Once thickened, fold in the chicken and peas. Give it a taste, and add more salt as needed.

Once your mixture is to your liking, transfer it to a baking dish. Grab your biscuits, separate them, then arrange them on top of the filling. Give the entire dish a good sprinkle of salt and pepper, then bake.

Bake until the filling is bubbling, and the biscuits are golden and risen. This should take around 22 to 25 minutes, but depending on the size of your biscuits, they may need longer in the oven. Once done, let the casserole cool for around 5 minutes before serving.

#6051. Jenna Ortega Won’t Be Boxed In

It was 10 p.m. and Jenna Ortega was alone at the gym, techno music blaring, stage blood still dripping from her ears. She was in Dublin, 5,000 miles from her Los Angeles home, shooting the third season of Netflix’s Addams family spin-off, Wednesday, while simultaneously training to play an elite gymnast in the forthcoming movie Nasty. The Wednesday set that day had involved blood rigs—tubes that spurt crimson-colored syrup—and it had run two hours over. But, Ortega had calculated, “if I get in the car quickly and I don’t rinse off all the way, I can probably get my session in.”

Ortega is telling me this at Chez Jeannette, an unfussy café in the Strasbourg-Saint-Denis neighborhood of Paris, on a disturbingly warm morning in September. Before Ortega arrived, I was nervous. Taika Waititi, who cast her as the lead in his new adaptation of Kazuo Ishiguro’s 2021 dystopian novel Klara and the Sun, described Ortega to me as “beautifully intimidating.” Tim Burton, who has directed her in Wednesday and in his 2024 Beetlejuice sequel, told me she has “a gaze that burns, like a laser beam, the back of your head.”

Ortega has chosen an old-school bistro with burnished mirrors and red leather banquettes and laminated cocktail menus on the table. She is exactly on time for our 10 a.m. meeting. (The waiter assumed our reservation was for 10 p.m., because who would bother with a morning booking here?) She greets me with a hug—more warm smiles than piercing stares—and sits down on the scuffed chair opposite me, unfazed by my choice of the quietest location, which happens to be next to the toilet. A choice that turns out to have been prudent: She is so soft-spoken that, throughout the interview, I occasionally panic that when I eventually listen back to the recording, her voice won’t be audible over the pop music and clattering plates.

EYE CONTACT Ortega wears a Balenciaga 55th Couture dress. Tiffany & Co. bracelet.

Ortega is known for her theatrical fashion choices on the red carpet—she wore a Balmain gown made of metal rulers to last year’s Met Gala and had to be clamped in by a blacksmith backstage—but today she is wearing a plain black T-shirt from a secondhand shop (a “nothing shirt” she bought to wear while painting the kitchen of her home), black cargo capri pants, and flip-flops. (“She’s not vain at all,” says stylist Enrique Melendez, who has been working with Ortega since she was 14.) Her nails are currently painted black for Wednesday, but she prefers to keep them bare. “It’s so much upkeep,” she says. “In my day-to-day life, I’m very practical, very comfortable. I’m a very low-maintenance person.”

Ortega, 24, does not have the résumé of a low-​maintenance person. Over the past few years, she has appeared in a string of horror films, from franchise stalwarts to indie upstarts. She joined the Scream franchise as Tara Carpenter, Ghostface’s first target in the series relaunch; was trapped in a sadistic farmer’s cellar in the A24 slasher film X; and descended into the nightmarish Netherworld in Beetlejuice Beetlejuice. She is currently not only starring in but executive producing Wednesday, which is the most-watched English-language Netflix series of all time.

The character of Wednesday “has had such a profound impact on my life,” Ortega says. “It’ll always be something people think of when they see me.” Ortega’s Wednesday Addams—a Sartre-quoting, graveyard-loving misfit at a school for magical “outcasts”—has inspired Halloween costumes, tattoos, and viral memes. The rare teen-girl protagonist who is outspoken, vindictive (she drops live piranhas into the pool where her brother’s bullies are swimming), and entirely indifferent to the approval of her peers, Wednesday has been embraced as an unlikely Gen Z role model.

NEW GROWTH In her role for Klara and the Sun, Ortega is cheerful and expressive where Wednesday Addams is deadpan and cynical. Ortega in Givenchy by Sarah Burton.

IGNITED Ortega was turned on to acting as a career after admiring Dakota Fanning’s performance in Man on Fire. Ortega wears Marc Jacobs.

But by this time next year, it’s hard to imagine that anyone will think of Ortega purely as a genre actress: She is increasingly choosing projects that match her own taste for the risky and the surreal and push her beyond gothic tween or manic pixie scream queen. First there is her role as Klara, the titular robot in Waititi’s new film, out this fall. Klara is—in both Ishiguro’s and Waititi’s portrayal—temperamentally the opposite of Wednesday: endlessly cheerful and expressive where Wednesday is deadpan and cynical. As an “artificial friend” built to provide constant support to a human child, Klara is “all about using her face, using her eyebrows, to show that she’s trying so hard to do right,” Ortega says. (Wednesday, meanwhile, smiles in only a handful of episodes.)

Klara is purchased for Josie, a lonely teenager with a mysterious illness, and goes to live with her on a remote estate in the country. A clumsy, out-of-date model, Klara stumbles like a toddler as she learns to navigate the world outside the toy store. “Jenna has this ability to attack the physicality of a role that is very impressive to me,” says Amy Adams, who plays Josie’s mother. The first time Waititi met Ortega, he compared the blurring of boundaries between Klara and Josie—Klara mimics Josie’s gait and tries on her clothes—to the possessive relationship between an ailing actress and her nurse in Ingmar Bergman’s 1966 Persona. “She was like, ‘Oh, I love Persona,’ ” says Waititi. “And I was like, ‘Okay, a young person who’s seen Persona—that’s fucking cool.’ ”

Ortega orders a black Americano and drinks it quickly as she walks me through what has brought her to Paris in the first place—it’s not a quick weekend getaway from the grueling Wednesday filming schedule, but rather a meeting with Leos Carax, the French art house director whose next movie she will star in. She tells me she discovered Carax when she was 15. His Holy Motors—which has been called incomprehensible by professional critics—“completely changed my perception of what movies can be,” she says. “Anything in that surrealist realm is super fascinating to me.”

Early success and early fame can sometimes impart a kind of cultural tunnel vision—an inability to look beyond the next project, or an insecurity when speaking beyond what’s coming up. But Ortega seems genuinely enthused by the prospect of stretching her limits. Growing up, she wanted to be, at various points, the president of the United States, an archaeologist, and an investigative journalist. She lights up when she describes the thrill of researching a character or learning a skill—fencing and archery for Wednesday; now gymnastics for her role in Nasty, which tells the story of a gymnastics prodigy and her overbearing coach (played by Rose Byrne). She laughs as she recalls her pride in mastering a new routine on the beam, only to be told by her coach that it’s the one typically assigned to eight-year-olds.

“She’s done so much work,” says Mary Bronstein, the director of Nasty, which will begin filming this fall. Ortega knew she wanted to work with Bronstein after she saw the director’s harrowing If I Had Legs I’d Kick You (2025), about a mother (also played by Byrne) unraveling under the strain of her daughter’s illness. It’s a film that thrums with visceral anxiety—much of it tied to the ceaseless demands of parenting and the tensions of a toxic marriage, not necessarily themes you’d imagine resonating with a 24-year-old. “But [Jenna] has been put in some little boxes,” says Bronstein. “I think that there are things that are in there that haven’t been brought out…. She has a poise to her that I’ve seen few people in their 40s or even 50s have.”

Ortega decided she wanted to become an actor—specifically, the Puerto Rican Dakota Fanning (she is of Puerto Rican and Mexican descent)—at the age of six, after being captivated by Fanning’s performance in Man on Fire. The fourth of six kids growing up in a “small, tumbleweed-esque city” in the Coachella Valley, about two hours east of LA, she began petitioning her parents to let her attend auditions. They resisted—her mother, Natalie, had grown up reading tabloid horror stories about child actors—and tried to appease her with a book of monologues. This backfired: Jenna devoured the book, memorizing several of the speeches, and Natalie was so tickled by one of Jenna’s dramatic recitations that she posted a clip to her friends on Facebook.

When the video somehow caught the attention of a casting director, her parents relented, and Natalie, an emergency room nurse, began shuttling Jenna back and forth to LA for auditions several days a week. Jenna booked commercials for Colgate and McDonald’s; played a shooting victim on CSI: NY; and then, at 12, landed the lead in the Disney Channel sitcom Stuck in the Middle. “I wanted to do it so desperately,” Ortega says.

Still, she says—after a long pause—“I don’t think I would start someone that young.” She has said that she would sometimes go all day on set without eating or drinking. There should be “less of a workhorse expectation put on these kids,” she says. While she felt protected by her mother, it’s a “very susceptible environment for things to get confused.” And when Wednesday blew up in 2022, Ortega felt profoundly disoriented. Children stopped her in the street; she saw her own face on T-shirts. There was “no one really showing you the ropes.”

She would now advise a child who wanted to act to instead develop their creative horizons. As an actor, “you need things to pull from.” She is grateful that her parents insisted she play team sports as long as possible, for example, but she still had an unusual childhood. She has portrayed a high school student in several movies, but she left conventional high school at 14. Though Ortega knew almost nothing about gymnastics when she opened the script for Nasty two years ago, the project has offered her a rare opportunity to examine her own trajectory. Her character, she says, “felt like she had to perform from a very young age. There were a lot of things I could understand.”

THE TIDE TURNS “No one [is] really showing you the ropes,” Ortega says of the disorienting effects of sudden fame. In Dior.

A few days later, I get on a Zoom with Ortega. Even in her enthusiasms, she had been so composed—taking the time to collect her thoughts before she responded to each question—that I had wondered if she was imagining how her words would appear in print. This would have been understandable given her years as a working actor, the degree of fame she vaulted into, the scrutiny she elicits. It was only at the end of the interview at the café that she became chattier: asking about my plans in Paris, if I liked London—where I live—how I met my husband, and, wasn’t it funny, she was just talking to someone else who met their partner on Hinge.

I hoped to reach this more open Ortega in our second conversation. She’s still in Paris for the call; the Wednesday team is there working on some off-camera prep. She’s sitting perched on a windowsill, the Paris street behind her, while we speak. She’s wearing no jewelry or makeup—her freckles are visible across her eyelids, her hair tucked behind her ears—and, again, a plain black T-shirt.

“There’s a lot of different trains rolling in my brain at the same time,” she tells me. Ortega was seven when she realized there was something different about the way her mind worked. She was waking up early, before her siblings, to perform a specific routine every morning before school. “I had to open the bedsheet a certain way, and open the drawer one way, and brush my hair in a slow-paced way.” She now recognizes that behavior as an early manifestation of obsessive compulsive disorder, which continues to impact her life. Sometimes, if she’s tired or jet-lagged or anxious about an upcoming job, “I just bite the bullet and do the routine”: repeating the phrase “take care” a specific number of times throughout the day; stepping onto set with her left foot first; holding her breath and counting to 71. She’s tried to reframe OCD as a game, which “helps to take some of the pressure off.” She’s tried medication. She’s worked with a therapist, and still sometimes does. Her career can be stressful, but it’s also a reprieve. When the cameras are rolling, “you have to breathe. That’s another reason why I like my job.”

While Ortega has been open about her anxiety and mental health struggles, she fiercely protects her privacy in other areas. When I try to inquire about her romantic life—unlikely that she was using Hinge—she deflects. “I don’t think I’ll ever approach that part of my life, my personal life, with the public,” she says firmly. Even some who know her well find her guarded. “She’s actually very mysterious to me,” says Waititi. “I never met Audrey Hepburn, but I imagine she could have been a good contemporary of hers.”

“We both don’t let a lot of people into our circle,” agrees Maddie Ziegler, Ortega’s close friend and costar in The Fallout, a 2021 indie hit about the aftermath of a high school shooting. “We are introverts, at the end of the day.” Ortega’s close relationships with her parents and siblings are one of her main sources of stability; she FaceTimes them when she’s abroad and sees them whenever she is in LA. She also credits her whippet terrier mix—whom she adopted in Dublin—with helping her feel grounded in foreign cities. (Ortega’s dog has joined her for stints in Paris and London.) “I spend a lot of time in dog parks, with the dog community,” she says. “I meet familiar faces there.”

She also maintains firm boundaries around her own relationship with her public image. She does not watch her own movies. She does not read reviews—“I think that that would send me into a psychosis of some sort”—or open the box office updates her team sends. “I don’t want it to become a numbers thing,” she says. She only encounters chatter about her movies through word of mouth or Instagram, “which I’m on less and less,” she says. “So much of what you see on there nowadays is just garbage AI.” Anyway, she says, “we all look at ourselves too much.” (There was a mirror behind my head during our meeting at Chez Jeannette, but she didn’t appear to glance at her reflection once.) Her phone recently broke, she tells me, and she waited a few days before getting it fixed. “If I needed to get somewhere, if I had a workout class, I would look at a map on the computer, memorize the streets. If somebody needed me, I’m like, ‘Well, I’m gonna be at this café at this time.’ And that was so freeing.”

The past year has offered her space to figure out what she’s invested in—and that itself has been a kind of freedom. “I want to be in environments that are stimulating and productive, but also just telling stories that I would want to watch.” She would like to try theater: “You get to be immersed in a different way.” When I ask about her dream role—a question that typically yields an answer like Hedda Gabler or Juliet—she describes a minor character in Sam Shepard’s 1978 rural drama Buried Child: a young woman who taunts her boyfriend’s disabled uncle with his own prosthetic leg. “I just thought it was kind of an insane thing for her to do,” Ortega says. I point out that a director might prefer that she play the lead. “It would be nice to just do this one scene,” she says. “I don’t need to be at the forefront of everything.”

Nasty is her “first baby” as a producer. She discovered the script—by Isabella Jarosz, a first-time writer in her 20s—in a batch from her agent. “The joy and the fulfillment” of assembling a creative team, which happens to be all women, “just wasn’t anything I was expecting.” She would love to direct too, but only once she feels “there’s something I need to say. I don’t want to do it prematurely.”

“I think I have a little more understanding of myself and what I need to work,” Ortega says—which includes time at home in LA. Last year, she found herself with an unexpected three-month hiatus after the Wednesday filming schedule was postponed. It was the longest break she’d had since she was 16. At first “it was terrifying.” But she found her rhythm: reading books and plays (current favorites include Federico García Lorca and Flannery O’Connor), going on hikes with her dog, hanging out with friends and family, watching two movies a day. “It sounds really mundane, but that stuff’s not always normal to me.”

“I would love to slow down a bit,” she says. But when I ask her what slowing down might entail, she describes studying abroad, learning about musical composition, or trying photography. “I do think I’m somebody who always has to be moving.”
