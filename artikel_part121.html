#399. General Motors beats Wall Street's Q3 earnings expectations, narrows full-year guidance

DETROIT — General Motors narrowed its 2026 earnings guidance and raised its free cash flow expectations after beating Wall Street's third-quarter expectations.

The Detroit automaker also announced a new multiyear stock buyback of $20 billion after GM completed a $6 billion share repurchase program that was announced in January. GM did not disclose a specific time to complete the program.

Despite the positive news, GM stock fell nearly 7% during trading Tuesday as the company's outlook missed Wall Street's expectations.

Wall Street analysts were mixed before the report on whether GM would raise its guidance Tuesday amid concerns of slowing sales and rising commodity costs. Analysts' expectations included a midpoint of $13.41 per share for the year compared with GM's new $13.25 per share midpoint, according to LSEG.

Several analysts in investor notes Tuesday said GM's third-quarter results were solid, but raised concerns about whether higher costs and pressures on vehicle pricing would limit earnings growth next year.

GM's new guidance includes adjusted earnings before interest and taxes of between $14.5 billion and $15.5 billion, or $12.75 to $13.75 adjusted earnings per share, and adjusted automotive free cash flow of between $11.5 billion and $12.5 billion.

That compares with its raised guidance from July that included adjusted EBIT of between $14 billion and $16 billion, or $12 to $14 adjusted EPS, and adjusted automotive free cash flow of $9.5 billion to $11.5 billion.

"I think the market's been pretty consistent from what we've said all year," GM CFO Paul Jacobson told CNBC's Phil LeBeau. "Our fourth quarter's going to be down sequentially. ... I think the fact that we're able to hold our guidance in the face of that late inflationary pressure in the year is another sign of the GM team executing really well."

GM on Tuesday said it expects commodity inflation costs to be around $2 billion, which was the high end of its previous guidance. That includes higher costs for logistics and dynamic random-access memory, or DRAM, chips that are impacting multiple industries.

While the inflationary costs were at the automaker's high end of guidance, GM said its gross tariff costs are expected to be at the low end of its expected $2.5 billion to $3.5 billion range. Warranty costs are expected to be at high end between $1 billion and $1.5 billion, GM said.

Jacobson said vehicle pricing is relatively flat as inflationary costs continue to increase. He said the company would assess where consumers are in the coming year when deciding if it should pass costs along by raising prices more than normal.

"GM is performing well, and we are working hard to continue our momentum despite growing inflationary pressures through strategic investments in new products and services, and through improved quality and operating efficiency," GM CEO Mary Barra said Tuesday in a letter to shareholders.

The automaker also said the costs of its pullback in all-electric vehicles announced last year were "substantially completed," saying it has recorded $11.2 billion in such charges since the second half of last year, including paying $4.6 billion of $7.4 billion in cash through the third quarter.

The company's net income attributed to stockholders was $3.3 billion during the third quarter, up roughly $2 billion from a year earlier.

GM's North American operations continued to lead the automaker's results, accounting for $3.6 billion of its $4.1 billion in adjusted EBIT results during the third quarter.

GM's 2025 third-quarter results included $48.59 billion in revenue, net income attributable to stockholders of $1.3 billion and earnings per share of $2.80 adjusted.

#398. Everything the Glee Cast Has Said About the Reboot

Kevin McHale, Heather Morris, Jane Lynch and more original stars of Glee reacted to news that the Ryan Murphy series is getting a reboot 11 years after the Fox finale.

The cast of Glee might be heading in a new direction.

After it was confirmed that Disney+ and Hulu have placed a series order for a Glee reboot, the O.G. stars of the Fox musical show admitted the news struck quite a chord, with several teasing they might be retaking the stage.

As Chord Overstreet, who played Sam Evans, wrote on X Oct. 21, “Reunited and it feels so good!”

His costar Kevin McHale, who portrayed Artie Abrams on all six seasons, shared his own thoughts on X, retweeting a 2020 post about Drake surpassing the cast of Glee on the Billboard Hot 100 Chart and writing, “Challenge accepted.”

McHale’s co-host on the Glee podcast And That's What You REALLY Missed Jenna Ushkowitz also reacted to the news, posting a carousel of herself as the character Tina Cohen-Chang on Instagram with the caption, “QUEEN T.”

She also hinted at the news in her Instagram Story, writing, “Busy day, did I miss anything in the news today?”

As for Heather Morris, she too appeared to confirm her involvement in the series, posting a throwback photo on Instagram Oct. 21 and saying, “Yes ladies and gentle men of women, rumors are true. Brittany S Pierce was in fact lost in the sewers for all these years. But I’m here to inform you that she is alive and well…And back for more #Glee.”

And Jane Lynch? Sue Sylvester herself reposted the news to her Instagram Story, although she did not confirm nor deny her actual involvement.

Though it remains to be seen which of the cast—which also included Matthew Morrison, Harry Shum Jr., Lea Michele, Chris Colfer, Amber Riley, Jane Lynch and more—will appear in the reboot, creator Ryan Murphy hinted that some William McKinley High School alums will be returning.

"I've been in touch with around 10 of the original cast members," he told Entertainment Tonight in September. "It's very sweet, and they all had a lot of questions, but they all were very enthusiastic about the idea."

Despite no casting confirmations, Michele—who played Rachel Berry—previously revealed she would be thrilled to return for a reboot if it felt right.

"I love Glee," she told E! News in September. "I will always love Glee. I love Ryan Murphy. It just holds such a special place in my heart for me. So, I will follow Ryan wherever he goes."

For a deep dive into all the behind-the-scenes Glee lore, keep reading.

A few years after Glee ended, Ryan Murphy reflected on the pop culture phenomenon and revealed how chaotic things were behind the scenes.

"It was the best time in my life and the worst time in my life," he told Entertainment Weekly, going on to admit, "There was a lot of infighting. There was a lot of people sleeping together and breaking up. It was good training for being a parent, I'll tell you that much."

He also reflected on his time on the show, looking back with some regret on how he handled things as the showrunner.

"I was there with them all day long, and then we'd finish work and we'd go out and have fun all night, and I guess in a weird, twisted way, I was trying to relive the childhood I never had," he told THR. "I thought they wanted a parent, and they didn't. They didn't want me to tell them what to f—king do. They didn't want me to tell them how to treat each other or what the world was like at the end of the day. I wish I could go back and do that differently with a lot of those actors. Some of them I'm still very close to: Lea Michele, Chord Overstreet, Darren Criss — but there were some that didn't work out well, and I regret that. I guess I just wish I had been able to let them figure it out for themselves."

Going on to create Scream Queens, American Horror Story, Pose and other hit series, Murphy said Glee "was a great lesson in what not to do moving forward."

Fun fact: Ryan Murphy wrote the role of Rachel Berry with Lea Michele, a Broadway standout thanks to Spring Awakening, in mind. But not every cast member's journey to becoming a Gleek was so easy.

Before he was cast as Blaine, Criss auditioned for the role of Finn, the lead quarterback.

"It was the first audition I ever had. I auditioned for the pilot," he revealed at the Paleyfest panel. "And then I remember watching the pilot to see who got the part [I went out for] and seeing Cory [Monteith got it] and thinking, oh, obviously, right."

Another star who also auditioned for a different part was Colfer, who read for Artie originally. Producers were so impressed by the teen that they created the role of Kurt specifically for him.

In fact, Murphy revealed Colfer's audition was the inspiration behind creating The Glee Project, the show's reality competitions series that gave aspiring actors the chance to be cast on Glee.

"It's such a huge honor and I mean that's really special because I feel like if I inspired something that is giving kids a platform that is incredible," Colfer said.

The final cast member to join the show? Dianna Agron, who played head cheerleader Quinn Fabray, landing the role the day before they filmed the pilot.

Initially, Quinn was supposed to be the stereotypical mean girl, but Agron's nature ended up changing the role for the writers.

"When we cast Dianna as Quinn, she ruined the part for me," Murphy told Rolling Stone. "She was supposed to be the Cybill Shepherd, Last Picture Show c—, so to speak, but she humanized it. She can cry at the drop of a hat. So now her character has a conscience, a soul and great vulnerability."

Agron would leave the show after season three, returning only for the 100th episode.

Just like in the hallways of McKinley High, the Glee set featured a lot of flirtation and relationships between its young stars. (And even before the show, as Lea Michele revealed in her 2014 memoir Brunette Ambition that she and Matthew Morrison "actually dated back in for the day for a Broadway beat.")

Michele and Cory Monteith, whose characters were one of the show's most iconic couples," dated for several years before Monteith's tragic death in 2013, while Blake Jenner and Melissa Benoist, who now stars on Supergirl, married in 2015 after meeting on the set in 2012 when they also played love interests; they split in 2016 and their divorce was finalized the following year.

Naya Rivera and Mark Salling briefly dated in the show's earlier days and it wasn't an amicable split, as Rivera would write in her 2016 memoir, "I think everyone should have that one relationship where you look back and ask yourself, 'What the hell was I thinking?' You'll learn something and you won't regret it. Unless, of course, that relationship was with someone who had a sizable stash of child porn on his computer. Then, by all means, regret everything." Rivera was referring to Salling's child pornography indictment in 2017. In January 2018, Salling committed suicide at the age of 35.

While they first met when she guest-starred on Glee, Gwyneth Paltrow didn't begin dating future husband Brad Falchuk, one of Glee's co-creators, until she returned to the show in 2014, over a year after her split from Coldplay frontman Chris Martin.

"When you're going through a divorce and you think about going out with somebody, it's a weird thing," she told Howard Stern of taking it slowly with Falchuk, a recently-divorced father of two. "So I don't think it's the same as being 23 and running into someone at a coffee shop. It's just different. You have kids, you have divorce—it's just a lot of stuff."

While there were whispers of a romance between Kevin McHale (Artie) and Jenna Ushkowitz (Tina) during the show's run, it turns out they were just in a showmance, which became the new of their hit podcast.

Given its popularity and millions of downloads (including huge bumps for the original versions of the songs covered by the cast), you would think any band or artist would give Glee the rights to their songs. But there were several major bands that wouldn't let the glee club put their take on their tunes: Kings of Leon, with Murphy calling the band members "self-centered assholes" because they "missed the big picture" aka how the show could inspire kids to join arts education through their songs, namely their hit "Use Somebody." A mini-Twitter feud followed.

Guns N' Roses guitarist Slash also rejected Glee's request, saying he wasn't a fan of the show. "Usually I find people who make those comments, their careers are over' they're uneducated and stupid," Murphy said.

The Foo Fighters didn't approve the show's request either, with Dave Grohl calling out Murphy in an interview with THR.

"You shouldn't have to do f--king Glee," he said. "And then the guy who created Glee is so offended that we're not, like, begging to be on his f--king show...I watched 10 minutes...it's not my thing."

Colfer and Ushkowitz lived together during the show's run and often had cast parties at their apartment.

"Those of us who were obviously over-age would drink themselves into the oblivion," McHale said on Showmance, his podcast with Ushkowitz, before Colfer chimed in, "Oh, I drank too." (During a 2013 Paleyfest panel, Colfer revealed he actually had his first drink at their apartment.)

Amber Riley (Mercedes) smoked her first and last cigarette during one of their many late nights there, with the foursome being so close they called themselves JACK (the first initial of all of their names).

They also reminisced on their wild nights out while on their concert tour, with Riley revealing she got into a fight with a random girl while in London with the cast. After realizing she had punched the girl, she told their security team who then paid the girl off to stay quiet. Colfer, still not 21 at the time, admitted to sneaking into a lot of bars.

Because they were a young, close-knit cast, many things the stars were going through ended up on-screen, especially early on.

"There have been a couple of times when I have gone to Ryan Murphy and told him a couple of things that have happened to me, and then he writes it into the show," Colfer said in an interview with PR.com. "Or he'll ask me what song I would want to sing, in this situation or in that situation. I don't think any of us directly try to give input on the character or on the storyline, but they definitely steal things from us. I remember I was talking to Ryan about when I was in high school and I really wanted to sing 'Defying Gravity.' The other students in my drama class and the teachers, when we were putting on this talent show, they wouldn't let me sing it because I was a boy and it was a girl's song. And then that was made [into] an episode."

And star Chord Overstreet's (Sam) plump lips even worked their way into a storyline...and song, called "Trouty Mouth."

During a Paleyfest panel, Overstreet said, "I never even realized my lips were anything until the jokes started coming. And they wrote that freakin' song!"

While he ultimately didn't snag the role of Finn (his first audition ever!), Darren Criss ended up becoming one of the biggest breakout stars of the series when he made his debut as Head Warbler Blaine Anderson in season two. His first scene, which featured his performance of Katy Perry's "Teenage Dream," set up one of the show's most popular couples, Klaine (Kurt and Blaine), but also delivered one of Glee's most successful singles of all-time, debuting at No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100.

Criss, one of the stars and creators of the popular YouTube "A Very Potter Musical" pre-Glee (He played Harry Potter, of course!), was quickly promoted to series regular and became one of the central characters.

And when Murphy was looking to cast the role of serial killer Andrew Cunanan for The Assassination of Gianni Versace: American Crime Story, he surprised many when he chose Criss, crediting his turn on Broadway in Hedwig and the Angry Inch.

"I just knew he could do it," Murphy revealed to EW. "More than that, I knew that he was super hungry and ambitious. I think people thought of Darren as a musical comedy star first. But, when I saw Hedwig, I knew he was capable of great darkness."

Criss' performance on the FX hit series earned him a Golden Globe, Emmy and SAG Award.

While she would go on to become one of the show's most beloved stars, Heather Morris was actually brought in by the show's choreographer just to teach Colfer how to do the "Single Ladies" dance because she was one of Beyoncé's back-up dancers. Producers then decided to cast her as a background player when they needed a third cheerleader, but they quickly realized they had a secret weapon in Morris, who had a knack for tossing out one-liners "(Did you know dolphins are just gay sharks?") and became a fan-favorite. She was promoted to series regular in season two, becoming the focus of the  Britney Spears tribute episode and one-half of one of the show's most popular couples with Naya Rivera's Santana.

For the fifth season, Morris became a recurring guest star after getting pregnant in real-life, with the actress choosing not to return as a full-time cast member.

At the height of its fame, it seemed like everyone appeared on Glee or wanted to appear on Glee. Some of the show's biggest guest star gets included Gwyneth Paltrow, John Stamos, Helen Mirren (via voiceover), Carol Burnett, Kate Hudson, Lindsay Lohan, Ricky Martin, Sarah Jessica Parker and Whoopi Goldberg, among many, many more.

Still, there were some announced guest stars who never ended up Gleeking out: Javier Bardem was supposed to play a rock star who befriends Artie, which never came to fruition. Anne Hathaway was set to play Kurt's lesbian aunt, but never popped up.

Jennifer Lopez also met with producers, but it never ended up materializing.

After tributes to Madonna and Lady Gaga, Glee staged its most successful tribute episode: in season two: "Britney/Brittany," an homage to Britney Spears. (They did a second Brit tribute episode in season four.)

It was [Britney's] idea," Murphy said of the pop icon's response to the honor. "I think she loves what the show's about, paying tribute to pop culture in a very loving, respectful, kind way. She responded to that."

Unlike Madonna and Gaga though, Spears actually appeared as herself in the episode.

"I loved it! Thank you guys SO much for making this happen!" the "I'm A Slave 4 U" singer tweeted.

And Spears was particularly taken with Morris, writing, "Awww Heather Morris is sooo cute! Brittany S Pearce. Ha! She was so fun to work with and was really sweet in person."

It was a major moment for Morris, who idolized Spears growing up.

"She was my idol from, like, age 12 to 16," Morris told TV Week. "I sang to her [songs] in my living room and danced to her—I learned all her moves. And I had my talent shows and I lip-synced to her. I just kept flashing back to growing up and being obsessed with her."

Glee was so popular that the main cast went on a national tour from 2010-11, selling out almost all of their 40 dates. (Eventually it went overseas as well, visiting European cities.)

"The response of the fans to our little show has been so immediate and so gratifying, we wanted to get out and thank them live and in person," Murphy said when the tour was announced. "And what show lends itself more to a concert than Glee?"

The tour would go on to gross $40 million, and resulted in the two-week theatrical release of Glee: The 3D Concert Movie, using footage from the cast's concert in New Jersey.

As for their music sales, the show's 16 soundtrack albums and six compilation albums had sold over 7.9 million copies by 2015, while their songs had been downloaded over 45 million times and sold 53 million copies.

You can't stay in high school, even when you're attending TV high school. Still, fans were shocked when hen Murphy told THR that the series' main trio Michele, Monteith and Colfer would not be returning for season four after their class graduated from McKinley. Rumors of a potential New York-set spinoff being explored by the producers surfaced, which would find Rachel and Kurt trying to make their Broadway dreams come true in the Big Apple.

"You can keep them on the show for six years and people will criticize you for not being realistic," Murphy said at the time, "or you can be really true to life and say when they started the show they were very clearly sophomores and they should graduate at the end of their senior year."

But the trio ended up staying on Glee, which focused on their post-college plans while also introducing new students into the mix at McKinley, with the network's then president telling reporters ahead of season four, "There will not be a Glee spinoff."

Still, season four presented a shake-up for the show, with Murphy telling Vulture he gave the original cast members the choice to exit the series if they wanted after their characters graduated. "I said to them, 'Anybody who wants to stay on the show will stay on the show,'" he explained. Almost everyone stayed but many dropped down to recurring or guest star status.

During Glee's third season, The Glee Project kicked off on Oxygen, which found the contestants competing for a role on the main show.

From its two season-run, Glee brought four contestants over, including season one's winners Samuel Larsen and Damian McGinty Jr., as well as runner-ups Lindsay Pearce and Alex Newell. Blake Jenner, the winner of season two, became a series regular in season five, while the runner-up Ali Stroker made an unexpected guest appearance in season four.

One of the show's breakout stars, winning a Golden Globe for his turn as Kurt, Colfer went on to write an episode of Glee in season five, later admitting he was terrified to take on the challenge despite writing several novels, as well as the film adaptation of his book Struck By Lighting.

"When they first approached me I said, absolutely not," he said during a Paleyfest panel. "I'm not putting words into my costars' mouths. There was a lot of pressure."

His onscreen paramour Darren Criss also penned something for Glee during the show's run, writing an original song for the series finale called "This Time."

"I basically wrote a love note to the entire experience," Criss explained of the song, which Michele sang in the episode. "Having Lea sing it to me is one of the most special moments of my career."

It was just as special for Michele, who said, "Darren gave me the greatest gift. Honestly. That was the day I fell to my knees and cried. I literally had to call my mother to come [get me]. It's so special, it was the most amazing memory."

The song would go on to be nominated for an Emmy.

In 2011, a Glee extra/fan found herself in hot water when she shared major spoilers on Twitter, revealing most of the major happenings at the prom. Co-creator Brad Falchuk put her on blast, responding, "Who are you to spoil something talented people have spent months to create?"

He then added, "hope you're qualified to do something besides work in entertainment."

The leaker Nicole Crowther, then 2, received "very, very hateful messages" from fans and said she was "black-balled" by every agency in town.

"I had no idea it would even come close to anything it's been," she told E! News. "I regret it and I can totally understand, not only where the fans are coming from, but why Brad and other people from Fox are furious with me. I can understand their point of view."

A few years later, the show had another Twitter scandal when someone hacked Colfer's account and tweeted he was leaving the show. "Due to personal issues, I have been let go from the cast of GLEE. Explanations will come shortly..." the message read.

After the mass hysteria from Klaine fans subsided when the show's rep denied Colfer was leaving the show, the actor appeared on Good Morning America, calling the hack "very upsetting."

"Some jerk hacked me," he said. "It was a pain, it really was."

In her 2016 memoir, Sorry Not Sorry: Dreams, Mistakes and Growing Up, Rivera revealed she got an abortion during the show's run.

In late 2010, Naya discovered she was pregnant just weeks after breaking up with actor Ryan Dorsey, whom she later wed in 2014 and is the father of her son, Josey Dorsey (they divorced in 2018).

She chose not share the news with Dorsey at the time, instead choosing to focus on her career; she terminated the pregnancy during a day off from filming an episode of Glee.

"I think every woman should have the right to choose, and it scares me to think of a world where the decision of whether to have a child is not her choice to make," she wrote.

After Monteith's sudden and tragic death from combined drug intoxication in 2013 at the age of 31, questions arose about how Glee would handle his passing and Finn's absence on the show (with Murphy telling E! News they even considered ending  the series).

After taking an extended hiatus, Glee addressed Monteith's death in "The Quarterback," a beautiful tribute episode that revealed Finn had died. It was an emotional and somber hour of television, both on-screen and off as each cast member was given a change to honor Monteith in the episode.

"Almost everything in that episode is from the first take of every performance because the actors and the crew had a really hard time shooting it,' Murphy said at the time. "Rachel comes back to Ohio, fulfilled and yet not, and walks into Finn's glee club," he explained. "'What are you doing here?' he would ask. 'I'm home,' she would reply. Fade out. The End."

In an interview with Rolling Stone, Michele revealed she and co-stars Ushkowitz and McHale got matching "Imagine" tattoos on their feet, a nod to The Beatles, during a night out and a few glasses of wine.

"I'm known to be the instigator to do some more risky stuff. So we have little tattoos on our feet now," she said. "It made a lot of sense that night."

Criss was also present Michele married Zandy Reich a month later in a ceremony officiated by Murphy. Becca Tobin, who joined Glee in season four and ow stars in E!'s Ladygang, was also present as she and Michele have continued to be best friends in the years since Glee.

In 2018, Morrison reunited many of the member of New Directions to meet his newborn son, Revel James Makai Morrison. "It's a GLEE-Union!!" Morrison captioned the photo of the gang, including Michele, Rivera, Riley, Morris, Colfer, Criss and more. "What a beautiful night hangin with my old kids & new kid! Ohana..."

In March, some of the Gleeks, including Crazy Rich Asians' Harry Shum Jr., reunited on-screen, competing against each other on Drop the Mic.

#396. Bud Light Boycotters Are Still Waiting for Their ‘Woke-Free’ Beer

“Go woke, go broke” was a culture war rallying cry of the Biden years, with American conservatives waging battles against any corporate entity that dared to pay lip service to diversity or LGBTQ rights.

But customers of one successful anti-woke alternative brand later found their own wallets a bit lighter—with nothing to show for it.

In April 2023, at the height of a right-wing freakout over a sponsored Instagram post in which transgender actress and singer Dylan Mulvaney showed off a can of Bud Light with her face on it, a Georgia man named Seth Weathers smelled an opportunity to cash in. Conservatives were vowing to ditch the Budweiser brand and take their business elsewhere. (A tearful Kid Rock even filmed himself shooting up a few cases with a semiautomatic rifle.) It was an angry political demographic in need of a new low-ABV lager.

#394. Klara and the Sun review: Jenna Ortega fans, respectfully look away

Klara and the Sun is a disaster of a movie, though on paper it was promising.

The film's source material is Kazuo Ishiguro's popular sci-fi novel. Its content taps into the emerging conversation around artificial intelligence, as the titular Klara is an Artificial Friend, designed to be the perfect companion for a lonely teen. In the lead role is rising star Jenna Ortega, who aims to show range from her hardened Scream character and devotedly monotone Wednesday by playing a smiley robot who's scorned for being "too empathetic."

Joining her are celebrated actresses like Amy Adams and Natasha Lyonne. And at the helm is Taika Waititi, who previously adapted Barry Crump's book Wild Pork and Watercress into the beloved coming-of-age action comedy Hunt for the Wilderpeople. However, it's been 10 years since Waititi gifted audiences with the winsome and weird tale of "bad egg" Ricky Baker.

Want more film and TV news and reviews delivered straight to your inbox? Sign up for Mashable’s Top Stories newsletter or get Mashable push alerts today.

Since then, Waititi earned mixed reviews for Jojo Rabbit, his adaptation of Christine Leunens' Caging Skies in which he cast himself as Adolf Hitler. Following the fun of Thor: Ragnarok came the blunder of Thor: Love and Thunder. And most recently, Waititi took the inspirational soccer documentary Next Goal Wins and turned it into an awful sports comedy of the same name.

Going from What We Do in the Shadows and Hunt for the Wilderpeople to Next Goal Wins was a warning that Waititi had lost the spark that made him an exciting filmmaker. Klara and the Sun confirms the spark is gone.

Post-World War III and post-internet, AI Friends are the must-have accessory for any wealthy household. Among a gaggle of older models, Klara is considered an oddity because she prays to the sun and stands "like a flamingo" with one leg raised. If someone doesn't buy her soon, she'll be sent back to the factory and scrapped for parts. So, when a gloomy teen named Josie (Mia Tharia) chooses her, Klara declares she's been saved from "certain doom."

Far away from the soot-covered city where the Artificial Friends store sits, Josie lives amid wheat fields in a big house, overdecorated by her smothering mother Chrissie (Amy Adams), who works tirelessly as an interior designer. It's a house full of things, but frozen in mourning. Years before, Josie's older sister Sal died, and then her father left, leaving her and her mother to stare at each other for years of failed connection. Thrust into this dynamic, Klara is a polarizing presence.

Josie is happy to have a friend devoted to hearing her out, as her mother is constantly criticizing or away at work. For Chrissie, Klara is meant to be a baby monitor who can watch over Josie. But why does Josie need watching over? Well, neither Chrissie nor her fiercely protective maid Melania (recurring Waititi collaborator Rachel House) will explain that to Klara, even as medicines are hooked up via IV and Josie begins experiencing violent fits.

As Klara tried to calibrate her surroundings and do her best by Josie, she meets new friends, like Josie's robo-curious crush Rick (Aran Murphy) and a scarecrow robot called Scarecrow (Jesse Griffin in a Rhys Darby-coded role). But most of all, when in doubt, Klara talks to the sun, convinced it is a benevolent god who has the power to heal her sickly human friend.

The premise sounds like a children's story at first, where a child-like robot learns from the world, even when it's cruel. (See also Steven Spielberg's AI.) However, as the contemporary conversation of AI is getting heated, with parents worrying about the negative affect chatbots can have on their children's mental health, Klara and the Sun feels achingly dated, presenting Klara as a wholesome, sentient servant, devoted purely to making her owner happy and healthy.

Beyond that, Waititi's approach to Ishiguro's novel is confounding. The first act is set in the AF store where a kindly manager (Natasha Lyonne) reads bedtime stories to her robot wards. Here, Klara seems eccentric in the way of Roald Dahl's Matilda, with an earnest curiosity and plucky resilience. With simple diction and a hiking outfit that recalls Dora the Explorer, Ortega's character seems suitable for a children's movie. But soon, Waititi's screenplay, co-written with Dahvi Waller, injects darker elements, including violence, blood, death, arson, and suicide.

Of course, Tim Burton has touched on those elements in spooky movies and in Wednesday, which families watch together. But I can't imagine families will be tempted to sit down in such a way for Klara and the Sun. It's not irreverently funny like Beetlejuice, whimsical like Edward Scissorhands, or fun amid its darkness. It's dreadful, bland, and mean. Klara is repeatedly called an "idiot," while the humans around her are thinly sketched scoundrels who lie, seethe, shout, and make selfish demands. Even the kindly store manager is cruel, locking Klara in a dark closet, away from the sun, which — as she is solar-powered — is tantamount to starving her.

In Matilda, there is abundant cruelty around its eponymous heroine, but there is also a beacon of hope in Miss Honey. Klara and the Sun has no Miss Honey, only Josie, who is caricature of an angsty teenager, fitfully sarcastic, cheerful, and sulky. Tharia is resigned to brooding silently as plot happens around her. With such flimsy roles, all of the actors struggle. Lyonne is amusing, but one note. Even six-time Academy Award nominee Amy Adams can't bring depth to Waititi's charmless movie.

In a world full of fears and pain, Klara looks unblinkingly to the sun. She prays, goes on a pilgrimage, and even offers a sacrifice in hopes her god will answer her plea to fix Josie. But Waititi's vision shows no belief in her convictions. Sure, Ortega brings a bright smile to the role, but she seems unmoored in this clumsily constructed world that doesn't reflect her whimsy — even though the film is told from Klara's perspective.

Waititi's color palette is muted by an overcast of sickly yellow/green, which makes the whole world reminiscent of the glum, genderless uniform the AFs wear upon purchase. The costumes across the board are ghastly. Josie dresses haphazardly, with nothing worth noting. Adams is wedged into drab dresses that work against her curves and natural beauty, while Klara is eventually slapped into a '80s soccer mom look, with high-waisted jeans and an asymmetrical jersey. The whimsy Ortega offers in her performance is swallowed by such bland choices, and mocked by a bob that is clunkily cut to look like Temu Amélie.

Bizarrely, the most interesting fashion choices come in a second-act sequence where Josie meets her soon-to-be schoolmates. All rich, entitled, and vicious, these teens are all dressed in garish textures, vibrant colors, and/or outrageous silhouettes meant to suggest they're wealthy and shallow. A curious choice from a filmmaker who has attended the Met Gala.

Otherwise, the score by Michael Giacchino is cloyingly sentimental, desperately flagging to the audience a scene is meant to be moving when even on the page it must have been flat. For instance, Waititi and Waller set up that Josie might be jealous of Klara. But rather than a moving scene of conflict, they dodge it twice, including a disturbing revelation sequence that should hit harder.

Simply put, Waititi utterly fails to adapt this book into a compelling movie. His screenplay is childish and plodding. His visual storytelling is lackluster and baffling. His leading lady throws herself into physical comedy and a taboo topic to show her range. But Waititi's direction makes Ortega look like she's auditioning for a Disney Channel TV movie from the early '90s.

The best Ortega's fans can do for her is to politely look away and forget Klara and the Sun ever happened.

However, that same impulse can homogenize the gaming experience. Think of all the big-budget shooters that play identically, or the glossy adventure games that signpost every step with yellow paint so players can’t possibly get lost. Look at the likes of Insomniac’s recent Marvel’s Wolverine­—rumored to have a development budget in the hundreds of millions—which launched to solid sales (analyst estimates suggest 1.9 million copies sold in its first three days) but tepid reviews and lukewarm player response, with many critiquing its linear progression and repetitive hack-and-slash gameplay.

The bigger games have gotten, the more time they demand from players too, and rarely in meaningful ways. Open-world games in particular feel increasingly crammed with time-eating diversions—Assassin’s Creed Valhalla notoriously needed 150-plus hours to see everything its accurately crafted bleak medieval English settlements had to offer. But much of it felt like busywork to showcase Ubisoft’s investment in creating such realistic environments.

Such virtual spaces are often impressive, yes, but will Grand Theft Auto VI be better with a world map reportedly three times larger than the already colossal Red Dead Redemption 2? Is it worth building something of that size if players don’t have time to experience most of it?

The solution to runaway costs, developer security, and the limited time players have to sink into games should be clear: aim smaller.

That’s not to say the entire industry should pivot toward indie-level games (though there are some absolute greats in that area), but rather that publishers should refocus on games that can still showcase their creators’ talents and offer satisfying but digestible experiences to players, without pouring dangerous sums of money into ever bigger, ever riskier titles. In short, rediscover the “AA”-scale game.

The textbook example of how successful this can be is Clair Obscur: Expedition 33. The debut game from French developer Sandfall Interactive appeared practically out of nowhere in April 2025, and exploded. The RPG earned rapturous reviews and endless fan acclaim, celebrated for its challenging dodge-and-parry twist on turn-based combat, its stunning world—a breathtaking blend of belle epoque and dark fantasy imagery, wrapped in a gorgeous painterly flourish—and its mature and emotionally complex story of explorers battling the inevitability of death.

In technology and production quality terms, it’s no slouch for a “smaller” game, either. Clair Obscur has motion-capture performances, a voice cast featuring Hollywood heavyweights like Charlie Cox and Andy Serkis, a full orchestral score, and dazzling visuals. It even packs a solid 60-odd hours of playtime, making it feel significant and not daunting. It ultimately swept up over 500 Game of the Year Awards and went on to sell 8 million copies as of April 2026.

While that number pales in comparison to Battlefield 6’s 7 million units in mere days, Clair Obscur did it all on a budget of less than $10 million, and with a tiny development staff. The game was hugely successful for its budget, letting the studio continue without resorting to painful layoffs. Sandfall’s success isn’t a one-off. Look at Supergiant Games’ Hades duology, AdHoc Studio’s Dispatch, or Mintrocket’s Dave the Diver for more evidence that studios and labels should focus on smaller but richer gaming experiences.

Another major benefit of aiming smaller is that it lets companies mitigate rising development costs for themselves and consumers. The memory crisis is forcibly extending the hardware life cycle, delaying new console generations and blocking supplies of powerful PC graphics cards, leaving many players without the means to reap the benefits of the most demanding games.

As Final Fantasy VII Revelation director Naoki Hamaguchi—no stranger to hugely ambitious games with incredibly long development cycles—says, “There isn't a single console that you can say, ‘As long as I have this, I'll be able to play everything, to do everything.’ Personally, I think that over the course of the next 10 years, if someone said, ‘Here's the next generation of consoles,’ the market and the audience are not necessarily going to just jump over.”

Hamaguchi says the industry will still straddle the lower-spec options, like a Steam Deck or Nintendo Switch 2, providing broader experiences to a wider base. It won't be so much about cutting-edge technology, but how developers can compete in game design. "I think we're going to be entering an age where that's really the focus of competition,” he says.

Sadly, this approach has one big problem: Developers are already trying it but failing to draw investment. There’s no easy answer—investors follow the money, and the money points to GTA-scale games, even though AAA development increasingly leaves carnage in its wake.

Even Rockstar appears to realize the current approach is unsustainable, with studio veteran Phil Hooker telling Bloomberg that “the engine, the tools, and the team we've built during the [GTA VI] project will allow us to do things more quickly” in the future. Hopefully, good news for fans who fear another decade-long wait for GTA VII.

Grand Theft Auto VI will be a success, and its longevity is already certain. But when every other publisher sees the inevitable slate of sales records the game will shatter, they’d do well to think twice before chasing it—the games industry might not survive if they do.

#382. US sanctions target Iran's rail sector as Tehran shifts cargo from sea routes to overland corridors

As US sanctions expand to Iran's rail sector, Tehran is relying more heavily on overland links with China and Central Asia. But capacity constraints, delays and costs limit rail's ability to replace maritime trade.

The United States has opened another front in its economic pressure campaign against Iran, this time targeting the railways Tehran increasingly relies on as maritime trade becomes more difficult.

On October 1, the US Treasury sanctioned three Iranian railway companies alongside seven automakers and five foreign suppliers of automotive parts as part of its Operation Economic Outcast.

Washington says Iran's rail and automotive industries provide revenue and logistical capacity to the state and are connected to networks involving the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC).

The timing of the move is significant. Since the US maritime blockade sharply restricted Iran's southern trade routes, Tehran has tried to move more goods through its land borders and railway connections with China, Central Asia, Russia and Turkey.

Rail transport has become more important just as Washington decided to target it.

Shahriar Naghizadeh, director general for foreign trade at Iran's railway authority, was quoted by the semi-official Mehr news agency as saying that the country has around 15,000 kilometers (9,300 miles) of main and secondary railway lines, more than 35,000 wagons and nearly 500 diesel locomotives.

The network carried around 44 million tons of freight in the Iranian year ending in March 2026, up from about 40 million tons the previous year, according to Naghizadeh. And authorities are targeting 54 million tons this year.

Iran is linked to neighboring markets through 13 rail crossings, providing overland access to Turkey, Azerbaijan, Afghanistan, Pakistan and onward routes to Central Asia and China via Turkmenistan.

The new US sanctions raise questions about how foreign railway operators, freight forwarders, banks and insurers will continue dealing with Iranian rail companies.

Bloomberg reported in May that freight trains traveling from the Chinese city of Xi'an to Tehran had increased from around one per week before the US blockade to roughly one every three or four days.

In September, a train left China carrying automotive parts, consumer goods and other cargo. It ran through Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan before entering Iran at Sarakhs with an expected transit time of 14 to 16 days.

It estimated that rail transit along the main route from western China to Tehran would take 50-60 days under current conditions. Road transport along a similar route is around 22 to 25 days.

The gap between a scheduled 14-day transit time and commercial estimates of up to 60 days highlights one of the central challenges facing Iran.

Customs inspections, gauge changes, payment problems and sanctions can all be another potential point of delay.

Increasing the number of trains also requires more locomotives, wagons and cooperation from several transit countries.

Alireza Salavati, managing director of London-based Middle East Analytica, said rail is increasingly useful for Iran but should not be mistaken as a replacement for maritime trade.

"A workable overland route now links China and Iran, but I would not call it a replacement for sea freight," he told DW.

Under normal conditions, ships can move far larger quantities at lower unit costs. Rail becomes more attractive for goods where speed and continuity matter more than the cheapest transport.

Salavati argues that the effect on companies cannot be measured only in freight charges.

Cutting the journey to 15 days could reduce that amount significantly, depending on payment arrangements. The reverse can be much more damaging.

#381. A new approach to a Covid-19 nasal vaccine shows early promise

Scientists in Germany say they’ve been able to make a nasal vaccine that can shut down a Covid-19 infection in the nose and throat, where the virus gets its first foothold in the body.

In experiments in hamsters, two doses of the vaccine – which is made with a live but weakened form of the coronavirus that causes Covid-19 – blocked the virus from copying itself in the animals’ upper airways, achieving “sterilizing immunity” and preventing illness, a long-sought goal of the pandemic.

WHO experts revise Covid-19 vaccine advice, say healthy kids and teens low risk

Although this vaccine has several more hurdles to clear before it gets to a doctor’s office or drug store, other nasal vaccines are in use or are nearing the finish line in clinical trials.

China and India both rolled out vaccines given through the nasal tissues last fall, though it’s not clear how well they may be working. Studies on the effectiveness of these vaccines have yet to be published, leaving much of the world to wonder whether this approach to protection really works in people.

The US has reached something of a stalemate with Covid-19. Even with the darkest days of the pandemic behind us, hundreds of Americans are still dying daily as the infection continues to simmer in the background of our return to normal life.

As long as the virus continues to spread among people and animals, there’s always the potential for it mutate into a more contagious or more damaging version of itself. And while Covid infections have become manageable for most healthy people, they may still pose a danger to vulnerable groups such as the elderly and immunocompromised.

Researchers hope next-generation Covid-19 vaccines, which aim to shut down the virus before it ever gets a chance to make us sick and ultimately prevent the spread of infection, could make our newest resident respiratory infection less of a threat.

China approves its first mRNA Covid-19 vaccine

One way scientists are trying to do that is by boosting mucosal immunity, beefing up immune defenses in the tissues that line the upper airways, right where the virus would land and begin to infect our cells.

It’s a bit like stationing firefighters underneath the smoke alarm in your house, says study author Emanuel Wyler, a scientist at the Max Delbruck Center for Molecular Medicine in the Helmholtz Association in Berlin.

The immunity that’s created by shots works throughout the body, but it resides primarily in the blood. That means it may take longer to mount a response.

“If they are already on site, they can immediately eliminate the fire, but if they’re like 2 miles away, they first need to drive there, and by that time, one-third of the house is already in full flames,” Wyler said.

Mucosal vaccines are also better at priming a different kind of first responder than injections do. They do a better job of summoning IgA antibodies, which have four arms to grab onto invaders instead of the two arms that the y-shaped IgG antibodies have. Some scientists think IgA antibodies may be less picky about their targets than IgG antibodies, which makes them better equipped to deal with new variants.

The new nasal vaccine takes a new approach to a very old idea: weakening a virus so it’s no longer a threat and then giving it to people so their immune systems can learn to recognize and fight it off. The first vaccines using this approach date to the 1870s, against anthrax and rabies. Back then, scientists weakened the agents they were using with heat and chemicals.

The researchers manipulated the genetic material in the virus to make it harder for cells to translate. This technique, called codon pair deoptimization, hobbles the virus so it can be shown to the immune system without making the body sick.

“You could imagine reading a text … and every letter is a different font, or every letter is a different size, then the text is much harder to read. And this is basically what we do in codon pair deoptimization,” Wyler said.

In the hamster studies, which were published Monday in the journal Nature Microbiology, two doses of the live but weakened nasal vaccine created a much stronger immune response than either two doses of an mRNA-based vaccine or one that uses an adenovirus to ferry the vaccine instructions into cells.

The researchers think the live weakened vaccine probably worked better because it closely mimics the process of a natural infection.

The nasal vaccine also previews the entire coronavirus for the body, not just its spike proteins like current Covid-19 vaccines do, so the hamsters were able to make immune weapons against a wider range of targets.

As promising as all this sounds, vaccine experts say caution is warranted. This vaccine still has to pass more tests before it’s ready for use, but they say the results look encouraging.

Health officials consider another round of bivalent boosters for the most vulnerable Americans

“They did a very nice job. This is obviously a competent and thoughtful team that did this work, and impressive in the scope of what they did. Now it just needs to be repeated,” perhaps in primates and certainly in humans before it can be widely used, said Dr. Greg Poland, who designs vaccines at the Mayo Clinic. He was not involved in the new research.

The study began in 2021, before the Omicron variant was around, so the vaccine tested in these experiments was made with the original strain of the coronavirus. In the experiments, when they infected animals with Omicron, the live but weakened nasal vaccine still performed better than the others, but its ability to neutralize the virus was diminished. Researchers think it will need an update.

It also needs to be tested in humans, and Wyler says they’re working on that. The scientists have partnered with a Swiss company called RocketVax to start phase I clinical trials.

Other vaccines are further along, but the progress has been “slow and halting,” Poland said. Groups working on these vaccines are struggling to raise the steep costs of getting a new vaccine to market, and they’re doing it in a setting where people tend to think the vaccine race has been won and done.

In reality, Poland said, we’re far from that. All it would take is another Omicron-level shift in the evolution of the virus, and we could be back at square one, with no effective tools against the coronavirus.

“That’s foolish. We should be developing a pan-coronavirus vaccine that does induce mucosal immunity and that is long-lived,” he said.

At least four nasal vaccines for Covid-19 have reached late-stage testing in people, according to the World Health Organization’s vaccine tracker.

The nasal vaccines in use in China and India rely on harmless adenoviruses to ferry their instructions into cells, although effectiveness data for these has not been published.

Two other nasal vaccines are finishing human studies.

Covid vaccine maker Novavax says it may not survive

One, a recombinant vaccine that can be produced cheaply in chicken eggs, the same way many flu vaccines are, is being put through its paces by researchers at Mount Sinai in New York City.

Another, like the German vaccine, uses a live but weakened version of the virus. It’s being developed by a company called Codagenix. Results of those studies, which were carried out in South America and Africa, may come later this year.

The German team says it’s eagerly watching for the Codagenix data.

“They will be very important in order to know where whether this kind of attempt is basically promising or not,” Wyler said.

They have reason to worry. Respiratory infections have proved to be tough targets for inhaled vaccines.

FluMist, a live but weakened form of the flu virus, works reasonably well in children but doesn’t help adults as much. The reason is thought to be that adults already have immune memory for the flu, and when the virus is injected into the nose, the vaccine mostly boosts what’s already there.

Another consideration is that live vaccines can’t be taken by everyone. People with very compromised immunity are often cautioned against using live vaccines because even these very weakened viruses may be risky for them.

“Although it’s strongly attenuated, it’s still a real virus,” Wyler said, so it would have to be used carefully.

Novo on Tuesday said its next-generation obesity drug, CagriSema, showed 25% weight loss on average among adult women in a new analysis conducted after a late-stage trial.

The Danish company said further work is needed to confirm the hypotheses of two post-study analyses. Still, the results are the latest evidence that the experimental injection can improve upon the weight loss and other benefits offered by Novo's GLP-1 drug semaglutide, the active ingredient in its blockbuster obesity shot Wegovy and diabetes counterpart Ozempic.

CagriSema, a combination of semaglutide and another drug called cagrilintide that targets the amylin pathway, is expected to launch early next year. Obesity drug leaders Novo and Eli Lilly have built new treatments around amylin, which involves a hormone released in the pancreas alongside insulin that helps regulate hunger and fullness, hoping it will give them a new revenue stream beyond existing GLP-1s.

In an analysis of roughly 2,300 women with obesity or who are overweight, CagriSema was associated with 25% weight loss on average at 68 weeks compared with 3% with placebo. The results are based on an ideal scenario in which all patients stayed on treatment without interruptions.

A second analysis of around 1,900 women grouped by their menopausal status found that weight loss reached 26.7%, 24.8% and 23.1% in premenopausal, perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, respectively, at 68 weeks, if all patients remained on the drug. That compares with approximately 2% across menopause groups with a placebo.

Across both analyses, CagriSema was linked to reductions in abdominal fat, based on waist circumference, among women overall and across menopause stages, Novo added.

The results suggest that women may experience slightly more weight loss on CagriSema than a broader population of both men and women with obesity saw in the Phase 3 trial.

Initial results from the study showed that CagriSema helped adults lose 22.7% of their weight on average at 68 weeks versus 2.3% in the placebo group. However, the majority of patients in the trial — around 67% of roughly 3,400 — were women.

Weight gain is very common for women during menopause. Declining estrogen levels can contribute to a slowdown in metabolism, making it easier for women to gain weight and harder to maintain muscle mass. After menopause, estrogen generally remains low and does not return to previous levels.

More than 86 million women in the U.S. are 40 and older, according to the U.S. Census Bureau, which are the years in which perimenopause, menopause and postmenopause occur.

"Many women with obesity or overweight experience weight gain as they move through menopause, and it is important to explore investigational products such as CagriSema across the reproductive lifespan," said Michelle Skinner Moore, Novo's interim head of clinical development, medical and regulatory affairs.

Wall Street has not appeared particularly excited about CagriSema, whose previous late-stage clinical trial results disappointed investors because the drug showed weight loss efficacy below their expectations. Novo, however, maintains CagriSema is effective and differentiated enough from its other drugs to draw significant demand.

Analysts expect CagriSema to generate $11.84 billion in sales for obesity specifically in 2028, according to a consensus estimate compiled by FactSet.

#373. A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea and unleashed a 330-foot tsunami

A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea roughly 43 to 46 million years ago, triggering a tsunami more than 100 meters (330 feet) high. Now, after more than two decades of scientific disagreement, researchers have uncovered compelling evidence confirming the ancient collision.

The impact created what is now known as the Silverpit Crater, a massive structure buried deep beneath the seafloor. Although scientists first discovered it in 2002, its origins remained fiercely debated, with some researchers arguing that geological processes, rather than an object from space, were responsible.

A research team led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson of Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh has now provided the strongest evidence yet that Silverpit was formed by an asteroid or comet. By combining advanced seismic imaging, microscopic examination of rock samples, and computer simulations, the scientists have reconstructed the violent event and identified minerals that could only have formed under extraordinary pressure.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, establish Silverpit as one of the relatively few confirmed impact craters on Earth.

A Mysterious Crater Beneath the North Sea

The Silverpit Crater lies approximately 700 meters beneath the seabed in the southern North Sea, about 80 miles off the coast of Yorkshire, England. The crater itself measures roughly three kilometers across and is surrounded by a much larger, 20-kilometer-wide region of circular faults, which are fractures in the rock where movement has occurred.

When scientists first identified the formation in 2002, several features suggested that it had been created by a high-speed collision with an asteroid or comet.

Its nearly circular outline, elevated central peak, and surrounding rings of faults resembled structures found at other impact sites. Central peaks can develop when rocks compressed by a powerful collision rebound upward, somewhat like the way water rises in the center after an object splashes into a pool.

Some geologists proposed that the unusual formation resulted from the movement of underground salt deposits, which can deform the rock layers above them. Others suggested that volcanic activity had caused part of the seabed to collapse, producing a structure that only appeared to be an impact crater.

The disagreement persisted for years. In 2009, geologists even voted on the competing explanations, as documented in Geoscientist magazine. Most participants rejected the asteroid impact hypothesis.

The latest findings show that the impact explanation was correct.

Scientists Find the Smoking Gun for an Ancient Asteroid Impact

The breakthrough came from combining newly available seismic images with microscopic evidence recovered from beneath the seabed.

Seismic imaging allows geologists to investigate structures hidden underground by analyzing how sound waves travel through different layers of rock. This technique can reveal buried faults, craters, and other formations that cannot be observed directly.

Using the improved data, Nicholson and his colleagues were able to examine Silverpit's internal structure in unprecedented detail.

But the most decisive evidence came from tiny mineral crystals discovered in rock cuttings collected from an oil well near the crater.

Dr. Uisdean Nicholson, a sedimentologist in Heriot-Watt University's School of Energy, Geoscience, Infrastructure and Society, said: "New seismic imaging has given us an unprecedented look at the crater.

"Samples from an oil well in the area also revealed rare 'shocked' quartz and feldspar crystals at the same depth as the crater floor.

"We were exceptionally lucky to find these -- a real 'needle-in-a-haystack' effort. These prove the impact crater hypothesis beyond doubt, because they have a fabric that can only be created by extreme shock pressures."

Shocked minerals are particularly important because their internal crystal structures have been altered by immense pressure. Such changes can occur when an asteroid strikes at tremendous speed, generating powerful shock waves that pass through the surrounding rock.

Ordinary geological processes generally cannot produce these distinctive microscopic features. Their discovery at the same depth as the crater floor provides strong physical evidence that an extremely violent impact occurred.

A 160-Meter Asteroid Unleashed a Monster Tsunami

With the crater's origins established, researchers used numerical models, or computer simulations based on physical laws, to reconstruct what happened during the collision.

Their results suggest that an asteroid approximately 160 meters across approached from the west and struck the seabed at a shallow angle.

Although much smaller than the asteroid associated with the extinction of the dinosaurs, an object of this size could still release an enormous amount of energy when traveling at cosmic speeds.

According to the simulations, the impact violently displaced vast quantities of rock and seawater, producing a towering curtain of debris before the material collapsed back into the ocean.

Dr. Nicholson said: "Our evidence shows that a 160-meter-wide asteroid hit the seabed at a low angle from the west.

"Within minutes, it created a 1.5-kilometer-high curtain of rock and water that then collapsed into the sea, creating a tsunami over 100 meters high."

That means the collision sent material nearly a mile into the air before it fell back into the sea, generating a tsunami taller than a 30-story building.

Unlike ordinary wind-driven waves, tsunamis involve the movement of enormous volumes of water. They are typically generated when a sudden disturbance, such as an earthquake, underwater landslide, or asteroid impact, rapidly displaces the water above it.

The estimated height of Silverpit's tsunami comes from computer modeling of the ancient impact rather than direct observation. Nevertheless, the reconstruction illustrates just how destructive even a relatively modest asteroid can be when it strikes the ocean.

A Scientific Debate Finally Settled After Decades

For Professor Gareth Collins of Imperial College London, the discovery brings a satisfying conclusion to a controversy he witnessed firsthand.

Collins participated in the 2009 debate over Silverpit's origins and contributed the numerical simulations used in the new research.

Professor Collins said: "I always thought that the impact hypothesis was the simplest explanation and most consistent with the observations.

"It is very rewarding to have finally found the silver bullet. We can now get on with the exciting job of using the amazing new data to learn more about how impacts shape planets below the surface, which is really hard to do on other planets. "

The findings do more than settle an old argument. They also give scientists a valuable opportunity to study the underground damage caused by high-speed asteroid impacts.

Because much of Silverpit's structure remains buried and well preserved, researchers can investigate how the collision fractured, displaced, and reshaped the surrounding geological layers.

These observations could help scientists interpret impact structures on other worlds, where the subsurface effects of collisions are often much more difficult to examine.

Earth has experienced countless asteroid and comet impacts throughout its history, but relatively few recognizable craters remain.

Weathering, erosion, sediment deposition, and plate tectonics continually reshape the planet's surface. Over millions of years, these processes can wear down, bury, or completely destroy evidence of ancient collisions.

As a result, exceptionally preserved impact sites like Silverpit are scientifically valuable.

Dr. Nicholson said, "Silverpit is a rare and exceptionally preserved hypervelocity impact crater.

"These are rare because the Earth is such a dynamic planet -- plate tectonics and erosion destroy almost all traces of most of these events.

"Around 200 confirmed impact craters exist on land, and only about 33 have been identified beneath the ocean.

"We can use these findings to understand how asteroid impacts shaped our planet throughout history, as well as predict what could happen should we have an asteroid collision in future."

Hypervelocity impacts occur when objects strike at extremely high speeds, typically several kilometers per second. At those velocities, even an asteroid measuring only a few hundred meters across can deliver a devastating amount of energy.

With its origins now confirmed, Silverpit joins a small group of important underwater and buried impact structures, including the Chicxulub Crater in Mexico, associated with the mass extinction that wiped out the nonavian dinosaurs, and the Nadir Crater off the coast of West Africa, another recently confirmed asteroid impact site.

Together, these craters provide evidence of the powerful role cosmic collisions have played in shaping Earth's geological and biological history.

By studying Silverpit's remarkably preserved features, scientists hope to better understand what happens when an asteroid strikes the ocean, how the surrounding rock responds, and what such an event might mean for Earth in the future.

#362. Apple iPhone Duo Review: Is It Worth It?

It’s a little hard to review the iPhone Duo. I’ve been toting Apple’s first folding smartphone for the past week, and several apps I’ve tried to open on the sprawling 7.6-inch screen look awful. Thick, black bars on the sides, with a phone-sized app in the middle of this tablet-like display. But you likely won’t encounter this problem once the Duo is in your hands.

In the past day, a handful of apps I use regularly updated to add full support for the iPhone Duo’s screen sizes and vertical user interface elements. Instagram, Threads, Slack, and even the Google app now have navigational icons on the right rail instead of the bottom center, and the app content is better laid out to make use of the larger screen.

Developers need to target the iOS 27.1 software development kit (SDK) for full Duo compatibility. That will take some time. But it is a little maddening that some app developers, who for years have never seemed to care about optimizing their app for Android folding phones, are so quick to embrace Apple’s fold. Slack, for example, never offered the two-pane view of its desktop app in the Android version, but it’s on the Duo. C’est la vie. Hopefully, Apple’s entry into foldables will result in a “rising tide lifts all boats” situation and Android fold lovers will get to enjoy those optimizations.

The iPhone Duo’s Standby mode turns on when the phone is propped up via a kickstand.

The iPhone Duo arrives seven years after Samsung’s first Galaxy Fold. In Apple tradition, its first entry into an existing category feels fully mature; the Duo has significantly less jank than the first few generations of its Android counterparts. The infamous crease—where the phone folds—is hardly visible thanks to Apple’s display stack and clever use of a matte-finish nanotexture screen. The phone is IP68 dust- and water-resistant (though avoid taking the Duo to the beach, as sand is notoriously detrimental to phone hinges). The software experience, barring the unoptimized apps, feels carefully considered.

Apple has also landed on the right shape, months after Samsung realized it following seven years of narrow, somewhat awkward book-like folds. The wider, shorter “passport” design feels better than what’s come before in this category. The 5.4-inch outer screen is still big enough to be useful, and the hinge glides open gracefully and stays put at various angles. The hardware feels polished.

That’s not to say Apple has completely trounced the competition. I still appreciate that Samsung’s new Galaxy Z Fold8 is super lightweight at 201 grams, as the iPhone Duo’s hefty 254 grams (heavier than an iPhone 18 Pro Max) can get tiring after extended doomscrolling. I don’t love the fact that, when closed, one side of the Duo has angled corners and the other is curved—the Fold8 looks better in that regard. Touch ID also takes a little getting used to after years of relying on Face ID, mostly when you’re holding the Duo with your left hand and you suddenly have to authenticate an app or purchase with your right hand. (It can mercifully auto-unlock if you’re wearing an Apple Watch.)

Apple also tightly controls the multitasking experience. If you have two apps split-screen on the Duo, Apple won’t let you resize one app to be slightly bigger than the other like you can on Android. Here, a 50-50 split is always enforced. You can’t add a floating third app when in split-screen either, even though Apple has offered a similar capability on iPads for years. (The exception is video apps—you can have them float in picture-in-picture mode.) In portrait orientation, you can split-screen only with a video app; Apple won’t let you stack two apps, something Android has enabled for years.

That rigid software philosophy extends to basic input handling, too. I am typing this review on the iPhone Duo using a compact folding keyboard with a tiny attached trackpad. The trackpad works OK, but I could only get it to work after turning on AssistiveTouch in iOS’ accessibility settings. If the Duo is the iPhone that lets you do more, Apple should natively enable cursor support just like it does on iPads.

Then there are the physical quirks. The Duo supports MagSafe, so you can magnetically stick the phone to MagSafe tripods, mounts, and the like. (When it’s open, the Duo’s weight makes the phone sag to one side.) But what about MagSafe wallets? They’ll work, but Apple’s official wallet sticks out past the phone’s lower edge—not what you’d expect from a company that pores over every detail. Apple doesn’t have an official solution, so you’ll have to rely on a third-party accessory (and I suspect there will be many Duo accessories).

The iPhone maker follows the industry standard with a dual rear-camera system, lacking the third zoom camera from its Pro iPhones, which I sorely miss. At least the selfie cameras—the one on the outer screen and the “hidden” under-display inner camera seem to produce good results. On a Zoom call, my editor remarked that the video quality looked “really good” with both selfie shooters. (Zoom, by the way, wasn’t optimized.)

When an app is properly optimized, the experience is chef’s kiss. Multi-pane views let you see a lot more in an app than candy-bar phones, a boost to productivity. Split-screening two optimized apps looks excellent, with plenty of room for each app to breathe and let you multitask. I haven’t found myself putting the Duo in angled positions much, mostly because I’ve really enjoyed using it with Apple’s Folio With Kickstand case, so much so that I wish the kickstand were integrated into the phone itself; I strongly recommend investing in an accessory to prop the Duo up.

Battery life has been pretty great. With average use, I’ve ended most days with around 40 percent left by bedtime. Right now, with over six hours of screen-on time because I’ve been working off the Duo for most of the workday, it’s almost 6 pm, and I’m at 45 percent. Impressive.

As for performance, the only thing I've noticed is the Duo can get toasty. This isn't necessarily tied to a demanding task, though. #355. Honey bees are bullying other bees, scientists discover

Honey bees are proving to be formidable competitors for food in the UK, and scientists have discovered that they frequently use aggression to drive other pollinators away from flowers. New research has documented how honey bees chase, displace, and intimidate rival insects, providing the first detailed evidence of how extensively they interfere with other species' access to food.

The study, conducted by researchers at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) and published in Ecological Entomology, followed pollinator activity over two years at five locations in and around Cambridge, England. Each site was situated within 300 meters of an established honey bee colony.

Researchers observed insects visiting bramble (Rubus fruticosus) and ivy (Hedera helix), two flowering plants that provide important supplies of nectar and pollen. Both attract large numbers of insects during their flowering periods, making them useful places to investigate competition for food.

Honey Bees Dominate the Competition for Flowers

To examine how different pollinators interact, the team divided flower visitors into five groups: honey bees, bumble bees, solitary bees, hoverflies, and an 'other' category containing butterflies, beetles, and wasps.

Over 93 hours of observation, the scientists documented encounters in which one insect interrupted another's feeding or prevented it from reaching a flower. Each interaction was classified according to its outcome. A 'win' occurred when one insect gained access to a flower at the expense of another, which experienced a 'loss' of feeding opportunities. A 'share' meant both insects were able to access the flower without conflict.

The results revealed a clear pattern. Locations with greater concentrations of honey bees generally had fewer other pollinators and experienced more competition for flowers. Honey bees also emerged victorious in a larger proportion of their encounters with other insects.

On bramble flowers, doubling the density of honey bees was associated with a 14% decrease in bumblebee abundance. On ivy, higher honey bee numbers were linked to reduced numbers of solitary bees and hoverflies.

The honey bees did not simply reach flowers before their competitors. They frequently initiated aggressive encounters, driving other insects away to secure feeding opportunities.

Other pollinators actively avoided honey bees in 28% of observed interactions on bramble and 36% of those on ivy. In at least 20% of the encounters honey bees won on either plant, they physically pushed aside or chased away competing insects.

Aggressive behavior initiated by species other than honey bees was uncommon. However, one insect stood out as an exception.

The ivy bee, a solitary species not native to Britain that was first documented in the country in 2001, was the only pollinator observed actively challenging honey bees. Unlike most other insects, ivy bees sometimes defended their feeding spots by chasing or displacing honey bees, successfully competing for access to flowers.

Climate Change Could Make Competition Worse

The findings are part of a broader research effort to understand competition among pollinators and identify ways to reduce its ecological effects.

The results also come as beekeeping continues to expand across the UK. The number of registered beekeepers increased from 19,000 in 2010 to 47,000 last year, reflecting growing numbers of managed honey bee colonies.

Researchers warn that rising temperatures could add to the pressure on wild pollinators. Their observations showed that competitive encounters became more frequent during warmer conditions.

One possible explanation involves changes in flower nectar production. When plants experience 'heat stress', they can produce less nectar, potentially forcing more insects to compete over a smaller supply of food.

Temperature also affects different pollinators in different ways. Bumblebees and hoverflies are sensitive to high temperatures and may forage more effectively in cooler weather. Honey bees, by contrast, generally become more active as temperatures rise. Previous research found that increasing the temperature by 12°C caused a tenfold increase in the number of honey bees leaving their nests.

Together, these differences suggest that continued warming could give honey bees an even greater competitive advantage over some wild pollinators.

Protecting Wild Pollinators as Honey Bee Numbers Grow

Lead author Victoria Buckle, a PhD student at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) in Cambridge, England, emphasized that the research is intended to improve conservation efforts rather than discourage people from keeping honey bees.

"The aim of our study was not to discourage beekeeping, but to better understand how competition happens between wild and managed pollinator species with the aim of helping to conserve our wild pollinators, which play such an important ecological role.

"We found that most interactions when competing for food were instigated by honey bees and they had a greater number of 'wins', resulting in a loss of forage for other pollinators. Bumblebees, solitary bees and hoverflies would avoid, be displaced by and on some occasions be chased off by honey bees.

"An interesting exception was the ivy bee, which would win contests more often against honey bees. It is the first time, to our knowledge, such behaviors have been observed and shows ivy bees are able to 'hold their own' in such encounters.

"However, honey bees outcompeted all other wild pollinators and based on our findings, future predicted warming in the UK is only likely to further exacerbate interference competition from dominant honey bees. Therefore, it's important to find ways to ensure honey bees and wild pollinators can continue to coexist, particularly in locations with a high density of hives, such as urban areas."

#351. A Design Flaw in Share Links Exposes Users’ Personal Conversations With Meta’s Muse

Muse, the new general-purpose AI agent from Meta, can expose sensitive information when users share conversations through web links, researchers reported Monday. In response to questions from Ars about the behavior, Meta said it’s suspending the sharing feature while the company reviews it and considers making changes.

Like many online services, Muse has allowed users to share searches, conversations, images, and similar content with other people. Such share features usually involve clicking an option to create a unique URL that anyone can use to load a page hosting the content. To keep potentially sensitive information from being accessible to unintended people, the URLs typically contain a long sequence of text that can’t be easily guessed.

Muse, which Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg said last month was “built from the ground up for privacy and security,” seems to take the same precautions. But a central design decision for constructing URLs has made the links easy to discover. As a result, researchers from security firm Claroc said they were able to enumerate 6,800 links Muse users created for sharing conversations with others. After opening a few hundred of them, the researchers found information that was clearly not intended to be publicly available, including:

“If you had asked us beforehand, we would have guessed that gathering these would take real effort,” the researchers wrote. “It took one query.”

In an emailed statement responding to questions from Ars, Meta said: “We opened the Muse bug bounty program so researchers could raise different scenarios and help us continue improving the product. People have the ability to share specific Muse artifacts, which creates a page that can be accessed by those with the link. However, we’ve paused this functionality while we review this report and consider changes to how these links can be discovered.”

Asaf Aprozper, a cofounder and CTO of Claroc, said he reported the findings through Meta’s bug bounty program on October 8 and also tried reaching people at Meta on LinkedIn but got no reply either way.

The easiest way to discover the URLs is to perform a passive DNS search through one of many available commercial services. Passive DNS records store the historical resolution of domain names to IP addresses, as well as nameserver, and mail-server records over time. Passive DNS systems typically collect DNS responses from sensors positioned at recursive DNS resolvers or other infrastructure. A passive DNS record is generally created when a monitoring system observes a DNS response. In the case here, once the intended party clicks on the share link, the DNS information is recorded in a searchable database.

Claroc researchers were able to discover the Muse share links because all of them use the domain name cf.metaaiusercontent.com. Discovery is further made easy by the decision to add the conversation name to the URL, along with a long string of text. Simplified examples of such links are home-sale-budget-9f21c8a3.cf.metaaiusercontent.com, q3-revenue-review-7b1e42f0.cf.metaaiusercontent.com, and loan-application-draft-a4c9e2.cf.metaaiusercontent.com. It’s this last design decision, putting the conversation title in the subdomain rather than in the URL path, that makes public discovery possible.

“Plenty of AI assistants share by public link,” Claroc said. “What makes Muse’s shares easy to find is the title sitting inside the web address, where public DNS records it for anyone to search. We call these GhostShares: pages that open for people who were never handed the link, and that those people can find on their own.”

Muse quickly catapulted to one of the top spots in Apple’s App Store. The assistant autonomously fills out forms, books appointments, makes purchases, creates documents, and carries out all kinds of other actions. To work as intended, Muse requires access to most of a user’s accounts. Meta has heavily marketed the assistant as a platform that’s worthy of the extraordinary trust it needs to work as intended.

Three weeks after the Muse launch, macOS security expert Patrick Wardle discovered a zero-day vulnerability that allowed any other app installed on the same device to gain complete control of the user’s Muse account. Meta downplayed the severity of the vulnerability on the grounds that exploitation wasn’t possible unless a device was already compromised. The company didn’t acknowledge Wardle’s finding that a simple ClickFix attack was all that was needed to hijack an account. The ease in discovering share links further justifies asking if Muse is worthy of the access it requires.

With Meta’s suspension of the feature, Muse users no longer face this particular threat, at least for now.
