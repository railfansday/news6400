#5600. Phoenix Reisen Adds Oceania’s Insignia as the Alvara

Phoenix Reisen has confirmed that it will add the Insignia to its fleet, with the ship set to begin sailing for the Bonn-based operator as the Alvara in November 2027.

Built in 1998, the 700-guest vessel currently sails for Oceania Cruises. Terms of the deal were not disclosed.

The ship is scheduled to arrive at the Lloyd Werft shipyard in Bremerhaven in September 2027, where it will be painted in Phoenix’s signature turquoise livery and undergo a series of modifications to bring the onboard experience in line with the rest of the fleet.

“With the Alvara, we are welcoming a fantastic ship into the Phoenix family,” said Managing Director Benjamin Krumpen. “It is exceptionally well-maintained, in excellent technical condition and is already equipped for shore power.”

Operations will be handled by two longtime Phoenix partners, with BSM providing ship management and sea chefs overseeing hotel services.

The Alvara’s inaugural program will include a series of short cruises, a Christmas sailing and two longer voyages in the Mediterranean. The cruises will be featured in the Phoenix magazine, due out Nov. 6, 2026, and the company’s “Seereisen 2028” catalog, due out Dec. 18, 2026. Online bookings open Oct. 23, 2026.

Following company tradition, the christening will not coincide with the maiden voyage. Instead, the ship will be named in Hamburg on Nov. 26, 2027, which marks the 54th anniversary of Phoenix Reisen’s founding.

#5599. Scientists Are Rewinding the Biological Clock Inside Human Eyes—and Patients Say They Can See Again

Epigenetic reprogramming is rewriting what we thought we knew about permanent vision loss.

The eye is a complicated organ, and damage or disease to any piece of this optical puzzle can have serious consequences. The build-up of protein and lipid deposits, known more commonly as drusen, starves retinal epithelium cells, leading to macular degeneration. An accumulation of proteins behind the eye’s lens can lead to cataracts. Random genetic mutations can kill light-sensitive photoreceptors, inflicting retinitis pigmentosa. Fluid buildup in the eye can damage the optic nerve and the retinal ganglion cells that feed it. The result is glaucoma, which slowly erodes the peripheral vision of millions of Americans.

All of these maladies are difficult to treat in different ways, but the era of regenerative medicine and gene therapy holds immense promise for each one. For example, scientists worldwide are racing to use stem cells to replace—or even rejuvenate—cells damaged by macular degeneration and retinitis pigmentosa. And now, Life Biosciences has administered a gene therapy to patients in hopes that epigenetic reprogramming can turn back the clock on aging ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve fiber layer and whose dysfunction drives glaucoma.

“While this Phase 1 study is designed to evaluate safety and tolerability, the preliminary observations of visual field improvements in two participants are encouraging as we continue to advance the ER-100 program,” Sharon Rosenzweig-Lipson, chief scientific officer at Life Biosciences, said in a press statement. “This milestone marks an important step in Life Bio’s progress translating epigenetic restoration into a new class of medicines for age-related diseases.”

This gene therapy, known as ER-100, is based on the work of David Sinclair, a geneticist at Harvard Medical School and co-founder of the Boston-based start-up. In 2020, Sinclair, along with lead author Yuancheng Lu and a team of colleagues from Harvard Medical School, published a study in the journal Nature detailing how the expression of three transcription factor genes restored youthful attributes to the ganglion cells of mice models. Time Magazine reports that the technique used six years later in human subjects remains remarkably similar, and eight weeks after receiving the initial treatment, two of the three participants showed meaningful improvement.

“In places where they weren’t seeing as well, or weren’t identifying lights, we are now seeing better evidence that they can,” Rosenzweig-Lipson tells Time. Similarly, in a phase 1/2a medical trial for treating macular degeneration with transplanted stem cells at the University of Michigan, researchers were initially only concerned with proving the safety of the procedure, but found that participants were already reporting significant vision improvements.

In a recent interview with Innovation & Tech, Sinclair also states that this approach could have therapeutic advantages far beyond just the eye.

“The basic machinery we are engaging exists in cells throughout the body, and age-related epigenetic changes occur across tissues, so the underlying principle should not be unique to the eye,” he says. “We are already seeing encouraging preclinical signals in very different organs, including the liver, which suggests this could become a platform, rather than a medicine only for the eye.”

For the entirety of human history, vision loss has been an inevitable byproduct of aging. But by unlocking the regenerative power of biology, what was once inevitable is quickly becoming less so.

#5598. Peek into hidden worlds revealed by the 2026 Nikon Small World contest

Winners include ink droplets frozen in flight and what a molting tardigrade leaves behind

From ink droplets in flight to a tardigrade shedding its skin, images honored in the 2026 Nikon Small World competition reveal details and fleeting moments usually hidden from view.

From left: Ole Bielfeldt; Dr. Gonzalo Quiroga Artigas; Eric Epstein; Satu Paavonsalo & Dr. Sinem Karaman; Faith Fernandes & Dr. James Glover; John-Oliver Dum

Nestled within a rebuilt inkjet printer, droplets of ink hang suspended above a page. Seen through a microscope, the droplets reveal how an inkjet printer produces crisp, uniform letters.

Ole Bielfeldt, a design student in Cologne, Germany, has been photographing the microscopic world since about 2021. His interest began as a quarantine hobby, when an old school microscope from his attic gave him a way to explore while stuck at home during the pandemic.

His printer image won first place in the 2026 Nikon Small World photomicrography competition. To capture the winning photo, he built a custom rig that moved the paper instead of the printer’s ink nozzles, keeping the nozzles stationary and in focus.

Ole Bielfeldt created this custom rig to catch impossibly fast ink droplets midflight. The contraption is only a bit larger than his head. Swipe through to see what word the printer was spitting out ink to spell.

Bielfeldt estimates that the droplets traveled at nearly 20 meters per second — too fast for even his best slow-motion camera to resolve. To freeze their motion, Bielfeldt worked in darkness, illuminating the scene with a single brief flash. He instructed the printer to repeatedly spell out “macro,” a nod to his social media handle, macrofying.

Before this project, a printer was “like magic,” Bielfeldt says. Photographing its inner workings helped him understand how it operates.

Bielfeldt no longer takes the magic printers spit out for granted. “It’s so, so advanced technically, but at the same time it’s so, so common,” he says.

His photograph is one of 86 images recognized in this year’s competition, whose winners were announced October 21. Here are some of our other favorites.

This pollen-dusted jumping spider offers a close look at the bristling hairs and some of its eight eyes that are easy to miss with the naked eye.

John-Oliver Dum’s fascination with spiders began as a childhood fear. Dum says he suffered a particularly painful arachnid-inflicted bite when he was young. Several years ago, when he was inspired to study the movements of a curly hair tarantula (Tliltocatl albopilosus), Dum became passionate about capturing their beauty.

Preparations for his photographs can begin years before he submits them to a competition. His wife grows plants selected for their pollen, which Dum uses in his images.

“The biggest challenge was finding a spider like this on the right plants, with the right pollen attached to it, at exactly the right time of year and at exactly the right moment,” he says. “You need to understand not only the spider’s behavior, but also the surrounding insect and plant life and their seasonal rhythms.”

Dum hopes the photograph, which won second place, encourages viewers to look more closely, too. “We first have to see the beauty of this largely unknown world before we can truly understand what we may be destroying,” he says.

It’s not a leaf and it’s not a flower. This cross section of a mouse eye is crisscrossed in fractals of blood vessels and tethered by the optic nerve.

Cell biologists Sinem Karaman and Satu Paavonsalo at the University of Helsinki visualize vascular networks, like blood vessels, to study how they work within tissues. The arrangement, Paavonsalo says, could even point to mechanisms underlying how these systems stop working in obesity and diabetes.

Paavonsalo says the duo set out to capture a mouse eye in 3-D, different from the flattened retinas typically used in her research. After meticulous mounting which required several attempts for an arrangement she was satisfied with, Paavonsalo still didn’t know for certain the image would turn out right.

“I had to hope that the sample was correctly positioned and that the staining was detected throughout the sample,” Paavonsalo says, which made her excitement upon seeing the image even more pronounced. The image won 19th place in the competition.

A water bear emerges from its shed cuticle, and oh! What’s that left behind? This egg will be protected by its mother’s discarded skin.

The tardigrade (Hypsibius exemplaris) is one of nature’s toughest creatures, able to survive space by going into a state of suspended animation. This species, emerging from its former skin, is only about two-and-a-half times as wide as a strand of hair. Evolutionary developmental biologist Gonzalo Quiroga-Artigas captured the image during his studies of these tiny wonders at the University of Montpellier in France.

In the tardigrade life cycle, “egg laying is closely synchronized with molting,” says Quiroga-Artigas. Tardigrades can lay up to about a dozen eggs at once, and the moment one sheds its old cuticle happens quickly enough that it’s easy to miss.

Snapping this image demanded painstaking mounting and imaging of many specimens. Quiroga-Artigas chose this particular one, which won 12th, because “the egg looks almost like a precious gem being left behind by its mother.”

This slime mold (Hemitrichia sp.) was found in the heart of Brooklyn, N.Y., sprouting from a decaying log in Prospect Park. The image features the mold’s gelatinous fruiting bodies, which release spores as part of reproduction. To bring the delicate structures into sharp detail, Eric Epstein stacked 240 images.

First, Epstein searched local parks for slime molds to photograph, carefully cutting pieces of waterlogged wood free without disturbing his subjects. “In this liquidy state, any touch would demolish” the fruiting bodies, he says.

One species of slime mold closely mirrors the cosmic web of gas and dark matter connecting galaxies, which helps scientists to more accurately map the cosmos. Another species has fascinated researchers for its ability to pack a store of bacteria to feed the next generation, when it’s near death.

Epstein hopes the photograph hints “at [a] vast array of activity beyond our unaided perception.” The image won sixth place in the competition.

This cell could have become anything in the body. Through some scientific nudging, it burst into a brilliant neuron.

Clusters of nuclei, looking like flashpoints in an intricate web, were captured by undergraduate student Faith Fernandes and structural cell biologist James Glover at the University of California, Berkeley.

The image commemorates the team’s first attempt at crafting neurons from a particular kind of stem cell. “I was so instantly enamored with this piece that I made it my computer wallpaper as soon as it was taken,” Fernandes says.

The eruption of color depicts human induced pluripotent stem cells, which can become any type of cell in the body, that have developed into neurons. The neurotubules, which contribute to the structure of and transportation of intracellular molecules, appear blue. Orange indicates where the DNA is located.

The stem cells are created by reprogramming adult cells to a stage before the cell was locked into a path to become a specific type of cell. They are studied for their many potential applications in regenerative medicine.

Fernandes says she hopes that studying these cells will illuminate ways to address neurodegeneration in diseases such as Parkinson’s disease. Already, researchers have used these cells to regrow eye tissue, address premature aging in mouse studies and grow organs. The shot won an Image of Distinction title in the competition.

#5597. Recycling has a hidden problem that crosses international borders

Shipping recyclable waste overseas may reduce local recycling in importing countries, raising questions about the true benefits of global recycling trade.

Recycling asks us to spend a little extra time on things we no longer want.

We rinse containers, flatten boxes and separate packaging, hoping those small efforts will keep useful materials out of the trash.

Once the collection truck leaves, most of the process happens out of sight. A bottle or bundle of paper may travel to another country, where a factory can turn it into something new.

That journey seems to fulfill the promise of recycling. Yet a new economic model suggests that sending waste abroad can leave the world recycling no more than before.

The catch lies in what happens at the other end. Imported recyclables may take the place of waste that the receiving country would otherwise collect from its own homes and businesses.

Countries buy recyclable waste because their factories need material. Paper, metal or plastic that one country wants to get rid of may be useful somewhere else.

A country that buys many manufactured products but makes relatively few can end up with more waste than its factories need. Another country may have busy factories but less waste available to supply them.

Economists Eugénie Joltreau and Luis Sarmiento examined how this mismatch shapes the recycling trade. Both are affiliated with the RFF-CMCC European Institute on Economics and the Environment in Milan.

Their study combined international trade records with a model of how countries handle waste.

The records showed that differences between what countries discard and what their factories need help explain where recyclables go.

Before anyone can recycle a bottle, someone has to collect it. That step can be surprisingly expensive, especially when waste comes from remote areas or households reluctant to sort it.

The study cites an example from France: in 2014, collecting packaging waste separately cost more than twice as much per ton as recycling it. Gathering the material was the bigger expense.

For a country whose factories need more material, buying waste already collected abroad can cost less than expanding collection at home.

The imported material supplies those factories, while some local waste stays outside the recycling system.

In the model, the country sending waste abroad throws away less, while the receiving country throws away more of its own waste.

The total amount recycled across the two countries stays the same.

The researchers found a possible example of this trade-off in China.

After China sharply restricted waste imports in 2018, recycling of the country’s own plastic waste rose steeply.

Recycling of imported plastic, meanwhile, nearly disappeared. That swing fits the idea that factories can switch between foreign and domestic waste to meet their needs.

The Chinese figures can’t show exactly how much imported waste had been replacing local collection.

The researchers described them as suggestive evidence and wrote that measuring the effect would take more detailed data.

So the model explains how the trade-off could happen, and China’s numbers are consistent with it.

Neither proves that every shipment of recyclables leaves an equal amount of waste uncollected somewhere else.

The materials people sort at home feed into a large international market.

Since 2010, roughly 190 million to 220 million tons of recyclable waste have crossed borders annually, according to the study’s records.

Iron and steel account for the largest share by weight, followed by paper, plastic, aluminum, and glass. Much of this waste comes from wealthy countries.

The same explanation did not fit every material equally well.

The link between material needs and waste shipments appeared more clearly for aluminum, paper, and iron and steel than for glass.

Plastic was less straightforward, with different methods producing different results.

The authors suggest that costs may matter more for plastic than the gap between what a country throws away and what its factories need.

National recycling figures do not necessarily tell people where the work happens.

EU countries can count exported packaging waste toward their targets when evidence shows it was recycled under conditions comparable to Europe’s.

Between 2018 and 2021, an average of 10% to 13% of EU countries’ packaging recycling happened outside the bloc. Before China’s restrictions, the EU exported about half the plastic it collected for recycling.

A country can therefore improve its recycling figures while relying on facilities overseas.

Those figures also leave out any local waste that the receiving country stops collecting because imports have become cheaper.

Even financial help for collection can encourage more exports, the model suggests. Making waste cheaper to gather does not automatically make it more worthwhile to recycle at home.

Recyclable waste can be something a buyer wants or something a seller pays to remove. Its value depends partly on demand from factories and the costs of handling it.

Joltreau described how these competing views surfaced when China reduced its waste imports.

“At the time, very different narratives emerged in the public debate: ‘We’re exporting our trash’ versus ‘They’re buying our recyclable materials.'”

Both descriptions can fit the market, but payments to take waste create another concern.

If those payments become large enough, the model shows an incentive to accept the waste for dumping rather than recycling.

The researchers found more promise in rules that require manufacturers to use recycled material.

These can encourage recycling at home more effectively than targets that countries can meet by exporting waste.

The idea is familiar: collecting old packaging is only part of the job. Manufacturers also need a reason to use the material recovered from it.

The EU already requires 25% recycled plastic in PET drink bottles.

Requirements like this create demand for recycled material, giving collection and processing a purpose beyond moving waste elsewhere.

The authors also argue that export restrictions should come with incentives to use local waste and generate less of it.

Otherwise, the financial pressure to send waste away can remain, including through illegal routes.

The model leaves out some real-world complications. It treats recycled material as a full substitute for new material, although recycled plastic can be lower in quality.

Its central result also depends on imports replacing waste collected locally. If those imports instead replace newly extracted raw materials, the outcome could differ, and the researchers do not rule that out.

For anyone carefully sorting bottles and boxes, the study raises an issue that household effort alone cannot solve.

Recycling systems need to account for what happens to both the material shipped abroad and the waste left behind.

The study is published in the Journal of Applied Economics.

A rare silver coin minted during the reign of a famous Viking king has been discovered in a massive hoard in Norway that now comprises 5,500 coins and counting.

The coin is inscribed with the name of the Norwegian king Olaf Haraldsson, who ruled from 1015 to 1028 and, as St. Olaf or "Olaf the Holy," is considered the national saint of Norway.

Fewer than 20 coins minted during Olaf's reign have ever been discovered, and the newfound coin is a "national treasure," according to a statement from the University of Oslo's Museum of Cultural History.

"Here we have a coin bearing the king's portrait and a cross, with another cross on the reverse," University of Oslo coin expert Svein Gullbekk said in the statement. "It is a Christianisation coin on a grand scale."

Olaf is credited with establishing Christianity in Norway — an early step toward its full conversion about a generation later.

"We can see how King Olaf used coinage to legitimise the new faith and to establish himself as a Christian king of European standing," Gullbekk said. "This is branding."

Haraldsson learned about Christianity when he joined Viking raids against England and France as a young man, and he was baptized in the French city of Rouen in about 1013, according to the statement.

He became Norway's king in 1015, and in about 1024 proclaimed that only Christianity would be legal, according to the University of Bergen. Much of Norway, however, remained faithful to the Norse gods during his rule, especially around the central Trøndelag region and in the eastern parts of the country.

The front of the silver coin portrays "Olaf the Holy," who is now Norway's national saint.

But Olaf was made a Christian saint shortly after his death in 1030. He is now recognized as one of the first Christian kings of Norway and the first Norwegian king to issue coins in his own name.

The newfound coin is part of the Mørstad Hoard, which was discovered near the village of Rena in April. At that time, the buried hoard was described as the largest ever found in Norway and was estimated to contain almost 3,000 silver coins. However, subsequent research has determined that it includes anywhere from 5,500 to more than 6,000 coins, the statement said.

Anne Skogsfjord, a museum researcher charged with cataloging the coins, thought the Olaf coin was an Anglo-Saxon penny at first. But then she read the inscription and realized it was minted in Norway under Olaf.

"I could see that it read ONLAF R+ NORMANORV. … Could this really be a coin of Olaf Haraldsson that I was holding?" Skogsfjord said in the statement. "And it was! It was a very special moment."

Olaf Haraldsson was one of Norway's first Christian kings; the other side of the coin features a cross.

The coin was struck between about 1024 and 1028, near the end of the Viking Age, and circulated for 20 to 25 years before someone deposited it in the hoard, the statement said.

Although the minting of the coin was a political and religious act, the coin also seems to have been used as currency; both sides bear numerous small cut marks, which was a common way of determining whether a coin was made completely of silver.

Gullbekk said Haraldsson himself had decided what the coins would look like and may have been present when they were minted.

"It is entirely possible that this very coin from Mørstad was once held in the king's own hand," he said in the statement.

#5594. Scientists Discover Chronic Back Pain Can Change How Your Brain Sees You

A chronic back pain flare may switch on a brain network tied to identity, linking physical pain with how you see yourself.

Researchers at UC San Diego School of Medicine found that movement-triggered flares in people with chronic low back pain activated a brain network associated with identity and inward reflection. Painful heat produced the opposite response, suggesting that a familiar back pain flare engages the brain differently from a new external threat.

Low back pain is the leading cause of disability worldwide, according to the World Health Organization. For people with chronic low back pain (cLBP), which lasts longer than 12 weeks, everyday movements can bring sudden spikes in pain, adding fear and uncertainty to ordinary activities.

The findings, published October 5, 2026, in the British Journal of Anaesthesia, offer a possible explanation for why treatments focused on pain symptoms often fall short. They also raise the possibility of treating the relationship between pain and identity.

The study involved 114 people with cLBP who performed a “leg raise test,” a standard procedure used to provoke back pain. Researchers measured brain blood flow using arterial spin labeling, a specialized functional MRI technique. Participants also received a painful but harmless heat stimulus on the calf, providing a comparison with acute pain from an external source.

During movement-triggered flares, the Default Mode Network (DMN) became more active. This collection of connected regions across the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes helps support thoughts about personal identity, memories, and internal feelings. Its involvement in inward reflection has earned it the nickname “self-network.”

Painful heat, by contrast, reduced DMN activity. According to corresponding author Fadel Zeidan, this response allows attention to shift toward an outside threat, such as something hot touching the skin.

“In every pain study, when you’re experiencing pain, the DMN deactivates,” said Zeidan. “What we show for the first time ever is that when you have a pain flare, it turns on instead. You start to identify the experience with who you are.”

The brain scans also revealed differences related to pain severity. Participants with worse chronic back pain had stronger connections between the DMN and the primary somatosensory cortex, which processes bodily sensations, including those from the back. More intense pain during the leg raise test corresponded to greater activity in sensory and motor regions, while heart rate increases reliably tracked the intensity of the flare.

Together, these findings suggest that a flare may draw on more than the immediate physical experience. Memories of earlier episodes, expectations about what movement will feel like, and worries about its consequences may all become part of the experience.

“The moment-to-moment experience is contaminated not only by the feeling of pain, but by the anticipation of it and recovery from it. It’s contaminated by past experiences like embarrassment about not being able to participate in normal activities and fear of movement when trying to avoid the pain.”

The team suspects that these different patterns of brain activity help explain why conventional pain treatments, including opiates, often provide insufficient relief for movement-triggered chronic pain.

“Current pain medicine is fixated on ridding patients of their pain symptoms, but that’s not the source of chronic pain. These data suggest that a chronic pain flare also encompasses one’s identity.”

Zeidan’s team has separately investigated mindfulness meditation and the psychedelic compound psilocybin as approaches that may help people separate pain from their sense of self. Early studies have reported reductions in pain intensity, although larger studies are needed to confirm the benefits and clarify how they occur.

“These types of mind-body and psychedelic medicine approaches target the whole person,” Zeidan said. “Mindfulness is a meditation technique that’s about cultivating non-judgmental awareness of the present moment that dramatically reduces pain in a very unique way. Psilocybin seemingly resets the pain system that integrates trauma, past experiences, and future worries to facilitate a restart, if you will. They’re not a silver bullet—you’re not necessarily getting rid of the pain, but you’re changing the relationship you have with that pain so it doesn’t contaminate your everyday life.”

Zeidan’s laboratory is now conducting several clinical trials using brain imaging to examine how mindfulness and psychedelics affect processes involved in thinking about the self. The goal is to understand whether changing those processes can alter how chronic pain is experienced and how much it intrudes on daily life.

Reference: “Brain mechanisms supporting movement-evoked chronic low back pain: an observational brain imaging study” by Morgan Gianola, Jon Dean, Conan Chen, Daniel Barrows, Gabriel Riegner, Thomas Liu and Fadel Zeidan, 5 October 2026, British Journal of Anaesthesia. DOI: 10.1016/j.bja.2026.08.010

#5592. New Evidence for an Old Prediction: Recoiling Supermassive Black Holes

There's ample evidence that large galaxies host supermassive black holes (SMBH) in their centers. And there's growing evidence from gravitational wave detectors that these SMBH merge when their galaxies merge. But astronomers think that sometimes they don't merge, and these are called recoiling SMBH.

A merger takes a long time, and in its final moments, powerful gravitational waves are released. Theoretical predictions state that if the waves aren't emitted equally in all directionsâor aren't isometricâthe imbalance can drive the merger remnant SMBH away from the galactic center with a powerful kick. This SMBH doesn't leave the galaxy entirely. Instead, it acquires a pendulum-like motion, swinging back and forth, oscillating across the galactic center.

Critically, an SMBH merger remnant like this doesn't have a constant speed. Instead, it moves fastest near the galactic center, and slows at the other end of its swing. This fact is important in new research.

The research is "Statistical evidence for massive black hole recoils in active galactic nuclei," and it's published in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society. The lead author is Bence BÃ©csy, an Assistant Professor in the School of Physics and Astronomy at the University of Birmingham.

"Many SMBH binaries are expected to merge after inspiralling due to gravitational-wave emission, and will be observable in the mHz gravitational-wave band by LISA," the authors explain. Uneven gravitational waves can give the merger remnant SMBH a recoil velocity between 360,000 km/h to 3.6 million km/h. The recoil drives its pendulum-like oscillation.

This artist's illustration shows two black holes merging. If the gravitational waves generated by the merger are uneven, it can create a recoiled black hole. Image Credit: NASA

"It is expected that such a SMBH can retain the inner region of its accretion disc and remain active for between 10 million and 100 million years after the merger, allowing it to be observable as a quasar (QSO) with detectable kinematic and spatial signatures of the merger," the authors add.

Evidence for these recoiling SMBH has been difficult to obtain. Researchers have identified some candidates, but in each case, there was an alternate explanation for their observations. This work is different because it doesn't focus on individual candidates. Instead, it takes what astrophysicists think is true about recoiling black holes and looks for it statistically in a population.

"We search for a population-level signature of gravitational-wave recoiling supermassive black holes: a positive correlation between dust obscuration and the magnitude of the line-of-sight velocity offset of broad emission lines relative to the host," the authors write. Their work is based on the 16th data release from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and its quasar catalogue.

Quasars are the brightest active galactic nuclei, which are SMBH that are actively accreting material. Their high luminosity makes them easier to find and observe. The researchers analyzed about 100,000 quasars in their work. What were they looking for?

Quasars are extremely luminous, and many of their properties are still poorly understood. Fortunately, their high luminosity aids their study. Image Credit: NOIRLab/NSF/AURA/J. da Silva (Spaceengine)

There are two pieces of critical information encoded in the light from quasars. The first is their velocity. Since quasars are SMBH, they are massive and have a powerful gravitational grip on gas in their vicinity. As a recoiling SMBH oscillates, its velocity alters depending on its position relative to the galactic center. Gas close to the SMBH moves along with it, and is called its Broad Line Region (BGR). The gas further away does not move with it, and is called the Narrow Line Region (NLR). Light from both bodies of gas is shifted by a small amount, relative to one another.

"In what follows, we assume that a recoiling SMBH will move together with its BLR, while the NLR will be left behind and exhibit a redshift consistent with that of the host galaxy and the dust torus at the galactic centre," the authors explain.

So the light signal from each body of gas, the BLR and the NLR, is different. It's called a doppler shift, and astronomers use spectrographs to detect it. By comparing the two signals, researchers can determine the SMBH's speed.

The second piece of evidence encoded in the light concerns dust.

Dust near the quasar reddens its light, in a phenomenon called extinction. If the quasar is redder than similar light sources, then it's suffering more dust extinction. Extinction can be measured with spectroscopy and photometry.

Dust is thickest near the galactic center, and that's also where the remnant SMBH moves the fastest. So a faster moving quasar should also be more reddened by dust extinction. This is the relationship at the heart of this work.

The results showed an astonishing difference among the most reddened quasars. Dust obscuration tracks black hole speed: the faster the black hole moves, the more likely its quasar is to be buried in dust, with the fastest group showing three times the obscured fraction of the slowest. Recoiling black holes would produce this result, but that doesn't mean these are all recoiling black holes.

LISA will be the first space observatory dedicated to detecting gravitational waves. It will have a group of three spacecraft that orbit the Sun while maintaining an equilateral triangle. The laser beams between all three will detect gravitational waves. If this research is correct, LISA should detect recoiled SMBH. Image Credit: ESA. Licence: CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO or ESA Standard Licence

What is new about our research is that, rather than looking for individual candidates, we searched statistically for traces of an entire population," lead author BÃ©csy said in a press release. "This enabled us to identify a relationship consistent with theoretical predictions.â

"While the correlation we find might be evidence for a population of recoiling SMBHs, alternative explanations are possible," the authors write. Winds, viewing angle, or other effects could also explain it. Still, finding the trend across a large statistical sample is intriguing.

More definitive proof for recoiling SMBHs might arrive with LISA, the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna, after its planned launch in 2035.

#5590. Archaeologists Found a Lost Roman Fort That May Solve a 1,700-Year-Old Mystery

Five trenches dug into a remote Welsh hillside may have just turned a medieval myth into history.

The Roman emperor Magnus Maximus ruled territory stretching from Spain to Britain in the late fourth century C.E. Medieval folklore also alleged he made it to Wales as well, pitching camp on the easternmost peak of the Preseli Hills—but that story has always been treated as a myth. Until now. Well, possibly.

Mark Merrony, a supernumerary fellow in archaeology at the University of Oxford, wrote that he’s found evidence putting Maximus in Wales, along with an army garrisoning a fort built roughly three centuries earlier.

The Welsh chapter of Maximus’ story begins with another story: the medieval collection of Welsh tales known as the Mabinogion. Experts have long held that the fourth-century ruler called Macsen Wledig in those writings stands in for Maximus. “The Dream of Macsen Wledig” recounts the emperor’s rise to power and tells how he dreamt of a beautiful woman, then crossed the sea to Britain to find her and win her hand. In the course of his military maneuvers there, the tale says, he climbed Frenni Fawr, the 1,300-foot easternmost peak of the Preseli Hills. “There the emperor pitched his tent,” according to a translation quoted by Smithsonian magazine.

Merrony believes the legend is more than folklore, and he recounted his own quest for Maximus in The Conversation. That search began in 2002, when he excavated a Roman villa first found in the 19th century, about 18 miles from the site where he now believes he’s identified a fort—one built in the first century and, he argues, later repurposed by Maximus and his troops.

“If Magnus Maximus was there, there must have been an army there,” Merrony told The Guardian. “It’s such a remote area but the tale mentions the easternmost peak of the Preseli Hills, which is where this site is. The presence of a heavily fortified Roman auxiliary fort may help unlock the mystery of a Mabinogion legend that has endured for nearly 1,700 years.”

The trail that led him there started at the villa. While working at the site, Merrony was asked by a colleague why such an impressive Roman building stood in an otherwise remote corner of Wales. The question sent him looking for a major Roman road nearby, and in 2022 he had what he calls a breakthrough: he traced sections of a Roman road, which in turn pointed him and others toward additional villas.

“My observations have attracted a good deal of skepticism, but my heart and mind felt that I was making considerable progress tracing the Roman road network from Pembrokeshire east into Carmarthenshire,” he wrote.

Then, while examining satellite imagery in 2024, Merrony spotted what he took to be the telltale outlines of a Roman auxiliary fort beside the probable line of that road, near Crymych in north Pembrokeshire. “The earthworks had a characteristic playing-card shape like auxiliary forts and were about three hectares in area, which is typical for this type of military installation,” he wrote.

Surveys on the ground followed, turning up Roman pottery and other scattered finds. “Our breakthrough came in early September with the excavation of five trenches,” he wrote. The team identified the site’s principal buildings—the commander’s house, a headquarters building, and granaries—along with the clay bases of floor structures packed with thin stone slabs, diagonal alignments of stones, further pottery shards, and a kiln filled with fragments of Roman ceramic tile. One trench cut across the base of a rampart.

The excavation also produced a lead spindle whorl, an iron stylus of the kind used to write on wax tablets, a woodworking axe, a gaming counter, a third-century coin and two coins from the later fourth century, Maximus’ own period. “The collective findings indicate that the site was once a typical timber fort,” Merrony wrote, adding that it was probably established during the campaign of the governor Sextus Julius Frontinus between 74 and 78 C.E.

If that earlier governor built the fort, Maximus could simply have taken it over and spared himself the trouble of building a new one, Merrony wrote. He plans to publish his findings in Antiqvvs, the magazine he founded, and to write a book about the discovery.

“The discovery represents an extraordinary fusion between myth and reality,” Merrony wrote, “and demonstrates that folklore is often underpinned by fact.”

#5588. Researchers Discover a Surprising Twist in How Our Cells Age

Scientists uncover a surprising paradox in how our cells age, revealing that more cellular cleanup isn’t always better and exercise may help restore a youthful balance.

Every day, our cells must dispose of damaged proteins and other unwanted material to keep themselves functioning properly. They accomplish this through a natural recycling process called autophagy, which packages cellular waste into specialized compartments where it is broken down into smaller molecular components that can be reused.

By preventing harmful waste from accumulating, autophagy plays an important role in maintaining cellular health. Scientists have long believed that this cleanup system gradually becomes less effective as people grow older. Declining autophagy is widely considered a hallmark of aging and has been associated with conditions such as cancer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and neurodegenerative disorders.

However, a new study suggests that the relationship between aging and cellular recycling is far more complicated than previously assumed.

Researchers at Sanford Burnham Prebys Medical Discovery Institute reported their findings in the journal Aging Cell. Rather than finding a consistent decline in autophagy with age, they discovered that recycling activity follows different patterns depending on the type of cell and whether it comes from a male or female participant. Their results also suggest that physical exercise may influence this process in humans.

“We commonly see people cite this idea that autophagy declines with age, but the actual evidence for this in humans is very thin,” said senior and corresponding author Caroline Kumsta, PhD, assistant professor in the Center for Cardiovascular and Muscular Diseases at Sanford Burnham Prebys.

“Interest is growing in targeting autophagy to develop new healthy aging interventions,” said Tatiana Moreno, PhD ’26, the study’s lead author and a former graduate student in Kumsta’s laboratory.

“We wanted to challenge assumptions and investigate what actually happens to autophagy during aging in healthy individuals.”

To investigate how autophagy behaves throughout adulthood, the scientists collected blood and skin samples from volunteers between 23 and 72 years old. All participants reported that they were free of chronic diseases. The researchers also converted some of the collected skin cells into induced neurons, allowing them to examine a third type of cell.

The team then analyzed RNA transcripts in each cell type. These transcripts are produced when genetic instructions stored in DNA are “read” as part of the process of making proteins, enzymes, and other essential cellular components. By examining the transcripts, the scientists could investigate how aging and cell type affected the activity of genes involved in autophagy.

Importantly, the researchers did not rely solely on these genetic measurements. They also directly measured how quickly cells carried out autophagy, making it possible to determine whether patterns of gene expression accurately reflected the recycling process itself.

The results challenged the conventional view that autophagy steadily weakens throughout the body as people age.

“We found that autophagic activity changes were not universal throughout the body and did not follow the idea that autophagy simply declines with age,” said Kumsta. “Rather, cellular recycling rates are altered differently with age in each of the cell types, and differently by sex in each of the cell types.”

In skin cells from male participants, autophagy activity decreased with age. However, the same process remained relatively constant in skin cells from female participants. Even more unexpectedly, induced neurons derived from female participants showed increased autophagy as age advanced.

Another important finding was that changes in the expression of autophagy genes did not reliably correspond to the actual speed of cellular recycling. In other words, measuring how actively these genes were being expressed did not necessarily reveal how much recycling was taking place.

“This shows that people using static measures such as transcription levels to infer what’s happening with autophagy need to be really careful about their interpretations,” said Moreno.

The scientists also explored whether autophagy activity was related to physical health and performance at different ages. Their findings raised another unexpected possibility: Higher levels of cellular recycling may not always indicate better health, particularly later in life.

Among participants aged 70 and older, greater autophagy activity was associated with worse measures of physical function across all three cell types examined.

Higher recycling activity in blood cells was linked to poorer aerobic fitness. In skin cells, increased autophagy was associated with a higher body mass index (BMI). Meanwhile, elevated autophagy in induced neurons corresponded with weaker grip strength, lower metabolism, and a higher resting heart rate.

These relationships suggest that the significance of increased autophagy may change as people grow older. Although cellular recycling is essential for maintaining health, its activity and effects may depend on a person’s age and the particular cells involved.

“I think this is an important distinction that merits continued study, as it appears that increased autophagy flux may be good in younger people yet problematic in older people,” said Kumsta.

“Autophagy and aging are so much more nuanced than previously assumed,” said Moreno. “To better translate autophagy research discoveries into potential therapies, we really need to understand what is happening on a cell-specific level and how it is affected by sex and age.”

After identifying these unexpected patterns, the researchers investigated whether exercise could alter autophagy activity in older adults.

In a small pilot experiment, five volunteers aged 77-88 participated in a gentle exercise program designed to reduce their risk of falling. The program lasted 12 weeks, and the researchers collected blood samples before and after the intervention to evaluate changes in cellular recycling.

The preliminary results suggested that exercise may lower autophagy activity in blood cells toward levels more commonly observed in younger people. These changes occurred alongside improvements in physical performance.

Although the experiment involved only five participants, the findings raise the possibility that exercise could help regulate cellular recycling in ways that support healthier aging. Larger studies will be necessary to determine whether the effects can be reproduced and how they relate to long-term health.

“It is exciting to have this exercise intervention that may have a positive effect on autophagy and healthy aging, and I’m really excited to see where that goes,” said Kumsta.

To build on these initial findings, the Kumsta laboratory received a pilot grant from the Altman Clinical and Translational Research Institute at the University of California San Diego (UC San Diego).

The funding will support further investigation into how exercise affects autophagy and physical function during aging. Researchers are now conducting larger studies with additional participants and improved testing methods in collaboration with the Exercise and Physical Activity Resource Center at UC San Diego.

The work could help scientists better understand when changes in cellular recycling are beneficial and when they may signal declining health. Such knowledge may eventually inform new strategies for promoting healthy aging by targeting autophagy in ways that account for differences in age, sex, and cell type.

New research suggests the use of naloxone, which reverses opioid overdoses, also fell

In Portland, Ore., a mobile health response team member lifts a patient who has just been treated with naloxone for a suspected opioid overdose into an ambulance. A drop in overdose deaths since mid-2023 doesn't appear to be linked to more use of the lifesaving treatment.

Drug overdose deaths unexpectedly took a nosedive in the United States a few years ago. But it doesn’t appear to be due to more people being revived than before.

At the peak in mid-2023, there were around 111,000 drug overdose deaths per year in the United States. By the end of 2025, the number had fallen to under 71,000 per year, and researchers are still piecing together why. One theory: The wider availability of naloxone, a medication that reverses opioid overdoses, made it possible to revive more people than before.

But a study of U.S. emergency medical services agencies found that emergency medical responses for nonfatal overdoses dropped along with deaths, and the use of naloxone also declined. That suggests that fewer overdoses overall occurred, not that naloxone stopped deaths more often, researchers report October 21 in the Journal of the American Medical Association.

“Naloxone still saves lives every day,” says Michael Barnett, a health services researcher at Brown University School of Public Health in Providence, R.I. “But it doesn’t look like the reason for the sudden turn” in overdose deaths.

State policies, community distribution programs and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s approval of the nasal spray version of naloxone as an over-the counter drug have expanded access.

Barnett and colleagues analyzed nearly 186 million emergency medical service activations — an ambulance or other EMS response after a 911 call — nationwide that occurred from May 2020 through December 2025. Of 2.5 million responses for nonfatal drug overdoses, 40.5 percent involved opioids.

From May of 2023 onward, EMS activations for nonfatal opioid overdoses fell on average by about 6,000, or around 33 percent, below what was expected. Total administrations of naloxone dropped as well, by around 4,700 on average, or 21 percent below what was expected. The study suggests that naloxone was not used more often than before by bystanders prior to the EMS response, but did not include data on cases when a bystander provided naloxone and no one called 911.

Additional possibilities to explain the steep drop in overdose deaths — which followed a three-decade increase — include changes to the illicit opioid supply or with the population of people at risk for overdoses. “My own read is that a disruption in the fentanyl supply best explains how sudden and widespread the turn was,” Barnett says. “Underneath it, the population at risk has probably been shrinking for years.”

But “a supply-driven decline can reverse,” Barnett says, noting that the decline in overdose deaths slowed in 2025. Traffickers can adapt “by finding new chemical sources or turning to more potent synthetic opioids.”

In astronomy, metals refer to any element heavier than hydrogen or helium, with common examples including oxygen, carbon, iron or gold. These elements are forged through nuclear fusion in the hearts of stars that are reaching the ends of their lives, before being sprayed into space and incorporated into new generations of stars.

“Not only did it help me mentally, it got me more active,” Tom says. “It helped me physically. It helped me emotionally. I live a fuller life now than I ever did before. We were all put on this earth for a purpose. Your goal in life is to find out what that is.”

Children whose mothers took extra choline during pregnancy showed higher IQ scores and fewer signs of social withdrawal at age four.

Watch a preschooler tackle a puzzle and the effort is easy to see. They turn a piece around, try the wrong space, try again and eventually find a fit.

That small success draws on several skills, including remembering the task and judging shapes.

The brain starts building the connections behind these abilities before birth, and researchers want to know how a mother’s nutrition influences that process.

A new study focused on choline, a nutrient found in eggs, meat and fish. The team gave some pregnant women extra choline, then followed their children to see how they developed.

By age four, children in the supplement group scored higher on an IQ test and showed fewer signs of social withdrawal.

The results suggest that a change during pregnancy could matter years later, though the study leaves some important questions open.

A baby’s brain needs nerve cells that can communicate with one another.

Choline helps build cell membranes and supports the production of a chemical that nerve cells use to send signals.

The baby gets choline from the mother, so researchers wanted to see whether increasing her intake could help.

They were especially interested in whether a supplement taken for a few months might make a difference years later.

Earlier studies offered encouraging but mixed results.

A smaller Colorado trial found fewer attention and social withdrawal problems after prenatal choline, while another team’s trial found no clear improvement in thinking skills.

The new trial involved 206 pregnant women who joined through University of Colorado prenatal clinics and affiliated hospitals.

At 16 weeks of pregnancy, researchers randomly divided them into two groups.

One group took choline supplements until delivery, while the other took matching placebo capsules with no added choline.

The supplement group swallowed 10 large capsules a day containing phosphatidylcholine, a form of choline found naturally in food.

Nearly every participant already took a prenatal vitamin containing folic acid, and everyone received guidance on eating choline-rich foods.

The study tested whether extra choline could offer benefits alongside that care.

Neither the women nor the researchers knew who had received the supplement until the children turned four.

Keeping that information hidden helped reduce the chance that expectations would influence their assessments.

For families, social development often shows up in everyday interactions.

In this trial, researchers focused on signs of withdrawal, including limited eye contact and little interest in other people.

Parents completed standard behavior checklists for 142 children between 18 months and four years.

Each group included 71 children, allowing researchers to compare how often parents reported these difficulties.

Seventeen children in the placebo group reached the study’s threshold for elevated social withdrawal problems, compared with three in the choline group. That worked out to 24% versus 4%.

The researchers counted children who met a particular score on the checklist, rather than relying on a general impression of how sociable they seemed.

Still, parents supplied those ratings, and clinicians did not independently assess social behavior for this measure.

At age four, 103 children took an IQ test designed for preschoolers. Children in the choline group averaged 106, while those in the placebo group averaged 98.

The differences involved working memory, which helps a child keep an instruction in mind long enough to follow it.

Children also differed in visual-spatial skills, the kind of thinking that helps them judge shapes and work out where a puzzle piece belongs.

Senior author Robert Freedman, a psychiatry professor at the University of Colorado Anschutz, pointed to the size of the difference.

“What this study showed that we have not observed before was an increase in the overall IQ of about eight points when measured at age 4. That put significantly more children into the higher IQ percentile on the test we used,” Freedman said.

The supplement did not clearly improve every outcome the team measured. Researchers found no clear reduction in attention problems, even though they had made attention one of the trial’s main measures.

An additional analysis found 11 children whose behavior checklist scores reached a range that calls for an autism assessment.

Those children also had language difficulties commonly seen in autism.

Ten belonged to the placebo group and one to the choline group.

However, the researchers had not planned this analysis in advance, and the checklists could not diagnose autism.

The authors treated that finding as preliminary. It does not establish that taking choline during pregnancy prevents autism.

Keeping families in a study for four years is hard, and about a third of the women who enrolled had dropped out before the year-four checkups.

Even fewer children completed the IQ testing. The dropouts didn’t happen at random.

Education made a clear difference. Among women with graduate degrees, 81% stayed through year four, compared with 43% of women who hadn’t finished high school.

That imbalance is important because a mother’s education is also linked to her child’s IQ scores.

The families who completed the study were mostly White and well educated, too, so researchers will need a broader mix of families before they can assume the findings apply to everyone.

The IQ finding also rests on a smaller group.

Only 103 children took the test, about half as many as the women who started the trial, and larger studies could show whether other children see a similar benefit.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, 20 women took extra choline after catching the virus or living with someone who had it. Ten came from each original study group.

However, the IQ result no longer met the usual cutoff for statistical significance.

That smaller analysis left more uncertainty about whether choline explained the difference, even though the average scores remained apart.

The trial found no increase in nausea, vomiting, dizziness or high blood pressure among women taking the supplement.

A safety board also judged three serious events unrelated to it.

The team’s earlier trial used seven capsules a day and found similar social withdrawal scores, so taking more did not clearly add benefits.

Researchers still have work to do on the most useful dose.

The mothers stopped taking the trial capsules when they gave birth, yet their children showed differences years later.

For families, the lasting value would lie in helping children learn, communicate and take part in everyday life as they grow.

Researchers now need to follow children through school and beyond to find out whether the early differences hold up.

#5580. Scientists Discover a $10 Flood Protection Trick That Works Better Than Sandbags

Stacking heavy sandbags against a doorway is a familiar response to flooding, but laboratory tests suggest sealing the gaps with ordinary duct tape could keep more water out.

Could a cheap roll of duct tape help protect your home from flooding? New research suggests it could make a surprisingly big difference.

In controlled tests, correctly applied duct tape on the outside of a household door reduced water leakage by 97-98%. The findings suggest that a simple low-cost measure could substantially reduce the amount of water entering a home during a flood. This is important as one of the key lessons from flood resilience research is that the best time to protect a property is before floodwater arrives.

The UK government’s 2025 FloodReady Review called for practical, affordable measures that help households understand their risks and to prepare for flooding. And one potential contribution to this agenda might sound unlikely, at first glance.

Duct tape is more commonly associated with household repairs than flood protection. However, tests colleagues and I conducted at the University of Hull demonstrated how much water can enter a property through an unprotected doorway during a flood.

Inside our laboratory, we tested modern wooden, uPVC, and composite household doors, installed by trade professionals. We captured any water that leaked through the door in a sealed enclosure behind the door, as we increased water depth in 5 cm increments to a maximum depth of 60 cm. We pumped out any water that leaked through the door and continuously measured the rate of water leakage to evaluate the performance of doors and different types of protection applied to them.

When testing sandbags, we stacked 54 sandbags with plastic sheeting between the door and the sandbags. For tape, the same person applied the tape for all tests to minimize any bias. It took about 10-20 minutes to apply the tape to each door.

For unprotected doors, leakage often reached thousands of liters per hour at 60 cm water depth. Applying duct tape across the external door reduced that leakage by 97-98%. Specialist sealing tapes achieved even greater reductions.

For decades, sandbags have been portrayed as the go-to emergency response to flooding. But sandbags have practical limitations. They are heavy to transport, labor-intensive to deploy, and difficult to dispose of after contact with contaminated floodwater. And of course people don’t keep a supply of sandbags ready to deploy.

In our tests, sandbags provided less effective protection against leakage through tested doorways compared to the tape-based sealing methods. Their performance varied with water depth and at their most effective, only reduced leakage by 70% compared to an unprotected door at the same water depth.

The cost difference is striking. A household may already have duct tape or be able to purchase a roll for £10 or less, while finding and deploying sandbags involves significant additional costs, transport, and effort.

Duct tape is not a substitute for professionally designed flood doors, barriers, or a property-specific flood resilience plan. It is a temporary measure that may reduce water entering through particular gaps; it cannot guarantee a dry home or prevent water entering through other routes, including walls, floors, and drains.

Its effectiveness will depend on the condition of the door and frame, the surfaces to which the tape is applied, the quality of the seal, and flood conditions. For properties at significant flood risk, professionally assessed protection remains important. For others, low-cost temporary measures could provide an additional layer of protection, though they cannot guarantee a property will remain dry.

Resilient households think ahead. They know whether they are at risk, sign up for flood warnings, and have a plan for protecting valuables, moving vehicles, and shutting off utilities if necessary. Adding duct tape to an emergency flood kit can be another small step in that process.

The wider lesson is that flood resilience does not have to start with a major investment. A £10 roll of duct tape cannot stop a flood. But our research suggests it could help keep out some of the water entering through a doorway. And that makes it a useful part of a wider plan to be prepared before the water arrives.

Scientific papers may be getting a 21st century update.

A system called Paper2Agent lets scientists do more than read a new scientific study. When a paper comes with code, methods and data, the system packages these into a tool set that an AI assistant can use to work with new data, researchers report September 16 in Nature.

The system is essentially “a virtual author of the paper,” available around the clock, says James Zou, a computer scientist at Stanford University.

In tests, Zou and colleagues tried to convert 100 computational biology papers into working tool sets and succeeded with 74. The others had obstacles such as missing code or data, or software that wouldn’t run. With the papers that were converted, the Paper2Agent tool set could run analyses of the published work and answer questions about the results. It did so more accurately and also more than twice as fast as the same agent working from the original papers and accompanying code.

Making another scientist’s code usable can take substantial work, says Peter Clark, a senior research director at the Allen Institute for Artificial Intelligence in Seattle who was not involved in developing Paper2Agent. Researchers already share code on platforms such as GitHub. But Clark says he often has to “grub around” for half a day to figure out how to get it running.

A general-purpose AI assistant such as Claude can help, he says, but it typically generates new code on the fly. Paper2Agent instead packages code associated with a paper into reusable tools, tests them against reference outputs and writes step-by-step instructions guiding an AI assistant on which tools to use and in what order. This streamlines and standardizes the process of using AI to interact with scientific research, Clark says. It’s “an exciting step” toward papers that actively help scientists.

To Zou, transforming scientific papers into agents seem like a natural next step for transmitting knowledge. We’ve already moved from stone tablets and papyrus to paper and then to digital formats, he says, and now it’s time to move from passive to active formats for human knowledge. A paper-agent can help one human scientist quickly use another’s code and methods. The tool can also search for paper-agents about similar topics with the aim of alerting their human authors about potential collaborations.

Zou says his team has already created “over 100 different paper-agents” from their own work and that these have already helped him find other scientists to collaborate with.

Clark notes that Paper2Agent specializes in helping scientists reuse methods and code, and not in answering general questions, like a true virtual author should be able to do. Also, he worries about what could happen if millions of low-quality papers get turned into agents capable of pestering scientists. That could make things “worse rather than better,” he says, recalling the days of Clippy, the much-maligned Microsoft Office assistant. But he’d love for a “non-annoying agent” to pop up and tell him about work that he should be paying attention to.

For more open-ended questions about a paper, Clark says he would still want to talk to the people who did the research. “The reason I would write to a corresponding author is for precisely the things they didn’t put in the paper.”

#5570. Whale songs reveal surprising clues about life in tropical oceans

Scientists are using underwater songs to uncover where elusive Omura's whales live, what they may eat, and how they survive year-round in tropical waters.

Spending the whole year in warm seas sounds like a comfortable arrangement. For a whale, though, warm water is only part of the deal: there also has to be enough to eat.

Many whales travel between cold feeding grounds and warmer breeding waters, drawing on stored fat while they travel and breed. Omura’s whale skips that routine and stays in the tropics.

Off Australia, researchers have never documented what this elusive whale eats. Now, they have found a possible food clue by listening to its songs.

The team detected whale songs most often in warmer waters, particularly where microscopic algae had flourished about a month earlier.

That delay could help explain how a large animal finds meals in the same seas throughout the year, without making the long trip to colder feeding grounds.

Scientists only recognized Omura’s whale as a separate species in 2003, having previously mistaken it for a small form of its relative, Bryde’s whale.

Most of what researchers know about its life still comes from a population off northwest Madagascar.

Spotting one off Australia remains unusual, but its song gives researchers another way to find it.

The whale repeats a two-part call lasting about 15 seconds, with some sounds lower than the deepest note on a piano.

Ciara Browne, a PhD student at Curtin University in Perth, led the team that searched recordings from 35 sites, collected between 2005 and 2023.

They reused sound files from other projects, letting software flag possible calls before checking every detection by hand.

“Omura’s whales are notoriously difficult to study because visual sightings are rare, so listening for their calls provides an important way of understanding where they occur,” Browne said.

The algae themselves are not the meal the researchers have in mind. They may start a chain in which small animals eat the algae, then become food for whales.

To look for that connection, the team used satellite measurements of chlorophyll, the green pigment in algae.

Those readings give scientists a rough idea of how much algae is growing, but readings from the day of a recording showed no clear connection to whale calls.

Looking back 30 days changed the picture. Higher algae levels a month earlier went with a greater chance of hearing whales, suggesting that food takes time to work its way through the ocean’s food web.

Small algae-eating animals may need those weeks to become more plentiful, and researchers have seen a similar sequence off Madagascar.

There, algae blooms have preceded swarms of krill, the shrimp-like animals that attract feeding Omura’s whales.

The recordings picked up calls in every month, with the busiest stretch running from September through May.

Calls became least common during the Southern Hemisphere’s winter, from June through August.

The team’s models showed a greater chance of hearing whales once surface water warmed beyond about 81.5°F (27.5°C). January through March offered the best odds.

Winter recordings placed more calling whales farther north, toward the equator.

Recorders in the Joseph Bonaparte Gulf and off the Kimberley coast heard calls throughout the year, perhaps because those northern waters stay warm through winter.

A separate model also pointed offshore, with the best odds of hearing whales about 124 miles (200 kilometers) from the coast.

The pattern suggested that Australia’s continental shelf and its outer edge could be important whale habitats.

The shelf gives researchers a broad area to search for feeding whales, but plenty of details remain unclear.

Depth, distance from shore, and distance from reefs overlap, so the researchers could not tell which feature best explained the pattern.

Wind supplied another possible clue, although its connection to calls was weaker.

Strong winds can push surface water aside and allow nutrient-rich water to rise from below, helping create conditions that concentrate krill and small fish.

Near Scott Reef, researchers tracking the direction of songs found that some whales stayed in one place for long periods.

They may have been feeding while singing, although the songs alone cannot confirm that.

Together, the clues fit the possibility that Omura’s whales keep eating throughout the year instead of depending mainly on one feeding season.

They probably still carry fat reserves, but regular meals could help sustain their tropical lives.

These whales live in waters that people use for shipping, commercial fishing, and offshore oil and gas operations.

Because they remain in the region throughout the year, they may face more sustained exposure to underwater noise than migrating whales.

That noise can interfere with communication and change animal behavior. It also makes whales harder for researchers to hear: near shipping lanes, the recorders picked up calls over shorter distances than in quieter deep water.

Knowing where whales spend their time gives people who plan offshore activity a better chance of considering them before making decisions.

Study co-author Christine Erbe directs Curtin’s Centre for Marine Science and Technology.

“This knowledge can help inform marine planning and improve our understanding of potential impacts from activities such as shipping, offshore industry and other sources of underwater noise,” Erbe said.

A recording tells researchers that a whale called within listening range, but it cannot reveal how many whales live there or what they ate.

Songs are the only vocalizations documented so far for Omura’s whales, and if only males sing, as in other baleen whales, the results may largely miss females and calves.

The listening network also left gaps because researchers originally placed the equipment for other projects.

Different sites recorded in different years, and the analysis assumes the whales’ range stayed fairly stable across those 18 years.

Sound-recording tags could capture calls beyond song, while genetic samples could help establish whether the singers are all male.

Scientists still lack enough information to judge the species’ extinction risk, which explains its Data Deficient listing on the International Union for Conservation of Nature‘s Red List.

As warming changes ocean temperatures and food supplies, these recordings give researchers a starting point for understanding where the whales’ habitat may shift.

The study is published in the journal Marine Mammal Science.

#5569. Advancing Astronomy at MIT with X-ray Reverberation Mapping

Black holes are one of the most misunderstood objects in the Universe. Much of the confusion stems from their name. When you hear the words "black hole," you might picture a tunnel in spacetime that leads to... certain death or something else. In reality, black holes are the collapsed remnants of stars that have become so dense that the gravitational force on their surfaces reaches the speed of light. As a result, nothing (including light) can escape their outer boundary, known as the "event horizon."

At MIT's Kavli Institute for Astrophysics and Space Research, Professor Erin Kara leads a group at the forefront of black hole physics. Using data from space- and ground-based telescopes, they study the properties of black holes, how they grow, and how they affect their environments. Of particular interest to the team are the supermassive black holes (SMBHs) that reside at the centers of massive galaxies, which play an important role in their formation and evolution.

Kara began her journey at the Institute of Astronomy at Cambridge University, where she pursued her master's degree in physics. After completing the year-long program, she stayed to complete her Ph.D. on the relatively new topic of black hole X-ray reverberation. Andy Fabian OBE FRS, a Royal Society Research Professor at the Institute of Astronomy and the leader of its X-ray research group, was her thesis advisor.

The anatomy of a black hole, emphasizing the accretion disk, event horizon, and other areas. Credit: NASA

In 2009, Fabian and his team were reviewing archival X-ray data when they noticed strange time delays from the crown-shaped, high-energy region surrounding black holes (the corona). They interpreted the signals as the first evidence of X-ray reverberations around a black hole. They further determined that these resulted from X-ray radiation generated by the black hole's corona bouncing off the swirling disk of gas and dust that circles a black hole (the accretion disk). As Kara explained:

They had only found these echoes in one black hole. But the archive was full of data of these reverberation signals that no one had analyzed in this particular way. So I had my whole PhD to kind of play with this archive, and it felt very discovery-driven.

This event led Kara to keep studying X-ray reverberations as a technique for mapping regions around black holes. After earning her PhD, she did her postdoctoral work at the University of Maryland and NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center, where she also worked on the Japanese mission Hitomi. While the mission failed after 40 days in space, the data it returned validated its microalorimeter instrument.

This technology later informed the X-ray Imaging and Spectroscopy Mission (XRISM), for which Kara leads a science group that analyzes X-ray signals emitted from SMBH coronas. In between, Kara worked with the MIT team that developed the Neutron Star Interior Composition Explorer (NICER), which is now integrated with the International Space Station (ISS) and measures X-ray timing from astrophysical sources.

She and her students analyze data from XRISM, NICER, and other missions to better understand how black holes grow, evolve, and affect the galaxies around them. In addition, her group also studies other extreme X-ray sources, ranging from tidal disruption events and periodic eruptions to black hole outbursts.

Their efforts have led to some surprising discoveries about black holes that challenge previous notions. Said Kara:

It used to be that we didn't have eyes on systems all the time. Now we're seeing that they can turn on and off at rates that are much faster than we ever thought possible. #5567. Scientists Turn Major Crude Oil Problem Into an Unexpected Energy Breakthrough

A simple membrane uses crude oil’s own deposits to separate lighter components at room temperature, with simulations suggesting nearly one-third less energy use when paired with distillation.

Refineries heat crude oil above 350°C (662°F) to separate a mixture that supplies transportation fuels and raw materials for plastics, packaging, textiles, and other consumer products. This century-old process, called distillation, vaporizes the oil and then cools the vapor to recover different fractions. Worldwide, crude oil distillation consumes approximately 1,100 terawatt-hours of energy each year, roughly the annual output of 130 gigawatt-scale nuclear power plants operating continuously.

An international research team has demonstrated a simple, inexpensive membrane that could handle part of this separation at room temperature, without heating. As crude oil flows through it, components of the oil create the narrow channels that allow the membrane to distinguish between lighter and heavier molecules.

The study, published in Nature, was led by Professor Dong-Yeun Koh of KAIST in collaboration with Professor Ryan Lively’s group at Georgia Tech.

Process simulations showed that using the membrane to pretreat crude oil before conventional distillation could reduce energy consumption by 31.6%, carbon dioxide emissions by 37.6%, cooling water use by 20.7%, and operating costs by 36%. Such savings could matter beyond the refinery, since refining costs influence the prices of fuels and many everyday products. Increasing cost pressures in global petrochemical markets have also intensified demand for more efficient separation technologies.

Manufacturing membranes for molecularly precise separation has been challenging. Conventional designs require ultrathin surface coatings that raise costs and risk defects at scale. Instead, the team passed crude oil through bare porous polyacrylonitrile (PAN), an inexpensive, chemically stable polymer widely used in industrial membranes.

“This study reveals a new scientific principle in which a membrane interacts with a complex mixture and spontaneously forms its own separation channels. Working with real crude oil supplied by HD Hyundai Oilbank allowed us to validate the technology under conditions relevant to industrial operation,” Koh said.

As the oil passed through the PAN membrane, heavy hydrocarbons selectively deposited on the pore walls. These deposits gradually narrowed the openings, forming separation channels smaller than 2 nanometers. Lighter fractions, including naphtha, gasoline, and kerosene, passed through rapidly, while heavier components were retained. The buildup known as membrane fouling, normally a cause of declining performance, became the mechanism that made selective separation possible.

The bare PAN membrane achieved crude oil permeation rates approximately 23 times higher than those of previously reported state-of-the-art crude oil membranes. It also maintained stable performance for 28 consecutive days.

The technology could fit into existing refineries as a modular filtration unit, reducing the need for major equipment replacement and making adoption easier. Lively said, “One of the key challenges facing membrane systems for crude oil separation was the low productivities of the membrane units – the PAN membranes with their surprising separation mechanism – dramatically increase the productivity of the membrane unit, to the point where industry should seriously consider adopting the technology.”

Distillation remains one of the refining industry’s largest sources of energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions. If adopted throughout Korea’s refining and petrochemical sector, the membrane technology could reduce greenhouse gas emissions by approximately 10 million metric tons annually, equivalent to the emissions of roughly four million internal combustion vehicles.

Dr. Jihoon Choi and Dr. Hyeokjun Seo of KAIST, the study’s co-first authors, said, “Our goal is to precisely control this spontaneous pore-constriction phenomenon and develop it into a membrane platform applicable to the entire refining process. We also aim to expand the technology to plastic recycling, biofuel purification, and other sustainable chemical processes that support carbon neutrality.”

For waste plastics, a proposed application is purifying pyrolysis oil, the oil obtained by breaking down plastics with heat. Other possible uses include recovering solvents used in battery manufacturing and pharmaceutical purification. These remain potential applications of the membrane platform.

Professor Jae W. Lee of KAIST, a co-corresponding author of the study, said, “By advancing large-area membrane modularization and long-term operational reliability, we hope to broaden the adoption of membrane-based processes throughout the refining and petrochemical industries.”

Lee, Ryan P. Lively and Dong-Yeun Koh, 24 June 2026, Nature. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10677-3

#5565. Extraterrestrial Fusion Reactors Could Unlock Limitless Energy—and Birth Human Space Civilizations, Physicist Claims

These futuristic reactors may offer advantages that are “entirely infeasible” for their terrestrial counterparts.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

For decades, scientists have tried to harness nuclear fusion—the process that powers stars—in reactors here on Earth. Currently, practical fusion is still a long way off, but there is enormous investment in this power source, because it could theoretically produce a virtually limitless supply of clean energy, perhaps exceeding global demand and enabling the transition to a decarbonized grid. Now, a researcher is pursuing a new twist on this longstanding goal: fusion reactors in space.

Picture massive doughnut-shaped reactors, known as tokamaks, floating in geostationary Earth orbit, or satellites blasting lasers across space to ignite fusion reactions. For now, these visions are speculative. But physicist Young K. Bae, PhD, believes that space-based fusion power could offer advantages over its terrestrial counterpart and may, one day, fuel the expansion of human civilization into space, according to his new paper published in the peer-reviewed journal Acta Astronautica.

“I’ve been working on this as an opening to a spacefaring civilization in the future,” Bae says in an interview. “That’s my goal right now—a footstep that humanity can build on.” While it’s not exactly a one-person job, Bae hopes to take a crack at it, he adds.

Bae is one of a small group of scientists interested in pioneering designs for fusion reactors in space. These structures could power off-Earth habitats, space manufacturing projects, orbital data centers, deep-space exploration, and all manner of other applications. His paper presents a rough roadmap toward a gigawatt-class tokamak—comparable to at least one large coal or nuclear plant capable of powering roughly one million homes—that might fulfill these lofty goals.

Space-based fusion power “enables higher power density for industrial-scale operations and deep-space missions where continuous power availability is critical” and “becomes increasingly advantageous at multi-gigawatt scales,” Bae writes in the paper.

Nuclear fusion occurs when two or more atoms merge into a single atom, a reaction that releases a colossal amount of energy. When you bask in the sun in the daytime or gaze at distant stars at night, you are experiencing the awesome power of fusion reactions. For more than a century, scientists have grappled with the tall technological order of reconstructing this process in reactors.

Tokamaks spark fusion reactions inside hot, charged plasma that is generated by powerful magnetic fields in donut-shaped torus chambers. This form of reaction, known as magnetic confinement fusion, has been demonstrated in tokamaks on Earth. While significant gains have been made, no reactor has ever come close to achieving “fusion breakeven,” which is a fusion reaction that produces more energy than was required to run the machinery that ignited it.

Bae’s paper suggests that space-based tokamaks might offer some advantages to ground-based versions. The microgravity environment could reduce the vast structural loads of tokamaks, and the lack of atmospheric pressure could significantly reduce the size and mass of vacuum chambers.

The study also speculates that the immense heat generated by a space tokamak could radiate directly into space with special panels operating at extremely high temperatures of roughly 1,340°F, which “could potentially dissipate heat more effectively on a mass basis than comparable terrestrial reactors,” Bae notes in an email.

And though the recent work focuses on magnetic confinement fusion in space, Bae is also optimistic that the other main type of fusion—known as inertial confinement fusion—could also be staged in space using high-powered lasers.

Bae has been pondering the potential of fusion power since he began his PhD at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1979. As part of his studies, he joined the fusion team at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and became intrigued by this exotic power source. As his career progressed, Bae also developed a research interest in space exploration, which neatly dovetailed with his interest in fusion research. Like many space enthusiasts, he believes fusion power is the key to sustainable off-Earth settlements, such as orbital stations or cities on extraterrestrial worlds.

Earlier this year, Bae founded Spacefaring Research—a research institute based in Corona, California, that is dedicated to advancing space exploration—and serves as its chief scientist. Recently, he has floated the idea of space-based nuclear fusion to his former mentors and colleagues. Though the response from his peers was rather understated, he joked, his growing fascination with the topic inspired him to start crunching numbers.

“My colleagues know fusion, but they don’t know space engineering,” Bae says. “I happen to know both. So I said: ‘Okay, I’m going to combine these two knowledges and see what’s going on.’”

Currently, most spacecraft run on solar power, which is abundant near the sun but tapers off in more far-flung missions. To provide more reliable power, the United States and China are also vying to establish nuclear fission reactors on the moon, which would support astronauts in lunar bases by splitting atoms as opposed to fusing them. However, fission reactors raise safety concerns due to the risk of radioactive contamination, as well as security issues over the prospect of weapons-grade nuclear fuel in space (though there are currently no plans to use bomb-grade fuel in these reactors).

If space-based fusion power were ever realized—a big “if”—it could generate far more energy than solar or fission, potentially supercharging space exploration by providing off-Earth power plants that could fuel up spacecraft in orbit or support extraterrestrial bases on the moon or beyond. In addition, many previous studies have explored the possibility of fusion reactors as a form of ultra-futuristic spacecraft propulsion for huge passenger ships or even interstellar travel.

Of course, there would be significant costs to building a tokamak in space, as these facilities are, at minimum, dozens of feet in diameter. As a consequence, an orbital tokamak would need to be launched in modules to be assembled in space, similar to the International Space Station.

While research into space-based fusion reactors is sparse, other scientists are also making progress. Earlier this year in a preprint study, a team based in Singapore and China explored the concept of a massive space power plant that runs on uncontrolled fusion reactions. In his study, Bae also pointed to the pioneering work of Terry Kammash, PhD—a fusion expert at the University of Michigan who died in 2024—who had argued for the potential of space-based fusion power since the 1980s.

Bae hopes his research into the topic will inspire scientists to seriously consider the operational constraints and benefits of fusion reactors in space.

“I really want to have my daughter, or the next generation of my relatives—and humanity—to really step on it, so then they can go to space and live,” he says.

#5564. Hot peppers reveal new clues that could lead to better painkillers

Scientists discovered that the pain receptor activated by hot peppers responds differently to heat and other triggers, opening new possibilities for pain relief.

Bite into a hot pepper and you’ll feel a burning sensation much like an injury. That’s because the same sensor on pain-sensing nerves responds to both.

Since that sensor was first identified in 1997, the working assumption has been that it is a simple switch: whatever flips it, it opens the same way. It doesn’t.

Recorded one molecule at a time, the sensor opened in a different way for each trigger.

Those differences may give drug designers a way to block the receptor’s painful openings while leaving its heat sensing alone.

The sensor is a protein called TRPV1. It sits in the outer membrane of pain-sensing nerve cells.

There it responds to heat, to the acid that comes with tissue injury and inflammation, and to capsaicin – the chemical compound that gives chili peppers their heat.

Jie Zheng’s lab at the UC Davis School of Medicine tested it with five different triggers and measured the current through it each time.

The receptor was the mouse or rat version, grown in cultured human cells rather than in nerves or animals.

Bo Hyun Lee, the study’s first author, is a former researcher in Zheng’s lab who is also affiliated with Gyeongsang National University in South Korea.

“Researchers have generally assumed that once TRPV1 is activated, the channel enters essentially the same open state regardless of what stimulus triggered it,” said Lee.

“Our findings show that the receptor is more sophisticated than that.”

TRPV1 is a channel. When it opens, charged particles pour through a pore at its center, and that current is the start of a pain signal.

The team recorded that current through single channels, switching triggers within one recording.

Capsaicin opened the channel to one current level. Resiniferatoxin, an even hotter toxin from a poisonous plant, opened the same channel to a substantially larger one.

A synthetic lab chemical called 2-APB produced a level of its own, and so did heat.

Adding more of a trigger didn’t change the size of the current, so the molecules weren’t simply clogging the pore.

A channel holding one, two, three or four resiniferatoxin molecules passed the same current each time. The type of trigger set the level, not the amount.

Different currents hint at different pore shapes, and the team had a sensitive test for that. It passed two kinds of charged particles through at once, sodium and rubidium, in varying proportions.

When two kinds of ions share a narrow pore they get in each other’s way, and the current dips in the middle of the range.

With 2-APB and with heat, the dip appeared. With capsaicin and with acid, it didn’t. The ions could tell the openings apart.

The team also tagged single spots on the protein with a fluorescent building block that changes its glow as its surroundings shift.

Of 28 positions tried, eight worked without disrupting the channel.

At one spot near the mouth of the pore, 2-APB changed the protein’s shape more than capsaicin did, and at a neighboring spot capsaicin changed it more.

Deeper in the pore, the two triggers looked much the same.

“We were able to see consistent evidence from several independent experimental methods that the pore of the channel does not look exactly the same when activated by different stimuli,” said Zheng.

“That convergence of evidence gave us confidence that these are genuine functional states rather than experimental artifacts.”

The receptor is built from four identical subunits, and the four move together to open the pore. Two triggers at once were expected to reinforce each other. They did the opposite.

Acid on its own opened the channel at one speed. With a small dose of capsaicin added, too little to occupy most of the four subunits, acid opened the channel more slowly.

The reverse held too: in mildly acidic conditions, capsaicin opened the channel more slowly than at neutral pH.

A channel engineered to hold capsaicin and resiniferatoxin at the same time passed a current between the two single-trigger levels.

The authors’ reading is that subunits pulled toward different open shapes obstruct one another.

TRPV1 has interested drug developers for years.

It sits in the pain pathway and works independently of opioid receptors, making it a promising target for non-addictive pain treatments.

Many attempts have run into problems with body temperature. The same receptor helps the body sense heat and hold its core temperature.

In clinical trials, some drugs that blocked TRPV1 caused dangerous increases in body temperature, while activating the receptor could lower it.

In these recordings, acid from injured tissue opened the receptor into a different state than heat did, so a drug might be built to block that state alone.

Vladimir Yarov-Yarovoy, a co-author and professor in the same department, called the study a framework for thinking about TRPV1 in a much more nuanced way.

“If different forms of activation produce different functional states, we may eventually be able to design therapies that selectively affect pain-related activity while leaving other essential functions intact,” he said.

Every measurement here came from cultured cells at room temperature, each repeated at least three times, not from a living nerve carrying nerve pain.

The authors also can’t yet rule out a simpler account.

In it, the pore has one open shape, and different triggers hold it steady for different lengths of time, which the recording equipment registers as different currents.

They argue that recent high-resolution images of the receptor, which caught the pore in several conducting shapes, make distinct states the likelier reading.

Either way, the states behave differently, the researchers noted.

The study points to a drug rather than producing one: a molecule that blocks the states tied to painful triggers and spares the one heat opens. The authors also raise a larger question.

AI-driven drug design is now producing molecules that bind to sites on proteins with no known natural partner. How a protein behaves when something new binds to it is still poorly understood.

The full study was published in the journal PNAS.

Climate change is making Earth progressively rounder, new research finds.

The findings are a signal of human-caused climate change, however, and tracking the shape of the Earth can help reveal just how much impact people are having on its ice caps.

Our planet is not a perfect sphere. Because of its rotation, it is a bit flatter at the poles and bulges in the middle. But the new study reveals that Earth is getting rounder at an accelerated rate, largely due to shifts caused by the melting polar ice caps.

This finding applies only to the solid parts of the planet; the ocean continues to bulge outward at the equator.

The study, published Sept. 9 in the journal JGR Solid Earth, used direct measurements from the global navigation satellite system (GNSS) to track the planet's shape-shifting over time. This system provides direct measurements, but there are limits in how it can be used. For example, there are few GNSS stations near the North and South poles and few in remote oceans because they must be deployed on buoys.

Christoforos Kotsakis, a professor of geodesy at the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki in Greece, developed a new method for analyzing GNSS data to better capture the changes in the planet's surface. He found that the poles are rising and the equator's bulge has been shrinking, each by about 0.04 inch (1 millimeter) per year between 2011 to 2015. That's a small number, considering Earth's diameter at the equator is about 7,926 miles (12,756 kilometers). But that change is bigger than between 1997-2000, when Earth was becoming about 0.02 inch (0.5 mm) rounder per year. This means Earth's shape is rounding at 0.016 inch (0.4 mm) more per year with each decade that passes.

The shift is being driven by polar ice melt, which takes pressure off the Earth's crust and allows it to rise slowly, similar to how rising bread dough springs back after you press a finger into it, Kotsakis reported. Meanwhile, increased mass toward the equator puts pressure on the crust there, reducing its bulge. However, there's a catch: When considering the solid Earth and the ocean together, the planet is getting flatter by about 0.03 inches (0.8 mm) a year because the ocean is getting bulgier at the equator as water shifts away from the poles. This 'water weight' overwhelms the rounding happening in the rock below.

However, the two methods can be used together to track the major effects of climate change, especially the melting of Greenland and Antarctica and the varying movements of ocean water.

The change has been drastic, she says. “I’m just really excited about the future.”

The treatment, a type of CAR T cell therapy, extracts immune cells from a person’s body and engineers them to attack the very cells causing the disease. First used to fight cancer, CAR T is now also targeting a variety of autoimmune diseases. If given the green light by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Montgomery-Haga’s treatment would be the first such therapy approved for an autoimmune condition — and it would be the first approved treatment for stiff person syndrome.

Stiff person syndrome may be just the beginning, says Carl June, the University of Pennsylvania immunologist and oncologist who spearheaded CAR T cell therapy for cancer decades ago. The “living drug” could herald a new era of therapeutics for autoimmune diseases like lupus, rheumatoid arthritis and even multiple sclerosis. “It’s revolutionary to think how far things have gone,” June says.

But he warns that it’s far too early to know if scientists have definitively cured stiff person syndrome. And there haven’t been enough people treated to get a good handle on all the possible side effects. “There could be long-term complications in autoimmune patients that don’t happen in our cancer patients,” he says. Other researchers urge caution. “It’s very important not to oversell it,” says Marc Scherlinger, a rheumatologist at Strasbourg University Hospital in France.

For Piquet, one crucial question is how long the treatment will last. In Montgomery-Haga’s case, the answer seems to be at least two years. Montgomery-Haga still considers herself disabled, or “disabled-ish,” as she calls it. What’s more, Montgomery-Haga says, “I think I’m still improving.”

There’s no single symptom that clued Montgomery-Haga in to her disease. In 2014, she started having difficulty picking things up off the floor. Then her boss asked her why she was limping — Montgomery-Haga hadn’t noticed. Her balance was off and her head felt weird, like “I was walking around in a fishbowl,” she says. It got to the point where Montgomery-Haga had difficulty crossing thresholds between rooms.

Montgomery-Haga would startle easily, prompting her body to stiffen up like a board. One time when cooking, she burned her finger, spasmed and collapsed, cracking the back of her head on the cabinets. She lay on the floor crying, but finally managed to right herself. “As soon as you hit the floor,” she says, “your muscles are like, ‘Fine,’ and you can get up again.”

Those unpredictable symptoms capture a slice of what life is like with stiff person syndrome, or SPS. The disease, which affects roughly 2 in 100,000 people in the United States, typically develops in a person’s 30s or 40s. It is about twice as common in women as in men.

Scientists don’t know what sets off the disease. But they think a person’s own antibodies start attacking a neurological network that helps muscles throughout the body relax. And when those tensed muscles are in opposition, like the biceps and triceps, they start fighting one another, Piquet says. That’s when people can end up in a stuck position, leading to stiffness. But because everyone’s immune system is different, patients’ experiences with the disease can vary greatly.

For Tara Zier, a former dentist based in Maryland, SPS started with extreme neck pain, blood pressure spikes, shortness of breath and feelings of impending doom. While some people struggle with mobility, Zier’s difficulties include standing and extreme fatigue. When she holds herself upright, her muscles clamp down on her spine like it’s in a vise. “Have you ever had a charley horse in your calf?” she asks. “Imagine one in your whole body that stops you in your tracks.”

Zier and Montgomery-Haga went years before learning what was behind their symptoms. Their cases mystified doctors — Zier saw 13 specialists and visited the emergency room nine times before getting a diagnosis. When she finally found out she had SPS three years after her first symptoms, Zier says she felt validated, like medicine was confirming what she already knew: “Something’s wrong with me.”

Montgomery-Haga’s 2018 diagnosis put her “over the moon,” she says. For nearly four years, she had gotten sicker and sicker — but doctors couldn’t tell her why. She felt as if they were giving up on her. With the diagnosis, Montgomery-Haga not only learned that SPS existed, she says, “I was told it’s treatable.”

Current treatments include muscle relaxers, anti-seizure medications and Botox injections in body parts that feel especially stiff. Although these off-label therapies can address people’s symptoms, they’re “doing nothing to treat the underlying autoimmune disease,” Piquet says. And some can come with potent side effects, like mental fog.

Zier, who has tried multiple combinations of different meds, remembers frequently losing her train of thought. She has since tapered off all but one drug, a low dose of Valium. “The trade-off,” she says, “is pain.”

People with SPS can also receive intravenous infusions of healthy antibodies, a treatment called IVIG, to decrease muscle spasms and stiffness. Montgomery-Haga found it helpful but time-consuming. She’d go to the clinic every three weeks for an infusion, which could take up to six hours.

“The bottom line is there are no FDA-approved therapies for this disease,” Piquet says. Now, the one most likely to cross the finish line first is something no one would have expected even a few years ago, CAR T pioneer June says. If you told doctors that CAR T cell therapy was going to be FDA approved for an autoimmune disease, he says, “people would have laughed.”

June may be best known for his work in cancer, but he’s been keenly following the progress in auto-immune therapy. His daughter, now in her 30s, was diagnosed with juvenile rheumatoid arthritis when she was 9 years old. For her, that’s meant over 20 years of treatment with drugs that bring a bundle of side effects, such as fatigue and risk of infections. “It would be much better to be cured,” he says.

In autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and SPS, a person’s own antibodies attack tissues throughout the body, from blood vessels to cartilage to neurons. Wiping out these rogue antibodies can stop the attacks. But CAR T cell therapy doesn’t act on the antibodies themselves. It goes straight to the source, snuffing out the cellular factories that manufacture them.

Those antibody factories are known as B cells. To eliminate them, doctors recruit T cells, which act as the body’s security system and work together to hunt down and destroy problematic cells. In the lab, scientists fine-tune these hunters, outfitting them with a molecular homing device called a chimeric antigen receptor, or CAR. When infused back into patients’ bodies, the newly-engineered T cells can target the antibody factories directly — like a saboteur told exactly what to destroy.

The CAR T cell therapy Lynn Montgomery-Haga received wipes out B cells, including the ones likely driving her disease. Doctors hope this therapy “resets” her immune system, getting rid of harmful B cells and letting healthy new ones take their place.

“The secret behind CAR T cell therapy is that you completely deplete B cells in the body,” says Georg Schett, a rheumatologist at Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg in Germany. Temporarily wiping out a person’s B cells lets the body replace them with fresh, unproblematic ones, Schett says. He calls it an “immune reset.”

CAR T cell therapy got its start in the 2010s, when researchers used engineered T cells to target cancer. In 2017, the FDA approved the first CAR T cell therapies for two blood cancers, acute lymphoblastic leukemia and large B cell lymphoma. Soon after, scientists saw the first tantalizing hints that it might work for autoimmune diseases, too. In some cases, cancer patients with autoimmune disorders reported improvements in both conditions after treatment.

But most researchers didn’t think it could be that easy, Schett says. After all, autoimmune diseases were driven by a complex network of cell types. How could targeting just one of them work?

That thinking underwent a paradigm shift in 2021 when Schett and his team published the first major paper reporting a successful treatment with the therapy. The patient was a 20-year-old woman with a severe case of lupus, a complex, sometimes-fatal illness that can damage the kidneys and other organs. Within five weeks of CAR T cell therapy treatment, signs of her disease began to vanish.

Results like these were “unheard of,” says rheumatologist Maximilian Konig of Johns Hopkins Medicine in Baltimore. Lupus is extremely difficult to treat, and for decades, most drugs tested against it failed. No one had ever seen the kind of improvements that Schett’s patient experienced.

After the paper came out, “everybody was talking about it,” Konig says. Today, more than five years later, the woman’s lupus hasn’t returned, Schett says. “We consider this patient cured,” he says.

The woman’s response was no fluke. The following year, Schett’s team reported similar results in five more lupus patients. All went into remission after CAR T cell therapy.

This was the true tipping point, June says. “It’s rare, very rare, that you get such striking results.”

What followed was an explosion of research around the world. Scientists are now testing CAR T cell therapy in nearly a dozen autoimmune diseases linked to harmful B cells, including lupus, rheumatoid arthritis and a connective-tissue disease known as systemic sclerosis.

Kyverna, the California-based company behind the SPS trial, is also running trials for neurological autoimmune conditions including generalized myasthenia gravis and progressive multiple sclerosis, or MS.

One reason using CAR T cell therapy for neurological conditions excites researchers is because of the cells’ potential to slip past the blood-brain barrier. That means they might be able to access pockets of smoldering inflammation inaccessible to other drugs, says neurologist Rebecca Farber of Columbia University.

That could be a huge help in diseases like MS, in which a person’s antibodies attack the protective sheaths around nerve cells, causing numbness, brain fog and difficulty walking. Still, Farber doubts that CAR T cell therapy could fix existing damage. “We’re not expecting it to repair the nervous system,” she says. But it’s possible the treatment could freeze the disease’s progress. Patients are already asking her team about future treatments.

For Piquet, who cares for roughly 100 people with SPS, Kyverna’s trial offered a compelling opportunity to help her patients. Montgomery-Haga cried when Piquet told her about the trial at one of their regular Zoom appointments. She recalls wondering, is this the treatment that “might help me be normal again?”

Though Montgomery-Haga leapt at the chance to try Kyverna’s treatment, called miv-cel, CAR T cell therapy is no walk in the park. Before starting, doctors sit patients down to “talk about the good, the bad and the potentially ugly,” Piquet says.

That includes the rigorous prep work needed before people receive their engineered T cells. Patients must first get low-dose chemotherapy, which flushes T cells from the body and makes space for the new ones. Chemo can ambush the body with symptoms including hair thinning, nausea, vomiting and diarrhea.

Treatment itself also carries risks. It destroys most B cells in the body — not just the ones that cause disease. That can lead to fever, inflammation and potentially risky infections. In Kyverna’s trial, for example, 15 percent of participants had severely low white blood cell counts after treatment. Such symptoms were expected, Piquet says, and were easily treated.

Other CAR T treatments seem to have fared far worse. In August, the pharmaceutical company Novartis paused clinical trials of its therapy, rap-cel, across a spectrum of autoimmune diseases after three patients died. Bristol Myers Squibb has also paused similar studies after some patients experienced neurological complications, according to the Wall Street Journal. Details of the incidents were slow to emerge and have set the field on edge. “It’s a tragedy,” Konig says. He calls such outcomes an important wake-up call.

But red flags in one trial don’t mean all CAR T products are dangerous, Konig says. That’s because many different designs and manufacturing processes exist, and scientists can’t always predict what will make one product toxic and another safe. Kyverna’s miv-cel, for instance, has been consistently well-tolerated in the more than 100 patients treated, a spokesperson for the company says.

CAR T cell therapies are transformative, Konig says. “They’re going to change the field — but there are risks.”

Another risk is that the once-vanquished disease will return. Doctors don’t know all the genetic and environmental conditions that trigger autoimmune disease. Some researchers wonder whether vulnerable individuals remain vulnerable, even after treatment. But because it often takes multiple insults for someone to tip into a state of disease, Scherlinger says, CAR T cell therapy could buy patients lots of time.

After 10 days in the hospital for her treatment, Montgomery-Haga returned to recover at home. In the following months, she started to notice some changes. It was easier getting around the house, for one. And she felt her balance and stability improving. She can now kneel down to garden and clean without the days of recovery it used to require. “I can vacuum my house,” she says. “It seems so little, but now I can vacuum!”

Montgomery-Haga can even sit down “crisscross applesauce,” she says. “I couldn’t do that for, like, 10 years.”

Four months after the treatment, Piquet’s team tested Montgomery-Haga’s walking ability. In a hallway at University of Colorado Anschutz’s research building, the team marked out 25 feet with little orange cones. At one end stood Montgomery-Haga. At the other, Piquet watched behind a video camera. This time, Montgomery-Haga’s gait was smooth and her arms were down, not hovering out into the air to catch herself if she fell. “You could see that she was walking with a lot more confidence,” Piquet says.

The team saw similar results in other SPS patients. Some who had difficulty walking before treatment now wanted to run, Piquet says. “It’s incredible to just see those patients speeding down the hallway.” She remembers smiles, high fives and a sense of celebration in the air. Eight of 12 people who had used a walking aid for the initial test didn’t need one for the follow-up, Piquet reported at the American Academy of Neurology meeting in April.

In September, Kyverna reported the trial’s one-year results: Patients maintained mobility improvements, with a third completing their 25-foot walk about as fast as a healthy adult. Additionally, nearly all patients no longer rely on immuno-suppressive drugs to tamp down harmful immune activity. That’s good news because such drugs can bring all sorts of nasty side effects, like vomiting, bleeding and infections.

The results with SPS are “really something to celebrate,” June says. But only time and many more treatments will reveal how safe CAR T really is, he says.

That’s a concern for Zier, who says she wouldn’t feel comfortable trying the therapy at this point. Zier doesn’t know how she’d respond. She’s a single mother with two kids; even a rare side effect could disrupt her life in a way she may not be able to manage. And these days, she’s managing as well as she can. Zier is the founder and CEO of The Stiff Person Syndrome Research Foundation, which supports research for SPS treatments.

Kyverna is in the process of submitting miv-cel for FDA approval. The company is seeking priority review and hopes the treatment might be available to patients in 2027. If SPS is the first autoimmune disease to have an FDA-approved CAR T cell therapy, myasthenia gravis could be the second, says Warner Biddle, Kyverna’s CEO. What’s more, miv-cel also appeared to improve symptoms in six patients with severe cases of rheumatoid arthritis, researchers reported in August in Nature Medicine.

As for what’s next, the door is wide open. Some scientists are working to make off-the-shelf forms of CAR T that wouldn’t require personalized engineering. Others are trying to reprogram T cells directly inside people’s bodies, rather than in the lab. Still others are trying to make T cells that target only the B cells responsible for disease. In the future, doctors might use such drugs to help patients avoid the cumbersome treatment process that Montgomery-Haga endured.

Montgomery-Haga’s own future looks far different than what she had imagined a few years ago. In 2026, she took a trip to Washington, D.C., to see the monuments. Now she’d like to visit Paris, Scotland and Ireland, too.

For so many years, Montgomery-Haga has looked at trips and activities — and even household chores — that most people take for granted, thinking, “Man, I wish I could do that,” she says. “Now I can.”

#5560. What if Dark Energy Was Once Negative? Scientists Uncover a Surprising New Possibility

A gravity model that lets dark energy change sign could help resolve a persistent disagreement over how fast the universe is expanding.

In an existing theory of gravity, the same present-day matter density can fit two very different histories of the universe. Researchers had largely pursued the one that kept dark energy’s density positive. Exploring the other has now revealed a possible history in which that density began below zero and turned positive billions of years later.

Dark energy is the mysterious ingredient believed to drive the universe’s accelerating expansion. The simplest explanation treats it as unchanged throughout cosmic history. In the new analysis, however, the effects attributed to dark energy arise from a modified description of gravity. Its density is an effective quantity derived from the gravitational equations, rather than a property of a newly introduced substance.

Prof. Özgür Akarsu of Istanbul Technical University’s (ITU) Department of Physics led the international study, published in 2025 in Physical Review D. The work was carried out in his ITU research group with postdoctoral researcher Dr. N. Merve Uzun and doctoral student Bilal Bulduk. They collaborated with Prof. Nihan Katırcı of Doğuş University, a former postdoctoral researcher in the group, and Prof. Antonio De Felice of Kyoto University’s Yukawa Institute for Theoretical Physics, one of the world’s leading centers for theoretical physics.

Akarsu and collaborators had already been developing a scenario called sign-switching ΛCDM, or ΛsCDM. In that scenario, the cosmological constant switches from negative to positive a few billion years after the Big Bang. Analyses have shown that this history can agree with observations from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) and ease several long-standing disagreements in cosmology. Among them is the Hubble tension, the mismatch between estimates of how fast the universe is expanding today.

The new gravity model offers a possible physical basis for that earlier scenario. It also shows preliminary promise in addressing the expansion-rate disagreement. In an extended version that includes a cosmological constant, the model remains consistent with the cosmic microwave background, the ancient light left over from the early universe, while accommodating the higher expansion rates measured locally.

That extension allows another possibility: the acceleration of cosmic expansion could have slowed, or even stopped, in the very recent past. Recent analyses of DESI data have raised this possibility as well, although the model still needs rigorous testing against the full observations.

Akarsu said, “For several years we have been studying a scenario in which dark energy switches sign, and it has held up remarkably well against data such as DESI’s. The open question was what physical mechanism could produce such behavior. So we deliberately looked at a branch of a well-known theory of gravity that had been set aside because it violates the usual expectation that the dark energy density stays positive. The sign change emerged naturally from the theory itself.”

The team’s starting point was teleparallel f(T) gravity. Whereas Einstein’s general relativity describes gravity through the curvature of spacetime, teleparallel gravity uses torsion, a different geometric property associated with twisting. Researchers have investigated extensions of this approach for years to see whether modified gravity could account for cosmic acceleration.

The exponential version examined in the study contains the two mathematical branches that lead to different cosmic histories. Earlier cosmological studies had favored the branch that followed the usual expectation, inherited from general relativity, of a positive dark energy density. Its evolution closely resembles the standard cosmological model.

On the alternative branch, effective dark energy density is negative during the universe’s first few billion years. It crosses zero at a redshift of around 1.5, corresponding to roughly 9.5 billion years ago, and remains positive afterward. The value governing the branch depends on the universe’s present matter density, so this behavior does not require an extra independently adjustable cosmological parameter. The basic model has six cosmological parameters, the same number as the standard ΛCDM model.

A modified theory must also account for the precise measurements that already support Einstein’s description of gravity. The paper finds that the alternative branch can remain consistent with Solar System tests because its departures from general relativity are naturally suppressed in dense environments.

On cosmic scales, those departures provide opportunities to test the model. Alongside its predictions for expansion, researchers can examine how galaxies and larger structures grow and how gravity bends light through weak gravitational lensing. Galaxy motions offer another test through redshift-space distortions, patterns produced when those motions affect the redshifts used to map galaxy positions. Together, these measurements can help determine whether the proposed cosmic history is plausible.

DESI is making such comparisons increasingly demanding. By measuring the positions of millions of galaxies and quasars, it is assembling the largest three-dimensional map of the universe and tracing expansion over billions of years. In March 2025, the collaboration released results from its first three years of observations. When combined with measurements of the cosmic microwave background, supernovae, and weak lensing, they strengthened earlier hints that dark energy might evolve rather than behave as a simple cosmological constant.

The strength of those hints varies with the datasets used, and they have not reached the level required for a definitive discovery. They also do not establish that dark energy changed sign. The ITU-led study was first submitted in October 2024, months before DESI’s March 2025 announcement, and provides a theoretical explanation to investigate.

The search for physical mechanisms that could produce a sign change remains active across cosmology. The authors suggest that other modified gravity theories may also contain possibilities overlooked because researchers required effective dark energy density to stay positive.

DESI has now completed its planned five-year survey, observing more than 47 million galaxies and quasars. Its full dataset will offer a more rigorous test of whether the apparent evolution of dark energy persists. The ITU team has not yet compared its model with that full dataset.

“We identified an alternative path for the Universe within an existing theory. The next step is to test it rigorously against the full observational data. The preliminary picture is encouraging, and we are particularly interested in what it may tell us about the Hubble tension,” said Prof. Özgür Akarsu.

#5555. Scientists Discover a Hidden Oxygen Source Deep Inside Earth That Could Rewrite Our Planet’s History

Ancient eruptions may have helped shape the air we breathe, with laboratory experiments revealing magma’s unexpected ability to store extra oxygen.

Molten rock deep inside Earth can take up an extra supply of oxygen under pressure, according to a new study. That finding raises the possibility that rising magma could release oxygen on its way to the surface, a process that may have contributed to increases in atmospheric oxygen billions of years ago.

Oxygen makes up more than half the atoms in Earth’s silicate interior, but its abundance does not necessarily mean it is available to escape into the atmosphere. Much of it is chemically bound to other elements. Geoscientists have generally assumed that the quantities and electrical charge states of elements such as silicon, magnesium, and iron determine how much oxygen magma contains. Knowing those ingredients should let them calculate the oxygen needed to balance the chemistry. Whether molten rock could absorb additional oxygen as a separate volatile component, as it does water and carbon dioxide, has remained largely unexplored.

The study, published in National Science Review, was led by Dr. Yanhao Lin and Dr. Ho-kwang Mao of the Center for High Pressure Science and Technology Advanced Research (HPSTAR). They worked with Prof. Haiyang Niu of Northwestern Polytechnical University and Prof. Wim van Westrenen of Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam.

To test whether magma could take up oxygen beyond that calculated amount, the researchers needed to give it an additional oxygen supply. They added platinum oxide (PtO₂) to create an oxygen-rich environment for synthetic mid-ocean ridge basalt, a laboratory-made version of volcanic rock from the ocean floor. Using piston-cylinder and cubic presses, they melted the basalt at temperatures of 1,200 to 1,800 °C (about 2,190 to 3,270 °F) and pressures ranging from 1 atmosphere to 7 gigapascals. At the upper end, that pressure is roughly 1 million pounds per square inch.

The team also ran experiments at the same pressures and temperatures without platinum oxide. Comparing the two sets would help reveal whether making extra oxygen available changed how much the molten rock retained.

After rapidly cooling, or quenching, the melts into glass, the researchers measured the oxygen remaining in each sample. Not all of it counted as extra. Some was needed to balance the other elements, and some was contained in water and carbon dioxide. By measuring the glass’s elemental composition and its water and carbon dioxide contents, the team could subtract those accounted-for amounts from the total oxygen. They called the remainder “excess oxygen.”

With platinum oxide added, excess oxygen was close to zero at 1 atmosphere but exceeded 5% of the sample’s weight at 7 gigapascals. The control experiments without platinum oxide yielded only 0.2% to 0.5% excess oxygen by weight. High pressure therefore appeared to help the melt take advantage of the additional oxygen supply. Some oxygen may have escaped during quenching, so the measured amounts likely underestimate how much the molten samples originally held.

There was another distinction to resolve: had the oxygen become part of the material, or was it simply trapped inside gas bubbles? Raman spectroscopy, which uses scattered light to probe a material’s structure, detected no signature of oxygen gas in the glass. That indicated the excess oxygen had been incorporated into the glass structure.

Simulations led by Niu examined how the melt could accommodate this extra oxygen at the atomic level. The team used deep neural network (DNN) interatomic potentials, computational models that describe how atoms interact, with the accuracy of ab initio calculations based on fundamental physical principles. In the simulations, oxygen entered the melt until equilibrium was reached, meaning there was no further net uptake.

As more oxygen dissolved, the electrical charges on oxygen atoms spread across a wider range, and a small fraction of those atoms formed peroxide-like bonds with one another. These changes offered clues to how the melt’s chemistry could accommodate oxygen beyond the amount predicted by the conventional accounting. The simulations used a simplified magnesium-silicon-oxygen system and produced lower oxygen contents than the basalt experiments, but they reproduced the same increase with pressure.

That pressure dependence resembles the behavior of water and carbon dioxide, which dissolve more readily in magma at higher pressures. The authors therefore suggest that oxygen should also be treated as a separate component of silicate melts. Doing so could change how researchers assess the evolution of magma oceans, the release of volatile substances, and the development of planetary atmospheres, with implications for long-term habitability on other terrestrial planets.

Within Earth, magma formed in oxidized regions of the mantle could collect substantial excess oxygen while under pressure. As it rises, the falling pressure could reduce how much oxygen it can retain, causing a gradual release. The oxygen could then drive oxidation reactions in the shallow mantle, crust, and atmosphere, allowing magma to transport oxidizing power from deep underground toward the surface.

The researchers propose that this process may help explain a timing overlap in Earth’s history. Major increases in atmospheric oxygen roughly 2.5 to 2.0 billion years ago coincided with the formation of some of the largest large igneous provinces (LIPs), vast accumulations of rock produced by volcanic activity. Their rough estimate suggests that if magma erupted during that period released just 0.27% of its weight as excess oxygen, it could have supplied as much oxygen as today’s atmosphere contains.

#5552. Sea Spirit: Available for Sale or Charter Starting in 2028

The Sea Spirit is available for sale or charter starting in October 2028, according to SunStone Ships’ website.

Having completed 35 years of service in 2026, the 114-passenger vessel is currently sailing for Poseidon Expeditions as part of a long-term charter agreement.

Originally part of Renaissance Cruises’ original series of megayachts, the former Renaissance Five was built at the Nuovi Cantieri Apuania in Carrara, Italy.

After sailing for a series of cruise lines over the years, it was converted into an expedition vessel in late 2010.

According to SunStone Ships, the Sea Spirit currently offers 60 guest cabins, in addition to a series of public rooms and facilities.

Among them are a 128-seat restaurant, a 110-seat presentation lounge and a mud room. The ship is also equipped with a gift shop, a gym, a wellness area, a medical center, a specialty restaurant and an outdoor jacuzzi area.

It underwent a $2.5-million refurbishment in 2017, which was described as a ship-wide restoration of public areas and staterooms.

The vessel also offers kayaks and Zodiacs for expedition service, in addition to a zero-speed stabilizer system.

Other features include fully air-conditioned interiors and two elevators, as well as accommodations for up to 85 crew members.

Before being chartered to Poseidon in 2014, the Sea Spirit also sailed for Quark Expeditions, Cruise West, Star Cruises and Sun Cruises.

Upon withdrawing the 4,200-ton vessel, Poseidon will take delivery of a new ship, as reported by Cruise Industry News earlier this year.

The expedition operator entered an agreement with United Waterways to operate one of the company’s newbuilds starting in September 2028.

According to United, the vessel is being developed as part of its white-label ocean platform, with features and further details to be announced at a later date.

#5551. Why this major e-bike brand just started selling battery-less electric bikes

Buying an e-bike without a battery might sound a little like buying a car without a motor. But that’s exactly what Minnesota-based e-bike company Monarc is now offering – and there’s actually a pretty good reason for it.

Monarc has just rolled out a new pricing structure for its highly acclaimed Marker and Tracer e-bikes, allowing customers to choose between two batteries, one battery, or no battery at all.

The latter option knocks a cool $500 off the price.

The Monarc Marker, which normally costs $1,999 with two batteries, can now be purchased for $1,749 with one battery or $1,499 without a battery.

The Monarc Tracer gets the same $250-per-battery reduction, dropping from $1,899 with two batteries to $1,649 with one battery or just $1,399 without either battery.

Of course, the battery-less versions aren’t really intended for someone buying their first Monarc. Instead, the company is targeting existing owners who already have compatible batteries and chargers sitting around.

That makes sense, since when the company first launched its premier e-bikes earlier this year, it did so with an impressively high-value package that included not just one but two batteries. At the time, it was seen as a bold move – especially since many e-bike companies hold back spare batteries as a significant upsell opportunity. But in this case, it looks like Monarc was angling for a different strategy more akin to the power tool world: build all your products around a single battery and then let owners swap just a few batteries among them.

If you already own a Monarc and want to add a second bike for a spouse, family member, or just a different style of riding, why buy another battery and charger if you don’t need them?

It’s a surprisingly logical idea, even if “battery-less e-bike” sounds like an oxymoron at first.

The one-battery option will likely have even broader appeal. While Monarc has traditionally included two batteries with its e-bikes, which was great for riders who want maximum range, not everyone wanted or needed a spare battery. And since batteries are also the single most expensive component on an e-bike, offering the models with a single battery is a great way to reduce cost and save owners money.

For riders who generally take shorter trips and can simply recharge at home, dropping the second battery saves $250 without changing anything about the bike itself.

Monarc launched earlier this year, coming out swinging hard with higher quality e-bikes than we’ve typically seen from direct-to-consumer companies, yet at prices that put bike shop brands to shame.

I’ve personally had the chance to get a much closer look at Monarc than I’ve often gotten with most e-bike companies. Earlier this year, I visited the company’s headquarters in Minnesota, where I met the team, saw its operation firsthand, and even chatted with members of its in-house customer service team between their customer service calls.

I’ll have an in-depth video and article on that experience soon, but suffice it to say that my detailed visit helped drive home what Monarc is trying to do differently.

The company is essentially attempting to offer the type of nicer, more premium e-bike experience we normally associate with much more expensive brands, but using a direct-to-consumer model to keep the prices considerably lower.

That’s an increasingly difficult needle to thread in the e-bike industry. There are plenty of cheap direct-to-consumer e-bikes available online, just as there are plenty of polished premium e-bikes sold through dealers for several thousand dollars. Trying to land somewhere between those two worlds – better bikes and real support without premium-bike pricing – is a much smaller target. But with the backing of its parent company Lectric Ebikes, which happens to be the largest e-bike company in North America, Monarc seems to have stuck the landing gracefully.

The brand has impressed me so far with how seriously the team seems to take its mission. It’s one thing to put a customer service email address on a website. It’s another to walk into a company’s headquarters and see the people actually answering those emails and phone calls, sometimes jogging over to the engineering team to ensure they’ve got the most up-to-date tech answers for customers.

The new battery pricing strategy is another interesting example of the company thinking a bit differently.

Monarc calls its traditional two-battery package “The Monarc Way,” and that remains the full-monty option for riders who want the most range and flexibility.

The new single-battery version, dubbed “Monarc Base,” is designed for riders who simply don’t need as much battery capacity and would rather keep another $250 in their pockets.

Then there’s “The Second Monarc,” which includes neither a battery nor charger. That one is specifically aimed at current Monarc owners who can share batteries between bikes.

And personally, I really like the concept. I have just two Ryobi batteries but probably six or seven Ryobi power tools. I’m not really doing a lot of sanding and angle grinding at the same time, so it makes sense. And in the same way you’re probably not commuting and mountain-biking at the same time, this model gives Monarc a way to let riders tackle different e-biking scenarios without the extra cost of several redundant batteries sitting on the shelf.

We’ve seen e-bike companies increasingly lock riders into proprietary battery ecosystems, but there can actually be an upside to that ecosystem once you own multiple compatible bikes. If you’ve already got two perfectly good batteries sitting in your garage, forcing you to buy two more just because you want another bike doesn’t make much sense.

I bought a batteryless conversion kit for one of my pedal bikes, plus a spare battery dock from AliExpress. The dock matches the battery from my Chinese ebike. Result: two bikes, one battery. I just swap it into whichever I'm going to use.

The $500 savings also makes Monarc’s pricing quite a bit more interesting. A $1,399 Tracer or $1,499 Marker sounds considerably different from an e-bike pushing two grand, even if you obviously need to already have a battery to make those prices useful. And to be perfectly honest, those bikes have no business costing that little, especially when you consider the higher end parts like Shimano CUES transmissions, Talon P4 quad-piston brakes, GNEISS reverse arch suspension forks, Bafang 750W motors, and other high-end parts adorning the bikes. These are budget bikes prices for more premium-end gear.

For first-time buyers, I suspect the $250 savings on the single-battery models will be the more meaningful development. Many riders simply don’t need two batteries, and now they don’t have to pay for capacity they aren’t going to use.

So yes, Monarc is now selling e-bikes without batteries.

But rather than another case of an e-bike company finding something to remove from the box to advertise a lower price, this one actually seems to make sense.
