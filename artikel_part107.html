#1100. FIFA president Gianni Infantino apologizes for World Cup privatization plans but still faces scrutiny

FIFA president Gianni Infantino apologized to members of the FIFA Council in a meeting on Thursday in Zurich, the first gathering of the organization's main decision-making body since Infantino announced and then scrapped a controversial plan to privatize the World Cup.

The specifics of Infantino's apology were not made public but FIFA said that the Council was updated on the legal background behind FIFA Forward Enterprise (FFE), which was a proposed subsidiary that controlled the commercial rights to the men's and women's World Cups and Club World Cups. FFE was valued at around $20 billion and minority stakes were to be sold to private investors headlined by Thrive Eternal, the investment firm of venture capitalist Joshua Kushner, the brother of U.S. president Donald Trump's son-in-law, Jared. The plan received criticism from the moment it became public knowledge in a report from the Times of London in July and was abandoned within a matter of days, though a legal battle over the proposal is now underway in the U.S. and the incident continues to loom large as Infantino seeks re-election.

Infantino fielded questions from Council members about FFE on Thursday and asked them to submit more queries for a subsequent meeting, the session ultimately drawing mixed reviews from those in the room. Ahmed Yahya, the president of Mauritania's federation, said he believes Infantino is the "right president" and that the majority of FIFA's member associations (MAs) feel the same way but others held back on endorsements. German Football Association president Bernd Neuendorf insisted he had no confidence in Infantino, while England's chairwoman Debbie Hewitt said Infantino was not forthcoming enough in his answers on Thursday.

"One of the disappointing things is that we didn't get answers to anything that we had specifically requested, for example, getting the papers from FFE," Hewitt said. "I asked the question, 'Why not?' and we were asked to submit those questions in writing. I think the man has to answer those questions and if he doesn't answer those questions now, he's not fit to be FIFA president and that was what was shared in the meeting. I've repeatedly asked for the papers to be shared. I haven't received them and still in spite of the request today, I haven't received them."

Infantino attempted to address some of the concerns around FFE, including agreeing to set up a working group to review governance procedures. FIFA also proposed an increase to the funds it usually distributes to the 211 MAs, who could now pocket $25 million over the next four-year cycle should the plan get the green light. This was in response to criticism from UEFA, Concacaf and the AFC that FIFA should use its existing reserves to further fund soccer projects around the world rather than FFE, which was unpopularly billed as a means to increase available resources for MAs.

The scrutiny on the FIFA president does not end there. He continues to face criticism for FIFA's handling of Folarin Balogun's red card suspension during the World Cup, in which the governing body cited a rarely used rule to lift his one-match ban for the U.S. men's national team's round of 16 defeat to Belgium. Infantino has been unable to shake the allegation that political pressure led to the decision, particularly after Trump said he called Infantino asking him to review FIFA's longstanding rules.

FIFA's legal team will now review the disciplinary procedures following the Balogun affair and are set to present their findings at the Council's next meeting in December. Infantino's detractors continue to cite the incident as an example of the president's shortcomings in recent months, including the awarding of the inaugural FIFA Peace Prize to Trump at last year's World Cup draw in Washington, D.C. It is unclear if the Peace Prize will be awarded again this year, since FIFA's Social Responsibility Committee will assess the award before the Council makes a decision on it in December.

The choices regarding the Peace Prize and Balogun's suspension were taken outside of FIFA's usual governance procedures, which is why Council members continue to ask for more information on how those decisions were reached.

"It is the rationale for why FFE happened in the way that it did, the Balogun case, the Peace Prize," Hewitt said. "The responses to those questions, which I still don't have answers to, will determine from my personal point of view whether or not Gianni is the right candidate. That will be the point at which or not we can say that any individual that was involved in FFE can or cannot be trusted because until we see what actually happened it's very difficult to say behavior needs or not to change … Those papers coming to Council are a crucial next part of us feeling comfortable as to what, if any, criminal proceedings need to take place."

Hewitt believed there was "some contrition" in Infantino's apology but said the president did not offer enough information to change her stance on his re-election.

"From my position, nothing has shifted," she said. "I've no new information that makes me feel any differently, other than we do have a president who is apologetic."

Infantino is seeking his fourth and final term in March's election, which will take place during the FIFA Congress in Rabat, Morocco. Ex-Switzerland international Ramon Vega is his only opponent thus far though prospective candidates have until Nov. 18 to submit their interest.

#1097. Teenage swimmer breaks two world records in four days

Sixteen-year-old Summer McIntosh broke the world record for the 400m individual medley at the Canadian swimming trials on Saturday – just days after breaking the world record for the 400m freestyle.

McIntosh finished the 400m individual medley in a time of 4:25.87, breaking the mark set at the 2016 Rio Olympics by Hungarian swimmer Katinka Hosszú with a time of 4:26.36.

The result continues the teenager’s brilliant week after she broke the 400m freestyle world record last Tuesday – breaking defending Olympic champion Ariarne Titmus’ previous record of 3:56.40, which was set last May, in a time of 3:56.08.

Speaking after her latest triumph, McIntosh shared her emotions.

“That’s the first time I really vividly heard the crowd during my race,” McIntosh said, per Reuters.

“It’s amazing to have all my family and friends in the stands, cheering me on. It really helped me in the last 100 meters.”

The Canadian teen made her Olympic debut aged 14 at Tokyo 2020, where she finished fourth in the 200-meter freestyle.

She bagged gold in the 200m butterfly and 400m medley at the world championships in 2022, as well as two golds at the Commonwealth Games in the 200m and 400m medleys.

She now trains in Florida under coach Brent Arckey with the Sarasota Sharks team.

#1091. World Cup 2034: Does Saudi Arabia shelving Neom Stadium spell trouble?

Since winning the right to hold the 2034 World Cup unopposed, Saudi Arabia has been pulling away from sport. With reports that its most ambitious stadium project will be abandoned, what does this mean for the World Cup?

It was billed as "the most unique stadium in the world," a key pillar of Saudi Arabia's World Cup 2034 plans. But now Neom Stadium, part of the kingdom's ambitious aims to build a 170-kilometer-long (111-mile) city in the desert known as The Line, looks set to be scrapped, with the broader project scaled back significantly.

A recent Reuters report said the stadium, which according to the country's bid book was going to be "350 meters (1,148 feet) above ground" and have "stunning vistas, and a roof created from the city itself," would no longer be built for the tournament, where it was to host a quarterfinal and several other games.

Observers of the gulf country saw this coming.

"When I was in Saudi Arabia in December there were already plenty of signals that the whole Neom project was being put on the back burner, if not abandoned altogether," F. Gregory Gause III, a regional expert from the Middle East Institute think tank in Washington D.C., told DW.

"I see it as part of a general reevaluation of their fiscal situation, both in light of the oil market before the current war and in light of the damage that the war is doing. The drawback from sports commitments is just part of a more general reassessment of priorities in the current, more straitened, situation."

Saudi Arabia's sports splurge that saw players like Cristiano Ronaldo join the country's football league and the kingdom invest in all sorts of sports in myriad ways has slowed dramatically. The country pulled funding for the multibillion dollar LIV Golf as well as events in snooker, tennis and rugby and also withdrew from ambitious plans to host the 2029 Asian Winter Games in Neom.

Sports had formed a key part of Saudi Arabia's Project 2030, which aimed to diversify the country's economy away from oil and increase its soft power. But Yasir Al-Rumayyan, the governor of the Saudi-government operated Public Investment Fund (PIF), which also owns Premier League football club Newcastle United, said earlier this year that the PIF was "reassessing its priorities" amid war in the region.

"The impact of the war has not only made the economic rationalization more important, but it's also accelerated the desire to rethink some of these projects in the sense that Neom is now being repurposed as an industrial center on the Red Sea, which has obviously assumed much greater urgency since the Strait of Hormuz was effectively blocked," Kristian Coates Ulrichsen, author of "Kingdom of Football: Saudi Arabia and the Remaking of World Soccer," told DW.

There are, Coates Ulrichsen continued, clear parallels with the 2022 World Cup in Qatar, where plans to build 12 stadiums resulted in only eight being used at the tournament. The Arab Spring uprising arrived shortly after the bid was accepted by FIFA and, though it didn't impact the country directly, it destabilized the region both politically and economically.

Neither Saudi Arabia's World Cup organizing authority or Sports Ministry has responded to questions from DW about whether we may again see fewer stadiums than the planned 15 for the 2034 World Cup, which could feature 64 teams.

"We're seeing the same obviously with Saudi Arabia now," said Coates Ulrichsen, who is also a fellow for the Middle East at Rice University in the US.

"[FIFA President] Gianni Infantino announced Saudi would be the only bidder on the 4th of October 2023. Three days later was October 7th, so within three days, the region was effectively in flames," he said.

Since then, there has been the Israel-US war against Iran, while Houthi attacks continue to hit Saudi Arabia as part of the conflict in Yemen, meaning that: "This whole hosting cycle is taking place against a backdrop of considerable uncertainty and kind of real tension."

But though the scope and cost of it may be reduced, Saudi Arabia's softening commitment to sport will not likely apply so firmly to the biggest sporting event in the world, said Gause.

"I think it continues to be a major priority, because of its global importance and because the Saudis will want to do at least as well, if not outshine, Qatar's hosting earlier."

Relations between Saudi Arabia and Qatar have improved since an agreement in 2021 but the pair remain regional rivals, something Coates Ulrichsen agrees will spur the 2034 hosts on.

As such, the 2034 tournament will still likely be a high-end affair, with Saudi Arabia building most stadiums from scratch. But, said Coates Ulrichsen, it may look somewhat different to what was planned.

"The fact that at least the Saudis were able to host a big event with 60,000 people in the stadiums, full stadiums without any security concerns [the Gulf Cup football final which they won this week] is a good sign," he said.

The regional expert thinks more matches could end up being hosted in the two biggest Saudi cities, Riyadh and Jeddah. This may be at the expense of some venues nearer the east coast such as Al Khobar or Al Ahsa. These are separated from Iran by the Persian Gulf, which includes the Strait of Hormuz, and could be reconsidered depending on the state of political affairs.

Saudi Arabia host football's Asian Cup in January, which Coates Ulrichsen thinks will be an "early test of whether that is viable to have a weeks-long major tournament under these conditions."

With nearly eight years until the World Cup, the political and economic situation in the country is difficult to predict. While the supposed shelving of Neom Stadium and general Saudi sporting withdrawal poses questions of commitment more broadly, it appears the World Cup show will go on. But probably not quite as billed.

#1090. NASA’s Webb reveals the wild weather of a mysterious world 20 light-years away

Scientists have discovered a powerful new way to decode the weather on distant worlds, revealing surprisingly organized patterns hidden within the turbulent atmosphere of a mysterious object 20 light-years from Earth.

Researchers at Trinity College Dublin used observations from NASA's James Webb Space Telescope to investigate SIMP 0136, a brown dwarf previously associated with phenomena resembling the northern lights. They found that its complex weather is largely governed by just two factors: changes in temperature and the vertical structure of its clouds.

The discovery offers a new way to understand the atmospheric conditions of distant worlds and could help astronomers investigate planets far beyond our Solar System.

How NASA's Webb Telescope Decoded Alien Weather

To investigate SIMP 0136, the researchers analyzed observations collected by NASA's James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) using a mathematical technique called Principal Component Analysis (PCA).

This technique helps scientists make sense of large, complicated datasets by identifying the strongest patterns within them. In this case, the team examined how the brown dwarf's light changed as it rotated, looking for subtle variations that could reveal what was happening in its atmosphere.

Rather than beginning with detailed assumptions about how the atmosphere should behave, the researchers allowed the observations to reveal the most important processes. The method also helped separate genuine atmospheric changes from minor fluctuations and random noise in the measurements.

Webb's exceptionally sensitive instruments made this possible by detecting extremely small changes in the object's brightness as different parts of its atmosphere rotated into view.

Scientists Discover Hidden Order in Chaotic Alien Weather

The results revealed something unexpected. Although SIMP 0136 appears to have a highly complicated atmosphere, nearly all of its observed atmospheric variability could be explained by just two dominant components, consistent with temperature fluctuations and changes in the vertical arrangement of clouds.

Even more remarkably, the researchers identified three recurring weather states that appear and disappear from view as the brown dwarf rotates.

Together, these states create a shifting atmospheric patchwork. Some regions are hotter and have thinner clouds, while others are cooler and contain thicker cloud layers that extend farther vertically through the atmosphere.

Instead of constantly changing in an unpredictable way, these weather patterns appear to follow an underlying structure.

The findings, published in Astronomy & Astrophysics, suggest that the seemingly chaotic atmosphere of SIMP 0136 is actually governed by a remarkably consistent set of processes.

Brown dwarfs occupy an unusual place between planets and stars. SIMP 0136 is larger and hotter than a typical gas giant planet, but it lacks the mass needed to shine like a star. Its atmosphere contains enormous cloud systems that evolve rapidly, making its weather more comparable to an extreme version of Jupiter's than anything experienced on Earth.

These immense weather systems can span planetary scales, continually changing the appearance of the distant world.

"We also discovered that these drivers of the weather patterns on SIMP-0136 persist over time, even as the detailed appearance of the atmosphere evolves over more than a dozen rotations," said first author Merle Schrader, a PhD candidate in Trinity's School of Physics.

"In relative terms, SIMP 0136 is one of the easier brown dwarfs for us to capture high-quality data from. These data have been studied before by established methods, allowing us to compare some of the results from this new technique to what we already know about this object. The technique has also helped us develop a better understanding of what drives the weather on this faraway world and how these weather patterns interact and co-exist, but perhaps even more importantly, it shows how this approach can be further refined and applied to other, less well-known brown dwarfs in different parts of space."

A Remarkable Connection Across 20 Light Years

For Schrader, the research also revealed an extraordinary personal connection to the distant object.

SIMP 0136 is located approximately 20 light years from Earth, meaning its light takes about two decades to reach us. The light captured by Webb in 2023, which provided the observations used in the study, had actually left the brown dwarf during the year Schrader was born.

"Light travels at around 300,000 km/s but, even at that speed, it took two decades to reach us, peering through the JWST lenses," she said.

"When you consider light takes just over a second to reach the Moon after leaving Earth, that gives a sense of how far away SIMP 0136 is, and how incredible astrophysical progress has been. I think it's amazing that we have been able to discern the intimate weather patterns of a distant world and map their interactions from our cozy little corner of the universe, when all we observe directly of these objects is a single pixel spread across the light spectrum."

The achievement highlights how much information astronomers can extract from seemingly tiny amounts of light. Even though SIMP 0136 appears as little more than a single point in telescope observations, changes in its brightness at different wavelengths can reveal detailed information about its atmosphere.

What This Discovery Means for Distant Planets

The new technique could have implications far beyond SIMP 0136.

Brown dwarfs are particularly valuable for investigating the atmospheres of giant exoplanets, which are planets orbiting stars outside our Solar System. By examining these objects, researchers can test how clouds form, how heat moves through an atmosphere, and how atmospheric circulation behaves under extreme conditions.

#1081. The uranium cubes that exposed Nazi Germany’s nuclear failure

During World War II, the United States raced to develop the atomic bomb partly out of fear that Nazi Germany might build one first. But how close did Germany actually come to achieving that goal? A new scientific investigation suggests the Nazi nuclear program was much further from success than its leading physicist claimed.

By examining surviving uranium cubes and previously documented historical evidence, researchers have uncovered major limitations in Germany's final experimental nuclear reactor. Their findings indicate that the project was not simply missing a few materials. The reactor would have required far more uranium and heavy water than Germany had available during the war.

The Manhattan Project, the secret American effort to develop nuclear weapons during World War II, was driven partly by the possibility that Nazi Germany might achieve a similar breakthrough first. Scientists knew that whoever succeeded could gain an unprecedented military advantage.

One possible route to an atomic bomb involved building a nuclear reactor capable of producing plutonium, a radioactive element that can be used in nuclear weapons. Inside a reactor, uranium atoms undergo nuclear fission, a process in which their nuclei split apart and release energy along with additional particles called neutrons. Under the right conditions, those neutrons trigger further fission reactions, creating a sustained chain reaction.

Although Nazi Germany pursued nuclear research through a program known as the Uranprojekt, historians have long debated how close its scientists came to developing a functioning reactor.

Germany's final attempt was an experimental reactor known as the B8 pile, constructed in 1945 under the direction of physicist Werner Heisenberg. The device used cubes of natural uranium suspended in heavy water, a specialized substance that played an essential role in controlling the nuclear reaction.

Why Heavy Water Was Essential to Nazi Germany's Reactor

Heavy water looks much like ordinary water, but its hydrogen atoms contain an additional neutron. This subtle difference makes it particularly useful in certain nuclear reactors.

In a reactor, heavy water serves as a neutron moderator, meaning it slows down the neutrons released during fission. Slower neutrons are more likely to trigger additional fission reactions in uranium, helping sustain the chain reaction needed for a working reactor.

For Germany, however, obtaining enough heavy water was a major obstacle.

During the war, the only European facility capable of supplying the heavy water needed by the German program was the Norsk Hydro plant in Vemork, Norway. Germany gained control of the facility after occupying Norway in April 1940.

Recognizing its importance to Germany's nuclear ambitions, Allied forces targeted the plant through sabotage and bombing operations. A major American bombing raid struck Vemork on November 16, 1943, contributing to the eventual end of heavy water production there.

Scientists Reexamine Hitler's Final Nuclear Reactor

When American forces discovered and dismantled the B8 reactor in 1945, it had never achieved criticality. In nuclear physics, criticality refers to the point at which a chain reaction becomes self-sustaining, with each generation of fission reactions producing enough neutrons to maintain the next.

Reaching this threshold is a fundamental requirement for operating a conventional nuclear reactor. Despite its name, criticality does not mean that a reactor is about to explode. It simply means the nuclear chain reaction can continue without relying on an external neutron source.

Heisenberg, who led the German reactor effort, maintained that the B8 experiment had come close to reaching this milestone. He argued that the device needed only a relatively modest increase in its uranium supply to become operational.

But a new study led by Timothy Koeth and his colleagues challenges that assessment.

The researchers examined two surviving cubes of natural uranium that had been used in the German nuclear project. They also studied historical records, technical reports, correspondence, and other archival documents to reconstruct the reactor's capabilities and determine what would have been required for it to work.

Their findings paint a very different picture of Germany's nuclear progress.

A Massive Uranium and Heavy Water Shortage

According to the researchers' calculations, the B8 reactor would have needed approximately twice as much uranium and more than twice as much heavy water as it actually contained to achieve a sustained nuclear chain reaction.

The required quantities exceeded the materials available to Germany during the war. In particular, the heavy water needed for a functioning reactor was far beyond the amount the German program could obtain from its Norwegian supply.

The researchers also examined whether German scientists could have avoided the heavy water shortage by using graphite, a form of carbon that can also slow neutrons in nuclear reactors.

This approach had worked for American scientists, who achieved the world's first controlled, sustained nuclear chain reaction using a graphite-moderated reactor in 1942.

However, the graphite available in German-controlled territory contained impurities that made it unsuitable for the same purpose. In particular, traces of boron could absorb neutrons that would otherwise help sustain the reaction, preventing the reactor from reaching criticality.

That meant Germany could not simply substitute graphite for heavy water to overcome its supply problems.

Why Nazi Germany's Nuclear Program Was Doomed

The findings suggest that Heisenberg significantly underestimated how much additional material his reactor would have required.

Rather than standing on the verge of a functioning nuclear reactor, the German scientists faced shortages they could not overcome with the resources available to them.

The research also provides a clearer explanation for why Germany's nuclear program failed to make the transition from experimental reactor designs to a working reactor capable of producing material for an atomic bomb.

Based on the designs and materials available during World War II, the researchers conclude that Nazi Germany lacked the resources necessary to make either its heavy water reactor or a graphite-based alternative operational.

More than eight decades after the war, the surviving uranium cubes have helped resolve a longstanding historical question, revealing just how far Nazi Germany remained from achieving a critical step toward building an atomic bomb.

Although Bloomer's original Crawzilla did not claim the top spot, it still earned a place among the largest specimens identified.

"Mine was not the biggest, but it did make the top 20, so I'm super proud of it," Bloomer said. "And it was also the only burrowing crayfish to make the list, so I'll take the win."

Scientists Identify the Largest Known North American Crayfish

The biggest specimen belonged to a Tennessee bottlebrush crayfish preserved in the collection at West Liberty University in West Virginia. It measured an impressive 86.6 millimeters, or approximately 3.4 inches, excluding its tail, claws, and antennae.

The record-holder was not a recent catch. It had been collected in 2011 by Dr. Zac Loughman and students from West Liberty University, making the discovery possible through the examination of existing museum specimens.

"I knew we had some massive crayfish in the West Liberty University Astacology Collection, but it was a surprise to learn we had the two largest specimens known from North America! Our record-holder, a Tennessee bottlebrush crayfish collected by Dr. Zac Loughman and West Liberty students back in 2011, shows just how amazing and diverse crayfish can be," said Zackary Graham, an assistant professor of biology at West Liberty University. "Its carapace alone is about the length of a credit card, and with the tail and claws, it stretches nearly the width of a sheet of notebook paper. Both of these giants are now proudly displayed in my office, and I show them to just about everyone who walks in."

Other institutions also contributed impressive specimens. The Illinois Natural History Survey, which belongs to the Prairie Research Institute at the University of Illinois, accounted for six of the 20 largest crayfish. The Smithsonian Institution's National Museum of Natural History contributed another four.

Why Can't North American Crayfish Grow Even Bigger?

Beyond settling the competition, the researchers uncovered a more intriguing scientific question. Their measurements provided some of the first estimates of the upper size limits for North American crayfish, and the results suggested that these animals may face a surprisingly consistent growth ceiling.

Despite differences among species and environments, the largest individuals tended to reach roughly 80 millimeters in the body measurement used by the researchers. That raised an obvious question: Why do North American crayfish appear to stop growing at around this size when some species elsewhere in the world, including the Tasmanian giant freshwater crayfish, can become dramatically larger?

"They all seemed to max out around that 80-millimeter mark, but we weren't sure why, especially given that there are absolutely massive crayfish in other parts of the world," Bloomer said. "We started looking at things like latitude, oxygen availability, and habitat type as possible correlates to explain why certain species and individuals were larger than others, but we weren't seeing any patterns."

The team examined whether geographic location, oxygen levels, or the type of environment where a crayfish lives could explain why some grow larger than others. However, none of these factors showed a clear relationship with maximum body size.

That unexpected result pointed the researchers toward a different possibility. Rather than being primarily controlled by environmental conditions, crayfish growth may be constrained by the animals' own biology.

One potential explanation involves their exoskeletons, the hard outer coverings that protect their bodies. Differences in shell structure or muscle anatomy might impose physical limits on how large North American crayfish can become.

For now, that remains a hypothesis. Further research will be needed to determine whether these biological features actually restrict their growth.

"This was a case where we saw something unusual in the field and it sparked a future research direction," Bloomer said. "We think that it could really be worthwhile to look at the physiology of crayfish to see if something there is influencing their body size."

Giant Crayfish Reveal How Much Scientists Still Don't Know

Crayfish are familiar inhabitants of streams, rivers, and other freshwater environments. Many people encounter them as children, making them among the most recognizable aquatic animals in North America.

Yet even with their widespread presence, scientists are still discovering basic facts about their biology, including how large they can grow and what determines their maximum size.

"Anyone that goes to a stream as a kid knows what crayfish are -- they're almost a universal onboarding for people to care about aquatic life. I still go out with people who are in their 50s or 60s, who recall catching crawfish in the creek when they were seven," Bloomer said. "It's just amazing how much we still have to learn about them."

Bloomer also emphasized that the investigation would not have been possible without museum collections. By comparing specimens preserved over many years, researchers were able to answer a fundamental question that would have been difficult to investigate through fieldwork alone.

What began with one unusually large crayfish and a disagreement between colleagues has now opened a new line of scientific inquiry into the biological factors that shape the size of these familiar freshwater creatures.

At the very start of the Frankfurt fair, the German Publishers and Booksellers Association issued an appeal to policymakers in Berlin and Brussels, calling for stricter regulation of artificial intelligence, which they said is more urgent than ever. More than 140 publishers have signed it.

But for now, it remains just an appeal. And the question of what authors themselves should be permitted to do with AI will undoubtedly continue to fuel debates in the future.

#1065. Brain cells meant to protect us may be fueling cognitive decline

Brain cells that normally help protect the brain may play an unexpected role in cognitive decline as we grow older. Scientists have discovered that cells responsible for maintaining the protective coating around nerve fibers can become dysfunctional with age, potentially contributing to the gradual loss of memory and thinking abilities.

The research, published in Nature Medicine, challenges the long-held belief that these cells are always beneficial. By examining human brain tissue and conducting experiments in mice, researchers found evidence that problems with these normally supportive cells can damage nerve fibers and interfere with cognitive function.

The discovery also points to a possible new approach for protecting the aging brain. Drugs that act on the biological pathway involved are already being used to treat other neurological conditions, raising hopes that existing treatments could eventually be adapted to combat age-related cognitive decline.

Scientists Discover Changes in the Aging Brain

The study was led by researchers from the University of Edinburgh and the UK Dementia Research Institute, who investigated how changes in the brain relate to declining cognitive abilities later in life.

Their research focused on the Lothian Birth Cohort 1936, a group of people whose cognitive abilities have been tracked from childhood into old age. This unusually extensive record allowed scientists to investigate how thinking and memory changed over decades.

Between ages 70 and 82, participants underwent assessments measuring memory, the speed at which they processed information, and their ability to understand spatial relationships.

Of the 1,091 people enrolled in the cohort, 866 completed additional cognitive testing after age 70. Nearly everyone experienced at least some decline, but the rate varied considerably. These differences gave researchers an opportunity to investigate what might distinguish brains experiencing faster deterioration from those aging more gradually.

When scientists examined brain tissue collected after death from a subset of participants, they discovered a striking pattern. Individuals who had experienced greater cognitive decline tended to have fewer large nerve fibers, along with an abnormal buildup of unhealthy myelin.

Myelin is the protective coating that surrounds nerve fibers, helping electrical signals travel efficiently through the brain. Although this insulation is essential for normal brain function, the findings suggest that excessive or abnormal myelin may become part of the problem.

The changes were especially pronounced around the large nerve fibers that remained. Importantly, these abnormalities were associated with how quickly cognitive abilities declined, rather than how well someone performed on cognitive tests at a particular age.

A Protective Brain Cell May Have a Hidden Weakness

To understand what might be causing these changes, the researchers turned their attention to oligodendrocytes, specialized brain cells responsible for producing and maintaining myelin.

Oligodendrocytes have traditionally been regarded as beneficial because they help keep nerve fibers insulated and functioning properly. However, the new findings suggest that these cells can become dysfunctional during aging and may contribute to the very problems they normally help prevent.

The researchers discovered that people with more severe cognitive decline had lower levels of a protein called NRF2 inside their oligodendrocytes.

NRF2 plays an important protective role in cells by regulating hundreds of genes involved in defending against damage and maintaining normal cellular function. When its activity decreases, cells may become less capable of protecting themselves and performing their usual jobs.

To investigate whether this protein was directly involved in the brain changes, the team conducted experiments in mice.

When researchers specifically reduced NRF2 in oligodendrocytes, the animals developed excessive myelin and lost large nerve fibers, closely resembling the abnormalities observed in human brain tissue. The mice also showed reduced improvements in cognitive performance as they aged.

These experiments provided evidence that declining NRF2 activity can directly disrupt oligodendrocyte function, potentially triggering changes in myelin and nerve fibers that contribute to cognitive decline.

Existing Drugs Could Offer a New Treatment Strategy

The findings may have important implications for developing treatments that help preserve cognitive abilities in older adults.

One particularly promising aspect is that the NRF2 pathway is already a target of medications used for other conditions, including a drug prescribed to treat multiple sclerosis (MS).

Previous studies have found that increasing NRF2 activity can improve cognitive function in people with MS. That raises the possibility that medications acting on the same pathway might eventually be repurposed to address oligodendrocyte dysfunction and age-related cognitive decline.

Although further research is needed to determine whether such treatments would benefit older adults experiencing cognitive decline, the discovery identifies a potentially valuable therapeutic target.

Veronique Miron, MRC Senior Non-Clinical Fellow and UK Dementia Research Institute Group Leader at the University of Edinburgh and St. Michael's Hospital, part of Unity Health Toronto, said:

"As the prevalence of cognitive decline is rising with an aging population and no current treatments exist, we are excited about this work as it points to a potential strategy for new therapeutic strategies to preserve cognitive ability in aging."

Beyond its potential medical applications, the research changes how scientists understand the role of oligodendrocytes in the aging brain.

Rather than simply providing support for healthy nerve function throughout life, these cells appear capable of becoming dysfunctional and contributing to cognitive problems.

Georgina Craig, the study's first author and a Postdoctoral Fellow at St Michael's Hospital in Toronto and the UK Dementia Research Institute, said:

"This study has fundamentally shifted how we think about these brain cells in aging. We have always considered oligodendrocytes as purely beneficial, yet here we surprisingly find that they can become dysfunctional and contribute to cognitive impairment in aging."

The findings suggest that maintaining healthy oligodendrocyte function could become an important strategy for protecting memory and thinking abilities as people grow older.

Published in Nature Medicine, the study was funded by the UKRI Medical Research Council (MRC) and the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR). The research also involved scientists from the University of Edinburgh's Lothian Birth Cohort studies and Northwestern University.

#1056. Operations Plan Germany: How the Bundeswehr trains for NATO collective defense

How prepared is Germany for national defense? The answer lies in the Operations Plan Germany. Military exercises such as "Red Storm Charlie" are designed to put these preparations to the test in realistic scenarios.

In a residential district of Hamburg at midnight, the streets are quiet and deserted. Suddenly, the silence is shattered: Several helicopters circle the night sky, and flashing blue lights illuminate the darkness. A long convoy of vehicles moves through the streets. Soldiers stand atop armored Bundeswehr vehicles, their machine guns at the ready. For local residents, it's an unusual, perhaps even unsettling sight in an otherwise peaceful port city.

"Red Storm Charlie" is the name of the exercise that brought parts of Hamburg, northern Germany's largest city, to a standstill for three days. The Bundeswehr, police and civilian organizations were jointly testing how well-prepared they are to respond in a crisis.

The scenario centers on a deteriorating security situation on NATO's eastern flank. NATO troops, represented in the exercise by Bundeswehr soldiers, arrive at the port of Hamburg, Germany's largest seaport, before moving eastwards through the Hanseatic city.

Acts of sabotage and demonstrations, also included in the scenario, hamper their advance. With around 1,700 participants, including 500 soldiers, the exercise held in late September was the largest of its kind to date.

"Red Storm Charlie" forms part of a classified strategy known as the "Operationsplan Deutschland" (Operations Plan Germany). Through this document, the Bundeswehr is preparing for the possibility of a Russian attack on NATO territory. The Operations Plan Germany is an integral element of NATO's broader defense planning and is closely aligned with the Alliance's overall defense framework.

In the event of war or a major crisis, not only the city of Hamburg but Germany as a whole would assume a pivotal role as a logistics hub for hundreds of thousands of NATO soldiers, weapons and pieces of heavy equipment.

"This is a highly detailed and strictly classified plan that specifies exactly where NATO allies would arrive in Germany, including which ports they would use and which routes would be required for their transit across the country," security expert Aylin Matle of the German Council on Foreign Relations told DW.

The Baltic states are widely regarded as the region where NATO considers a Russian attack most likely. In such a scenario, the alliance would concentrate large troop contingents from its 32 member states there. To reach the region, soldiers from southern and western Europe would need to pass through Germany.

"In concrete terms, this means that around 800,000 soldiers and 200,000 vehicles would have to move through our country within six months. Those are the calculations," said Marcel Bohnert, a Bundeswehr officer who contributed to the first version of the Operations Plan.

Speaking with DW in a personal capacity rather than as a representative of the Bundeswehr, Bohnert explained: "We must ensure that soldiers can use our roads and ports safely. That is why we take care of all their needs: We arrange accommodation, provide food, ensure their security and guide them through our transport network."

The plan also covers transport in the opposite direction, from east to west, including the evacuation of wounded soldiers and civilians fleeing the conflict. Its development was commissioned by Bundeswehr Inspector General Carsten Breuer in the wake of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

As Germany's highest-ranking military officer, Breuer is far from alone in his concern that Russia could target additional countries. Germany is already confronting a wide range of Russian hostile activities, from cyberattacks to acts of sabotage directed against the Bundeswehr.

A new dimension of the threat emerged with the attempted attack at Leipzig/Halle Airport in early August. An explosive-laden drone was deployed there and failed to detonate only through luck. The German government holds Russia responsible.

"The threat is becoming more tangible. It is serious, acute, and increasingly visible. This is exactly what the Russian president wants. Moscow is testing our response," Defense Minister Boris Pistorius of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) told the Bundestag on September 23.

The Bundeswehr is also addressing this threat through the Operations Plan Germany, which is continuously being refined and updated. Lessons learned from exercises such as "Red Storm Charlie" are incorporated into the plan. According to Bohnert, the plan also serves as a deterrent.

"We hope to discourage Russia from further aggression by demonstrating our strength through the rapid buildup of military capabilities and the highly organized, swift deployment of forces to the borders," he said.

How well-prepared is Germany for its role as a military logistics hub? Matle identifies one major vulnerability in the country's ageing and, in some cases, deteriorating infrastructure. Germany, she argued, must address the maintenance and repair of the bridges, roads and transport routes required for troop movements "far more quickly and comprehensively."

"Some of this infrastructure dates back to the Cold War and was not designed to accommodate heavy loads or military equipment," she pointed out.

A further challenge lies in striking the right balance between confidentiality and public involvement. To guard against acts of sabotage, sensitive details such as the precise transit routes for NATO troops must not fall into Russian hands. At the same time, the plan can only succeed through close cooperation between the Bundeswehr and civilian society, including local and regional authorities, the police, and relief organizations.

"Parts of the plan must be publicly accessible because we need support from society, as well as from ministries and governments, to make it work," said Bohnert.

The debate over the confidentiality of the more than 1,000-page document recently resurfaced after the far-right Alternative for Germany(AfD) emerged as the strongest party in recent elections in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt.

Security experts expressed concern that the party, which is classified in part as right-wing extremist and is regarded as Russia-friendly, could pass information from the plan to Moscow if it were to assume the office of state premier.

The Defense Ministry has rejected these concerns, noting that no federal state is given access to the complete document. Instead, the sensitive sections of the Operations Plan are shared only in excerpts and strictly on a need-to-know basis.

According to observers, however, the growing number of hybrid threats is underscoring the need for secrecy. During the "Red Storm Charlie" exercise in Hamburg, accredited journalists were not informed of routes or locations until the very last moment because of concerns over potential disruptions and acts of sabotage.

#1055. This bird flies thousands of miles, but its wings have a catch

A bird that migrates thousands of miles has revealed a surprising secret about how wings work. Scientists have discovered that European nightjars sacrifice flight efficiency for agility and use an unexpected wingbeat technique previously associated mainly with bats and insects.

The European nightjar (Caprimulgus europaeus) is a nighttime hunter that makes an extraordinary annual migration between Europe and southern Africa. Yet the wings that carry it across vast distances must also perform a very different task: helping the bird catch insects while flying slowly and making sharp turns.

Scientists at Lund University have discovered that these competing demands come with a surprising trade-off. Although the nightjar is built to handle long migrations, its wings are not perfectly optimized for efficient flight at higher speeds.

Why Nightjar Wings Sacrifice Efficiency for Agility

To investigate how nightjars fly, researchers studied the birds in a wind tunnel, recording their movements at different speeds and measuring the patterns of airflow left behind their wings.

The experiments revealed that the nightjar's broad wing tips, which likely help it maneuver during slow flight, become a disadvantage as the bird flies faster. At higher speeds, its wings generate lift less efficiently. Lift is the aerodynamic force that helps keep a bird airborne.

The findings challenge the assumption that birds traveling enormous distances must have wings optimized primarily for efficient long-distance flight.

"This runs counter to the intuitive image of a long-distance migrant as a perfectly optimized flying machine. Instead, the nightjar's wings appear to be a compromise between several different demands. The bird can fly slowly and maneuver while hunting, but the trade-off is reduced efficiency in lift generation at higher speeds," says Christoffer Johansson, associate professor at Lund University.

Scientists Discover an Unexpected Wingbeat Trick

The researchers also uncovered another surprising feature of the nightjar's flight. Even as the bird raises its wings during the upward portion of each wingbeat, it continues to generate thrust, the force that helps propel it forward.

This is unusual because such an active upward wing movement, known as an upstroke, has previously been linked mainly to the flight mechanics of bats and insects rather than birds.

The discovery suggests that animals with very different evolutionary histories may have developed similar aerodynamic solutions to the challenges of flying.

"What's interesting is that the same type of aerodynamic solution seems to appear in quite different animals. This suggests that the upstroke does not have to be merely a passive or lift generating part of the wingbeat. It can be actively used to generate thrust when the wings cannot be folded very much," says Christoffer Johansson.

What Bird Flight Reveals About Evolution and Flying Robots

The results offer a broader lesson about how evolution shapes animals that depend on the same body structures for different activities.

Rather than producing wings perfectly suited to one purpose, evolution can favor designs that balance several competing needs. For the nightjar, that means combining the ability to migrate across continents with the agility required to hunt insects at low speeds.

Understanding these compromises could also lead to improvements in aviation technology, particularly drones and other robotic aircraft that use flapping wings.

Like birds, these machines may face mechanical restrictions on how much their wings can fold during each wingbeat. Learning how nightjars generate thrust during the upstroke could help engineers develop more effective ways to move through the air.

Researchers now hope to determine whether this unusual flight mechanism is widespread among other birds and flying animals or whether it represents a more specialized adaptation.

"We now want to investigate how common this way of generating force is among other birds and flying animals. It could help us understand which aerodynamic principles are specific to the nightjar and which are more general. This knowledge may also inspire the development of new types of flapping aircraft," Christoffer Johansson concludes.
