#799. Physicists create a bizarre new quantum state called a fractional fermi sea

Physicists have uncovered a strange new form of quantum behavior in which particles organize themselves in ways that challenge established theories. Even more surprising, the particles maintain a remarkable hidden order despite being driven into an extremely energetic state.

In a study published in Physical Review Letters, researchers from the group led by Hanns-Christoph Nägerl, working with theoretical physicist Alvise Bastianello of the CNRS and Université Paris-Dauphine, describe how to engineer an exotic quantum state known as a "fractional Fermi sea."

The breakthrough involves pushing ultracold cesium atoms far beyond their normal conditions by repeatedly changing how strongly they attract or repel one another. Rather than simply becoming disordered, the particles rearrange themselves into an unusual configuration that behaves differently from the quantum systems scientists have traditionally studied.

The theoretical findings provide the foundation for recent experimental research conducted by Nägerl's team at the Department of Experimental Physics.

Pushing Quantum Particles Beyond Their Normal Limits

Under ordinary conditions, quantum particles follow well-established rules that determine how they arrange themselves, particularly at extremely low temperatures.

One important example involves fermions, a category of particles that includes electrons. These particles cannot all occupy the same quantum state, so they fill available energy levels according to specific restrictions. At very low temperatures, this arrangement produces what physicists call a Fermi sea.

But what happens when researchers deliberately disrupt the conditions that normally govern how particles behave?

As Alvise Bastianello explains: "Fermions, for instance, stack neatly into the available energy states to form the so-called 'Fermi sea'. But what happens if one forces interacting atoms to continuously cycle through extreme conditions, smoothly shifting them from strongly repelling each other to strongly attracting each other?"

To investigate this possibility, the researchers studied ultracold cesium atoms confined to a one-dimensional system. This means the atoms are restricted to moving along a single direction, creating an environment in which their quantum interactions can be studied with exceptional control.

The team examined what happens when the interactions between these atoms are repeatedly adjusted, cycling between powerful repulsion and attraction.

Their calculations revealed something unexpected. Starting from the ground state, the lowest energy configuration available to the system, the atoms could be driven into a highly excited state without losing their underlying organization.

The researchers call this unusual arrangement a fractional Fermi sea because its particles appear to obey a modified rule governing how quantum states are occupied.

"Instead of simply heating the system, the interaction cycle reorganizes the atoms into a new many-body state," says Yi Zeng, the leading author of this study. "This gives us a controlled way to explore quantum matter beyond the usual equilibrium paradigms."

A Strange New Quantum State With Hidden Order

One of the most intriguing features of the fractional Fermi sea is that its particles remain organized even though the system has been pushed far from equilibrium.

In physics, equilibrium describes a condition in which a system's overall properties have settled into a stable balance. Systems driven far from equilibrium can behave in complicated and unexpected ways, making them particularly challenging to understand.

Yet the fractional Fermi sea is not simply a collection of excited particles behaving randomly. Instead, it contains distinctive mathematical patterns that reveal a deeper structure.

Among these patterns are pronounced ripples called Friedel oscillations. These are wave-like variations associated with the way quantum particles influence one another. The researchers also identified unusual patterns in how correlations between particles weaken with increasing distance, even when the repulsive interactions are relatively weak.

These signatures are important because they differ from predictions associated with Tomonaga-Luttinger liquid theory, a long-established framework for describing how interacting quantum particles behave in one-dimensional systems.

The findings therefore point toward a previously unexplored type of quantum behavior that cannot be fully described using this familiar theoretical picture.

"This state is highly excited, but it is not random," says Hanns-Christoph Nägerl, the group leader. "It has a hidden order that becomes visible in its correlations."

The strange behavior also raises questions about the effective particles that emerge within the system. Physicists call these collective excitations quasiparticles because they can behave like individual particles even though they arise from the coordinated motion of many interacting particles.

Nägerl suggests that the unusual excitations might eventually deserve a name of their own.

"We are not yet sure how we should name these new quasiparticles. Perhaps 'super-Fermions'?"

Opening a New Frontier in Quantum Physics

The discovery suggests that fractional Fermi seas represent an exotic critical phase of quantum matter, with characteristic patterns of particle correlations that extend beyond existing descriptions of one-dimensional quantum systems.

It also demonstrates the growing power of quantum simulation, a technique that allows scientists to use carefully controlled quantum systems to investigate physical behavior that would otherwise be difficult to reproduce or study.

Ultracold atoms are particularly useful for this purpose because researchers can precisely adjust their interactions and observe how the resulting quantum states change.

Rather than limiting these experiments to recreating phenomena already understood through established theories, scientists can now explore deliberately engineered states that reveal entirely new possibilities.

As Hanns-Christoph Nägerl says: "The discovery of fractional Fermi seas shows how far we can push quantum simulation: not only reproducing known models, but creating and probing states that go beyond established paradigms."

The current publication establishes the theoretical framework for fractional Fermi seas. A companion study describing their experimental realization through quantum simulation is undergoing peer review.

Together, the research points toward new ways of investigating the fundamental behavior of quantum matter, including unusual collective excitations and hidden forms of order that emerge only when particles are pushed far outside their normal conditions.

#790. Next Generation 2021: why slow and steady sometimes wins the race

Our NextGen picks from five years ago show that breaking through early does not guarantee longevity or future success

In 2021, as every year, we picked 80 players from around the world who had a chance of making it in world football – 20 from the Premier League and 60 from the rest of the world – as part of our Next Generation series.

We then follow these talented boys on their journeys for five years. Often it is almost impossible to predict how they will get on (OK, when Sid Lowe sent over Lamine Yamal in 2024 we had an inkling he might do well).

Five years ago we had an exciting array of young players on the list, most of them 17 years old but some 16, with one youngster from Brighton being profiled like this:

double quotation markThe ball-playing central midfielder comes from a footballing family and academy staff believe his tactical awareness is born from growing up around the game. His father, Adam, played for Brighton and is now manager along the coast at Worthing FC, while Martin (his great uncle), Paul (his grandad), and Wally (his great grandad), all enjoyed careers as players.

It was quite an understated entry, especially compared with this one in the world section:

double quotation markThe youngest to play in the Bundesliga at 16 years and one day, youngest player to appear in the Champions League 17 days later, youngest goal scorer in Bundesliga 11 days after that and youngest player and youngest goal scorer for the Germany Under-21 team before he has turned 17.

Borussia Dortmund’s Youssoufa Moukoko went on to play for Germany at the World Cup the following year, at the same time as Jack Hinshelwood was promoted to the Brighton Under-21s. Different trajectories, different pressures. Moukoko was already known around the world and tipped for a long career at the top. There was something completely carefree about his style, young Michael Owen-esque. Take a look at his early (and subsequent) goals in the Bundesliga here.

Then life interfered. Moukoko sustained some injuries and struggled to make the Dortmund starting XI. To be fair, he also had some decent competition up front as Erling Haaland had arrived in 2020. Moukoko had a really good spell at the start of the 2022-23 season, after Haaland had joined Manchester City, but sustained an ankle ligament tear and never quite got his starting place back.

These days Moukoko is playing in the Turkish top flight with Corum, on loan from Copenhagen, where he moved in 2025. His switch to the newly promoted Turkish top-flight side was low key with the club welcoming Moukoko to the “family” with a short statement. He is yet to start a game but his cameos off the bench have offered flashes of his quality – most notably marking his home debut with a sharp assist in a 3–0 win over Eyüpspor. His market value is €5m (£4m) reckons transfermarkt.com.

Meanwhile, Hinshelwood has made 67 Premier League appearances and was included on Thomas Tuchel’s 55-man longlist for the 2026 World Cup. A frustrating ankle injury has hampered him at the start of this campaign but his star is rising and he has a market value of €28m (£24m). Not that Brighton are about to sell him.

Neither player chose their path and whether they broke through early. They simply developed at a different pace. Hinshelwood was not ready to play top-flight football at 17; Moukoko was. Did the early exposure have a negative impact on Moukoko? It is impossible to say.

Not that it has been straightforward for Hinshelwood either. It never is. His father, Adam, told Sky this year that “it is definitely not just a straight line”, saying: “There were lots of bumps and ups and downs along the way.” There was one moment when Hinshelwood, then with the under-15s, was set a challenge to play with an older age-group, and that arguably made him as a footballer. “It was a big catalyst for Jack,” Adam said. “He rolled up his sleeves and became a proper footballer.”

Out of our 60 world talents, 24 have gone on to represent their countries. Moukoko stands on two caps, some distance behind players such as Julio Enciso and Gavi. Some have had to swap allegiances to play senior international football, such as Livano Comenencia, who started out with the Netherlands age-group teams but has more than 20 caps for Curaçao and played at this summer’s World Cup.

From our Premier League picks in 2021 astonishingly few players are with the same club. Hinshelwood is one, Liverpool’s Kaide Gordon another, but that is it. The other 18 are playing around the globe, including in the US, Qatar and Hong Kong.

The 20 players have played a total of 145 top-flight games, not a bad tally, but Hinshelwood accounts for almost half of those. Adrian Blake, who was with Watford in 2021, has carved out a very successful career in the Netherlands, having joined Utrecht in 2023. But he too, struggled in the beginning. “It hasn’t been easy and a lots of ups and downs,” he told Football-oranje.com in 2025. “But now I am getting my rewards and showing why I made the right decision by moving here.”

Blake moved to the Netherlands when he was 17. It was a huge gamble for someone so young but he says he chose the Eredivisie “because it is a great league for young players”, offering opportunities that often do not exist in England. “For me it was a no-brainer,” he said.

The 21-year-old has almost his whole career in front of him, as do Moukoko and Hinshelwood – and yet they have experienced so much already. What will the next five years bring?

#788. Why Brighton are scoring SO many goals

Published: 17:00 BST, 19 October 2026 | Updated: 18:39 BST, 19 October 2026

On par with Real Madrid and behind only Barcelona. Brighton, at last, have found their rightful place in the world.

Fabian Hurzeler’s side, after yet another rout against Crystal Palace, have now racked up more goals across Europe’s top five leagues than anyone bar the Spanish giants. The Seagulls have more than Bayern Munich, too. Who needs Harry Kane when you have Charalampos Kostoulas?

In fact, only three Premier League sides have ever scored more in their opening seven games of a campaign than Brighton’s 23 (27 by Pep Guardiola’s Man City in 2019-20, 24 by Manchester United in 2011-12 and 24 by Arsenal in 2009-10). None of these teams, however, went on to win the title. Can Brighton buck the trend?

It helps, of course, when you get to play sides as incapable at defending as Crystal Palace, who have already conceded 17 goals from their opening seven games with Pierre Sage clinging to his job at Selhurst Park.

When you stick three past Arsenal, three past Chelsea and four past Aston Villa, though, it suggests you’re doing something right.

It also helps when nearly everything you touch turns to goals. For those who bow to the emotionless number crunching of expected goals (xG), Brighton have already scored eight more goals than they should have done based on the quality of chances they have created. In just seven games, that’s more than any side over-performed across the entirety of last season.

Only three Premier League sides have ever scored more in their opening seven games of a campaign than Brighton’s 23

At some point, you imagine, that will prove unsustainable. At some point, Pascal Gross, Maxim De Cuyper, Chema Andres, Jack Hinshelwood and Jaouen Hadjam will stop scoring with every shot on target they muster. Lewis Dunk won't always score from 30 yards, nor Diego Gomez from 50.

And yet none of that should take away from the fact that, despite overperforming it, Brighton are still behind only Manchester City for the xG they have created. Only Manchester United have taken more shots.

No team has created as many ‘big chances’ – defined by data giants Opta as one a player should be reasonably expected to score. They are still one of the most attacking, creative and potent teams in the Premier League.

So, how has Hurzeler managed it? This is a coach, after all, who was under pressure from a section of Brighton's fanbase at the start of the year.

Firstly, as we saw in their dismantling of Arsenal before the international break, their press is relentless. It’s been getting better and better each season under Hurzeler but now it’s in a league of its own. The Seagulls have won possession in the final third 51 times, 14 more than any other side and more than twice as many as 12 Premier League clubs including Arsenal, Liverpool and Chelsea.

They allow opponents the fewest passes before attempting to win it back, a stat known as PPDA (passes per defensive action) which is often used as a barometer for an intense press.

That sees Brighton force opponents into dangerous mistakes. No team has cajoled the opposition into more errors leading to a shot than the 12 by the Seagulls. Their attacks start closer to goal than any other team too, so when they do win it back they are likely to punish you.

And yet for a team that is so deadly out of possession, they are not – like so many modern sides in the anti-Guardiola era – looking to have less of the ball. Brighton average 60 per cent possession, behind only Arsenal and City, and are content to build up slowly from the back, baiting opposition presses, and then playing quickly through the lines at speed.

Centre back Lewis Dunk thumped in a 30-yard strike in the 5-0 win over Coventry last month

Brighton are relentless in their pressing and then immediately turn possession into a dangerous attack

That is why, despite not being a traditional counter-attacking team – one that soaks up pressure and spring the attack – they still rank in the top three so far for fast breaks, shots from fast breaks and goals from fast breaks this term.

Brighton have such great variety in their play. Only City and Liverpool have scored more goals coming at the end of sequences of 10 or more passes, while the Seagulls also have the most goals from both outside the box and from corners. They kill you on the counter, with passing moves and from set-pieces. And anyone can get you. Brighton’s 11 different goalscorers is the highest tally in the division.

They swarm you without the ball but then, importantly, with it. As they break forward they commit so many runners from deep to overload the box, all rotating their positions to make them even harder to track. Take their third goal against Palace.

Kostoulas breaks into the area but blue shirts flood forward behind him from midfield in the form of Gomez, Yasin Ayari, De Cuyper and Gross (image below). Ayari, the No10, is the furthest back. The ball is squared, Gross dummies it and Ayari sweeps in to slot the ball home. After just 10 minutes, it's 3-0 and the contest is over.

Even if Kostoulas’s pass had been intercepted, Brighton have players in prime position to pounce to win it back as usual.

And Brighton have Gross, still pulling the strings at the age of 35, showing he remains one of the most influential and creative players in the Premier League. He has the freedom to roam from central midfield, taking up unorthodox positions, with the pinpoint passing accuracy to open up defences. Only two other players in the league have run more miles than the German.

Only Florian Wirtz has created more chances than Gross this season, though not one of Wirtz’s was classified as a ‘big chance’. Six of Gross’s 19 were. He is making things happen, from both open play and set-pieces.

Charalampos Kostoulas (in possession, right-hand side) breaks into the area against Crystal Palace before squaring the ball to Yasin Ayari, who makes it 3-0 inside 10 minutes

Fabian Hurzeler (left) embraces Pascal Gross, who at 35 is still one of the most influential and creative players in the Premier League

None of the players who have created more open-play chances than Gross get anywhere near him from set-pieces, and none of those with more from set-pieces come close to him in open play.

So while Brighton might be riding the wave when it comes to scoring quite this many goals, they are doing everything right to give them the chance to score them.

Opta’s ‘expected points’ table also has them third behind Man City and Arsenal. They are, indeed, right where they belong.

Alcohol bans came into force at all German train stations when the clock struck midnight and Thursday began.

All the roughly 5,700 stations across Germany, including the train platforms themselves, are now booze-free zones.

Pubs, restaurants and other such hospitality businesses at train stations are exceptions to the rule, though, and can still serve alcohol. Consumption of alcohol will also remain legal on many trains, with long distance ones still serving beers and other tipples at their on-board bistros.

Rail operator Deutsche Bahn says the change aims to make train stations safer and cleaner, and more pleasant places to spend time waiting for a (who knows, perhaps delayed) train.

Various misdemeanors and incidents had prompted discussions on alcohol at stations over the years, but two this year in particular brought the issues into sharp focus.

A 26-year-old security staff member for Deutsche Bahn was beaten and kicked by a drunken passenger when trying to check their ticket. During the struggle the train door buckled and opened, and the Bahn employee fell from the train, suffering severe injuries. Earlier in the year, a train conductor was killed when attacked by a man without a valid ticket.

The new rules are part of DB's house rules, rather than being criminal law — meaning that at least in the first instance it is a matter for Bahn security staff to enforce rather than police.

"The alcohol consumption ban applies at stations every day, also on days with major events," Bahn spokeswoman Anja Bröder said when asked about often beer-fueled train usage days, like when football fans flock to Bundesliga games.

Around 40 stations piloted the project for some time prior to its implementation, including some of the largest like Cologne, Munich, Hamburg and Berlin. DB says it has had positive experiences with the early experiments, reporting more peaceful stations with less litter and travelers and employees saying they preferred the atmosphere.

A federal group dedicated to helping the homeless has criticized the measure, however, saying that for homeless people with addiction illnesses, train stations often served as "important, weatherproof and comparatively safe places" to shelter. A spokeswoman also told the Rheinische Post newspaper that if they were driven from sites like train stations, it would be harder for social workers to locate them.

#786. Scientists win 2026 Nobel Prize for controlling brain cells with light

Scientists who worked out how to control brain cells with light have won this year’s Nobel prize in physiology or medicine. Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel were honored for their research into “light-gated ion channels and optogenetics.”

Peter Hegemann, at Humboldt University of Berlin, and Georg Nagel, at the University of Würzburg, wanted to understand how a tiny, single-celled alga called Chlamydomonas senses light. Light-sensing is not unique to animals with eyes: organisms throughout the living world use light to detect their surroundings and respond to changes in their environment. Even single-cell organisms have proteins that respond to light. These proteins are used to move towards or away from light, to generate energy and to respond to the various demands of a 24-hour day.

Hegemann and Nagel discovered that Chlamydomonas has a beautifully ingenious mechanism of light detection that relies on a single protein called channelrhodopsin.

Channelrhodopsin is built into the outer membrane of the algal cell. Part of the protein faces the water outside the cell and part faces the inside. The protein also contains a small molecule called retinaldehyde, which is able to absorb light.

When light hits the retinaldehyde inside channelrhodopsin, it changes shape. This makes the channelrhodopsin protein change shape too. That change opens a tiny passage through the cell membrane. Positively charged particles, including sodium, can then flow from the water outside the alga into the cell. This movement of charged particles sends a signal inside the alga that helps it swim.

How fantastic to have solved such an interesting biological question, but why the Nobel prize in physiology or medicine? The answer lies in the way nerve cells work. Nerve cells use the movement of charged particles (called “ions”) across their membranes to control their activity. When positively charged particles flow into a nerve cell, they can make it more active.

The researchers realized that if they could put channelrhodopsin into nerve cells, they might be able to switch those cells on simply by shining a light on them. That is exactly what Hegemann, Nagel and the third Nobel-prize winner, Karl Deisseroth, a psychiatrist and bioengineer at Stanford University in California, went on to do.

The ability to control nerve cells with light is called optogenetics. It has become a powerful research tool because scientists can use it to switch specific groups of nerve cells on and off and see what happens.

First, scientists deliver the gene for channelrhodopsin to the nerve cells they want to study. The gene is usually carried into the cells by a virus that has been altered so that it cannot cause disease. The cells then make the channelrhodopsin protein, which makes them respond to light.

In animals such as mice, scientists can shine light into the brain through a thin optical fibre. When the light reaches the cells, channelrhodopsin opens and the cells become active. Nearby nerve cells that do not contain the protein are unaffected.

Light can be aimed at a very small part of the brain and switched on and off extremely quickly. This makes optogenetics a powerful way to investigate how the brain works. For example, if scientists want to know whether a particular group of nerve cells is involved in memory, sleep, sensation or movement, say, they can switch those cells on with light and see what happens.

In one experiment in 2012, a team at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology gave mice a mild electric shock in a particular cage. They used optogenetics to tag the nerve cells in the brain’s memory center that were active during that the fear-conditioning experiment. Days later, in a different cage, they switched those cells on with light. The mice froze in fear, as if they remembered the shock.

Mice that were not shocked did not freeze, which showed that the light was reactivating a specific memory. Deisseroth was one of the study’s authors.

Optogenetics has transformed scientists’ ability to investigate how the brain and nervous system work. It may also have a future as a treatment. Optogenetics is already in early-stage clinical trials for people with retinal degeneration. In these trials, researchers introduce light-sensitive proteins into nerve cells that remain in the retina after the cells that normally detect light – called rods and cones – have been lost. This can make surviving cells respond to light and restore some vision.

Researchers are also investigating whether optogenetics could eventually be used to treat neurological conditions, including Parkinson’s and epilepsy.

This year’s Nobel prize celebrates work that started with a question that may sound esoteric: how do Chlamydomonas swim toward light? This question has no obvious practical importance. It doesn’t address an urgent need in human health, nor is it a clear route to riches. The success of optogenetics is an illustration of the importance of continuing to ask questions simply to understand the world around us because it’s impossible to predict where knowledge will take us.

#776. Thousands of German gamblers could sue for their money back

German gamblers may finally be able to beat the house, thanks to new court rulings in the EU and in Germany. The decisions could help gambling addicts — but the legal road might be difficult.

A German court has followed the European Court of Justice in finding that gamblers can seek compensation from gambling companies if they lost money when online casinos and fruit machines were illegal in Germany.

The verdicts could have major consequences for gambling addicts, as thousands of people could potentially bring civil lawsuits against gambling companies.

A series of court decisions at regional, federal, and European level have found in favor of gamblers in the last year, beginning with the Higher Regional Court Stuttgart in September 2025, which agreed with a plaintiff in the state of Baden-Württemberg who sued the Malta-based online betting company Tipico Games.

The plaintiff lost around €10,000 ($11,200) between 2020 and 2022, but because online gambling was prohibited in Germany until 2021, the court found that operators had therefore received the winnings without legal basis and must return them.

Tipico Games then appealed to the Federal Court of Justice (BGH), the highest civil and criminal court in Germany. In an initial verbal hearing in mid-September this year, the court followed the principle of the plaintiffs' case on one key point: That gamblers have the right to claim back losses if the companies did not have a license in Germany at the time the losses were incurred. But a final decision on the case is only expected to be made in January 2027.

Online casinos and fruit machines were banned in Germany from 2008 to 2021, but several gambling operators were active in the country because they had gaming licenses in other EU countries.

#774. Something strange is happening beneath a volcano near 2 million people

A volcano famous for its glowing lava lake is hiding a surprisingly complex system of magma deep underground. Scientists at Penn State have uncovered evidence of two previously unrecognized magma sources beneath Nicaragua's active Masaya volcano, raising new questions about how molten rock moves beneath the surface.

The discovery is particularly important because approximately 2 million people live within 13 miles of the volcano, which is also a popular tourist destination. Understanding its underground activity could help scientists recognize warning signs of future eruptions and better protect nearby communities.

Using six years of satellite observations, researchers tracked subtle changes in the ground around Masaya. They discovered that one part of the volcano initially sank before beginning to rise, possibly indicating that fresh magma was entering a deep reservoir. Meanwhile, the ground around its active Santiago crater and lava lake continued to sink, suggesting that a separate, shallower magma reservoir was behaving differently.

The findings, published in Geophysical Research Letters, reveal that the volcano's underground magma system may be considerably more complicated than previously understood.

"These observations help scientists better understand how magma moves beneath Masaya and may improve monitoring and eruption forecasting in the future," said lead author Lizzie Johnson, who earned her bachelor's degree in geosciences from Penn State in May.

Located near Managua, Nicaragua's capital, Masaya is a geologically complex volcano featuring multiple overlapping craters and calderas. Calderas are large depressions that generally form when the ground collapses after magma is removed from an underground chamber.

Although scientists have studied Masaya for years, the movement of magma through its underground reservoirs and passageways remains poorly understood. These systems can change over time, making it difficult to determine exactly what is happening beneath the volcano.

"Having a thorough understanding of Masaya's volcanic activity and its related hazards is critical as around 2 million people live near Masaya and the caldera is a national park and popular tourist destination," Johnson said. "Although Masaya last produced a major lava flow in 1772, its persistent degassing often produces dangerously high concentrations of sulfur dioxide volcanic gas."

Sulfur dioxide is a potentially harmful volcanic gas released as magma approaches or remains near the surface. Even without a major eruption, elevated concentrations can create dangerous conditions for visitors and surrounding communities.

Young Cheol Kim, who completed his doctorate in geosciences at Penn State this summer, explained that monitoring the volcano continuously is essential for distinguishing normal activity from changes that might signal increasing volcanic hazards.

"Doing continuous monitoring allows us to understand the baseline activity and identify see if things are changing," he said, explaining that certain shifts in volcanic behavior could indicate a period of heightened activity or a possible eruption. "And then if there is a need to warn people, we can do that in a timely manner."

Satellites Reveal Hidden Movements Beneath the Volcano

To investigate Masaya's underground activity, the researchers analyzed measurements collected by the Sentinel-1 satellite, which observes the volcano approximately once every 12 days.

The team used a technique called interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR), which can detect extremely small changes in the elevation of Earth's surface. By comparing satellite measurements taken at different times, scientists can identify areas where the ground is rising or sinking.

The technique combines two important capabilities. Synthetic aperture radar sends microwave signals toward Earth's surface, allowing satellites to collect measurements even when clouds obscure the ground. Interferometry compares the returning radar signals to detect changes in distance between the satellite and the surface.

This is especially valuable in tropical regions, where frequent cloud cover can make conventional satellite imagery less reliable.

"Cloud penetration is important for volcanoes because many of them, like Masaya, are in tropical areas where there is often cloud cover," Kim explained. "And when we look at all the different satellite images across multiple years, we can see how the distance between the satellite and the ground changes, where it is uplifting or subsiding."

By examining these subtle movements over time, researchers can investigate processes happening far below the surface without directly observing the magma itself.

Two Magma Reservoirs Behaving in Different Ways

The satellite measurements revealed an intriguing pattern between 2018 and 2024. During the first part of the study, the ground within Masaya's caldera gradually subsided, meaning it moved downward. Later, the same general area began to rise.

Scientists believe this transition could reflect changes in the amount of magma stored underground. When magma drains away from a reservoir, the overlying ground can sink. When additional magma enters and increases pressure within a reservoir, the surface may rise.

The most surprising finding was that the area surrounding Santiago crater did not follow the same pattern. Instead, it continued to sink throughout the six-year study, even as the broader caldera began rising.

"When it's subsiding, that means that there's probably magma draining away from the volcano, but once the ground starts uplifting again, that can mean that there's potentially new magma moving in," Johnson said. "At the same time, the area directly around the active Santiago crater deflated over the entire study period independently of the transition to inflation of the main reservoir. This indicates that the plumbing system is more complex and could be due to decreasing magma in a second, shallower reservoir."

Together, these observations suggest that Masaya may have at least two distinct parts of its magma storage system undergoing different changes. A deeper source could be supplying additional magma to the main reservoir, while a shallower reservoir near Santiago crater may be losing magma.

This would help explain why different sections of the same volcano moved in opposite directions.

What the Discovery Means for Future Eruptions

Understanding these underground movements is important because changes in the ground can provide clues about where magma is accumulating, where it is moving, and how it might eventually reach the surface.

The researchers emphasized that their observations did not reveal any immediate danger to communities surrounding Masaya. However, identifying the volcano's normal behavior and understanding its magma supply system could make it easier to recognize unusual changes in the future.

Continued satellite observations, combined with more detailed computer models, could also help scientists better understand the connections between underground magma reservoirs and volcanic activity at the surface.

"Further long-term geodetic and modeling studies will provide a more thorough understanding and allow for effective hazard monitoring and mitigation at Masaya and other lava-lake hosting basaltic volcanoes," Johnson said.

The findings may therefore have implications beyond Nicaragua, particularly for other active volcanoes that contain persistent lava lakes and complex underground magma systems.

From Undergraduate Research to Volcano Science

Johnson became involved in the project after attending a presentation by Christelle Wauthier, a Penn State professor of geosciences and computational sciences hub director at the university's Institute for Computational and Data Sciences.

She completed the research as part of her Schreyer's Honors College thesis, working alongside graduate researchers, including Kim, to analyze satellite measurements and develop her scientific skills.

"I appreciated learning how a research group works," said Johnson, who has recently started a doctoral program in Earth sciences at the University of Hawaii at Manoa, where she will continue studying volcanoes using seismic waves to image below the surface of Kilauea volcano. "I had never had any experience with research, so it was great to be able to join in the group meetings and get to hear about everybody's work. And learning coding was also useful as I continue with my Ph.D."

#757. Why some people escape Alzheimer’s despite carrying a high-risk gene

Some people carry one of the strongest genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease yet never develop it, remaining mentally sharp well into their 80s. For years, scientists have wondered what protects these individuals despite their increased genetic vulnerability.

Now, researchers at Columbia University have uncovered a biological mechanism that could help explain this remarkable resistance. Their findings point to a rare genetic mutation that protects the brain's blood vessels from damage, potentially opening a new path toward Alzheimer's treatments.

The discovery builds on research the team published two years ago involving APOE ε4, a gene variant strongly associated with Alzheimer's risk. The scientists found that some people carrying APOE ε4 also possess a rare protective mutation in the fibronectin (FN1) gene.

Fibronectin is a protein that helps support the structure of tissues. However, in people with APOE ε4, excessive amounts can accumulate around blood vessels in the brain, potentially compromising a critical protective system known as the blood-brain barrier. The rare FN1 mutation appears to limit this buildup and the damage associated with it.

That earlier discovery suggested an intriguing possibility: What if scientists could develop a treatment that recreates the protection provided by this rare mutation?

A new study published in Nature Aging brings that possibility closer by identifying how excess fibronectin damages the brain's protective barrier and revealing potential ways to interrupt the process.

"This new study takes us from a genetic clue to a disease mechanism," says Caghan Kizil, the study's corresponding author and professor of neurological sciences in the Department of Neurology and the Taub Institute for Research on Alzheimer's Disease and the Aging Brain at Columbia University Vagelos College of Physicians and Surgeons.

"We knew that changes in fibronectin could protect against Alzheimer's, but we didn't know why excess fibronectin was harmful in the first place. This study gives us that detailed mechanism -- and with it, ideas for how to reproduce that natural protection."

How an Alzheimer's Risk Gene Weakens the Blood-Brain Barrier

The blood-brain barrier serves as a protective boundary between the bloodstream and the brain. Made up of tightly connected cells surrounding the brain's blood vessels, it carefully regulates which substances can enter brain tissue while helping maintain conditions necessary for removing potentially harmful waste.

When this barrier becomes damaged or excessively permeable, substances that would normally be kept out can enter the brain more easily. Such disruptions may contribute to inflammation and other processes involved in neurological diseases, including Alzheimer's.

To investigate the role of fibronectin, the Columbia team combined several advanced research approaches. They examined human brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid surrounding the brain and spinal cord, using genetic and epigenetic analyses to explore changes in DNA and gene activity.

Their investigation also included single-cell sequencing, which reveals the activity of individual cells, and spatial imaging to examine where biological changes occur within tissues. Additional experiments involved human stem-cell models, three-dimensional cultures of blood vessels, and studies in zebrafish and mice.

Despite the variety of methods, the findings consistently pointed to the same mechanism: APOE ε4 promotes the production and accumulation of fibronectin around the brain's blood vessels.

In mice engineered to carry human APOE ε4, researchers measured nearly twice as much fibronectin in the brain as in mice carrying APOE ε3, another common version of the gene. The greater accumulation was accompanied by evidence that the blood-brain barrier had become leaky.

Scientists Discover How Fibronectin Causes Brain Damage

One experiment provided especially important evidence that fibronectin does more than simply accumulate in damaged blood vessels.

The researchers increased human fibronectin specifically in astrocytes, specialized support cells that help maintain the blood-brain barrier and keep the brain functioning properly.

Increasing fibronectin in these cells was enough to disrupt the barrier, allowing it to become more permeable. Conversely, reducing fibronectin improved the barrier's ability to function normally.

These results provided experimental evidence that excess fibronectin can directly contribute to blood-brain barrier damage rather than merely appearing alongside it.

"The animal experiments allowed us to move beyond association," says co-first author Prabesh Bhattarai, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "When we increased fibronectin in astroglia, the barrier became permeable. When we reduced fibronectin, barrier function improved."

The team then investigated the molecular events responsible for the damage.

They found that excessive fibronectin interferes with communication between cells by activating abnormal signals through integrins, proteins that help cells sense and respond to their surroundings, and an enzyme known as focal adhesion kinase.

These abnormal signals disrupt a network of growth factors called VEGF, HBEGF, and IGF1. Although growth factors are often associated with cell development and repair, these particular signaling molecules also help astrocytes and blood vessel cells coordinate their activities to maintain a healthy blood-brain barrier.

When their communication becomes disrupted, the barrier can lose its integrity, potentially leaving brain tissue more vulnerable to damage.

"Single-cell analysis allowed us to examine this pathway in the different cells that maintain the brain's blood vessels," says co-first author Elanur Yilmaz, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "We observed the same biological pattern across experimental models and human Alzheimer's tissue, making the mechanism especially compelling."

Evidence From Human Brains Strengthens the Alzheimer's Connection

Importantly, the researchers found signs of similar biological disturbances in human samples, suggesting that the process identified in laboratory experiments may also contribute to Alzheimer's disease in people carrying APOE ε4.

Analyses of brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid showed that elevated fibronectin levels were associated with inflammation in astrocytes.

Genetic and gene-regulation analyses also connected FN1 to blood vessel disease and changes involving VEGFA, a growth factor that plays an important role in maintaining healthy brain blood vessels.

Together, these findings provide multiple lines of evidence linking excess fibronectin to the vascular damage associated with APOE ε4.

"The convergence of these independent human datasets strengthens the case that fibronectin is part of the APOE ε4-related vascular disease process," says Badri Vardarajan, associate professor of neurological science in the Department of Neurology, Gertrude H. Sergievsky Center, and Taub Institute, who collaborated on this discovery of the FN1 mutation.

A Rare Genetic Defense Could Inspire New Alzheimer's Treatments

The potential implications are significant because APOE ε4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease. Approximately one in five people carries at least one copy of this gene variant.

Although carrying APOE ε4 does not guarantee that someone will develop Alzheimer's, it increases their likelihood of developing the disease. Understanding why certain carriers remain unaffected could reveal new opportunities for prevention and treatment.

The latest findings suggest several possible approaches.

One strategy would be to prevent harmful amounts of fibronectin from accumulating around the brain's blood vessels. Another would involve blocking the abnormal cellular signals triggered by excess fibronectin. A third possibility would be to restore the growth factor signaling that allows astrocytes and blood vessel cells to maintain the blood-brain barrier.

Researchers are already investigating these possibilities through laboratory experiments.

However, translating the discovery into a treatment will require considerable care. Fibronectin performs essential functions throughout the body, including supporting tissue structure and helping damaged tissues heal. Any future therapy would therefore need to target its harmful accumulation or effects without disrupting these beneficial activities.

By identifying the molecular mechanisms involved, scientists now have a more precise framework for designing such treatments. The findings may also help researchers determine when interventions would be most effective and how to measure whether they are successfully protecting the brain.

"By showing how fibronectin contributes to early vascular damage, this study gives us new therapeutic targets and potential ways to measure whether treatments are working," says Richard Mayeux, chair of the Department of Neurology and a collaborator on the project. "Clinically, it connects a major genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's to an early disease process that may be possible to modify."

Although these potential therapies remain experimental, the discovery offers a promising new direction in Alzheimer's research. Rather than focusing only on the damage that develops as the disease progresses, scientists may eventually be able to reproduce a natural genetic defense that helps protect vulnerable brains before significant damage occurs.
