Escher inspired games in this world There are a few, like Monument Valley, Manifold Garden and Superliminal, amongst a handful of others, you just don't find them all that often in spite of how striking a sense of space is found in Escher's work. Escher's Dream is not, unfortunately, a game of its own, but it is a neat looking Portal 2 mod with some trippy levels in it.

The mod comes from one Jack Phoenix, the latest in a line of map mods they've made over the years across multiple Source-made games. What makes this mod unique (and potentially nauseating for those who aren't so good with this sort of thing) is its approach to level design. There are of course bits where there are stairs titled at angles impossible to walk on but the thing that's interesting about it all is the ways in which you are encouraged to bend the rule of gravity.

One level sort of seems to loop in on itself and you have to angle your portals in such a way where you fall back into the room you were just in. Another sees you using some green gel which shifts the axis of gravity allowing you to walk on walls, so to speak. It's a mod about repositioning yourself in whichever way the level dictates you to do so, and it's quite delightful! It should be noted that the whole mod is quite short, only three maps long, but it's worth a punt. Mostly it has me hankering for a couple of things. One is another full game inspired by Escher's works (seriously, someone hop on that and let me know when you do). The other is an expanded version of this mod. The original Portal 2 is such a short game so it's always a treat when someone rocks up with some custom made levels that add something fresh after all these years.

#3999. Not All Runners Feel Welcome on the Trails. These Groups Are Getting Everyone Outside.

Runners are moving to the trails—and making space for people from all backgrounds to be included.

Welcome to the Wild World of Trail Running! We're diving deep on the races, training tips, and gear you need to know to get outside off paved roads to run a whole new way. Click here for more stories about the trail explosion.

ANTOINE HAUGABOOK, 46, has been immersed in the road running community for more than a decade. A Nashville-based account manager in information technology, Haugabook has served as the co-captain of the city's local chapter of Black Men Run since its inception in 2013 and has also formed relationships with other clubs throughout the region. But when Haugabook and his wife, Yolanda "Yo" Lopez Haugabook, ventured off the roads they were familiar with and into the local trail running scene, they felt like they didn't belong.

"We immediately noticed we were the only people of color and didn't feel like we really related to anyone else out on the trails," Lopez Haugabook, 41, says. "On our third Saturday trail run, one of the other runners finally acknowledged us, saying 'You're back,’ and followed it up with '...Y'all ain't had enough yet?'"

The couple had hit the trails to try something different. After feeling burned out with road running, Antoine applied to and was accepted to the Running Industry Diversity Coalition's 2025 Freedom to Run: Back Outside program, which was designed to get runners of diverse backgrounds to explore the trails. The RIDC program was launched alongside a downloadable resource titled "Opening the Path to Trail Running: A Toolkit for Runners, Retailers, and Communities," which provides BIPOC runners with useful information and resources that act as a welcoming and approachable introduction to trail running. The Haugabooks, kitted out with the gear and coaching provided by RIDC, ventured out on the trails, only to be met with that disconcerting reception.

These types of programs are unfortunately necessary because it’s not an uncommon experience for many people of color when they try to get outside. Nearly 69 percent of outdoor participants are white, according to The Outdoor Industry Association’s 2024 report. Not feeling a sense of belonging is one of the biggest factors that can deter minority runners from exploring trails—but it’s not the only one. Runners who spoke to MH also shared concerns about safety, discouraging finish time cutoffs, and financial constraints, such as high entry fees.

That first experience felt very personal to the Haugabooks. Having long been acquainted with the message that running was for everyone, that microaggression messaged that not everyone was willing to include people who looked like them in the community and reinforced stereotypes that people of color fear and don't enjoy the outdoors.

Those early moments were uncomfortable—but the pair weren’t detered from staying on trail. That just served as fuel to their fire and led them to start Sin Miedo Trail Runners, a group for people of color that currently has around 60 members—most of whom are at the beginner level and experiencing their first introduction to trail running and racing.

"'Sin miedo' literally translates to 'without fear,' but our message is intended more as 'live fearlessly,' because fear tends to take over a lot of BIPOC experiences, whether it's the fear of being persecuted or excluded, and not just in running," Lopez Haugabook says. "'Sin miedo' was a catalyst saying we're going to show up and we will not be afraid to take up space, we will not be afraid to experience the outdoors, even though, culturally, we've been told to be afraid of being in the woods by ourselves, and rightfully so."

Witnessing this lack of representation firsthand prompted Detroit-area content creator Tommie Bailey, a.k.a. "Tommie Runz," 42, to create his "Black Skin Looks Good on Trail" campaign. Bailey has a long list of trail bonafides: he took on the 340 mile Speed Project course from Los Angeles to Las Vegas solo in 2025, he ran this year's Western States Endurance Run 100-miler, and has been a presence at the prestigious Ultra Trail du Mont Blanc (UTMB) series in France over the last two years with major brands.

"I've heard from so many Black and Brown runners over the years saying that they've been inspired to get out on the trails after seeing the content I've put out over the years, and I feel blessed that I've always felt like I've had the freedom to talk about these topics even when collaborating with brands at these events," Bailey says. "We aren't being highlighted to the same degree because there just aren't a lot of Black Americans at the top of roads or trails because it's still new to a lot of us, but I think we need to have the conversations about why Black and Brown people don't feel like they're being represented at these races."

THE CONCEPT OF creating their own spaces isn't new for many minority community leaders, especially as the running industry and corporate America as a whole have shifted away from commitments made during the 2020 social justice reckoning that came after the tragic murders of Ahmaud Arbery and George Floyd. Large brands, including Google, Meta, and Target, have cut DEI programs and positions meant to support employees from marginalized groups, and running community leaders have had to work even harder to highlight instances of racial injustice and accessibility and remind the industry and community that problems weren't solved in 2020.

In 2025, Alison Mariella Désir, co-founder of the RIDC and author of Running While Black: Finding Freedom in a Sport That Wasn't Built for Us, created the We Out Here Trail Festival, which offers race distances from 1.9 miles to two hours, in her home base of Seattle, along with her friend and local running coach and personal trainer Aaliyah Earvin.

"We chose these distance options because most trail races tend to be focused on longer/ultra distances and have discouraging finish time cutoffs that may make newer trail runners hesitant to sign up," Désir, 41, says. "We also wanted distances that people could not compare to road distances so that there were no expectations around what it should feel like, and so they could fully embrace trail running apart from their experiences in road running."

Having grown tired of calling out prominent trail racing leadership for their lack of concrete efforts to diversify their fields and create welcoming communities, she was inspired to create her own event where minority runners could show up as their true selves.

"We Out Here Trail Fest centers the experience of people of color, but white people are there too; we just created it with us in mind," Désir says. "If you can welcome people who are new to the trail and give them this incredible experience, and then they go off to other spaces, but they have this fundamental understanding that they belong, which is a huge service that we can do for the community. I want people to leave with a feeling that trail running is joyful and that it's for them."

Sabrina Pace-Humphreys, 48, a Cotswolds, U.K.-based ultrarunner, similarly used her experiences to create her own community. In 2019, while training for a race in the Italian Alps, Pace-Humphreys was traversing a narrow ridge on a mountain range and had a near-fatal experience when she slipped off the path and was hanging on to the side of the mountain. She recalls being ignored by five men who ran past her as she screamed for help.

"It was kind of a sliding doors moment because I had asked for help and hadn't been given it, and I very much felt as though had I been blonde-haired, blue-eyed, and white, I would have got help a lot quicker," she says. "This incident made me realize that I had to make a decision, which was to either leave trail running because I cannot continue to be in spaces where I feel physically unsafe and uncared for and unseen, or I start something of my own."

This led Pace-Humphreys to create Black Trail Runners, a global community and nonprofit that uses trail running to connect BIPOC runners and their allies with the outdoors. The organization hosts events like Black to the Trails, which organizers call Europe's most ethnically diverse trail running event, with 1K, 5K, and 10K races.

"At the time, there were a lot of running group crews in the U.K., but none that specialized in trail, and none that centered the Black experience in trail running," she says. "With Black Trail Runners, we qualify trail leaders and trail coaches while also addressing barriers to access, skills and representation, both out on the trails and through the media."

DÉSIR ALSO HOSTS the Earth Movers podcast with professional ultramarathoner and 2021 USATF 100-mile national champion Stefanie Flippin and Verna NezBegay Volker, a fellow activist and founder of Native Women Run, a nonprofit dedicated to representing and empowering Native women across the U.S. and Canada. Now in its third season, the three women created the podcast with the goal to amplify the stories and people of color on the trails and beyond, many of whom have not had the opportunity to do so in a corner of the sport that frequently uplifts the same familiar, homogenous voices.

"Trail running is very white-forward right now and it continues to center the experiences of those who are benefiting the most," Flippin, 37, says. "For example, there's the current narrative that women are showing up so much more in trail running, which is incredible, but it's still equally important to highlight the experiences of those who are less represented and things like their safety and other barriers to access that they've had to consider to also exist in this space."

For Flippin, who is based outside of Denver, such experiences are personal. She was on the receiving end of anti-Asian verbal harassment at the height of the Covid-19 pandemic.

"Pre-pandemic, safety was definitely a consideration for me out on the trails, but it wasn't at the forefront of my mind like oh this could be a potentially dangerous place for us to be living in or for me," Flippin says. "During that time, I was harassed, stared at and called racial slurs out on the trails, and now I just don't have the same sense of empowerment and autonomy to run by myself on the trails like I used to, especially now that I live in a much more rural area."

FOR MANY TRAIL and ultramarathon aficionados, races like Western States and UTMB are viewed as the holy grail in this corner of the space. But they're also some of the least accessible, as they are notoriously difficult to gain entry. Entry fees are over $400 and $500, respectively, and participants also have to account for significant travel costs, as well as shoes, gear, and other training necessities.

"UTMB week is pitched as like the 'Super Bowl of trail running, or the who's who of trail running,' where amateur athletes can run the same trails as elite athletes. But in order to do that, you have to have a good disposable income, so we need to look at the fallacy around 'if you want to be here, you can,' when it's really more like 'if you look a certain way and if you have a certain means of financial support, you could be here,'" Pace-Humphreys says. "Brands and race directors need to put their money where their mouths are, because what we're seeing geopolitically across the board at the moment is a massive pullback in terms of budgets for any kind of DEI initiatives."

Désir emphasizes the need for tracking racial demographic data at large races, even though being confronted with and potentially publicizing low numbers is likely to make leadership uncomfortable. Bailey echoes that sentiment, noting that trail running brands and community leaders need to be cognizant about who they choose to partner with if they care about making meaningful changes.

Although the trail running community still has huge strides to make to become less homogeneous, there are small signs of progress that will hopefully encourage more runners of color to enter the space. This year, Ben Dhiman, who is Indian-American, won the prestigious 108-mile UTMB race, becoming the first runner to break 19 hours and just the second American man to win. Earlier this year, Jennifer Lichter—who was born in Bogotá, Colombia, and lived there until she was nine years old—set a new women’s course record at the Black Canyon 100K, becoming the first woman to finish in under eight hours. Lichter also broke the women’s course record at Western States, which was also her first 100-mile race.

Outside the pro ranks, the programs and creator-led efforts leading the way are also making their mark to bring more everyday people outside.

"Our experience with the RIDC program showed that the doors of the trails are open for you to come as you are—that you already belong," Haugabook says. "It's allowed us to take up space unapologetically—space that's rightfully already ours, and not the other way around."

After all, expanding the field is the only way true growth happens. Bailey has seen that in the mainstream running world—and he knows that’s the way forward on the trails, too.

"Road running is not booming because it stayed white and pushed white and just kept going the same—more voices from different communities and different countries now affect what we all look like and feel like, and how we dress, and the shoes we're wearing," Bailey says. "All of that has come from more voices pouring into it, whether that's in run clubs or who brands decide will wear their clothes and send to events. Trail leadership needs to also remember that is how things are grown and move forward, and if you want to evolve, and be more welcoming in general, then you have to start investing in inviting people that maybe wouldn't otherwise be there."

#3998. Play, the Uncanny Valley, and Halloween

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Play often rewards us emotionally, with a thrill or a feeling of wonder. A roller coaster ride or a peek through a telescope trained on the cratered moon, so different, both captivating and edgy, yet easily understood as occasions for play. The body responds. The mind travels.

But consider two real or hypothetical events that lurk uncomfortably nearby in memory or imagination—a near car crash in our lingering felt-experiences, or terrifying thoughts of extinction-level planetary invasions that Hollywood loads us up with. You know the stories: a soulless hive-mind eyes our green and pleasant lands; an extra-terrestrial vanguard seeds Earth with pods that germinate with malicious, lookalike non-human infiltrators; or an emissary from a galactic police force arrives to put the planet on notice that its hungry warlike nature, its appetite for conquest, will not long be tolerated in this neighborhood of a peaceful galaxy.

We surely have enough real potential catastrophes to worry about in this Halloween season—orbiting nukes that could detonate and fry our power grid, for example, or glacial collapse that would flood coastal cities and stifle the Gulf Stream, or election subversion from power-hungry office-holders.

Yet in this spooky season, we celebrate the macabre as play and laugh in its face. Play is therapy.

In the movies, a hero or a heroic coalition or natural defenses or a fundamental change of heart routinely defeat the threats. A grounded pilot steals a fighter jet and explodes a bomb in the intake valves of the mothership; the invading force succumbs to terrestrial microbes; a sobered world puts aside its squabbles to unite against extraplanetary menace. Our homeland may not live happily ever after, but it will live wiser, if sadder and more resolute.

In this season in our neighborhoods, in school classrooms, and at the office, we fight fear with play. We welcome costumed trick-or-treaters on our doorstep—all those Pokémon characters, one-eyed pirates, and haughty princesses appeasable with chocolate. Our kids dress up as heroes or spooks or superheroes or sports-heroes in classroom parties. (Plenty of Supergirls and Josh Allens this year…)

And this year too, so says BuzzFeed, among the witches and ghosts, office parties will welcome improvised Miss Trunchbulls, Taylor Swifts, Ted Lassos, bushy-headed Bob Ross-es with a brush and a palette in hand, or Flo-s from the insurance commercial. Some of these, like a nun in spider-web stockings or an orange-fright-wigged clown in a bulging blue suit and a dragging red tie, may not be quite safe for work.

But Halloween comfortably flirts with the edge of disorder and dismay, as a way of both satirizing everyday obedient humdrum and combatting haunting feelings. At Halloween again, we laugh our fears away.

Our modern, ironic, jokey, playful, immunizing approach to Halloween contrasts sharply with the spiritual view and deep psychological underpinnings of our Puritan founders.

For the theologians among them and for their faithful congregants, the Devil was not a remote occult figure out of medieval pagan (that is to say Catholic) hocus pocus, but a literal present force, a swindler that roamed their “errand” into the wilderness and undermined their City Upon a Hill. I have read enough closely argued 17th-century Puritan sermons that contained paragraphs starting with “fifthly” and “sixthly” to suss how their view was not irrational, not as such, but rather based meticulously in rational argument, however strange and ill-founded their premises.

Peering outward, Puritan divines saw that the dark woods at the perimeter of New England settlement was inhabited by Native Americans whose spiritual, animist assumptions were literally devilish. Satan ruled “savage” land as his dominion, literally. Indigenous people were his decoys and instruments of his malice, literally. Among Puritan authorities we may remember Cotton Mather best, because he had a hand in justifying the Salem Witch Trials. “We know not when or how these Indians first became inhabitants of this mighty continent,” he wrote, “yet we may guess that probably the Devil decoyed these miserable savages hither.”

There were no coincidences in the fearful worldview of people who lived on the edge. Who could be trusted? Every deviation, each sin, gave the Devil a foothold and an entryway. Signs of imminent invasion lay everywhere in plain sight; manifest in a destructive storm, for example, in the failure of crops, or in the outbreak of sickness. And infamously, also in the hysterical conversion neurosis (as we moderns would understand it) that teenage girls suffered in the villages around Salem, Massachusetts, in 1792.

The Devil had possessed them, literally. They had been easily tempted and recruited. Their demonic possession posed a contagious risk. Failure to prosecute these crimes zealously invited divine disfavor and punishment of the whole society. And so, in the careful court proceedings that followed, judges marshaled “spectral evidence” and convicted 30. Twenty-five died by hanging, under torture, or from disease in filthy prison cells.

Those of us who have taken a ride on a smoothly plunging roller coaster will recall a sense of pleasing vertigo; in these experiences, weightlessness alternates with strong g-forces in wacky, swooping succession. Riders, feeling victorious and bolstered, will typically giggle or guffaw as the cars pull in. But designers of some modern amusements calculate them to scare the pants off us. I have in mind a clattering old ride at Ohio’s Cedar Point called “The Mean Streak” that left passengers so tossed about and battered that none were left smiling.

In a similar way, most Halloween costumes are scary-cute or silly-scary: witches in pointed hats, their faces powdered green, for instance, a striped Beetlejuice with a goofy painted-on smile, or a vampire nurse-phlebotomist who aspires to comedy. Once at a hip Halloween party, I met a woman whose face was framed in a spray of spiky white pillows airbrushed in yellows, oranges, and reds. I wanted to know what she was. “I’m a flaming as*hole,” she said. “Don’t be so hard on yourself, dear,” I replied, helpfully.

Other outfits without a trace of humor strike an opposite note: lurching vacant zombies, soulless mummies, merciless zipper-faced robots, or most unsettling but simpler, translucent but altered plastic masks that blur reality into an expressionless pod-person stare.

These apparitions seem uncanny, standing close to reality but not close enough for comfort. They seem hollow, and therefore deeply unsettling.

On Halloween, the border between fun and fright, humor and horror, and teasing and terror becomes porous and occasionally leaky, reverberating with a distant echo of Puritan elemental dread.

Dreamcast is rarely thought of as the start of anything. It was the full stop on Sega's reign as a hardware company, the console that died as PlayStation 2 thrived. But it was also, Rockstar says, the birthplace of the era's defining PlayStation game. You know, the one your mum heard was about killing prostitutes: Grand Theft Auto 3.

The story goes like this: GTA's developer, DMA Design, had just opened a new office in Edinburgh after a decade in Dundee. Change crackled in the air. The '90s were ending, and all around DMA the game industry was transitioning to three dimensions. The young team behind an early 3D game named Body Harvest believed they were ready to do the same with GTA.

Yet the studio's old guard was unconvinced. The team had already experimented with tilting the camera a little in GTA 2, but weren't sure it was possible to go further. A simulated city, navigated at breakneck speeds in whatever direction the player chose? Surely not in 3D.

"We had a bit of pushback from them," recalls Rockstar North co-studio head and art director Aaron Garbut. "So we did a tech demo of a city environment on the Dreamcast with cars driving around and characters walking the streets. We got it working over a period of weeks to the point where you could steal a car and drive it about, then we got it in front of Sam."

Sam Houser was the executive producer of Take-Two's new publishing label in New York, which had rather audaciously named itself Rockstar Games. He'd been a backer of GTA since its earliest days, egging its team on as they embraced player criminality and transgression. Now he was more than keen for the series to take its next revolutionary step.

"Sam has always been the voice of the player," Garbut says. "He has always encouraged us to go further, to take risks, to make every game our best yet and never to settle. He has also given us the support, the room, and the flexibility we've needed over the years to get things where they need to be. He's still that person for the team now."

The GTA 3 team were galvanised by the Dreamcast demo, basic though it was. It became a source of inspiration and a focal point for their ambitions. "We knew what we were doing in 3D and were young, arrogant, and naïve enough to feel like it was easy and exciting," Garbut says. "We just knew how far we could take things. We could see the impact of an urban environment in 3D and were filled with ideas first and foremost about how we could bring it alive. That was our initial focus – building the toys and playset, making a world that functioned as well as we could make it, a living, breathing city."

From the previous games came the premise: the arresting idea of being an antihero, a criminal who worked for even worse criminals. GTA had always had multiple mission-givers, albeit at the other end of a phone. And its developers had already invented Wanted levels, not to mention their antidote, the Pay And Spray shop. But the GTA 3 team consciously rejected the arcade-like elements that had come before – in particular, the running point total for destruction that Garbut describes as "pinball scoring". In an immersive 3D world, those abstract touches no longer seemed to fit. Conversely, DMA leaned into the natural sense of cinema and emotional connection that came with a 3D camera. "We didn't really approach it like a sequel," Garbut says. "We weren't slavishly looking at how the earlier GTAs had done things. We worked in broad strokes and without referring too much to them."

Some of GTA 3's greatest development challenges were the fundamentals we take for granted in open worlds today. As Garbut points out, DMA didn't have any contemporaries working with densely populated urban environments.

"We didn't split things into nice manageable, loadable chunks."

"The density was the difference," he says. "There were simpler games – driving games set in urban worlds but without too much interaction – that were designed to traverse at speed." Though Garbut doesn't name any, 1999's Driver springs to mind.

"Then there were denser games like Zelda that did amazing things but spread it out or split it across empty spaces and denser dungeons," he continues. "We didn't split things into nice manageable, loadable chunks, we didn't spread it out with empty areas, we didn't design it to only work at speed or to explore in detail on foot in small areas. We were trying to offer it all in one experience, and to make it feel as alive as possible."

"It’s terrifying, and releasing GTA 3 was cathartic. We were a young team, relatively inexperienced. There’s a lot to carry before a game comes out; experience helps with that."

Players of the time were gobsmacked by the way GTA 3's world was constantly streamed into being, just outside their vision. Well, except for the rare occasions when a wall only appeared in time to tear the bonnet from their Banshee. It's a system that Garbut clearly remains deeply proud of, all these years later.

"The art team broke the world apart by hand in the best way to squeeze all we could into memory while getting it off the drive fast enough," he says. "The code team built an elegant streaming system and we got as granular as writing the files to the DVD in specific orders to keep areas of the world together physically on the disk. It's a testament to the talent and ingenuity of the team that we made this work."

The decision represented a huge risk, but in the end the results spoke for themselves – Liberty City rendered at great distances, across moody bays and packed urban thoroughfares, and did so seamlessly, without long loading screens. "Getting it right effectively removed a lot of limitations for scale and detail," Garbut says. "We could build what we wanted as long as the area around the player would fit in memory and we could get it off the DVD drive fast enough."

If GTA 3 was a living world, then DMA Design was the hospital crash team tasked with keeping it breathing, against the odds. "You have to ensure the system can cope with a random pileup round the corner exploding at the same time you are also trying to run an intense mission," Garbut says. "It's all about managing the chaos and the competing systems rather than just focussing on what you see. Keeping the world alive and allowing real interaction with it is one of the big challenges with open world games."

The making of GTA 3 established the methodology that Rockstar North, as the developer is known now, still uses today. The studio always begins with the place and then, once it's fit to live in, moves up one layer to the toys. In GTA 3's case, the toys were the cars – the aspect of the game players would spend more time with than anything else.

"Seeing the world come alive through these vehicles, that's when I remember starting to feel things come together," Garbut says. "Every so often a build comes along with a feature you've been discussing or planning forever – it's normally rough and only there when you squint a bit. But it's there. #3994. Ultra-Processed Food Intake May Not Be Linked to Colorectal Cancer

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Ultra-processed foods as a group do not increase the risk of colorectal cancer, according to a new study.

Researchers from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) report that they found no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer incidence among the more than 400,000 adults they studied.

They said their findings are important because ultra-processed foods now account for more than half of adults’ energy intake in the United States. They published their research on October 15 in PLOS Medicine.

The study comes amid recent research that has connected ultra-processed foods to an increased risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and obesity.

However, the link between colorectal cancer and ultra-processed foods has been less clear.

A 2025 study reported that ultra-processed foods may increase the risk of colorectal cancer in females under 50.

A 2022 study found that males who consumed high rates of ultra-processed foods had a higher risk of developing colorectal cancer.

NCI scientists noted in their new study that ultra-processed products represent a wide range of foods.

They pointed out that foods such as whole grains and dairy products were associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer among study participants, while foods such as processed meats were associated with a higher risk.

“We did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence,” said Erikka Loftfield, PhD, a former investigator with the NCI, in a news release. “However, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention.”

Experts not involved in the study told Healthline that the NCI scientists appear to be on the right track.

“While the study did not find that overall ultra-processed food intake was associated with colorectal cancer risk, the findings suggest that simply classifying foods by their level of processing may not tell the whole story,” said Marissa Shams-White, PhD, a senior principal scientist in population science at the American Cancer Society.

“One of the challenges with studying ultra-processed foods is that the category includes a very wide range of foods and beverages, and we don’t yet know whether they all relate to colorectal cancer risk in the same way,” she added.

Ketan Thanki, MD, a colorectal surgeon specializing in benign and malignant diseases of the colon, rectum, and anus at the MemorialCare Todd Cancer Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California, agrees.

“Just because it is ultra-processed doesn’t mean it is inherently bad or good for you,” he said. “The source of the food and the additives like preservatives, flavorings, sugars, and pigments that are added to increase palatability probably play a much larger role in increasing cancer risk.”

“A whole grain cereal and a hot dog are both UPF, but they push risk in opposite directions,” Thanki noted.

In their study, the National Cancer Institute researchers analyzed data from 461,682 adults aged 50 to 71 years who enrolled in the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study between 1995 and 1996.

The participants recorded their eating habits using a food frequency questionnaire. Researchers then used that data to estimate each person’s intake of ultra-processed foods. The median consumption of ultra-processed foods among participants was 43% of daily median intake.

The participants were then followed until 2018, for a median of nearly 19 years. The scientists reported that 10,075 cases of colorectal adenocarcinoma were diagnosed during that period.

The researchers reported no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and the incidence of colorectal cancer.

However, they said their findings varied by food type. Higher intake of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium derived from UPF sources was associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence, while meat from UPF sources was associated with higher incidence.

Researchers also found that replacing 10% of gram weight from UPF intake with minimally processed foods was associated with a 2% lower incidence of CRC.

The researchers acknowledged limitations to their study.

They noted that the participants were primarily older, white Americans. In addition, UPF intake was only measured once at the beginning of the study period, so the findings cannot account for changes in eating habits or the increasing availability of ultra-processed foods.

Nonetheless, Ashkan Farhadi, MD, a gastroenterologist at MemorialCare Orange Coast Medical Center in California, who wasn’t involved in the study, said he found the results “very interesting.”

“The definition of ultra-processed foods is so broad,” he told Healthline. “It’s important to designate what type of ultra-processed foods you’re talking about.”

“You shouldn’t just go by whether a food is designated as ultra-processed,” Farhadi added. “You should go by what’s on the [ingredient] label.”

Shams-White shared a similar approach. “When it comes to diet, it is important to follow an overall healthy dietary pattern,” she told Healthline. “Emphasize a variety of fruits and vegetables and whole grains and limit red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, ultra-processed foods, and refined grain products.”

“Eating a diet rich in fiber and whole grains, dairy and calcium, and limiting processed and red meat and alcohol is what really reduces your risk of developing cancer,” added Thanki.

The incidence rate is slightly higher in men than in females.

The organization reports that the rate of colorectal cancer cases in the United States has declined since the mid-1980s, but they note that most of that decrease has occurred in older adults.

The incidence rate among people under 50 increased by nearly 3% from 2013 to 2022. Colorectal cancer is now the leading cause of death from cancer for U.S. adults under the age of 50.

#3990. Does Trauma Shatter Memory? What the Research Actually Shows

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

One familiar explanation of trauma goes like this: overwhelming fear disrupts the brain’s ability to store an experience, leaving scattered images, sensations, and emotions instead of an organized memory. It sounds plausible. But how well does the evidence support it?

In a recent article in The Conversation, psychologist Michele Patterson Ford describes traumatic memories as frequently fragmented. She explains that intense stress can overwhelm the hippocampus, disrupting memory storage, while heightened emotional activity contributes to vivid sensory recollections. Her practical message is compassionate: people should not dismiss survivors because their accounts seem disorganized or their behavior seems unexpected.

That message deserves attention. However, the proposed explanation of memory is more contested than her article suggests. Harvard psychologist Richard McNally’s review, Are Memories of Sexual Trauma Fragmented?, challenges the idea that trauma typically breaks memories into disconnected pieces. His question is straightforward: fragmented compared with what?

All autobiographical memories are incomplete. We remember some details and forget others. We reconstruct experiences when describing them, rather than replaying an internal recording. We only encode information that we attend to. Finding gaps in someone’s account therefore does not establish that trauma caused a special disruption of memory.

McNally highlights a study by David Rubin and colleagues involving 60 trauma-exposed adults, half with PTSD. Participants described traumatic, positive, and important experiences. Researchers evaluated their accounts using 28 measures, including participants’ judgments, independent raters, and computerized analyses.

Overall, traumatic memories were no less coherent than the other memories. Nor were the trauma accounts of participants with PTSD less coherent than those of participants without PTSD.

This does not mean everyone remembers trauma clearly. It means the study did not support a general rule that trauma produces unusually fragmented memories. McNally’s review presents evidence against broad fragmentation claims, rather than proving that fragmentation never occurs.

Someone may struggle to describe an experience because discussing it is distressing. That difficulty does not, by itself, reveal how the experience was originally stored.

McNally describes experiments showing that questioning methods can influence how coherent people judge their own memories to be. Answering difficult questions about specific details can make a memory seem less coherent than simply telling the story.

Likewise, a vivid image or sudden emotional recollection does not establish that the broader memory exists only as disconnected fragments. The experience of remembering and the organization of the remembered event are related questions, but they are not interchangeable.

McNally also reviews a treatment study in which improvement in PTSD was not reliably associated with changes in narrative fragmentation. That finding challenges the assumption that recovery necessarily depends on assembling scattered memories into an orderly story.

Psychologist Lawrence Patihis approaches the issue from another direction. In Did Dissociative Amnesia Evolve?, he asks why natural selection would favor a mechanism that blocks access to memories of dangerous experiences.

Remembering an attack could help an individual recognize a threat, avoid a dangerous place, or warn others. Forgetting the event could remove information useful for survival. Patihis therefore argues that we should generally expect threatening experiences—particularly their central, survival-relevant features—to be well retained.

Consider someone who survives an animal attack. Remembering the animal, its behavior, and where the attack occurred could offer future protection. Similar benefits could apply to remembering violence by another person.

Patihis’s argument specifically concerns dissociative amnesia: the proposed psychological blocking of a stored autobiographical memory that may later become accessible. His paper therefore adds a challenge to theories of protective forgetting, rather than directly disproving memory fragmentation. Patihis offers a theoretical challenge, not experimental proof, that memories of traumatic experiences should not be of lower quality or accuracy, compared to other memories.

McNally acknowledges that intense fear may strengthen memory for central features while leaving peripheral details poorly remembered. He also distinguishes trauma itself from other causes of memory disruption, including alcohol blackouts. Being unable to move during an assault does not necessarily mean being unable to remember it.

The practical implication is to avoid prescribing how a survivor’s memory should look. A disorganized account does not establish dishonesty. A coherent account does not establish that an experience was harmless. Neither coherence nor fragmentation, by itself, establishes accuracy, nor does either characteristic establish that an event was in fact traumatic.

Traumatic experiences can leave memories that are vivid, incomplete, distressing or difficult to discuss. The evidence McNally reviews challenges the broader claim that trauma generally shatters memory into disconnected pieces. We can listen patiently, take suffering seriously and evaluate evidence carefully without assuming that every survivor’s brain follows the same script.

#3977. Roman sarcophagus inside Westminster Abbey originally held a different body

Published Oct 20, 2026 7:01 PM EDT

A formerly inexplicable Roman sarcophagus discovered at England’s Westminster Abbey over 150 years ago is likely a rare example of medieval funerary recycling. Although archaeologists stopped short of definitively identifying who was buried inside, the team confidently narrowed down their candidates to a pair of high-ranking Benedictine monks.

“We have two viable contenders for the occupant of the sarcophagus,” said Westminster Abbey archaeological consultant Warwick Rodwell, co-author of an accompanying study published today in the Journal of the British Archaeological Association.

The mysterious limestone tomb was originally crafted during the 4th century CE, when the Roman empire’s jurisdiction still extended as far away as Britain. By the mid-13th century, King Henry III constructed a Benedictine sacristy above the sarcophagus, and it remained undisturbed for nearly 600 years. In 1820, workers accidentally cracked its lid into six pieces after unintentionally exposing the tomb. It would take until 1869 for archaeologists to finally repair the damage, as well as discover its confounding origins.

The Roman sarcophagus has baffled researchers ever since. The limestone enclosure featured an inscription to Valerius Amandinus, a rich local who lived nearby sometime between 300 and 400 CE. But the sarcophagus lid clearly displayed a much more recent, medieval Latin cross. The cross implied the sarcophagus possibly housed someone other than Amandinus, but who?

Extensive stonework analysis and skeletal carbon dating finally point towards two probable suspects. The first possibility is the Westminster Abbot Aelfwig, who died around 1020. The second is his successor, Wulnoth, who died in 1049. Further skeletal examinations indicate the man was 35 to 49 years old and around 5-foot 9-inches tall when he died. Interestingly, this contradicts the initial 19th century theory of a slightly shorter, “robust young man” less than 30 years old.

“We do not know the date of birth of either. But either would fit equally well if we assume the Abbot was in his early twenties when the appointment was made and close to fifty when he died,” explained Rodwell.

If Rodwell had to pick between the two, he may lean towards Aelfwig, since he was already a Westminster monk during that era.

The monk’s associates likely first placed him in a timber coffin that they lowered into the sarcophagus. Next, they sealed it with a re-carved cover displaying the Latin cross. But regardless of who resided in the tomb, its reuse wasn’t considered a slight. Instead, its original craftsmanship was intended to honor the deceased monk. It’s unlikely the mourning monks gave much thought to anyone else’s opinion on burial recycling, especially those living nearly 1,000 years after their respected Abbot’s passing.

#3974. Monkeys also avoid germs after viral outbreaks

Published Oct 20, 2026 7:05 PM EDT

When a new disease emerges, things can get a little weird. We start to clean groceries with antibacterial wipes, the sound of a sneeze can trigger our fight-or-flight response, and we generally become more cautious. Humans may not be the only primate species that change their behavior in the face of disease.

According to a study published today in the journal Royal Society Open Science, Japanese macaques (Macaca fuscata) that lived through a deadly outbreak in 2020 became noticeably more cautious about food that appeared contaminated, even though this disease was not spread by food or feces. This indicates that purposefully shifting behavior to avoid a pathogen can be shaped by our appearance and that disgust may have deep evolutionary roots. It also sheds light on the behavioral immune system—a collection of behaviors that help animals avoid an infection before the biological immune systems respond.

“We can show now, in non-human primates, that this is the same for them,” Federico Rossano, a study co-author and cognitive psychologist at the University of California San Diego, said in a statement. “Even with no knowledge of how disease is transmitted, being exposed to others getting sick and dying changes the likelihood of engaging in behaviors that potentially could lead to some form of sickness.”

In 2020, the staff at a primate sanctuary in southern Texas confirmed the presence of simian retrovirus Type II. This disease can cause AIDS-like immunodeficiency and is primarily transmitted through bites and scratches. It caused severe illness and death in some of the monkeys. The sanctuary staff separated the groups and implemented testing and quarantine measures in response to the outbreak. Since the groups live in separate enclosures, scientists then had the chance to compare animals with similar backgrounds that had experienced very different social and disease environments.

In total, three groups of monkeys experienced the outbreak while one had not. The team tested

55 macaques to see how they responded to a choice between food that was put on a clean wooden surface and food placed on a surface covered with real macaque feces. The food with the feces was sealed under a layer of clear epoxy, so that the monkeys could see the food and poop without risking infection.

In the group that had been exposed to the outbreak, some of the individuals had been infected while others had lived alongside sick monkeys without getting sick. This allowed the researchers to question if experiencing an outbreak without getting infected could change the macaque’s behavior. To test that further, the clean surface was replaced with a brightly colored kitchen sponge in some of the scenarios to see if the animals were just avoiding unfamiliar objects.

Previous studies found that macaques aren’t necessarily deterred by contamination—as long as the payoff is tempting. When presented with a favorite treat (such as a peanut) wild Japanese macaques would still eat food from a surface covered in feces, but will avoid it for food like grains of wheat.

The team found that macaques from groups that went through the outbreak were far less likely to eat the food from the contaminated surface. They ate from the fake contaminated surface in only 47 percent of trials. The monkeys that did not experience the outbreak ate from the potentially contaminated surface in 79 percent of trials.

The monkeys from outbreak-exposed groups also behaved similarly whether or not they themselves had been infected. According to the team, this suggests the behavioral change was associated with their experiencing living through the outbreak, instead of actually getting sick

“Groups have histories, and those histories show up in behavior,” added study co-author and UC San Diego cognitive psychologist Stephan Kaufhold. “Living through the disease outbreak appears to have made those monkey groups more cautious around food that looks contaminated.”

The virus was spread through blood or saliva from bites and scratches, and not through contaminated food. Interestingly, the macaques who saw their fellow primates get sick, were more reluctant to eat food placed on a surface that visually suggested contamination in some of the tests.

In addition to shedding light on how illness affects primate behavior, it also offers insight into the evolution of disgust. The team did not measure disgust directly, but found that monkey’s avoidance of potential sources of infection can change with their experience, similar to what humans do.

Human disgust is believed to play an important role in protecting us from pathogens. We recoil from the smell and sight of poop, spoiled food, and other potential sources of infection. Our sensitivity to those cues can also change depending on context.

Since primates are capable of social learning, the team believes that heightened pathogen avoidance could be learned from other members in the group and persist over time. Future studies could see if newly introduced animals adopt the behavior of a group that has already experienced an outbreak.

#3971. Venting Frustration: What Are We Really Saying?

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Last Sunday, my favorite football team lost a game. There’s nothing remarkable about that.

However, it was the way they lost that provoked me. They were well ahead at halftime, shutting out a heretofore winless squad. After the break, however, a series of error-filled plays ensued. The losing team rallied and gained confidence. What looked like certain victory for my home side became a collapse of commitment and, seemingly, character.

Having no one to complain to, I consulted a social media site for “fan reactions” to the debacle. What I discovered was a treasure trove of abusive comments posted just after the game. Nostrils flaring, writers claimed that both the coach and general manager should be fired. The players, save for a couple, were declared both overpaid and incompetent. Several people said they would no longer be supporting the team. Ten years of grievances were aired.

Interestingly, no one raised their voice in the team’s defense. Naysayers claimed the day, at least until this weekend’s chance for redemption.

Nothing written above should surprise the reader. We live in a society dominated increasingly by the activities of large organizations, economic and governmental. As individuals, we feel there is little we can do about their behaviors. Similarly, we consume media reports of events that swirl around us. Those events seem both distant (perhaps a school shooting in a faraway state) and intimate (with videos broadcast into our homes). We mourn the victims but see few policy changes to make the dangers stop.

Even day-to-day interactions acquire this quality of impersonality and lack of control. A stranger, driving wildly, cuts in front of us. A company we depend on unexpectedly suspends its services. A doctor’s office fails to notify us of some important test results.

As psychologists explain, frustration occurs when personal goals are blocked. The more important and immediate the goal (perhaps securing food for our family) and the more persistent the blockage, the greater the frustration. When blockage is too vast or impenetrable or when goals are too lofty, ambition may give way to resignation. By contrast, frustration lives in that more proximate zone where people believe that their desires are legitimate and that they should have ways of achieving these. Few of us expect the luxuries of a five-star hotel. We do expect clean sheets and a bug-free room when we take our modest vacation.

Commonly then, we express frustration in the form of verbal complaints. This may mean face-to-face confrontation with someone (the hotel receptionist?) who can “do something” about the problem at hand. However, the person in charge (the manager, perhaps) often is “away.” We are directed to the customer service department. We can’t get through the automated call system. Emails go unanswered. Frustration builds.

At that point, who among us hasn’t gone public with our displeasure? That means sounding off to friends and family, writing negative internet reviews, and lodging complaints with regulatory agencies. Such demonstrations are now accustomed elements of modern culture. Pointedly, we rarely stress our own role in the difficulty. Other people, organizations, or even “the system” are to blame.

Much as an enclosed automobile gives us the sense we can safely curse or make obscene gestures at an obnoxious driver, so we choose venues for other forms of venting. Both ordinary mail and email give writers the opportunity to state complaints more sharply than they would in person. Presumably, negative responses to those complaints, if any, can be ignored or discarded.

Social media platforms, such as the one displaying criticisms of my losing team, are even better. These provide pseudo-identity within an instant “community” of similarly inclined people. Venting is the rule rather than the exception.

In-person complaints carry greater risks. Sometimes, we choose seemingly anonymous settings like public demonstrations and marches where we are surrounded by others who support our views. For one-on-one conversations, we choose familiar others whom we know to be on our side. Entirely unsatisfactory is venting to someone who gives us grief for our concerns.

Frustration (and its release) has emotional and physiological components. We feel ourselves getting “worked up.” Releasing that tension in words and actions feels better, at least momentarily. However, venting also has an important cognitive aspect.

When we express ourselves, we make public a certain account of a difficult situation. Typically, we recite the history of that situation, our (relatively innocent) role in unfolding events, and the incompetence or malevolence of other parties. External factors, such as government policies or blind chance, may be added to the story.

Proclaiming this view of things is our attempt to characterize the “reality” we’ve lived through. We want listeners to support that viewpoint. More importantly, we want to convince ourselves of our own judgment and behavior.

Oddly perhaps, we may acquire elevated status by being a complainer, crank or troll. In our grandest visions of ourselves, we want to be known as someone who tells the truth about situations and who helps others find courage to express similar concerns.

The social media site I consulted for commiseration about my team’s loss is organized in this fashion. People post texts, images, links, and so forth regarding the topic at hand. Readers then “vote” this content up or down, effectively increasing the prominence of the post and its creator. Over time, the site calculates user “karma,” essentially a reputability score reflecting the commentator’s ability to attract readers and incite reactions. People (or their personas) establish themselves as community leaders, provocateurs, and wise old heads.

The real world also has its place for doomsayers, rebels, and cranks. As I described in another post, people become known for their “signature” complaints and aversions, that is, things they can be depended upon to avoid or reprove. They take pride in being the ones who hate Parmesan cheese, New York sports teams, or politicians of a certain party. Any success by those elements is sure to set them off. Unsurprisingly, we may find pleasure in “winding them up.”

Be clear that people respond to frustration in different ways. For some, complaining is strategic, a form of work. That is, the malcontents want to see results from their activity, such as a refund check or letter of apology. To return to my opening example, the general manager of my losing team was indeed fired the next day. Complaining can also be play. Many posters are intentionally provocative. They want to stir people up, challenge their comments, and watch the ensuing dialogue.

For others, complaint is an ongoing commitment, a ritual that revisits the same issues and takes the same form. Such people effectively tell the listener: “Don’t think that I will stop. This is who I am.” Finally, complaint can be a form of communion. Expressing frustration is a way of soliciting support. Commenters want to know who else is feeling this way and whether they will unite in protest.

In every case, venting is not some uncoordinated lapse in personal control. It's a calculated attempt to communicate with those who share a common predicament.

Physical activity may help support mood and improve quality of life during the menopause transition, according to a study published on October 13 in Menopause, the journal of the Menopause Society.

This review of 12 clinical trials involving over 1,000 people ages 40 to 65 found that both aerobic and mind-body exercises may be associated with reduced depressive symptoms and improved well-being.

It is common for individuals to experience psychological symptoms during menopause, including depression, anxiety, low mood, stress, and difficulty with thinking and memory. These symptoms can have a significant effect on quality of life, with an estimated 1 in 10 women leaving work due to menopause symptoms.

This study found that regular physical activity may help improve certain psychological symptoms and overall quality of life during menopause.

The types of physical activity in the review were broken into two categories: aerobic-based exercise (ABIs) and mind-body activities (MBAs).

The researchers found that ABIs showed the strongest effects on reducing depressive symptoms and improving overall quality of life. This may be particularly relevant during perimenopause when women may be more vulnerable to mood instability due to estrogen fluctuations.

Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB/GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, pointed out that there are various ways that ABIs can have positive effects.

Ross explained that aerobic exercise may help improve menopausal symptoms through several mechanisms, including the release of endorphins, reduced inflammation, improved cholesterol levels, and better regulation of body temperature and sleep cycles.

MBAs were shown to have a greater effect on improving psychological quality of life. These exercises involve mindfulness, body awareness, and breathwork. They may help improve emotional regulation.

ABIs tended to require fewer sessions. This may mean these types of exercises are easier to fit in, especially if you are facing time or energy constraints. For example, in one trial, participants were instructed to walk for 30 minutes, three times per week. In another trial, participants attended three hour-long Zumba classes per week.

In contrast, one trial using MBAs instructed participants to attend two 75-minute yoga classes each week, and another had participants attend daily one-hour yoga classes.

Stephanie Faubion, MD, Director for the Mayo Clinic Center for Women’s Health and Medical Director for The Menopause Society, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that these findings are encouraging.

However, she also cautioned that this was a relatively small body of evidence. “While physical activity is an important component of a healthy lifestyle during midlife, I would be cautious about interpreting these findings to mean that exercise is a treatment for depression related specifically to menopause,” she said.

Research has shown that physical activity can positively affect mental health. Studies have also found that regular exercise can help improve symptoms of depression and anxiety.

Faubion told Healthline that, broadly speaking, regular physical activity may support mood through multiple pathways, including better sleep, improved physical function, and overall well-being.

However, she also noted that this study cannot tell us precisely why the improvements occurred or whether aerobic exercise is superior to other forms of activity.

Faubion said that recommending physical activity during and after menopause extends well beyond mood.

“Physical activity is important for cardiovascular and metabolic health, maintaining muscle mass and physical function, and supporting bone health,” she said.

“These become particularly important at midlife as women experience age- and menopause-related changes in body composition, bone, and cardiometabolic health,” Faubion added.

Ross agreed that physical activity is important for more than just mood and mental health improvement. She also noted that there are other types of exercise beyond ABIs and MBAs that can benefit people during menopause:

“These types of activities help with weight loss or allow women to maintain a healthy weight. If you are not ready for aerobic exercises, stretching and simple exercises help with balance and stability,” Ross said.

Faubion told Healthline that physical activity is an important part of healthy aging and should be encouraged during the menopause transition, but it should not be suggested that women can simply exercise their way out of significant depression or other bothersome menopause symptoms.

“Women experiencing persistent depression, anxiety, or symptoms that interfere with their quality of life should seek evaluation and discuss the full range of evidence-based treatment options with their healthcare clinician,” Faubion said.

HAWTHORNE, Calif. — A moon buggy carrying a history-making supercomputer is ready to launch to the lunar surface. And it's taking some inspiration from Mars.

#3954. Distant space ‘hamburger’ hides a baby gas giant

Published Oct 21, 2026 12:04 PM EDT

A cosmic Quarter-Pounder is providing us with a near-perfect view of a distant gas giant’s earliest moments. Known as Gomez’s Hamburger (or GoHam for short), the massive protoplanetary disk surrounds a young star located in the Scorpius-Centaurus stellar region of our home galaxy about 453 light-years away from Earth. As researchers explained in a study recently published in The Astrophysical Journal, GoHam’s vantage from Earth is virtually unparalleled for anyone hoping to glimpse the subtle layers of dust and particles that will one day coalesce into a humongous gas giant.

Early observers first spotted GoHam in 1985, but initially pegged it for a planetary nebula as far as 6,500 light-years from Earth. Astronomers have since revised their assessments with data gathered by the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) in northern Chile. As its full name implies, ALMA measures millimeter-and-smaller wavelengths, allowing scientists to map the miniscule dust grains and gaseous molecules that compose distant objects. GoHam is a particularly unique subject for ALMA, because its position makes it a new benchmark example for testing models of how planets evolve.

“GoHam gives us a rare and clear view of the vertical and radial structure of a very large, nearly edge‑on disk,” explained study co-author and University of Minnesota astronomer Charles Law.

According to Law, its mixture of cosmic winds, large formational asymmetries, massive disk size, as well as the potential for planetary formation makes GoHam a “perfect laboratory.” With ALMA, Law and his colleagues analyzed GoHam’s layers of carbon monoxide, and sulfur-bearing molecules. As predicted, the lightest gas is in the highest layer above the formation’s midplane, while the heavier sulfur components lay beneath it. Meanwhile, a thin but concentrated section of millimeter dust is visible in the midplane itself.

Comparisons to GoHam’s fast food namesake only go so far, however. Its dust clouds lean north–south, with one side containing brighter and wider emissions. This likely stems from a disturbance such as a cosmic vortex, which may capture additional material for planetary formation. Dim, thin carbon monoxide emissions along the disk’s northern section also suggest GoHam’s central star is blowing gases into deep space as what’s known as photoevaporative wind.

Astronomers also noted that GoHam is one of the largest protoplanetary disks ever observed. At nearly 93 billion miles wide and with an exponentially larger dust mass than the typical protoplanet disk, GoHam may ultimately host not just one gas giant, but an entire planetary system.

#3953. "We are here to give you something to believe in": the former Disco Elysium devs of Summer Eternal reveal new RPG Red Rooster, a game of fire, water and alternate selves

But there's a long way to go till completion

Earlier this month, the former Disco Elysium developers of worker co-operative studio Summer Eternal sent me a 500-page anthology describing their new game Red Rooster. It's a 3D, real-time, third-person RPG you could broadly style as a spiritual Disco successor, working in the tradition of Planescape: Torment and Vampire: The Masquerade - Bloodlines, but which also owes much inspiration to Baldur's Gate 3 and Dark Messiah of Might and Magic. It takes place in a magic realist, "our-world-adjacent", mightily flammable and divided river city called Arta-Tantor, created with Unreal Engine and housing a "physics-powered sandbox of objects you can individually move, manipulate – and burn."

Here, you will live out the story of Redrick Roost, a convict serving a life sentence after accidentally rendering thousands of people catatonic by means of an "auto-meditation" headset. Much like Disco's Harry, Redrick has a whole world of characters in his head – a hallucinatory vacuum shop or "chicken coop" full of alternate selves or "roosters", who vie for dominance as the game goes on, affecting your dialogue options and abilities. Redrick's broad goal, having been recruited by government spooks, is to identify the arsonist working at a fire station run by his own hero brother. You've got a week to pull this off before your parole hearing. Your colleagues include Dr Astrid Albers, a mathematician with an opiate problem who wakes up one day to find that mathematics has Gone Wrong.

Much like that last paragraph, the Summer Eternal book is a dense, knotty, wild-eyed thing. It's divided into two parts. The first is an account of Summer Eternal's formation that aims to situate the new studio within (and often, against) today's videogame industry and culture. There are essays on aesthetics, generative AI and the difficulty of pursuing anti-capitalist game development while being obliged to compromise a little for the sake of funding. There are open-ended interviews with several well-known game developers, including former BioWare devs Mark Darrah and David Gaider, together with sci-fi novelist Ted Chiang, Marxist economist Richard Wolff, and union organisers at United Videogame Workers-CWA. Also, a chat with those scruffy apparatchiks at indie journalist outfit Aftermath... and an acknowledgements page in which Summer Eternal thank myself and Mark Warren for our previous online coverage of the studio's founding. Look, we hacks have got to get some limelight now and then.

The book is further evidence that Summer Eternal are the most Marxist of the post-Disco studios. Put it this way: there is a double-page spread in which the eponymous red rooster is pictured striking down the vulture of venture capitalism, while studio founder and former ZA/UM narrative designer/principal writer Dora Klindžić decries "bourgeois virgins who administrate their battles from office desks".

At the risk of being branded a bourgeois virgin, I do think that Marxist writers can always benefit from some tactical self-deprecation – Disco Elysium was very good at seeing the funny side, and still had teeth as a political document. Still, this is a manifesto for a studio, not a piece of fiction, and I genuinely got a bit tearful about Klindžić's visions for a studio run for the benefit of workers and artists, in a universe that is being strangled by finance and cannibalised by LLMs. "We are preparing a conflagration, an end to mourning, an end to the poison fog of demoralization and the apathy tears of the mentally imprisoned," she writes. "With charred fingers, we are here to give you something to hope for. We are here to give you something to believe in."

More specifically, they are here to give us an RPG. The second half of the book is a massive pitch document and lore bible for Red Rooster, a game that is in the "early pre-production phase", with 3-4 years to go before release... providing Summer Eternal can pull together the funding. Obviously, the funding situation is a concern: I'd like to write more about the studio's structure, which divides shares between creatives and investors with a small portion set aside for fans. In the meantime, I'll say that Red Rooster sounds fascinating.

The section on Red Rooster opens with a lengthy fictional university essay about the history of Arta-Tantor, which is sort of three cities or "member-territories" nested close together - the result of centuries of war, rivalry and overseas exploitation with a broad theme of fire vs water. The city's factions include groups of heavily armed game wardens and couriers, together with a long-suffering fire service that originally consisted of slaves, and is now a mix of professionals, amateurs, and prisoners doing community service. Another, redacted in-world memorandum hints at a psionic organisation of some kind.

And then there's the cast. The book tells you a lot about Redrick and Astrid, including some details I'd rather you discover for yourself. Redrick's history is complicated, to say the least. His parents died in a house fire. At the age of 12, he was seduced by a school science teacher, who was later imprisoned not for statutory rape but commandeering a postal vehicle. As an adult, he fell in with the inventor of the aforesaid "auto-meditation" device, who capitalised on the infamy created by his "affair" for marketing purposes.

When the Baku headset malfunctioned, inducing "mass mental fracturing", it also gave rise to the Coop, Redrick's eerie inner sanctum, which is modelled after the vacuum shop he once worked in. Here, you'll spend XP to modify your build, while interacting with various Rooster-selves, who may form factions as they seek to dominate one another and shape Redrick's overall persona. Possible Roosters include a "Rooster-Raconteur" who can't stop debating people, a pathological gambler, and an "Overkompensaaator" who wears sunglasses indoors. More Roosters will appear as the game goes on, and you'll get to decide whether to admit, strengthen, and repress them to alter Redrick's stats and dialogue options, similar to the Copotypes in Disco Elysium. "Repression" is an actual dice-driven encounter, with several potential outcomes besides victory and failure. It's also possible for repressed or rejected Roosters to push through and hijack the Coop, if you continue to behave in a way that leans into their archetype.

Redrick's initial companion character Astrid, a maths prodigy working at a local research institute, isn't quite as messy, but she isn't exactly Miles Tails Prower either. She teams up with him after being caught overdosing on opiates and forced into a state-supervised rehabilitation program. Her job is to keep an eye on Redrick while he searches for the aforesaid arsonist. In the book, Astrid is defined by her claustrophobic relationship with her overbearing novelist father, her tendency to lie, and her realisation, one day, that the laws of mathematics have somehow altered, right down to the bones of the prime number system – an event that might have something to do with an on-going heatwave.

There is much to say about Astrid, but all I'll add for the moment is that her visual inspirations include Patti Smith and Gollum. She has stage presence for lightyears, while Redrick is characterised as earnest and apologetic and not-quite-there. Redrick can bolster his look using a rhizomatic clothing system where you set up fashion synergies on a grid, rather than the usual equipment screen in which clothing is categorised by body part.

If these characters are absolutely the kind of people you'd expect to meet in Disco Elysium, Red Rooster cleaves from that inspiration with its unhealthy interest in physics. There's a chapter in the book where game director Argo Tuulik frames the game's manipulable object world as a key point of differentiation: "this visceral connection, developed through a myriad of small interactions that form the all-encompassing connective tissue, is the holy grail. The missing physical counterpart to the ultra-reactive psychological role-play. The logical next step in the RPG genre. It is the most important pillar of Red Rooster."

The game will, amongst other things, allow you to lob and break objects with different weight, fragility and elasticity, from chairs to bike tyres. A "simplified but realistic real-time fire simulation" plays out Redrick's torturous relationship with fire, spreading between surfaces according to material properties: by the sounds of things, acts of arson are how you'll pursue particular subplots and revelations, though not everything in the world can burn.

The world is also subject to change and entropy, independent of your activities: lightbulbs will burn out, falling leaves will cover staircases, trash bags will be emptied, local sculptors will slowly turn hunks of stone into statues. These unfolding phenomena sit within a web of 'terrain factors' imposed by Arta-Trantor's weird tripartite political culture, with a "topologically complex map where international borders criss-cross the streets, bisect buildings and bedroms, and create enclaves and counter-enclaves within the same neighbourhoods".

The devs say you'll move across a multitude of "soft borders" as you explore, which creates bizarre possibilities and constraints. You might cross a street to move into a different jurisdiction and thereby throw off a cop. You might cross a bar when the clock strikes 10pm to carry on drinking in a territory where it's legal to sell booze until 2am. Or you might have to make use of a window to get around a revised border policy, rather than opening the front door.

I'll end by stressing that Summer Eternal is still far more pitch for a game than a game. Much of the above may change, and the developers make no secret of the fact that they're still raising funds. But they've certainly sketched out some wonderful, macabre territory. I'm especially keen on all the hypnotic rhetoric around fire, as somebody who's written a bit here and there about procedural fire systems in videogames. I'll likely be diving back into the book for more stories. In the meantime, you can order a copy yourself via the official website.

#3952. ACOG Strongly Recommends Hormone Therapy for Menopause Symptoms

  • The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) released expanded menopause management guidelines strongly recommending menopause hormone therapy (MHT) for vasomotor symptoms (VMS) and related sleep disturbances in eligible individuals.
  • For individuals who can’t or don’t want to use MHT, ACOG strongly recommends nonhormonal options, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and neurokinin receptor antagonists, to manage VMS.
  • The guidelines also conditionally recommend nonpharmacologic approaches like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness-based interventions, regular exercise, clinical hypnosis, and acupuncture, typically used alongside medications to manage menopause symptoms.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) published updated recommendations for menopause management in Obstetrics & Gynecology on October 15. 

These expanded recommendations are the most comprehensive guidance to date. They include new clinical guidelines for menopause hormone therapy (MHT), nonhormonal medications, and nonpharmacological treatment of vasomotor symptoms (VMS), which include hot flashes and night sweats, and sleep and mood changes. 

According to the ACOG guidance, 50%–92% of females experiencing menopause report VMS. 

Around 16%–66% of females in the United States report menopause-related sleep issues, and more than one-third of perimenopausal and postmenopausal females report experiencing depressive symptoms. 

“In recent years, misconceptions about the risks of hormone therapy have been widespread, which has made some clinicians reluctant to prescribe it and some patients hesitant to use it,” Bruce Pier, MD, clinical professor and associate fellowship director at the University of North Carolina and collaborator on the ACOG clinical practice guidelines, told Healthline.   

“This guidance offers a comprehensive review of the evidence to support individualized counseling and shared decision making. Our hope is that it clears up those misconceptions and expands access to treatment.”

The ACOG is also developing companion guidance for genitourinary syndrome of menopause, expected in 2027. 

ACOG hormone therapy recommendations for VMS and sleep

The ACOG strongly recommends the use of MHT with estrogen and progestogens (or estrogen alone for people without a uterus) for both VMS and vasomotor-related sleep disturbance, in individuals who can use hormones. 

It also conditionally recommends conjugated estrogen plus bazedoxifene (Duavee) as an alternative for these issues. 

According to a meta-analysis from 2021 that was cited by the guidelines, estrogen therapy, with or without progestogens, significantly reduced VMS frequency and severity compared to a placebo or no treatment. A 2017 meta-analysis ranked transdermal estradiol plus a progestogen as the most effective option for reducing VMS frequency. 

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis, also cited by the guidelines, found that MHT was associated with improved self-reported sleep quality. Once again, transdermal therapies showed some of the largest effects. 

ACOG recommendations for starting MHT

The ACOG recommends initiating MHT before age 60 or within 10 years of the onset of natural menopause.. 

It also suggests that the decision to start MHT for VMS and vasomotor-related sleep disturbances should be based on a shared decision making discussion about an individual’s symptoms and the benefits and risks of therapy. Consideration should also be taken regarding an individual’s: 

  • age 
  • medical history
  • baseline risks of cardiovascular disease, breast cancer, and venous thromboembolism (VTE)
  • individualized treatment priorities and goals

Inequities in menopause care

Research shows that factors such as race, culture, socioeconomic status, gender identity, and sexual orientation can influence how individuals experience menopause and the care they receive.

According to the SWAN study, Black women reported more menopause symptoms than white women but were less likely to be treated for them. 

“This updated document shines a light on these inequities. Clinicians can proactively help right now by asking every patient in midlife about symptoms, rather than waiting for them to bring it up. Long term, we hope this document drives more research funding to understand these gaps and begin closing them,” said Pier. 

ACOG MHT recommendations for depressive symptoms

The ACOG stated in the guidelines that, “The available evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against the use of estrogen–progestogen or estrogen-only MHT for the treatment of menopause-related depressive symptoms.”

It recommends that perimenopausal and postmenopausal individuals who meet the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder (MDD) or those whose most noticeable symptom is depression should be treated according to depression management guidelines. 

Current treatment guidelines for depression include therapy, medication, or a combination of the two.

Psychological treatments for depression include psychotherapy, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), while antidepressants are among the most commonly prescribed medications.

ACOG recommendations for nonhormonal treatments

The ACOG strongly recommends the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) for the management of menopausal symptoms like VMS and depression in individuals who don’t want to use MHT or can’t use it. 

Currently, paroxetine is the only antidepressant that is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the management of VMS. 

The ACOG also strongly recommends the use of neurokinin receptor antagonists to manage VMS and vasomotor-related sleep disturbances in people who don’t desire hormone therapy or can’t use it. 

There are currently two neurokinin receptor antagonists approved by the FDA to manage menopause symptoms. Fezolinetant (Veozah) was approved in 2023 to help manage hot flashes. In 2025, the FDA approved elinzanetant (LYNKUET) for the management of moderate to severe VMS due to menopause. 

Another nonhormonal treatment for VMS that the ACOG conditionally recommends is gabapentin. 

ACOG recommendations for nonpharmacologic treatments

There are various nonpharmacologic treatments that the ACOG conditionally recommends for the management of VMS, vasomotor-related sleep disturbances, and mood changes. These are treatments that don’t involve the use of medications. 

The nonpharmacologic treatments conditionally recommended include: 

  • CBT
  • mindfulness-based interventions
  • regular exercise
  • clinical hypnosis
  • acupuncture 

It is generally recommended that these interventions are used alongside hormonal or nonhormonal medications. 

The ACOG noted that there is not enough evidence to support the efficacy or safety of phytoestrogens, such as soy isoflavones, or herbal supplements, which are not regulated by the FDA in the same way medications are for safety, purity, or efficacy. 

“Menopause is finally getting the attention it deserves, and this document shows what evidence-based care should look like in this space. It lays out a range of safe, effective options for managing menopause symptoms. My message to anyone struggling with these symptoms is simple: talk to your clinician. You don’t have to suffer through them,” said Pier.
