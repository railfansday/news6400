#4700. Google Cloud introduces Agent Compute Engine to host AI agents

Google LLC’s cloud unit today introduced Agent Compute Engine, an infrastructure service optimized to host artificial intelligence agents.

AI agents run in technology environments called sandboxes. They host the applications, data and user instructions that agents need to carry out their work. Sandboxes also contain cybersecurity guardrails that block malicious activity.

Google refers to Agent Compute Engine’s implementation of sandboxes as microVMs. The company says that the technology combines the hardware efficiency of containers with the security of traditional virtualization. In particular, Google brought over hypervisors’ ability to isolate virtual machines from one another to prevent the spread of malware.

The company runs microVMs on specially configured appliances. The appliances are stateless, which means that they don’t permanently retain the files used by customers’ agents. The data that the systems do store is kept in an immutable file system that can’t be modified or deleted.

Google verifies the integrity of the appliances’ firmware using its custom Titan cybersecurity chips. The appliances’ central processing units, in turn, feature mitigations against side-channel attacks. Those are cyberattacks that analyze data about a CPU’s physical state to glean the customer information it processes.

The microVMs that run on an Agent Compute Engine appliance have only limited access to its storage resources. As a result, hackers can’t read or delete data if they take over one of the sandboxes. Another set of guardrails protects the network that connects the appliance to the rest of the host data center. Traffic from microVMs must go through multiple proxies before it can proceed to its destination.

“All inbound and outbound traffic must traverse an isolated, point-to-point IPC boundary,” Google Cloud distinguished engineers Ramanathan Venkatapathy and Rohit Jnagal explained in a blog post. “This boundary terminates at a local, unprivileged mediation proxy, which forwards packets to an external proxy. The external gateway then validates request scopes, and enforces strict, policy-driven outbound brokering.”

AI applications often pause the agents they use to automate tasks for users. When an agent shuts down, the sandbox that hosts it must remain available because the agent may be reactivated later. Such idle sandboxes can consume a significant amount of hardware resources, which increases costs.

Google says Agent Compute Engine detects when a sandbox goes idle and shuts it down. That prevents unnecessary hardware usage. When the sandbox is needed, the service quickly reactivates it.

“This lets a single physical node host orders of magnitude more living agent microVMs than conventional VMs,” Venkatapathy and Jnagal wrote. “We couple this with features integral to agents like cloning, snapshot/forking, rewind and rollback that allow the harnesses to use the system efficiently without resource duplication.”

Some agents use login credentials to access the applications with which they perform their work. According to Google, Agent Compute Engine stores those credentials outside the sandbox that hosts an agent. That means hackers can’t steal any sensitive data if they take over the sandbox. When an agent needs to log into an application, a Google Cloud service called Agent Gateway retrieves the relevant credentials and submit them on the agent’s behalf.

Google will roll out Agent Compute Engine in the coming months alongside a new version of Agent Gateway. The latter service, which helps customers block insecure agent traffic, is set to gain a model routing capability. The feature will enable it to send each agent request to the model that can process it in the most efficient manner. Agent Gateway is also gaining a tool for regulating agents’ inference costs.

“Token management provides control over token consumption across agents and models, allowing you to attribute spend, enforce budget guardrails, and provide a single source of truth for token metrics,” Google Cloud vice presidents Nirav Mehta, Michael Bachman and Kevin Hutchins wrote in a blog post.

#4698. Palmer Luckey teams with dire wolf billionaire Ben Lamm to launch $200 million Warhound, unleashing genetically engineered clone dogs of war

The Colossal and Anduril founders want to clone, gene-edit and armor America’s working dogs, and bring the supply chain home from Europe.

The U.S. military’s oldest autonomous system has a supply-chain problem: most of its dogs come from Europe. Fewer than half make it through training. And the Pentagon is bidding against foreign governments for the ones that are left.

Ben Lamm is the co-founder and CEO of Colossal Biosciences, the Dallas de-extinction company that announced in April 2025 it had brought back the dire wolf, a predator that died out more than 10,000 years ago. What Colossal actually produced was three pups, Romulus, Remus and Khaleesi. Its scientists took gray wolf cells, edited about 20 sites across 14 genes to match DNA recovered from ancient dire wolf remains, cloned the pups from those edited cells and had domestic dogs carry them to term.

Lamm and Anduril founder Palmer Luckey think the same tools, plus defense hardware, can fix that. Warhound, a defense tech startup the two came up with, is coming out of stealth with a $200 million seed round, Fortune can exclusively report. David Spirk, a former Marine and the Pentagon’s former chief data officer, is CEO. The company is based in Texas.

The backers are In-Q-Tel, Sequoia Capital, ARK Invest, Franklin Templeton, 8VC, USIT, Bob Nelsen, Breyer Capital, CAZ Investments, Neogenesis, Builders VC and Lux Capital, among others.

“A dog is the oldest autonomous system there is,” Lamm said in emailed responses to Fortune’s questions. “For thousands of years, dogs have gone into danger ahead of the people they protect.”

The Army set up the K-9 Corps in 1942. Today the Department of War’s Military Working Dog Program has more than 2,200 dog teams, according to the company, and more than 80% of U.S. military working dogs are bought from Europe. Lamm cited a Senate Armed Services Committee warning that “due to the finite number of breeders overseas, as well as rising market demand, the cost… to procure whelped military working dogs from Europe is skyrocketing.” He also cited the American Kennel Club’s assessment that “there is now a shortage of even mediocre quality foreign dogs available.”

The U.S. is “competing against other country governments” for the same animals, Lamm said, China and Russia among them.

The losses after purchase are expensive, too. According to the company, fewer than half of the dogs bought are judged fit for service after a roughly three-year acquisition and training pipeline. That means the U.S. has to buy and train about 200 dogs today to field 100 working animals later.

Lamm described the plan in stages. Warhound will find top dogs that have finished training and biobank their genetic material. It will then clone those proven dogs, though Lamm said “training and environment will always influence performance.” Alongside that, it will work on targeted gene edits.

The first group of dogs has already been biobanked and is being edited now, Lamm said. He expects to deliver the first dogs in about two years. “Breeding and raising a dog takes the time it takes,” he said.

Warhound will screen out known harmful variants tied to muscle degeneration and other inherited diseases, so that “no dog enters service carrying genetic markers known to cause common career-ending conditions.” Lamm said the company is still working out which other traits are “scientifically and responsibly actionable.” Even so, he said Warhound is “planning future genetic enhancements to improve their already exceptional performance.”

Anduril is supplying equipment including eye and ear protection, body armor, and integration with its Eagle Eye system, so that “dogs, handlers, and autonomous systems operate as one team.” The release announcing Warhound calls these “cybernetic Augments.” Lamm said the dogs are not a step toward robot dogs, nor a hedge against them. “No machine today matches a dog’s nose or its judgment in complex environments,” he said.

Warhound has no signed customers yet, as Lamm said the company is working with canine experts across the national security community. It also isn’t quoting a price per dog, because “a young dog, a partially trained dog, and a cloned dog of proven health with training and support through retirement are very different things.”

Lamm said Warhound is discussing a “Canine-as-a-Service model,” in which the government would buy a steady supply of trained dogs instead of individual animals. Software companies have sold to the Pentagon by subscription for years, and now the model is coming to the kennel.

Cloning working dogs isn’t new. South Korea’s customs service deployed six cloned sniffer dogs in 2009. A decade later, Chinese scientists said they had cloned a top police dog, a Kunming wolfdog named Kunxun, to cut training time, and talked about producing more. By that November, six cloned K-9s had joined the Beijing police.

Nothing at the scale needed exists yet, Lamm said, so the company plans to build national-scale kennel and training infrastructure step by step, “starting with private capital.” It has hired what Lamm called “one of the top dog handlers ever to serve in the U.S. military” as director of canine operations. The American Humane Society will certify Warhound’s training programs and help reunite retired dogs with their former handlers.

The company says Lamm and Luckey came up with Warhound after a conversation at Peter Diamandis’ Moonshots Summit. Lamm said Luckey “loved our dire wolf announcement,” and the two got talking about the military’s working dog programs: where the dogs come from, how many wash out and what it costs to replace them.

“I’m all about origin stories, especially when we’re talking about the Captain America dog program,” Lamm said. In the comics, the super-soldier program took a scrawny Army reject and made him the best soldier in the service. Warhound is aiming at the dogs who don’t make the cut.

Then the two founders mostly stepped back. “Palmer and I don’t describe ourselves as co-founders,” Lamm said. “We developed the concept.” Luckey “doesn’t even own equity in the business,” he said. “I decided to be the one of us to join the board and tap Dave Spirk to do this,” Lamm added, saying he knew him well, as Spirk had spent four years as an adviser to Colossal and had watched the de-extinction work up close.

Spirk spent eight years in the Marine Corps and close to 20 more as a government civilian in the intelligence community and special operations. He was the first chief data officer of U.S. Special Operations Command and then of the Department of War, and he was a plank holder on Project Maven, the Pentagon’s AI targeting program. He also served as deputy associate director of national intelligence for Afghanistan and Pakistan. More recently he was a senior counselor at Palantir, a stint Lamm said Spirk calls his “Palantir diploma.” For four years he advised Lamm at Colossal and watched the de-extinction work up close.

“My focus is building a team that can turn scientific advances into capabilities that work in the field,” Spirk said in a statement.

The dire wolf launch drew some criticism from scientists who said Colossal had made gene-edited gray wolves and not a revived species. Lamm dismissed that criticism, while noting that doesn’t come up here: “Our warhounds are genetically modified dogs just like our dire wolves were genetically modified wolves. I assume there is no way for anyone to debate the definition of a dog.”

Lamm rejected the idea of forming some kind of combined Colossal-Anduril company. “LOL no there is no Colossal – Anduril conglomerate,” he said. Colossal licenses technology to Warhound and has an economic interest in it. Spinning companies out of Colossal’s research is “an intentional part of our model,” Lamm said.

Luckey offered a statement in the release: “Man’s best friend deserves man’s best tech.”

Sky's second token burn removed 7.37 million SKY from circulation, yet opening the corresponding Etherscan transaction reveals 31 separate transfers and no obvious burn label.

When you open the transaction behind Sky's second burn, you will not find a line explicitly marked burn. Instead, the top-level recipient field points to an unlabelled contract, the function call reads cast(), and the page logs 31 individual token transfers. Nevertheless, only one of those transfers represents the actual burn event.

Sky reported that 7,372,288 SKY tokens were permanently removed from circulation. That figure represents roughly 2.6 times the magnitude of the protocol's first burn of 2.86 million SKY, funded via 5% of the monthly Net Protocol Surplus. To verify whether the blockchain records match the public announcement, inspecting the transaction line by line clarifies the full execution process.

While most token burns involve a single direct transfer to a dead address, Sky processes its burns through governance via an executive spell. This mechanism bundles multiple approved protocol actions into a single atomic transaction.

As a result, the transaction log appears crowded. Alongside the primary SKY movement, the transaction mints DAI and USDS, burns DAI, and distributes funds to multiple external recipients including Grove. Expecting a single isolated row can make the page look incorrect, but all bundled operations belong to the same governance execution.

Because a failed transaction burns zero tokens, verification always begins with the execution status. The transaction displays a status of Success, recording an execution timestamp of October 12, 2026, at 14:01:47 UTC.

Tracking the precise timestamp is essential. While Sky announced the burn on October 14, the transaction executed on-chain two days earlier on October 12. Consequently, citing the announcement date instead of the execution timestamp introduces an accurate timeline error.

The recipient address listed in the top-level field is 0x4bdD1Cc8540E4c43ee210a6ff0EE96ad0Bba297A. This address corresponds to neither the official SKY token contract nor a standard dead burn address. Instead, it identifies the spell contract itself.

This structure mirrors the pattern seen in multisig and treasury burns, where the top-level field displays the contract being called rather than the ultimate destination of the tokens. In short, the initiating field identifies the caller, whereas the lower token transfer section records the final destination of the assets.

Examining the spell contract's page directly provides vital execution context. The contract is officially named DssSpell with verified source code, having been created on October 8, 2026, at 13:15 UTC. Its historical log shows two distinct interactions consisting of a Schedule call followed by a Cast call.

This sequence reflects standard Sky governance mechanics. Spells are first deployed and scheduled, after which a mandatory delay passes before the community executes the Cast function. Furthermore, this means the burn parameters were locked on-chain four days prior to execution and six days before the official public announcement.

The underlying details sit within the ERC-20 Tokens Transferred section. The 31 transfer entries repeat in pairs, logging DAI minting and burning alongside USDS minting to recipients in figures such as 4,218,121, 1,148,408, and 2,927,190.

These secondary movements are separate protocol operations bundled inside the same governance spell. Therefore, filtering out unrelated activity requires isolating the specific token contract for SKY and bypassing the surrounding stablecoin transfers.

Isolating the SKY asset movement highlights two specific transfers. The primary line records 7,372,288 SKY moving from the Sky MCD Pause Proxy contract directly to the zero address.

Two specific factors confirm the validity of the burn. First, the sender is the Pause Proxy through which governance spells execute, establishing authorized origin. Second, the zero address possesses no private key, rendering the tokens irrecoverable. In addition, the transferred 7,372,288 SKY rounds precisely to the reported 7.37 million figure.

A secondary SKY transfer within the same transaction requires careful distinction. Approximately 1,933,534 SKY leaves the Pause Proxy contract but routes toward an active protocol address rather than the zero address.

That secondary transfer is not part of the burn. Adding that figure to the burned total would yield roughly 9.3 million SKY, producing an inaccurate headline metric. By rule, only tokens transferred directly to the zero address represent true permanent burns.

Comparing the verified transfer against the first burn figure of 2.86 million SKY confirms a 2.58 times increase, validating the reported 2.6 times scaling metric.

Regarding overall supply impact, the main SKY token page recorded a total supply near 23.46 billion SKY prior to execution. The 7.37 million token burn represents approximately 0.03% of total circulating supply. Evaluated at prevailing market prices near $0.076 listed on DefiLlama, the destroyed tokens carried a monetary value of roughly $560,000.

While a zero-address transfer indicates token destruction, checking the token contract's total supply parameter provides final verification. Ideally, the contract total supply should drop by the precise amount sent to the null address.

Minor discrepancies across external tracking sites typically stem from delayed indexing intervals rather than ledger mismatches. Meanwhile, verifying the underlying contract address ensures analysis remains focused on the authentic token contract.

On-chain explorer logs prove that the transaction completed, but they do not automatically calculate the protocol's financial metrics. The stated funding source of 5% of Net Protocol Surplus relies on internal accounting inputs not explicitly printed in the transaction payload.

Because surplus calculations depend on broader governance reporting, cross-referencing protocol revenue dashboards provides necessary context. For example, DefiLlama tracking reflects active burn mechanisms alongside quarterly buyback records.

That’s why defensibility should start at the idea stage. Your moat might be a brand, intellectual property, distribution, a niche you dominate or operating systems that took years to perfect. The strongest businesses often combine several.

Being first can give you a head start, but what matters is what you do with it. If a well-funded competitor launched against you tomorrow, what would they have the hardest time copying?

If you struggle to answer, you’ve identified an important weakness.

One company I’ve been involved with started as Treat-a-Dog. In 2018, we acquired Paw.com and reinvented the company around that brand. Buying the domain was expensive and time-consuming, but in the year after the rebrand, the business more than doubled in sales and profitability.

#4688. Early signs of macular degeneration, and when to see an eye doctor

Age-related macular degeneration often starts quietly. In its early stage, the condition frequently causes no symptoms at all, and a person can carry it for years with vision that seems normal. When changes do appear, they tend to be small: a bit of haze while reading, a door frame that looks slightly crooked, a need for a brighter lamp. Many people put these down to getting older and move on.

That habit can delay care. The National Eye Institute identifies AMD $AMD as a leading cause of vision loss among adults age 50 and up in the U.S. The disease targets the macula, a small area at the center of the retina responsible for the sharp detail people rely on when they look straight at something. Peripheral vision usually survives. As a result, AMD seldom leads to complete blindness. The damage falls in the middle of the visual field instead, the part used for reading, driving and recognizing faces.

AMD comes in two forms. Dry AMD is the more common type and moves slowly. Wet AMD, sometimes called neovascular AMD, develops when abnormal blood vessels form beneath the retina and leak fluid or blood. It occurs less often, but it can cause severe central vision loss quickly. A dry case can also convert to the wet form. A dry AMD diagnosis therefore still calls for ongoing monitoring.

Knowing the early signs of macular degeneration gives people a way to tell an ordinary visit from an urgent one. Some signs are things a person notices, such as blur, distortion or a gap in the center of what they see. Others show up only in an eye doctor's chair, during a dilated exam or a retinal scan. A few checks can happen at home between appointments.

The 10 slides that follow cover a selection of these signs and steps, not every possible symptom. They describe what each change looks like, why it happens and what it means for timing. They also cover who faces higher risk and how often to book an exam. None of them replaces a professional diagnosis. Several of these changes overlap with other eye conditions, and only an exam can sort them out.

A soft, hazy patch in the middle of whatever you look at can be a sign of age-related macular degeneration (AMD $AMD). The edges of your sight may stay fairly sharp, and a new eyeglass prescription does not fully clear the blur.

The reason lies in where the damage happens. The macula is a small area at the center of the retina, the light-sensitive tissue lining the back of the eye. It handles the fine, straight-ahead detail needed to thread a needle or read the time on a wall clock. When AMD damages the macula, the central part of the image loses its crispness.

Glasses and contact lenses solve a different problem. Nearsightedness, farsightedness and astigmatism occur when the cornea and lens bend incoming light so that it lands slightly off target. A corrective lens shifts that light back into place. AMD involves the retina itself. Even perfectly focused light then falls on tissue that can no longer render detail clearly. A camera offers a rough comparison. Cleaning or swapping the lens will not fix a worn-out sensor.

In daily life, the blur tends to sit exactly where attention goes. A street sign straight ahead can look fuzzy even though parked cars to either side are easy to make out. A coffee mug in the middle of a table may look smeared while the plates around it stay crisp.

The change can begin in one eye. When that happens, the other eye can compensate, and the blur may go unnoticed until the stronger eye is covered by chance.

Cataracts can also make vision look cloudy. A cataract clouds the eye's natural lens and is treated with surgery. Its haze tends to spread across the whole view rather than gathering in the center. The two conditions can occur together. Only an eye exam can show which one is behind the blur.

Anyone whose updated glasses still leave the middle of their vision soft should mention it to an eye doctor and ask whether a dilated exam is needed.

Straight lines that look wavy, bent or warped can be an early sign of macular degeneration. Eye doctors call this distortion metamorphopsia. It is one of the classic symptoms of age-related macular degeneration, or AMD $AMD.

The effect is easiest to see on objects a person already knows are straight. The edge of a door frame may seem to bow in the middle. A line of printed text can look as if it ripples across the page. The grout between bathroom tiles may appear to kink or sag in one part of the pattern while the rest looks normal.

The distortion starts in the macula. This patch of retina sits at the back of the eye and supplies the sharp vision used to look directly at something. The light-sensing cells there sit in a tightly ordered layer. When something pushes that layer out of shape, the cells no longer line up the way the brain expects. The picture the brain builds from them comes out bent to match.

With AMD, that push often comes from fluid. In wet AMD, also called neovascular AMD, abnormal blood vessels form beneath the retina and seep fluid or blood into nearby tissue. That buildup can lift and wrinkle the macula. Metamorphopsia is linked especially closely to this process. Because wet AMD can cause rapid loss of central vision, new distortion is a warning sign worth acting on.

The bent area tends to stay in the same spot within a person's field of view. It travels with the gaze, so whatever sits directly in the center of sight is the thing that looks warped.

Distortion is easy to overlook because the two eyes work as a team. If only one eye is affected, the healthier eye can hide the problem during ordinary activities. Some people notice it for the first time only when they happen to close or cover one eye.

Any new waviness in straight lines is a reason to contact an eye doctor rather than wait for a routine appointment. Distortion has possible causes other than AMD. Only an eye exam can identify which one is responsible.

A gray, dark or blank patch right where you are looking can mean age-related macular degeneration is damaging the macula. Eye doctors call this a central scotoma, and it tends to grow larger as the disease advances.

The patch sits at the exact point a person is focusing on. It travels with the eyes wherever they move. Shift your gaze to a clock on the wall, and the spot lands on the clock face. Look at a doorway, and it covers the middle of the door. The area around the edges of the patch usually looks normal.

Not every scotoma looks like a solid black hole. Some people describe a smudge or a gray haze. Others see a gap where part of an object seems to vanish. Some notice only that a small detail disappears when they look straight at it. The brain fills in missing information using the surrounding image. A small spot can therefore go unnoticed for a long time.

The other eye can hide it too. With both eyes open, a healthy eye covers for a damaged one. A gap in one eye may only become obvious when that eye is tested on its own.

In the dry form of AMD $AMD, a blank area often traces back to geographic atrophy. This advanced stage involves patches where retinal tissue gradually wastes away. The light-sensing cells in those patches stop working. The matching section of central vision goes blank. The atrophic areas can expand over time, and the missing part of the visual field grows with them.

Size and speed both matter to an eye doctor. A spot that appears suddenly or gets bigger over a few days calls for a prompt appointment rather than a wait for the next routine visit. A slower change is still worth reporting. It gives a specialist a record of how the disease is moving.

People who live with a central blank area often learn to look slightly off to one side. That shift lets a healthier part of the retina pick up the image. Low-vision specialists teach this technique. They also recommend magnifiers and other aids that make daily tasks more manageable.

Difficulty reading text or identifying familiar faces can be an early sign of age-related macular degeneration, or AMD $AMD. Both tasks rely on the macula, the part of the retina that resolves fine, straight-ahead detail.

Reading asks a lot of central vision. The eye has to land on each word and pick out the small strokes, gaps and curves that separate one letter from the next. When the macula starts to fail, that detail breaks down. Some people notice letters vanishing from the middle of a word. Others see characters blur into one another, and a line of print becomes hard to follow.

Small type can turn into a struggle while larger print still reads clearly. A medicine label, a restaurant menu or the fine print on a bill may take more effort than a newspaper headline. People may reread sentences, lose their place on the page or hold material at an angle in search of a clearer view.

Faces create a related problem. Telling one person from another depends on subtle features around the eyes, nose and mouth. Those details sit right where central vision does its work. A person with AMD may look at a friend across a table and see a blurred face. The wall, the window and the room around that friend can still look sharp.

That mismatch follows from where the damage lands. AMD works on the middle of the visual field and tends to spare the edges. A person can still walk through a room or catch movement off to one side. Meanwhile, the face or page they look at directly loses definition.

Driving draws on the same central detail. Reading road signs, checking a dashboard and judging what lies ahead all require clear straight-ahead sight. Anyone who finds these tasks harder should raise it with an eye doctor instead of guessing at the cause.

Trouble reading or recognizing people has many possible explanations. Only an eye exam can identify which one applies. Describing the specific difficulty — which letters disappear, or whose faces look unclear and from how far away — gives an eye doctor useful information to start with.

Reaching for more light to read may point to early age-related macular degeneration (AMD $AMD). The same goes for a slow return of sight after stepping from a bright space into a dim one. Both changes are easy to mistake for ordinary aging. They are still worth raising with an eye doctor.

The first change often shows up at home. A person may pull a lamp closer to the page or swap in a brighter bulb. Some carry a book or a menu over to a window. Tasks that once felt easy under ordinary room lighting start to feel like work.

The second change involves dark adaptation. This is the process that lets the eyes recover sight after the lighting drops. Stepping from a sunlit sidewalk into a restaurant, a movie theater or a dim hallway can leave someone with AMD waiting longer than expected before shapes and details return.

Both changes trace back to the retina, the light-sensitive tissue that lines the back of the eye. The macula sits at the center of the retina and handles sharp, straight-ahead vision. When AMD affects that area, the retina can have trouble forming a clear image in low light. It may also respond more slowly when light levels shift.

Neither symptom points to AMD on its own. Plenty of adults need more light for fine print as they age. Other eye conditions can also make dim settings harder to manage. That overlap is part of the difficulty. Because the changes feel ordinary, many people quietly adjust their habits instead of mentioning them.

An eye doctor can work out the cause. A person cannot see their own macula, but an eye doctor can examine it directly. Describing the lighting changes, even ones that seem minor, gives the doctor useful context.

Slow adjustment to darkness also has practical effects. A dark parking garage, an unlit stairwell or a bedroom at night can become harder to move through safely.

Anyone who notices a steady need for brighter light can bring it up at an eye appointment. Mention it, too, if dim rooms take longer to adjust to than before.

Colors that look duller or less saturated than they used to can be a symptom of age-related macular degeneration. This is a less specific warning than warped lines or a blank central patch. That makes it a reason to book an eye exam, not a reason to reach a conclusion at home.

The explanation starts with the eye's anatomy. At the center of the macula sits the fovea, a tiny pit packed tightly with cone cells. Cones are the photoreceptors responsible for color and fine detail. When AMD $AMD harms tissue in and around the macula, those cells can stop working normally. The colors a person sees straight ahead may then lose some of their richness.

The change can be hard to put into words. A red sweater might look closer to rust or brown. A blue sky might seem grayer than expected. Two shirts that once looked clearly different in shade may become harder to tell apart. Navy and black socks can start to look like a pair.

The shift often arrives slowly. It rarely registers as a single moment. Some people only notice it when one eye sees a color differently from the other. Others hear about it first from a family member who points out a mismatched outfit.

Faded color is not unique to AMD. Several other eye conditions can also make the world look less vivid. That overlap is why this symptom carries less weight on its own than distortion does.

The pattern of other changes around it can help an eye doctor sort out the cause. Glaucoma, for example, harms the optic nerve, and its effects typically show up at the edges of sight before the center. AMD works the other way. It targets the middle of the visual field and tends to leave peripheral vision intact. An eye doctor weighs duller color alongside any blur or distortion straight ahead when judging whether the macula is involved. A complete eye exam is the only way to separate these conditions.

A gradual fading of color is worth raising at the next scheduled eye appointment. A change that appears quickly needs faster attention. The same goes for faded color that arrives with other new problems in central vision. Either one deserves a call to an eye doctor without waiting for a routine visit.

Drusen are small yellow deposits that build up under the retina. An eye doctor can spot them during a dilated exam, but the person who has them cannot see or feel them. They are a hallmark finding in age-related macular degeneration, or AMD $AMD.

That makes drusen different from the changes people notice on their own, such as a blurred patch or a bent line. A person with drusen may read, drive and recognize faces with no trouble at all. Nothing in daily life hints that the deposits are there.

A dilated exam is how they come to light. Drops widen the pupil, which gives an optometrist or ophthalmologist a broader view of the back of the eye. That view takes in the retina and the macula, the small central area that handles sharp, straight-ahead vision. Against the surrounding tissue, drusen show up as yellowish flecks.

The size of the deposits matters. Larger drusen are linked to a higher risk that AMD will progress to a more advanced stage. That link shapes follow-up care. A doctor who sees large deposits has a reason to watch the eye more closely over time.

Optical coherence tomography, or OCT, adds more detail. The scan produces detailed cross-section images of the retina, showing its individual layers one above the other. Those images help a doctor see where deposits sit in relation to the surrounding tissue. Repeat scans at later visits make it possible to compare the retina over time.

Timing is the practical issue. Drusen give no warning. A person who waits for blurry or distorted vision before booking an exam may already be past the point at which the deposits could first have been found. An exam at that earlier stage gives patient and doctor a record to measure later changes against.

A finding of drusen is not a diagnosis of vision loss. It marks the start of monitoring. What comes next depends on what the dilated exam and any imaging show.

An Amsler grid is a square chart of evenly spaced straight lines with a single dot at its center. It checks one eye at a time for changes in central vision. It is a tool for keeping watch between appointments, not a way to diagnose macular degeneration.

The design is deliberately plain. Every line on the chart is straight, and every square is the same size. Against that uniform pattern, small distortions in the middle of the visual field tend to stand out.

Setup takes only a moment. Anyone who normally wears reading glasses should put them on first. The aim is to judge the retina, not the eye's ability to focus up close. Then hold the grid at the distance you would usually hold a book or a phone.

Next, cover one eye with a hand or a card. With the uncovered eye, look straight at the center dot and keep your gaze fixed there. The point is not to scan the page. While you stay locked on the dot, notice how the rest of the grid looks around it.

Repeat the same steps with the other eye. Testing each eye separately matters. The brain blends the images from both eyes, and a healthy eye can mask a problem in its partner. As a result, a change may go unnoticed when both eyes are open.

Several results count as warning signs. Lines may look curved, rippled or pulled out of shape. Part of the grid may seem to be missing, faded or covered by a gray or dark patch. Some areas may look blurry. Any of these is a reason to contact an eye doctor rather than wait for a routine visit.

Keep the conditions the same each time. Use the same lighting, the same distance and the same glasses. That consistency makes it easier to tell a real change in vision from a difference in setup. An eye doctor can advise on how often a particular patient should check.

A normal-looking grid does not rule out disease. Macular degeneration can be present without any visible distortion on the chart. Some retinal changes can be seen only by an eye doctor during an exam. The grid supplements professional care. It does not replace it.

A sudden change in central vision calls for an eye exam within a day or two. Straight lines that bend, a new dark spot in the middle of sight or a fast drop in sharpness can all point to wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD $AMD). In that form, prompt treatment matters.

Wet AMD, also called neovascular AMD, involves abnormal blood vessels that grow under the retina. These vessels can leak fluid or blood into the macula, the small area that handles sharp, straight-ahead vision. Once that happens, central sight can worsen within a short span.

The warning signs often arrive abruptly. A person may notice one morning that the edge of a bookshelf looks warped, or that a gray smudge now sits over the center of a clock face. A change in one eye can go unnoticed for a while. The other eye often compensates for it. That makes the first days of a problem easy to miss.

The right response is to call an eye doctor's office the same day. Describe what changed, which eye is affected and when it started, then ask to be seen within a day or two. Waiting for a routine appointment months away is too long when central vision shifts quickly.

People already diagnosed with dry AMD should follow the same step. The dry form can progress to the wet form, and a new symptom may be the earliest clue that this has happened.

Wet AMD is treated with anti-VEGF medicines injected into the eye. VEGF stands for vascular endothelial growth factor, a protein that drives the growth of new blood vessels. Drugs such as aflibercept and ranibizumab block that protein, which can slow further vision loss. Results are generally better when treatment starts earlier. That is why the timing of the first visit matters for this form of the disease.

Dry AMD has no cure. In 2023, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved two treatments for geographic atrophy, an advanced stage of the dry form. They are pegcetacoplan, sold as Syfovre, and avacincaptad pegol, sold as Izervay. An eye doctor can explain whether either one fits a particular patient's case.

Only an eye doctor examining the retina can confirm whether leaking vessels are behind a sudden change or whether another problem is responsible. That diagnosis comes before any treatment decision.

Older age, smoking, family history and certain inherited gene variants all raise the risk of age-related macular degeneration. Of those, smoking is the one a person can change.

AMD $AMD becomes more common as people get older. Age alone does not mean a person will develop the disease. It does raise the baseline odds for everyone.

A family history of AMD also raises the odds. People with a parent or sibling who had the disease carry a higher risk. Certain gene variants are also linked to AMD. Genes cannot be changed. Knowing about a family pattern, though, can prompt a person to start eye exams earlier and keep them regular.

Tobacco use differs from the rest of this list because it is a habit rather than a trait. Age, ancestry and genes are fixed. Quitting is a step a person can take on their own to cut that particular risk.

Risk also varies by race. AMD risk is higher in white people than in other racial groups. Heart and blood vessel disease, along with obesity, has been linked to higher risk as well. Such a link shows that the conditions tend to occur together. It does not prove that one causes the other.

For adults with no symptoms and no known risk factors, the American Academy of Ophthalmology advises a baseline eye exam at about age 40. That first visit gives the doctor a reference point to compare against later. The academy then recommends exams more often as patients get older.

A routine schedule is a minimum, not a limit. Anyone with a family history of AMD should book an appointment instead of waiting for the next routine visit. The same applies to anyone who notices a change in central vision.

The National Eye Institute's AREDS and AREDS2 formulas are named for the trials that studied them. They combine zinc, copper, vitamins C and E, lutein and zeaxanthin.

In people who already have intermediate AMD, these formulas can lower the odds of the disease reaching its advanced stage. They do not prevent AMD from developing. Trials have not shown a benefit for people without the disease or for those at its early stage.

The stage of the disease decides whether a formula makes sense. An eye doctor can confirm that stage. Patients should raise the question with one before starting any supplement.

#4684. How CertiK Verifies a TPS Claim and What It Cannot Tell You

AI-assisted summary, reviewed by NullTX editors.

When a blockchain protocol claims it can process 40 million transactions per second, verifying the underlying arithmetic becomes essential.

Sui's October 7 announcement presented that figure on a Basecamp stage in Singapore, and CertiK's subsequent audit report placed a precise, verified figure alongside it. Comparing the initial marketing announcement with the final verification report reveals how independent auditors evaluate extreme throughput benchmarks.

This guide outlines how a security verifier like CertiK evaluates throughput results, using its TON benchmark audit as a foundational template and Sui's tunnel performance test as a real-world implementation.

Throughput figures can easily be inflated under controlled conditions. A development team might select a simplified workload, calculate transactions using generous parameters, or present a single peak burst as standard network behavior. While these practices are common in marketing, unverified TPS figures remain speculative until an independent party recomputes the raw ledger metrics.

Sui's initial announcement reported 40,614,180 TPS using its off-chain tunnel architecture at Sui Basecamp. The team also noted that CertiK would audit execution journals and publish formal findings. With CertiK's verification report now public, we can examine how third-party technical verification works in practice.

Verification begins by defining what constitutes a single transaction, as this definition determines the final throughput total. In the Sui evaluation, CertiK counted a transaction only if it proved valid under protocol rules, contained every required authorization proof, and appeared exactly once within the canonical execution journal.

Furthermore, the audit restricted valid counts strictly to transactions committed inside the active test window. This approach filters out duplicate entries, unassigned user actions, and pre-test setup data. Consequently, if a throughput claim fails to define its underlying transaction unit, the figure carries little practical value.

CertiK's TON write-up begins by documenting environmental parameters. The auditors verified the testnet infrastructure, which comprised 256 active nodes, specific software versions, and defined validator sets. Because performance on a test network does not guarantee identical results on mainnet, auditors must specify exact deployment parameters.

Similarly, the Sui evaluation documented its operational setup. The benchmark ran on mainnet on October 7 across 10 distinct test runs covering five workload types consisting of simple payments, flash loans, blackjack, tic-tac-toe, and battleship games. The report also noted that workloads launched at staggered intervals, with all 7,680 tunnels operating concurrently for a duration of 148.545 seconds.

Matching deployed execution code to public repositories provides critical assurance during performance testing. In the TON audit, CertiK confirmed that stress-test smart contracts deployed on-chain matched the source code published by the foundation. The testing also utilized contracts with complex execution logic rather than basic balance transfers.

Without this step, a project could benchmark stripped-down programs and present them as full-featured application performance. #4675. Cleveland’s big lesson for Seattle: A city’s job is to attract people

by Charles Fitzgerald on Oct 20, 2026 at 2:39 pmOctober 20, 2026 at 2:45 pm

Editor’s note: GeekWire has been examining the economic challenges facing Seattle and the lessons the region can learn from Cleveland’s industrial decline and ongoing comeback. This three-part series from GeekWire columnist and angel investor Charles Fitzgerald explores how Seattle can navigate its own economic transition, adapt to a changing technology landscape, and preserve the prosperity that generations of innovation have built.

As you think about Seattle’s next act, let me offer a catchphrase: Don’t be Cleveland.

Before anyone from Northeast Ohio takes me to task again, let me quickly clarify: Don’t be the Cleveland of the last half of the 20th century. Today’s Cleveland is a story Seattle should study.

I’m far from the only one worried about Seattle. City Hall, the Seattle Metropolitan Chamber of Commerce, and the newly formed Partnership for a Competitive Puget Sound are all sounding alarms about the region’s economic future.

An independent 127-page City of Seattle-commissioned economic assessment — Seawall: Building a Resilient Seattle Economy — takes issue with the Cleveland comparison I penned for GeekWire earlier this year. Hyperbolic is far from the worst thing I’ve been called.

Wages rose across the income spectrum, with the biggest gains at the bottom. Even after adjusting for living costs, workers at the 25th percentile earned roughly 15% more than their counterparts in Atlanta, Charlotte, and Minneapolis.

It also makes Seattle’s current economic anxiety harder to dismiss — and its prosperity worth protecting. If Seattle’s performance disappoints, where exactly would you rather be? Growth brings problems, especially when poorly managed. But the alternative is worse.

We saw the aftermath firsthand when we visited Cleveland earlier this year. Its story is a reminder that prosperity isn’t permanent — and rebuilding an economy after decades of decline is a lot harder than keeping one healthy.

Much like Seattle today, Cleveland 75 years ago was a very successful city riding decades of strong economic and population growth, and home to some of that era’s Big Tech giants. The Cleveland Browns even dominated the 1950s NFL.

But Cleveland’s prosperity unraveled quickly.

The world changed. The Rust Belt started to rust. Employers closed down.

Cleveland’s population dropped 59% from its 1950 peak to 2020 — from 914,808 to 372,624 residents.

It went from one of the nation’s richest cities to one of the poorest. Seahawks fans take note: the Browns have never been to the Super Bowl.

When we visited Cleveland earlier this year, we asked everyone from the mayor to real estate developers to arts leaders what they wish they’d done differently and sought their advice for Seattle.

We learned a lot from a city hungry, united and focused on building prosperity for its residents.

Amazon and Microsoft have cut more than 10,000 jobs across the Seattle metro area since 2023. Downtown office vacancy remains among the highest in the country. The city’s economic report puts the challenge bluntly: “The version of the tech sector that drove the last wave of economic growth will not drive the next one.”

The report warns that Seattle’s next generation of startups may not be ready to replace the growth engines of the past. The risk isn’t just layoffs or empty office towers. It’s the company never founded, the midsize business squeezed by rising costs, or the engineer who leaves to build a startup somewhere else.

So how does Seattle prepare for its next economic chapter?

At its simplest, a city’s job is to attract people — and give them reasons to stay.

Population isn’t a perfect measure of prosperity, but it’s a useful proxy for a city’s appeal. People come for opportunity. They stay when the opportunities and quality of life are worth it. Growth brings challenges, but those are preferable to the alternative.

As Cleveland Mayor Justin Bibb told us, “I envy problems around growth.”

Seattle’s population has grown nearly 40% since 2000.

But that growth is slowing as our economic engine sputters.

The Boeing Bust of the 1970s, when Seattle’s population fell 7% and unemployment hit 17%, is a reminder that what goes up can come down.

Today, cities compete globally for talent, entrepreneurs and employers. That competition will only intensify as populations age and immigration restrictions tighten.

The most talented workers and entrepreneurs can go where they see the greatest opportunity — and where they feel wanted.

Consider what Seattle’s last century might have looked like without three people: Bill Boeing, Bill Gates and Jeff Bezos.

Boeing helped turn Seattle into an aerospace powerhouse. Gates chose to move Microsoft from Albuquerque to the Seattle area to usher in the software boom. Bezos and his then-wife, MacKenzie, famously drove their Chevy Blazer from Texas to Seattle to start Amazon.

Each helped shape the region’s economic future. We can’t predict who the next generation of business builders will be. But does Seattle have the same pull today? Or are the people we need choosing Austin, Miami, San Francisco or somewhere else?

Attracting newcomers matters. So does keeping the people who are already here. Do the economic opportunities still justify the cost of living? Is Seattle still a place where people want to live, work and play?

Residents have plenty of reasons to question the deal: public safety, housing costs, homelessness, schools, government services and taxes. Do the positives still outweigh the negatives?

Decades of growth brought extraordinary people and companies to Seattle. Our best chance of building the next era of prosperity is to keep them here — and give them room to build what comes next.

Yet we risk losing people and businesses we can’t easily replace: talented workers, entrepreneurs, executives, inventors, technologists, engineering teams and global headquarters.

As Seattle’s mayor waves goodbye to departing business leaders, the city’s own Seawall report offers a blunt warning: “Headquarters and top-tier tech jobs are very hard to replace.”

The report makes the broader case for growth, too. Losing tech jobs and companies won’t make Seattle more equitable, it warns. “If they leave, Seattle won’t become more equal, it will just become poorer.”

There’s a practical reason for that. Seattle’s fiscal model depends on a growing economy.

More residents and businesses generate property and sales tax revenue without requiring higher tax rates on those already here. As the report puts it, “it is easier to distribute the benefits of a growing pie than a fixed one.”

That’s another way of saying that when government spending outpaces population growth, a stagnant economy leaves fewer options for paying the bills — a challenge we’ll revisit in the next post.

Thanks to Cleveland for reminding us prosperity is fragile, decline is real, and recovery can take generations.

Seattle’s next era of prosperity is ours to build — or squander.

In the next post, we’ll look at what Seattle needs to do to get the fundamentals right to ensure the city is a great place to live, work and play.

#4673. Zoom’s next act: From video company to AI data platform

GUEST COLUMN by Zeus Kerravala

Since its launch, Zoom has been defined by one word: video. Customers, consumers, investors and competitors have long known Zoom as the industry’s easiest-to-use and best video platform. However, even before the pandemic, the company had been evolving into something much bigger than the unified-communications-as-a-service and contact-center-as-a-service buckets that analyst firms put it in.

Those efforts culminated at this week’s Zoomtopia 2026, where Zoom’s second act was on display and about much more than video. Zoom’s “conversation to completion” vision, anchored by a new Unified Context Layer, positions Zoom as an artificial intelligence data platform, with its most valuable asset being the conversational data flowing through meetings, phone calls, chat, contact center interactions, events, interviews and sales calls. Video and other communications tools are the capture mechanism. The data and actions are the product.

That’s a significant shift, and it changes both where Zoom can play within an enterprise and how it needs to go to market.

I’ve said many times that AI is only as good as the data it can access, and that data without context can be misleading. The problem with connecting AI to documents, tickets and databases is that documented knowledge is always out of date. Decisions are made in conversations and are written down later, if at all.

Zoom’s Unified Context Layer is designed to close that gap. In the analyst prebriefing, Zoom explained that it combines three sources: general knowledge from the model itself, documented knowledge from files and connected applications such as Salesforce, ServiceNow and Workday, and dynamic context from live conversations. Zoom builds a graph that maps people, topics and decisions and keeps it updated in near real time.

During his portion of the keynote, Chief Executive Eric Yuan (pictured) explained, “AI models are only as powerful as their understanding of your business, and that context makes all the difference.”

The strategic implication for Zoom is that it no longer needs to win the meeting to win the account. It needs to be the system that captures and understands conversations wherever they occur. That’s why it’s extending My Notes to other meeting platforms and in-person discussions, and why it now offers 15 MCP servers, Search and Ask application programming interfaces, and connections to Claude, Codex and Copilot. Zoom is making its context available to any AI tool a customer prefers, a clear signal that it sees itself as a data layer, not just an application.

This transformation positions Zoom as much more than a communications vendor serving a narrow audience. Once Zoom defines itself by conversations rather than meetings, the addressable market expands. Every business function runs on conversations, and several of the most valuable ones have historically been out of reach for a communications vendor. Zoom has used acquisitions and product investments to follow those conversations into new buying centers.

Revenue operations. Zoom Revenue Accelerator, or ZRA, captures and analyzes sales conversations to deliver coaching, deal intelligence and forecasting. Zoom said in the prebriefing that 65% of ZRA users report a significant or transformational impact on their sales. The company’s fiscal 2026 shareholder letter noted that ZRA customers grew by 50% over the year.

The acquisition of Common Room, which closed in July, pushes Zoom further upstream. Common Room unifies CRM, product, marketing and engagement data with real-world buying signals to build person-level buyer intelligence, with AI agents handling research and prospecting. Chief Strategy Officer Abhisht Arora described the deal as “extending Zoom’s system of action upstream.”

At Zoomtopia, the combination appeared as ZRA enrichment and prospecting that surfaces decision makers not yet in the CRM, plus signal-based orchestration that routes in-market buyers to the right rep with full context. Zoom now covers the revenue journey from the first buying signal through the sales call to the forecast. That puts Zoom in the conversation with chief revenue officers and RevOps leaders, buyers who have never had much reason to talk to Zoom beyond licensing seats.

Human resources and hiring. The same logic applies to talent. Zoom acquired BrightHire in December 2025 for $98 million in cash. BrightHire records and transcribes interviews, generates candidate summaries, structures evidence against hiring criteria, and automates first-round screening with an AI interviewer. Millions of job interviews already take place on Zoom, making the fit natural. With Workvivo, Zoom now spans the talent lifecycle from interview to engagement.

BrightHire also demonstrates how Zoom’s trust capabilities create value across functions. Zoom’s Deepfake Risk Detection is already being used by BrightHire in interviews, where candidate fraud is a growing concern. Zoom said it will extend the feature to workplace meetings in December.

Typically, I’ve never been excited about human resources software because companies rarely invest in transforming this area. However, the hiring process is something every department cares about. If a sales leader, chief information officer, head of marketing or anyone else can hire faster and with better-qualified people, that will appeal across companies.

Customer experience. Zoom is relatively new to the customer experience space, but customer and partner feedback has been overwhelmingly positive, and the context story reinforces that. The context layer feeds the virtual agent, live agent, and back-office expert, so customers don’t repeat themselves. Zoom cited Cricut, which cut customer wait times by 89%.

The common thread is that each of these products generates conversational data that feeds the context layer and benefits from the context generated by the others. That’s the flywheel Zoom is building, and it’s what makes the AI data platform positioning more than marketing.

The technology story is compelling. The challenge for Zoom is more of a commercial problem. Zoom’s salesforce and channel partners were built to sell meetings, phone and contact center to information technology and communications buyers. Historically, nearly everything Zoom sold was an add-on to the meetings relationship.

Revenue Accelerator, Workvivo, Common Room and BrightHire are different. The economic buyer is a chief revenue officer, a RevOps leader, a head of talent acquisition or a chief people officer. Those buyers have different priorities, budgets and evaluation criteria, and they already have established relationships with vendors such as Gong, Clari, ZoomInfo and Greenhouse.

I asked about this directly during the prebriefing: Are partners leading with products like ZRA in greenfield accounts, or mostly adding them to the existing Zoom installed base? Chief Product Officer Russell Dicker (below) said Zoom is seeing a mix of both but didn’t share numbers. He noted that even companies standardized on another meeting platform often have sales and recruiting teams running external conversations on Zoom, creating a real opening.

When I followed up on whether there’s a prescriptive guide for the field and channel, Dicker emphasized that Zoom aims to solve problems in a best-of-breed way and isn’t trying to force customers into a wall-to-wall suite. At the event, I asked Chris Barwick, head of Americas sales and channel, about this, and he explained that the company is orienting sales and partner enablement around specific buying centers: knowledge workers, CX, go-to-market (RevOps and marketing ops, including events and ZRA), talent and HR (BrightHire and Workvivo), and a growing developer ecosystem.

That’s the right approach, but it understates the difficulty. Selling to a new business unit requires the right messaging, sellers who speak the language of pipeline coverage and quality of hire, and references in those functions. Most Zoom partners are UC and CX resellers whose contacts sit in IT, so walking into the CRO’s office is a big leap. Many won’t succeed without specialist overlay teams, new incentives or new partner types, such as RevOps consultancies and HR tech integrators. But partners that do invest should reap the rewards: bigger deals with higher margins that are significantly stickier.

One partner that has made the leap is Converged Technology Professionals, one of Zoom’s top resellers. At Zoomtopia, I spoke with Joe Rittenhouse, co-CEO of CTP, who said ZRA is becoming a gateway to accounts. “We may start with ZRA before we do UCaaS or CCaaS, just replacing Gong,” he told me. “As soon as they see the intangibles and the data, there’s a hunger and a thirst for more, and that’s how we get into notes and the other aspects of the platform. That flips the traditional Zoom motion on its head, with the meetings business following an application sale rather than leading it.”

Rittenhouse was also candid that the partner business model must change to support it. “Traditionally, we were there to help with the sales motion, handle the implementation, and probably sell a maintenance contract. It was break-fix,” he said. “That has completely changed. We have to get involved with the business, sign an NDA, understand what they’re trying to do and where the low-hanging fruit is, and put together a pragmatic plan.” That kind of discovery lengthens sales cycles, but, as he noted, it also produces better, more strategic deals.

Zoom CMO Kim Storin believes Zoom’s best opportunity lies in the line of business. When I spoke with her at Zoomtopia, she explained why sales and recruiting naturally align with the core platform. “When you think about conversations that matter, nothing matters more than your customer conversations and sales,” she said. “Nothing matters more than your hiring conversations.” She added that she and Yuan see the revenue organization as “the wedge back into these companies that may be standardizing on Teams or Google,” with the sales, talent, or marketing leader serving “as the gateway to break through into those organizations.” I agree.

Microsoft Corp. has a lock on the CIO’s calendar, and Zoom won’t outmuscle it there. But Storin was also realistic that going around IT isn’t a free pass. She noted that IT has been tightening purchase controls over the past nine months because of unpredictable token and consumption costs. That’s where the governance capabilities Zoom announced become a sales asset. The line-of-business buyer may start the deal, but Zoom will need to give IT a reason to say yes.

Zoomtopia 2026 marked the moment Zoom stopped asking to be valued as a video company. The Unified Context Layer, the headless platform approach and the acquisitions of Common Room and BrightHire add up to a coherent strategy: Own the conversational data, make it available to any AI, and use it to complete work across every business function.

The strategy is right. Zoom is one of the few companies with the reach to capture business conversations at scale, and its growing presence in RevOps and HR makes it more relevant to the business than ever.

But relevance and revenue aren’t the same thing. Zoom’s success over the next two years will depend less on what it ships and more on whether its salesforce and partners can earn a seat at the table with buyers who haven’t considered Zoom a strategic vendor. If Zoom can build that motion, the “video company” label will finally fade. If it can’t, it risks having a great platform story that only IT hears.

#4670. ‘Difficult relationship’: UW task force proposes splitting Allen School from College of Engineering

by Todd Bishop on Oct 20, 2026 at 5:00 pmOctober 21, 2026 at 10:55 am

University of Washington leaders are weighing whether to separate the Allen School of Computer Science & Engineering from the broader College of Engineering, after a task force recommended the move as part of proposed changes to UW’s computing and AI programs.

The split would give new independence to what has become one of the world’s leading schools for computer science and AI, and a major source of talent for the Seattle region’s tech industry.

As an independent, college-level school, the Allen School would gain greater control over its finances and a seat at the table with UW’s other deans. It could also put more of its own revenue toward teaching, as student demand for computing courses continues to outstrip its capacity.

The task force’s report, released this week, concluded that UW’s successes in computing, data science and AI have come “not because of the organizational structures we have in place but in spite of them.” The nine-member group included UW faculty leaders from across campus, two outside academics and an Amazon executive.

The report also described a “difficult relationship” between the Allen School and the College of Engineering, and “significant distrust” across campus over resources and priorities.

UW Engineering Dean Nancy Allbritton, in a statement to GeekWire on Tuesday, called the relationship “complex rather than difficult.”

The College of Engineering supports the university’s decision to “carefully evaluate” the task force’s recommendations, Allbritton said. She praised the Allen School and said the school and college share a long history of working together, with a commitment to students, research and service to Washington state.

The stakes extend well beyond university politics: The Allen School’s graduates are in high demand across the tech industry. Nearly 100 graduates from the 2025-26 class went to work full time at Amazon, and more than 70 went to Google, Microsoft or Meta, according to a survey of recent graduates by the school. In all, graduates went to more than 150 different companies.

No decision yet: UW President Robert J. Jones and Provost Tricia Serio said Monday that they aren’t making a decision yet. In a letter to the UW community, they announced two implementation committees.

Both committees are due to report by the end of winter quarter 2027, and UW leaders plan to announce next steps in spring 2027. Any changes would still need approval through the university’s usual processes, including the Faculty Senate and Board of Regents, with a goal of putting them in place by the end of the 2027-28 academic year.

The task force was co-chaired by Andy Connolly of UW’s eScience Institute and Kathy Yelick of Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. Its other members were Dan Grossman, now the Allen School’s interim director; Remzi Arpaci-Dusseau of the University of Wisconsin; UW’s Elizabeth Nance (chemical engineering), Abel Rodriguez (statistics), Emma Spiro (Information School) and Peter Tarczy-Hornoch (UW Medicine); and Anne Szeto of Amazon.

Allen School faculty voted 83-0 in late September in support of “moving forward with a process to determine how the Allen School would become an independent school,” with 13 not voting because they were absent or on leave, according to the school. A formal decision and vote would follow that process, the school said.

Budget and a seat at the table: The issue comes down to money and influence. UW’s budget model sends tuition and a share of research grant funding to the college where the courses are taught and the research is based.

For the Allen School, that’s the College of Engineering. As an independent school, the Allen School would control those funds directly, and its leader would sit with UW’s other deans when the university sets strategy on AI and other priorities, a voice the task force says computing has largely lacked.

UW’s Information School already operates as an independent, college-level unit. But the report says its focus on the human, societal and organizational side of computing and information is different from the Allen School’s focus on computing as a discipline.

As an initial step, Jones and Serio invited Grossman to participate in the Board of Deans and Chancellors. The letter says the move is “independent of any future decision about administrative structure.”

In a message to the Allen School community, Grossman called the invitation “a reflection of the Allen School’s scale and reach,” adding, “I’m pleased to have this opportunity to make meaningful contributions to discussions among UW’s leaders.”

He described the task force’s proposals as “exciting recommendations that will be great for the whole of UW and its impact on Washington state and beyond.”

Grossman also said there are “no planned changes to the Allen School’s structure, staffing, budget, policies and procedures, or degree programs” while the committees do their work.

A longtime Allen School professor and its former vice director, Grossman was named interim director this summer when Magdalena Balazinska’s nearly seven-year term as director ended. At the time, UW said it was opting for an interim leader for about a year while it weighed the task force’s recommendations, which hadn’t yet been made public.

The board seat was one of several early steps the task force recommended to show that UW was committed to the process. The task force also suggested having the Allen School’s director report jointly to the provost and giving the school more control over its budget, but UW hasn’t taken those steps for now.

Rooted in Paul Allen’s gift: The prospect of independence goes back to the Allen School’s creation in 2017.

When a $40 million gift from Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen turned UW’s longtime computer science department into the Allen School, the gift agreement said the school would “remain a unit of the College of Engineering, at least for the time being,” according to a copy obtained by GeekWire.

The agreement called for the school to receive the same prominence, visibility, support and privileges as UW’s other schools and colleges “to the greatest extent possible,” and anticipated that it could someday become “a free-standing School or College.”

The agreement also spelled out what would happen to the name if the school were ever “elevated to a College of the University”: it would change to reflect the new structure, but would “always prominently include” Allen’s name.

Impact on Engineering: The College of Engineering would lose its largest unit. The Allen School, which awarded 894 of the college’s 3,132 degrees in 2025-26, accounts for a little more than a quarter of the college by degrees, enrollment and faculty, according to figures from the college.

The report acknowledges “significant short- and long-term financial implications” from a separation and suggests options such as permanent financial support for Engineering to offset the loss.

Allbritton said the college is committed to helping shape an outcome that “strengthens the College of Engineering, strengthens computer science as an independent discipline, and advances the University of Washington’s broader mission of innovation, discovery, economic opportunity and public impact.”

The Allen School “is an extraordinary unit that has experienced remarkable growth and operates at a scale unlike any other department in the College,” she said. “As both organizations have evolved, there have naturally been questions about structure, governance, and how best to support future growth.”

She also noted that the College of Engineering has nearly doubled in size over the past decade “amid increasingly constrained public resources,” and said the college’s focus is on ensuring that students, faculty, staff, research partnerships and industry relationships “continue to thrive.”

“What has remained constant is a shared commitment to student success, research excellence and service to Washington state,” Allbritton said.

The report doesn’t detail the friction between the Allen School and Engineering. It says independence “does not directly address concerns voiced about collegiality” but could remove “a structural asymmetry that has complicated those relationships.” Opinion across campus on Allen’s independence was “genuinely divided,” the report says.

The federation’s role: The proposed federation is partly a way to keep the rest of campus on board. Other units worried that the Allen School would win independence while the federation never got real resources, according to the report, which recommends that the split not be finalized until the federation is up and running.

Much of the “distrust” cited in the report centers on whether one recommendation would advance at the expense of the other.

If the Allen School’s separation is finalized first, the report says, “other stakeholders are unlikely to trust that the federation will ever achieve the size and stature it needs.”

If the federation moves ahead without a clear commitment to independence, the Allen School and its supporters “are unlikely to trust that their important contributions toward the federation’s success will achieve meaningful change.”

The Allen School is already helping lead cross-campus efforts, including a new AI minor for students in all majors, developed with 18 academic units and set to launch in spring 2027.

Toward a college of computing: Other top universities have taken similar paths. Carnegie Mellon and Georgia Tech run computer science as standalone, college-level schools, while MIT, UC Berkeley and Cornell have created broader colleges of computing, according to the report.

Peer institutions’ computing colleges were “frequently propelled by a transformational naming gift,” the report notes, as with MIT’s Schwarzman College of Computing and Cornell’s Bowers College of Computing and Information Science.

The report describes the federation as a possible “transitional phase” toward a full College of Computing at UW, which would house the Allen School alongside other units, and recommends revisiting that idea in about five years.

That leaves aviation with one top alternative for today: sustainable aviation fuel, or SAF.

Instead of pumping more oil from underground, SAF uses carbon already on the earth’s surface. Manufacturers — including two in the American West — are making the fuel from sources such as cooking oil, animal fats, oil-rich plants, farm and forestry waste, or even captured carbon.

“SAF really fits a need that we have today and long into the future,” said Ryan Spies, managing director of sustainability at Alaska and Hawaiian Airlines. “SAF is the answer because it’s a drop-in fuel. The fuel remains in limited supply and use — only 1% of Alaska Air Group’s fuel is SAF, for example — but efforts are underway to expand production and adoption worldwide.

In tumbleweed country in central Washington, a startup called Twelve opened its AirPlant One facility last year and began commercially producing SAF from captured carbon dioxide this summer.

The site sits in an industrial stretch of Moses Lake in a largely agricultural area. Its neighbors include a fertilizer producer and battery materials manufacturer Sila. Low-cost, carbon-free hydropower has drawn businesses to the region, though data centers are creating competition for the energy.
