#2849. Not Bitcoin. Not Ethereum. This Once-Popular Cryptocurrency Could Be the Biggest Winner of 2027.

If you're looking for a big winner in 2027, you'll likely need to widen your search beyond Bitcoin and Ethereum. In bull market cycles, these two cryptocurrencies perform well, of course, but nowhere near as well as more speculative altcoins with much smaller market caps.

In 2027, there is one type of altcoin that I'm expecting to perform particularly well: beaten-down cryptocurrencies that have pretty much been left for dead in 2026. With that in mind, there's one name that I've already circled for a potential major rebound next year: Cardano (ADA +0.36%).

Just five years ago, Cardano ranked as the fourth-largest cryptocurrency on the planet, trailing only Bitcoin, Ethereum, and BNB. At the time, Cardano had a market cap of $70 billion, and appeared to have a path to toppling Ethereum as the world's premier Layer-1 blockchain network.

But that never happened. Down another 40% in 2026, Cardano now trades for a bargain-basement price of just $0.21. Its market cap has slid to less than $8 billion. In fact, Cardano's fall has been so precipitous that it has already dropped out of the list of the top 10 cryptocurrencies (as measured by market cap).

But a few factors are working in Cardano's favor right now.

One of these is a renewed emphasis on decentralized finance (DeFi). At the beginning of the year, the Cardano developer community released a far-reaching strategy document ("Cardano 2030 Strategic Framework") with details on how they planned to turn things around in DeFi. And they gave very specific goals for user growth, transaction activity, and market share gains.

That might sound like pie-in-the-sky thinking, except for one fact: Cardano co-founder Charles Hoskinson is also one of the co-founders of Ethereum and knows a thing or two about putting together a world-class blockchain network.

Hoskinson is currently working on a new programming language for the Cardano blockchain platform, and that could mean big things ahead if it opens up new opportunities in fast-growing areas such as artificial intelligence.

If Cardano can turn things around next year, a good template could be Solana (SOL +0.53%), which saw a monster turnaround after losing 94% of its value in 2022. The following year, Solana soared by 924% as investors realized that it had been oversold amid broader fears of a full-fledged crypto meltdown.

Like Solana, Cardano is a Layer-1 smart contract platform. And, similar to Solana several years ago, Cardano is currently trading at a massive discount to its all-time high.

This is not to say that Cardano will 10x in 2027. But I wouldn't be surprised at all if it climbs back past the $1 mark. As recently as January 2025, Cardano traded for $1. That's nearly a 5x return, and much higher than Bitcoin or Ethereum is projected to return. It's a risky bet, of course, but at a price of just $0.21, Cardano is just too cheap to ignore.

A series of judgments and settlements don't seem to be slowing Meta's provocative privacy practices.

Meta was hit with a $25 billion penalty on Wednesday when a judge in New Mexico found the company had misled users about data privacy practices. The decision marks one of the steepest corporate payouts ever levied against a technology company.

Under the ruling, Meta, which owns Facebook, Instagram and other platforms and services, was ordered to pay the fee for misleading social media consumers about their data privacy in a case dating back to Cambridge Analytica’s privacy violations involving the 2016 presidential election. The ruling against Meta imposed civil penalties for violating New Mexico’s Unfair Practices Act after a jury trial found Facebook liable for nearly 44 million violations of that law.

Meta could appeal the verdict; it was unsuccessful in its filings to cap its penalties at $3.45 billion. It could have been worse for the company. Some estimates had suggested it could have been hit with $219 billion in penalties.

A representative for Meta didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment.

“A New Mexico jury found nearly 44 million violations of our consumer protection statute, and this penalty order demonstrates that those violations carry real consequences,” New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez said in a statement. “Today’s result makes clear that no corporation is too powerful to be held accountable.”

Meta previously paid $725 million to users to settle a lawsuit over Cambridge Analytica, a high-profile scandal in which a British consulting firm working with the Donald Trump presidential campaign was given access to Facebook user data. That data purchase is credited with helping sway the 2016 election results.

Just two months ago, the company settled with multiple US states to the tune of up to $18 billion for intentionally designing its social media platforms to be addictive and harmful to children and teens. Before that, Meta had especially bad luck in a previous New Mexico case, with orders to pay $375 million in March, then another $567 million in August over failures to warn the public about the dangers its social media platforms, including Instagram, pose to children.

But, surprisingly, Meta’s gigantic payouts (never mind its mountain of legal fees) haven’t turned one of the world’s largest tech companies into a meek player in social media or in news cycles.

Almost weekly, the company is taken to task for privacy-related overreach, most recently over its Muse agentic AI platform and its hit hardware product, Meta Glasses.

Just this week, the company suspended a feature for sharing Muse conversations after researchers found that the links it generated could be accessed by people who weren’t meant to receive them.

Meta has also been fending off cycles of outrage over whether it will add potentially intrusive facial-recognition features to software in its smart glasses. In September, reports surfaced that Meta is at least partially acknowledging the backlash by working on models that don’t include cameras. But those would be in addition to all of the versions of Meta Glasses that still carry them.

Assuming the verdict in New Mexico stands, Meta may pay a large penalty. But it’s unlikely that the case will directly impact its newer business ventures other than serving as a lesson on how not to treat user data on Facebook. The New Mexico case still hasn’t determined what changes, if any, Meta will need to make to comply with the state’s privacy laws and whether that will be a bigger hit to the company than a $25 billion payout.

“What changes a company is a court order that dictates how the product has to work, with someone independent checking,” said Michael Stokes, senior vice president of emerging technologies at Veilant, a data security software and technology company.

For Meta’s newer technologies, including AI and AI-assisted hardware, the ruling would have little impact.

“Settlements written for 2018 don’t cover those devices,” Stokes said.

Meta could continue to pay out billions of dollars in additional cases involving privacy and the effects of its platform design on younger users. Cases are pending in Washington, DC, Florida and Texas, among other places, covering issues such as more Cambridge Analytica fallout and WhatsApp privacy.

For this new verdict, users shouldn’t expect another payout; the money will go to New Mexico’s public school fund.

The sum may sound huge, and in education, it could go a long way, but Stokes said it won’t destroy Meta’s business.

“Meta’s stock went up the day the judgment went down,” he said. “That tells you how the market sees a fine on its own.”

Still, a judgment against the company that could lead to even more litigation can’t be good for Meta. It’s not alone. Many other tech companies, from Google to OpenAI to Snap, are facing similar lawsuits over how they collect or use data.

“The question in every case is the same: Do a company’s privacy and safety promises match how the product works?” Stokes said. “Any company making those promises should expect to be tested on them.”

#2841. In the biotech world, you can’t see everything

A reporter’s unsettling takeaway from You Can See Everything.

Earlier this week, I attended a screening of You Can See Everything, the buzzy new documentary about the disgraced former biotech CEO Elizabeth Holmes.

I went in not really knowing what to expect. I’d avoided spoilers (and won’t be giving anything away here, either!). I was wondering what new information this film could reveal, given how familiar many of us are with the Theranos story thanks to years of media coverage, the best-selling book Bad Blood, and the Hulu series The Dropout, starring Amanda Seyfried.

Like many others who have seen the film, I came away feeling unsettled. But not just about Holmes’s fraud and the somewhat disturbing interactions between the subjects. The film reignited a concern I’ve had about covering science for most of my career: Despite our best efforts, we never can actually see everything.

In case you’re rusty on the details, Elizabeth Holmes dropped out of Stanford University in 2003 to run Theranos, a diagnostics company she founded, when she was just 19. She went on to claim that her company had invented a way to run over 100 diagnostic tests on just a few drops of blood.

Holmes raised hundreds of millions of dollars for Theranos, which was valued at $9 billion in 2014. Forbes named her the youngest self-made female billionaire—she was worth $4.5 billion when she was just 30 years old.

Things started to unravel in 2015, when an investigation by the Wall Street Journal raised concerns by former Theranos employees that the tests didn’t work. Some also said that the company’s device had not been tested properly, and that blood samples had been analyzed on machines from other companies instead. MIT Technology Review named it one of the biggest technology failures of 2015.

Forbes had revised Holmes’s worth to $0 by 2016. That year, a damning government report said that the company’s lab was struggling with basic procedures and gave patients results even when they knew they could be wrong.

In 2018, Theranos, Holmes, and former company president Ramesh Balwani were charged with “massive fraud.” In 2022, she was sentenced to a little over 11 years in prison for defrauding investors. She began serving her sentence in May 2023.

It was around a month before this that filmmakers Nathan Fielder, Lance Oppenheim, and their colleagues began documenting Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, at home with their two young children. I’m not giving anything away when I say it’s an extremely intimate portrayal—at least of how the two adults interact with Fielder, who is also a central character in the film.

As you might know if you’ve seen the trailer, there are plenty of super close-ups. I felt as if I spent most of the film staring straight into the faces of those people. And almost the whole way through, they all seemed to be trying to figure each other out. Holmes is known for her dishonesty, but she invited cameras into her home in an apparent bid to reframe the narrative and position herself as an honest person with an unusual mind. Will anyone believe her?

I couldn’t help feeling some empathy for Holmes, a mother we see preparing to leave her two kids for a lengthy prison sentence. And even though I have long known the facts, Holmes is clearly an effective manipulator. You can see her plant the seeds of doubt in Fielder’s mind. At times, I felt she was convincing me, sitting in my cinema seat. Maybe she really didn’t mean to lie?

But then I remembered how other journalists felt after being taken in by her claims. “I blew it,” Fortune’s Roger Parloff reflected in 2015, a year after profiling Holmes and her company. “I’m pretty sure she lied to me,” STAT’s Matthew Herper wrote recently, having interviewed Holmes in 2015. Even the former employee turned whistleblower Tyler Shultz, who knew the product didn’t work, said that he could “have a five-minute conversation with her and walk away feeling like my work was changing the world,” he said in 2022.

I didn’t cover Theranos in the 2010s. If I’d interviewed Holmes, would I have fallen for it all?

One of the early red flags in the Theranos case was the lack of published, peer-reviewed data. Much of the company’s work was classified as a “trade secret.” Scientists tend to publish their work in journals—a slow process that involves having the work evaluated by often anonymous experts in the field, who provide feedback, highlight questionable conclusions, and often ask for clarification. Peer review is meant to add rigor to the publication process. You should be able to trust published scientific papers.

Scientists aren’t perfect. Some will exaggerate their results or inflate their importance. Some manipulate data to serve a hypothesis. Sometimes these infractions are caught during peer review, but not always. There are plenty of examples of scientific findings that could never be replicated by other labs and ended up collapsing under the weight of real evidence. Many papers ultimately end up with corrections. Some are withdrawn—10,000 research papers were retracted in 2023.

At any rate, there’s been a shift to sharing research in other ways. Today, many scientists opt to publish their work at preprint servers like arXiv, medRxiv, and bioRxiv before they submit them to established journals. Those outlets bypass peer review. So do company press releases, social media posts, and many conference presentations. And I get a lot of my stories from conversations and interviews.

I’ll admit I’ve looked at a scientific finding and thought: A scientist has just said they’ve done that. How can I be sure they really have?

I like to think that I hold myself to high journalistic standards here, as does everyone at MIT Technology Review. I would never publish a story about some new scientific development without doing my own form of peer review, and asking scientists—ideally people I know and trust—what they think of a new claim.

But the film is a stark reminder of how easy it can be to fall for an appealing narrative. While most scientists are, I hope, pretty honest, and Holmes is certainly an outlier, we should all continue to interrogate the claims being fed to us. If you’ve seen the film, let me know what you think!

Mortgage rates are constantly changing, since a major part of how rates are set depends on reactions to new inflation reports, job numbers, Fed meetings, global news ... you name it. For example, even tiny changes in the bond market can shift mortgage pricing.

Here's what's been happening lately. Mortgage rates had been edging lower as Treasury yields came down. Mortgage rates are generally benchmarked to the yield on 10-year Treasury notes, so where the yield goes, rates go, too.

The yield is basically the return investors get on a bond. A bond is a little piece of government or corporate debt; when an investor buys a bond, they're making a tiny loan to the issuer. The issuer agrees to pay them back a set amount over the bond's lifespan (that's the yield), and when the bond matures, the investor gets the bond's original price back. That's why unlike anything-could-happen stocks, bonds are generally considered relatively low risk.

A bond investor who buys a bond at issuance knows immediately: Here's my cost, here's what I'll earn and here's when my initial investment will be returned. When investors sell bonds to each other, things get more interesting, because the market now determines the price, and that shifts the relative value of the yield.

Here's how that works. If you bought a $100 bond with $5 annual yield payments, that yield is 5%. But say you want to sell that bond but can’t find a full-price buyer; the best price you can get for it is $80. The annual yield payments are still $5, but since they're relative to that lower price, the yield is now 6.25%.

For much of the summer and early fall, we saw lots of Treasury bond selling, which drove prices down and yields up. That kept pushing mortgage rates higher. Additionally, tech companies issuing loads of corporate bonds to help fund AI buildout meant that bond investors had a sexier option than Plain Jane Treasuries.

#2822. 8 Things to Know About the iPhone Duo

The iPhone Duo is finally here. Apple's first folding smartphone arrives seven years after Samsung pioneered the category with the Galaxy Fold. That gave Apple plenty of time to refine the hardware behind closed doors rather than in public view—and the results show: The Duo is a pretty dang good foldable.

Whether your Duo just landed on your doorstep or you're still weighing that $1,999 price, here's what you need to know before unfolding it.

The iPhone Duo is a new shape and size for Apple, so many of your apps likely aren't optimized to show up properly on the unique 5.4-inch outer screen or the 7.6-inch inner display. Apple also changed how navigational elements appear on the Duo, aligning them on the right rail instead of centering them on the bottom like every other iPhone.

Apple apps, redesigned for the Duo, look great. Many third-party apps look like the Google Home app in the picture above: a strip down the middle with thick, black bars on the sides. Several major app developers have already optimized their apps for iOS 27.1—Slack, Instagram, DoorDash, and WhatsApp, for example—but I've noticed that even some optimized apps like Instagram can still have broken user-interface elements. It will take time for every app in your library to work perfectly across these new screens.

It might be a good thing that the Duo is back-ordered for several weeks. By the time you snag yours, app developers will likely have caught up to the new device, and you might enjoy a richer folding experience.

Nearly a decade ago, Apple introduced Face ID as the future of the iPhone, eventually replacing the fingerprint-based Touch ID. So it might be confusing to hear that Apple's first folding phone doesn't have Face ID at all and instead relies on the older biometric authentication method. (Likely because Face ID tech takes up too much space in the thin iPhone Duo.) Touch ID never disappeared—Apple has used it on the iPhone SE and still features it on iPads and MacBooks—but this is the first time it has returned on a flagship iPhone.

Using your thumb again will take some getting used to, but it's not a deal-breaker. Some folks will miss the convenience and speed of Face ID, where all you needed to do was stare at the screen, instead of now reaching for the Touch ID sensor on the right side. Others who hated facial authentication might actually like the return of the fingerprint sensor.

Apple also introduced the Dynamic Island a few years ago—the pill-shaped island around the Face ID camera sensors that shows live app activities, like the next turn on your navigation or music currently playing, giving a second job to the camera cutout. Without Face ID, Dynamic Island hasn't disappeared from the iPhone Duo. Instead, Apple moved it to the top-right edge around the outer selfie camera; on the inner display, it appears at the top-right edge when a live activity is in progress.

The iPhone Duo supports MagSafe just like the rest of the iPhone lineup, so you can magnetically stick your favorite phone grip on the back, or attach the phone to a magnetic car dock, bike mount, or wireless charger. One thing to note: If you unfold the Duo and attach it to a car dock or tripod, the phone will tip to one side because the magnet can’t keep it held straight given the uneven weight distribution. Third-party accessory makers could address this with anti-rotation magnets, but it might mean upgrading your current accessories.

Wallets, unfortunately, are the one accessory that won't really play well with the Duo. Yes, you can still attach a MagSafe wallet, but because the Duo is shorter than other iPhones, the wallet sticks out awkwardly past the lower edge. Apple doesn't have an official solution, so if you want to use a magnetic wallet with your iPhone, you'll have to look at third-party accessories.

Everyone knows the rear cameras on your smartphone are better than the selfie camera on the front. One of the best features on a folding phone is the ability to take a selfie with those superior rear cameras and still be able to see a preview with the outer screen.

Don't confuse this with “Duo Preview” as I did, though. That's a feature that lets you show the person you're photographing the camera's viewfinder, so they can make sure they look picture-perfect before you tap the shutter. The setting to enable this is on the left edge of the camera app, where you can also turn on “Kids Cue” to have Snoopy animations play on the outer screen to get the attention of a kid for the perfect photo.

But if you're taking a selfie yourself and want the camera controls on the front screen, all you need to do is launch the camera and flip the phone around—you'll see a slider that says, “Slide to show camera.” Slide it, and the full camera app will show up on the outer screen, so you can switch modes and access Photographic Styles right there and enjoy the higher-quality results from the superior cameras. Doing this also crucially disables the inner screen, so your fingers won't accidentally switch modes.

Filling the inner 7.6-inch screen with two different apps running side by side is incredibly handy and one of the cornerstone features of any folding phone. Whether you're on a phone call and want to pull up a confirmation email, or want to browse two different Safari webpages, you can drag an app from the bottom and pull it to the left or right side to enter the split-screen state.

One trick that isn't immediately obvious is that you can create app pairs. Just tap the middle bar between the two apps once, and it'll glow white, meaning that those two apps are now locked and can be accessed at any moment from the Recent apps menu. You can't save that as an app icon combo, though. This is still very handy if you know you'll always want to open two apps simultaneously on the big screen.

I get it, you're going to want to protect the fragile screens of your $1,999 iPhone. A case is smart—folding phone cases aren't like normal cases, as they usually come in two shells that attach to each side of the phone. You can also totally add a screen protector to the outer 5.4-inch screen.

But for the 7.6-inch matte inner screen, exercise some caution. You might see chatter online that you can “remove” the Duo's nano-texture finish, but it's an “integral” component of the inner display—as Apple says on this support page. “Don't attempt to remove it,” Apple writes. (Apple also has a page on cleaning the nano-texture display properly here.) Attempting to remove the finish is likely a surefire way to void your warranty. Apple doesn't say anything about using third-party screen protectors, but using one could compromise the display performance.

If you scratch or damage the nano-texture finish, Apple says you can take it to any Apple Store or an Apple Authorized Service Provider and have it replaced for $19 (or $49 if the phone is out of warranty). That's not the same as if there's an actual crack in the inner display—that'll run you a cool $999 if you don't have AppleCare+.

The iPhone Duo is a wide phone. If you've complained about the size of modern smartphones and how it's hard to reach the top of the screen, well, you might still have some issues reaching the sides of the Duo. There's some good news, though. Apple lets you split the keyboard to make typing on the inner display a little easier.

Later this year, Apple will also issue a software update bringing Reachability to the Duo. On other iPhones, this brings the top of an app down, making it easier to reach. On the Duo, it will move an app to the left or right so you won't have to stretch those fingers as much.

Have you always wanted to use a stylus on an iPhone? Soon! Steve Jobs is probably rolling in his grave.

Apple will bring support for the USB-C Apple Pencil (and only that Apple Pencil model, not others) in a software update later this year. Paired with the 7.6-inch screen's nano-texture finish, it should make writing and drawing on the iPhone Duo feel paper-like. Sadly, there's no place to store the stylus on the iPhone like you can on an iPad. I'm sure someone will make an accessory for it.

Data released this week reveal a housing market where somehow, nobody's coming out ahead.

Mortgage rates eased downward a little this week, but they're still firmly north of 7%.

The average rate on a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage slid eight basis points to 7.26% APR in the week ending Oct. 22, according to rates provided to NerdWallet by Zillow. (A basis point is one one-hundredth of a percentage point.) We calculate our weekly average using daily APRs recorded over the past five business days.

Mortgage rates have been above 7% for roughly a month, which is about how long they spent at that level in spring 2024, the last time we saw rates this high. Rates spent significantly longer in 7% territory in fall 2023, and it's starting to look like that's what we may be in for. Major forecasters are uniformly predicting the 30-year will stay at this level into next year.

I could try to give you some kind of but-hey-look-on-the-bright-side cheer. Honestly, though? If there ever was a week to wallow in the state of the U.S. housing market, it might be this one. A slew of new data was released this week, and it truly emphasizes how challenging this market is for every player in it.

Potential home buyers are being stymied by rising mortgage rates, though to be fair, it isn't just about rates. High home prices and limited inventory are making it harder for buyers, too.

I mentioned a couple of recent instances of mortgage rates above 7%, but mortgage rates were above 7% for basically all of the 1970s, '80s and '90s. Back then, though, lower prices (and higher wages relative to those prices) made it easier to afford a home. Thirty years ago, Freddie Mac's average 30-year mortgage rate came in at 7.86%. But the median price of an existing home in October 1996 was just $121,400, according to data from the National Association of Realtors. (That's roughly $258,000 in today's dollars.)

It’s safe to say that home prices have outpaced inflation in the years since. We don't have October 2026 numbers yet, but in September the median sale price for an existing home was $417,500. So today's prices make mortgage rates in the 7% range hurt that much more.

On Tuesday, the NAR released data on pending home sales in September. Pending means an offer's been accepted and the home is under contract, but the deal hasn't closed, so it's a look-ahead figure. With how long it takes to close on a home, September's pending data is likely a preview of October's sales numbers. Spoiler alert: It's not looking great.

In September, the rate of pending home sales decreased 7.3% month over month and 11.3% year over year. And not just at the national level, either. The NAR divides the country into four quadrants, and each saw the same trend: significant decreases from last month and last year's data. There's usually at least one corner where things look a little better, but not this time.

BEST THAT YOU CAN DO: If you are in a position to buy at today's mortgage interest rates (and home prices), at least there's not much competition. That means you can take your time and negotiate. In many local markets, buyers have more leverage than we've seen in a while. Want the seller to pay for points or a temporary rate buydown? Don't be afraid to negotiate.

If home prices are so high, you'd think it's still a good time to be a home seller, right? But the thing about selling your home is that it usually means you're now also a home buyer — and today's rates and prices can be tough even if this isn't your first rodeo.

This morning, Realtor.com released an analysis of Federal Housing Finance Agency data on outstanding mortgages (in other words, mortgages held by current homeowners). As of the second quarter of this year, 49.1% of outstanding mortgages have an interest rate that's 4% or lower.

I know, I know, no one's pitying the sad homeowners with extremely affordable mortgages. But think about the math on selling a home in that situation. Not only are you looking at a mortgage rate that's potentially double; you're also facing home prices that are significantly more expensive. (If you bought or refinanced at a time when you could snag a sub-4% rate, that means you probably also bought when home prices were lower.)

Forget trading up. For many would-be sellers, even moving to an equivalent home would significantly raise their housing costs. That potentially leaves a big cohort of homeowners in homes they feel like they can't afford to sell, even if they'd really like to leave.

BEST THAT YOU CAN DO: If this sounds like you, talk to a couple of local real estate agents to get an idea of what price your current home might fetch if you were to sell. If it looks like you'd net enough profit after paying off your current mortgage to take a sizable bite out of your next mortgage, that would allow you to borrow less. A more manageable loan balance could make taking on a pricier home — even at a higher mortgage rate — a little more palatable.

Lower mortgage interest rates would certainly help get the market moving, but more inventory — particularly, more affordable housing — would be a real game-changer. There's certainly enthusiasm for it; new construction was a huge part of the enormous bipartisan housing bill that became law this past July.

But at the moment, home builders are struggling, too. October data released Monday by the National Association of Home Builders and Wells Fargo found that builder sentiment is at its lowest level in 14 years. To be fair, they have plenty of reasons to be unhappy. Inflation has pushed up material costs, immigration enforcement has exacerbated persistent labor shortages and higher mortgage rates are sidelining potential buyers.

Speaking of rates: Builders have to borrow, too, and rising interest rates push up their costs. So in a moment where new construction would really help, building is slowing down. On Tuesday, the U.S. Census Bureau released September data on building permits (basically, applications for new construction) and housing starts (exactly what it sounds like, construction beginning). Both were down month over month and year over year.

It feels pretty dim, but maybe this is the bright side: It's tough for everyone, so no one's singled out. If you're feeling frustrated trying to buy or sell (or build) a home, it's not you — it's the market.

The Premier League's appeal process will ultimately determine whether the commission's findings stand, while any punishment will be addressed through the relevant disciplinary proceedings.

For Lahm, however, the broader principle is clear: the quality of a team's football should not exempt its club from accountability when financial regulations are breached.
