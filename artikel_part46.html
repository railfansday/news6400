#4146. Wall Squats, Planks Once a Week May Help Lower Your Blood Pressure

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Isometric exercises, even if done only occasionally, could help improve blood pressure, according to a new study.

Researchers report that study participants who did these types of exercises once or twice a week saw reductions in blood pressure over a short period.

They added that participants who engaged in isometric exercises three times a week maintained improvements in blood pressure over a longer period.

The researchers said that previous studies had indicated that isometric exercise training reduces blood pressure more than other forms of exercise and even some drug treatments.

However, they said previous research had not established how often isometric exercise training needs to be performed to maintain these health benefits.

Isometric exercises involve a person holding a pose for a few minutes at a time. Examples include wall squats and floor planks.

The researchers said their study provides important information for people who don’t have time to exercise daily.

The researchers wrote that the study “offers evidence that lower frequency [isometric exercise training] can help maintain short-term [blood pressure] reductions, providing a pragmatic option for individuals with limited time or adherence challenges.”

They published their findings on September 29 in BMJ Open Sport and Exercise Medicine.

Experts not involved in the research said it provides helpful guidance for people who want to improve their blood pressure.

“The main takeaway is that you don’t need to exercise every day — or even several times a week — to experience significant blood pressure benefits from isometric exercise,” said Michael Fredericson, MD, a professor of orthopaedic surgery at Stanford Health Care.

“We already knew that performing isometric training for even just a few weeks can lower blood pressure,” added Amanda Paluch, PhD, an associate professor of kinesiology at the University of Massachusetts Amherst and chair of the American Heart Association’s Physical Activity Committee. “This study adds to the research by looking at how much training is needed to maintain those gains.”

“It is encouraging to see measurable improvements in blood pressure with a relatively brief and infrequent exercise routine,” said Jennifer Wong, MD, a cardiologist and medical director of Non-Invasive Cardiology at MemorialCare Heart and Vascular Institute at Orange Coast Medical Center in California. “These findings suggest that even small, consistent efforts can make a meaningful difference.”

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that nearly half of adults in the United States have high blood pressure. That’s about 120 million people.

The condition, also known as hypertension, increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.

High blood pressure occurs when the force of blood pushing through blood vessels is consistently too high.

Blood pressure readings consist of two numbers. One is the systolic pressure (top number), which measures pressure when your heart beats. The second is diastolic pressure (the bottom number), which measures the pressure between beats.

Treatment usually involves medications such as beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors, as well as lifestyle factors such as a healthy diet and exercise.

In their study, the researchers aimed to determine how much isometric exercise was needed to lower and maintain blood pressure.

The researchers recruited 100 adults with an average age of 37. Subjects included male and female participants with normal-to-high-normal systolic blood pressure (120–140 mm Hg) who engaged in less than 150 minutes per week of moderate physical activity.

The participants also had no injuries or illnesses, were not taking and had not previously taken blood pressure medication, were not smokers, and did not consume more than 14 units of alcohol per week.

The participants were divided into five groups. One group served as a control and did no isometric exercise, while the other four groups followed the same four-week home-based lower-body isometric training program.

The regimen consisted of four sessions of isometric wall squat exercise, separated by two minutes of seated rest, performed three times a week.

During the next four weeks, the participants were split into four training schedules.

One group continued to exercise three times a week. A second group exercised twice a week, while a third group exercised once a week. The remaining group stopped the exercises entirely.

Blood pressure, heart rate, and other cardiac measurements were taken at the start of the study and after four weeks and eight weeks.

The researchers reported that the four groups who completed the initial four-week exercise routine showed significant reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared to the control group.

Over the next four weeks, the researchers found no statistically significant difference in diastolic blood pressure among the groups.

However, the researchers reported that participants who continued isometric exercise training three times a week achieved the greatest reduction in systolic blood pressure compared with baseline. That group also maintained improvements in other cardiac measures.

The group that had stopped doing isometric exercises after the initial four-week period showed a reversal of their blood pressure improvements.

The researchers wrote that the study “demonstrates that while lower frequency [isometric exercise training] may be sufficient to maintain [blood pressure] reductions over the short term, higher frequencies are required to preserve these benefits and the associated vascular and cardiac autonomic adaptations.”

The researchers noted that the eight-week study period was limited and that further research is needed. They also acknowledged that the participants were from a relatively small cross-section of people.

There are several ways isometric exercises may help lower blood pressure.

“Repeat isometric exercises (contracting a muscle and holding a position) seem to change how the blood vessels regulate pressure between workouts,” said Kevin Shah, MD, a cardiologist and program director of Heart Failure Outreach at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California.

“Blood pressure comes down to how hard the heart pumps and how much resistance the blood meets in the vessels,” added Paluch.

“Research suggests isometric training works mainly on that second part. It helps your blood vessels relax and open up, so blood flows through more easily.”

“Isometric exercise seems to promote cardiovascular adaptations that can lower blood pressure,” explained Fredericson. “Over time, this training can enhance the cardiovascular system’s ability to regulate blood pressure.”

Experts interviewed by Healthline say that the wall squats used in the study are one of the most beneficial isometric exercises.

They also recommend static leg extensions, handgrip exercises, planks, and exercises that involve sustained muscle contraction.

“Start slowly, with manageable goals,” Wong said. “Even one or two short sessions per week can be a starting point,” she said. “Begin with brief holds, allow adequate rest between sets and sessions, and gradually build up as strength and comfort improve. The goal is to establish a routine one can maintain consistently.”

Paluch recommended starting small and keeping it simple. She says 10 to 20 minutes per session is sufficient.

She suggested four 2-minute holds of a wall sit or handgrip squeeze with 2 minutes of rest between each hold. Those can be done two to three days a week.

“The message is that consistency matters,” she said. “If you’re able to maintain three times a week, that appears to be the best way to maintain the gain, but it’s not an all-or-nothing situation.”

“You don’t have to maintain the initial pace forever to get something out of the training, especially when life gets in the way and adherence or time becomes a challenge,” Paluch added. “Even getting in one day a week is better than nothing.”

For those just getting started with exercise, Shah recommended starting with a short walk before adding isometric exercise.

“A 10-minute walk on most days is a manageable first step,” Shah said. “Gradually increase the length of walks and then add strength training. Consistency matters more than an exhaustive routine that is not sustainable. Remember: Progress and not perfection.”

Fredericson advised starting slowly and building from there.

“For someone completely inactive, even 5 to 10 minutes of movement per day is a meaningful starting point,” he said. “Walking is an excellent option because it requires little to no equipment or preparation. From there, they can incorporate strength training two or three days a week.”

#4145. Ethereum delays Glamsterdam test after security devs flag risk to existing contracts

Ethereum developers have called off Monday's Glamsterdam upgrade on the Hoodi test network after security researchers identified a problem that could change how existing smart contracts handle failed transactions, potentially allowing errors to become permanent.

The upgrade was due to activate on Oct. 26 at 17:42:48 UTC. Developers postponed it during Thursday's All Core Devs Execution meeting to investigate how a proposed change to Ethereum's gas accounting interacts with contracts already running on the main network.

The concern centers on EIP-8037, which changes how Ethereum charges for creating new records in its database. The proposal is intended to limit the growth of that database as Ethereum increases the number of transactions its network can process.

Security firm ChainSecurity published an analysis on Oct. 13 showing that the change, combined with EIP-8038, could alter how contracts respond when another contract they call runs out of gas, or the fees used to pay the network’s operators.

Some contracts are designed to catch a failed operation and continue processing. They may record that an attempt failed, disable further attempts or move to another step. Developers sometimes rely on the remaining gas being too low for those actions to finish, causing the entire transaction to reverse instead.

Glamsterdam changes that calculation. Under the proposed rules, gas used to create certain records can be restored when those changes are undone. That can leave enough gas for a contract to complete a failure-handling operation that previously would have reversed.

There is also a risk in the opposite direction. ChainSecurity calculated that creating a new storage slot could require 110,020 gas when a separate allowance is exhausted, compared with 22,100 under the old rules. A contract using an existing gas budget might fail while carrying out an otherwise valid operation.

The findings concern how existing software would behave under proposed rules. ChainSecurity has demonstrated the mechanisms but has not publicly established that a major production protocol is exploitable.

Glamsterdam brings several changes to Ethereum, including a new system for choosing block builders and improvements intended to let the network handle larger blocks.

It completed its Sepolia testnet activation on Oct. 6. Hoodi was the next public rehearsal, alongside a planned increase in its gas limit to 200 million — which has also been postponed now.

But if you spent the second week of October scrambling for a missing 1099 at midnight or frantically find yourself stumbling toward an important deadline, you probably don’t realize why this deferral tactic happens over and over.

Most advice on procrastination treats it as a scheduling problem. Experts tell you things like download an app, use an artificial intelligence (AI) agent to coordinate your schedule, set a timer, or break the task into smaller chunks. These strategies might work, but they treat symptoms, not the underlying cause of your productivity grief. Imagine if your doctor handed you painkillers for recurring headaches without asking when the headaches occurred; wouldn't you be suspicious? Eliminating procrastination requires the same root cause analysis.

Procrastination isn’t about laziness. It is intentional task deferral when deadlines matter, despite knowing the delay may hurt your performance, reputation, or peace of mind. The psychological costs of procrastination are real. Procrastination is consistently linked to lower-quality work (Kim & Seo, 2015), and in a large study following more than 3,500 Swedish university students, the researchers concluded that higher procrastination predicted lower mental health, poorer sleep, and more angst, even months later (Johansson et al., 2023). So why do smart, otherwise productive people keep procrastinating?

Part of the answer is that procrastination often works just fine, or, at least, that’s what you believe. When you pull off a last-minute win, the habit is rewarded (neurologically) and becomes more likely next time. Students sometimes use deadline pressure as a motivator and even turn delay into a game, waiting until the last minute to test their own abilities (Schraw et al., 2007), but to the chagrin of these hopeful procrastinators, the evidence shows that deferral strategies do not actually improve performance. The feeling of efficiency was real. The actual efficiency was wishful thinking.

A deeper dive into the causes of procrastination reveals a pattern of self-doubt about one's competence and abilities (Covington, 1984). That sounds illogical, because delay makes failure more likely, not less. The contradiction disappears once you understand self-worth. Everyone wants to feel competent and to be seen by others as capable. We judge our competence not only by results but also by how we interpret the reasons why we achieved those results. Two people can earn identical outcomes and walk away with different conclusions. A first-time author who publishes a book feels validated; a veteran author with the same book may feel defeated because results don’t match prior achievement. Falling short of our own standards, especially after real effort, produces guilt, shame, and humiliation. Covington, creator of self-worth theory, showed why avoiding perceptions of incompetence is paramount to acknowledging any other personal limitation. Covington cautioned that “one’s first priority is to act in ways that minimize the implications of failure, namely, that one lacks ability” (1984). If we procrastinate and fail, we rationalize that it’s because of the delay, and not because of any personal shortcoming.

Procrastination is a mask. If you wait until the last minute and fail, you tell yourself, "If I had started earlier, this wouldn’t have happened." The failure belongs to the strategy, not to you. If you wait and succeed, your self-image actually increases because you imagine what you could have done with real effort. Either way, your ability is never in question because procrastination becomes a winning formula to avoid a loser mentality. Procrastination has cousins that serve the same purpose: setting impossibly high goals, setting goals so low they require no effort, defensive pessimism, and using a "wooden leg," which is a single excuse that explains away a lifetime of underachievement. All of them redirect attention away from the self and keep self-worth intact. We would much rather assess our shortcomings as a consequence of inept strategy than admit the reality of incompetence.

#4138. Low-Energy Diet Plus Structured Exercise Reverses Early Onset Diabetes

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

A new study has found that a combination of a low-energy diet and structured exercise reversed early onset type 2 diabetes in about half of participants.

The research focuses on adults under 45 years old who developed type 2 diabetes early in life. The authors presented their findings at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) Annual Meeting, held September 28 to October 2. The study was published on October 1 in The Lancet Regional Health Americas.

Type 2 diabetes is a condition in which the body becomes less able to respond to insulin or has difficulty producing it, leading to elevated blood sugar and low energy. If untreated or not properly managed, it can lead to serious complications such as vision problems, kidney disease, and nerve damage.

These findings offer hope for better treatment options for people with this condition, which the authors say is known for causing faster health decline when compared to later-onset diabetes.

The study was conducted at three centers in England and Canada, linked with the University of Leicester, McGill University, and the University of Alberta.

Researchers selected adults ages 18 to 45 who had early onset type 2 diabetes and obesity but were not using insulin therapy.

A total of 96 participants were randomly divided into two groups: one received usual care, and the other took part in a program combining a low-energy diet with supervised and independent exercise.

The intervention group followed a strict diet of 800-900 calories per day, consisting of 30% protein, 50% carbohydrates, and 20% fat. Participants were encouraged to drink at least 2 liters of calorie-free fluids daily and were given a fiber-based laxative if needed.

For the first two weeks, the diet was entirely based on meal replacements (Optifast). From week 3 to week 12, the same calorie limit was maintained, with greater food variety, including one meal per day made from regular foods such as protein sources, one portion of fruit, and two portions of non-starchy vegetables.

The exercise program involved supervised aerobic and resistance training twice a week and one session of independent exercise per week during the first 12 weeks. After this, from weeks 13 to 24, participants followed a weight-maintenance diet and exercised independently.

Participants stopped all glucose– and blood pressure-lowering medications at the start of the diet unless they had a specific kidney condition called albuminuria (high levels of a protein called albumin in the urine).

The main goal of the study was to determine how many participants achieved remission of their diabetes after 24 weeks. Remission was defined as having a blood sugar level below the diabetes threshold (measured by glycated hemoglobin or HbA1c less than 6.5%) and not taking any diabetes medication for at least 12 weeks.

The study also examined secondary outcomes such as fitness levels, body composition, and heart health.

After 24 weeks, 54% of participants in the diet and exercise group achieved remission, compared with only 4% in the usual care group.

They also lost weight. The diet and exercise group lost an average of 8.4 kilograms (about 18.5 pounds), while the usual care group lost only 1.2 kilograms (about 2.6 pounds).

After adjusting for various factors, the diet-and-exercise group lost about 7.5 kilograms more than the control group.

Additionally, most of this weight loss was fat, with about 6.7 kilograms of fat lost at 24 weeks, which the researchers note is important for improving diabetes.

According to the researchers, the intervention helped preserve muscle mass, which is usually lost during weight loss. This is significant because maintaining muscle is important for long-term health, strength, and metabolism.

They further highlighted that early onset type 2 diabetes can cause serious health problems at a young age.

However, their study shows that a combined approach of diet and exercise can not only help people lose weight and improve health but can also lead to real remission of diabetes, without needing medication.

This is important because early onset type 2 diabetes usually worsens quickly and is harder to manage than diabetes that starts later in life.

The researchers additionally believe their approach could be especially helpful for younger adults who may be planning to have a child, as managing diabetes early is critical for health during and after pregnancy.

The study’s success in a multi-ethnic population also adds to its significance, showing the program could work for diverse groups.

The researchers noted that most people in the diet-and-exercise group experienced side effects early in the program, including constipation, headaches, dizziness, and fatigue. However, these were mostly mild and resolved over time.

Avery Zenker, a registered dietitian and medical writer, explained the importance of this work. “We have evidence that type 2 diabetes doesn’t have to be an inevitably progressive disease,” Zenker told Healthline.

“This can give hope for people with diabetes and help empower them to make impactful lifestyle changes.”

Zenker, who was not involved in the study, added that type 2 diabetes remission is not only possible but more likely in younger people who tend to have a shorter disease duration and a less complicated health history.

Jennifer Pallian, BSc, a registered dietitian and founder of Foodess, who wasn’t involved in the research, said that you can put these findings into action right now.

First, you can start a lower-calorie diet. “A reasonable target used in the research was to reduce daily calorie intake by about 500–750 calories, with the goal of losing at least 5% of starting body weight,” she told Healthline.

Zenker noted there are other factors to consider besides the number of calories. “There’s a big difference between a low calorie diet that’s predominantly whole foods compared to one that’s mostly ultra-processed and nutrient-poor,” she said.

“It’s important to consider other recommendations like whole foods, high fiber, and reduced intake of ultra-processed foods.”

Along with making dietary changes, Pallian advised working toward getting at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week. “It also helps to make the plan measurable,” she said. “Set specific nutrition and activity goals, keep track of what and how much you eat, and record your physical activity too.”

Pallian suggested taking note of situations where it’s harder to stay on track so you can spot patterns and make adjustments.

Zenker further noted the importance of starting small and not feeling as if going to the gym is the only way to get exercise.

“Some simple ways one might start to increase daily activity could include adding a short body weight routine in the morning, taking a short walk after a meal, or dancing to music at home,” she said. “Even activities like yard work or cleaning the house can count as physical activity.”

Finally, Pallian advised regular visits with a healthcare professional to monitor blood sugar and blood pressure. These values may improve with weight loss, which may require your doctor to adjust your medication regimen.

#4135. Malaysia, Thailand join Singapore’s cross-border stablecoin push

BLOOM seeks to cut costs by helping financial institutions coordinate cross-border use of stablecoins and tokenized deposits.

Bank Negara Malaysia and the Bank of Thailand joined Singapore’s BLOOM initiative on Friday as policymaker members.

BLOOM, short for Borderless, Liquid, Open, Online, Multi-currency, is a public-private initiative that intends to help financial institutions use stablecoins and tokenized deposits for cross-border payments.

According to the Monetary Authority of Singapore, BLOOM aims to cut costs and help institutions bring services to market faster by reducing the need for institutions to set up arrangements with each counterparty or market.

MAS launched BLOOM in October 2025, building on Project Orchid, which began in 2021 to explore uses for a digital Singapore dollar. BLOOM’s work also covers corporate cash management, trade finance and payments executed by AI agents within predefined limits.

BLOOM’s participants include Airwallex, Mastercard, Ripple, Visa and the World Bank Group.

Bank Negara Malaysia separately announced in February that three initiatives would test ringgit stablecoins and tokenized deposits for domestic and cross-border wholesale payments in 2026.

#4131. Letter From the Editor-in-Chief: Is Worrying About Sleep Keeping Us Awake?

  • A new study analyzing wrist tracker data from 95,000 people found that higher amounts of REM and deep sleep were linked to a lower risk of 83 diseases combined, including dementia, hypertension, and Parkinson’s.
  • Nearly 70% of Americans say anxiety disrupts their sleep – and for some, the sleep trackers meant to help may be making it worse, a phenomenon researchers call orthosomnia.
  • Healthline’s Editor-in-Chief Robin Hough shares why he’s taking the data, leaving the anxiety, and still setting his alarm for a very insistent 8-year-old.

As a busy, sleep-deprived, middle-aged father of two young kids, including an irrepressible 8 year old ball of energy who is, without fail, up and ready to take on the world at 6 o’clock every morning (usually with a steady stream of questions about dinosaurs, the natural world and the origins of the universe in his back pocket), do I really need another set of headlines informing me that not only am I falling short in getting the requisite 40 winks a night but I’m also at risk of 83 diseases into the bargain? 

Apparently, I do. 

Another day, another headline-grabbing sleep study lands, this time linking a greater amount of sleep – specifically sleep of the rapid eye movement (REM) variety, also known as dream sleep – to a lower risk of dozens of diseases.

What a 95,000-Person Study Reveals About Sleep

The recent study published in PLOS Medicine found that REM sleep is linked to a lower risk of 83 diseases, including dementia, Parkinson’s, and multiple sclerosis (MS). As an added bonus, the study also found that a higher amount of deep sleep was associated with a lower risk of seven health conditions, including depression and type 2 diabetes. 

So far, so alarming for a self-confessed “bad sleeper”. 

Yet also fairly unsurprising. The accepted science, combined with anecdotal experience, has long told us that the right amount of good quality sleep corresponds directly with improved short and long-term health outcomes, both mental and physical. Evidence suggests that 7-9 hours of sleep per night is typically the sweet spot for a healthier life. Where this particular study differs from those that have come before is that scientists used wrist trackers to gather much more detailed information over a multi-year timeline about participants’ sleep stages, including irregular sleep patterns and variations in sleep duration. The findings were drawn from real-world data from more than 95,000 participants in the UK Biobank database.

Orthosomnia: When Tracking Your Sleep Causes Anxiety

Now, as the aforementioned and self-proclaimed VBP (very busy person – if you know, you know), who works long hours across UK and US timezones for the day job whilst serving as a sub-par encyclopedia for the real boss (the aforementioned 8 year old) outside of work, the general demands and pressures of daily life are more than sufficient to make consistently good sleep something that is all too often out of reach. 

I know this to be true because the wearable I use reminds me of the fact with cold, hard data on a daily, if not hourly, basis. One of the first things my wife and I do each morning (after fending off existential questions from a third grader) is hawkishly plug into our Oura app to trade sleep scores and discuss what did or didn’t contribute to our invariably bad or, very rarely, good night’s sleep. Deep sleep, REM sleep, resting heart rate, heart rate variability, latency, even sleep debt – a laundry list of data points that we pore over in a futile attempt to make our sleep consistency optimal whilst living a life that doesn’t readily allow for it. Scientists have even given this phenomenon a name – Orthosomnia – where the anxiety of analyzing and perfecting sleep scores actually disrupts rest and intensifies poor sleep, and in the most extreme cases, insomnia.

It turns out we’re not alone. Results of a Gallup poll from 2024 – with the rather on-the-nose headline “Americans sleeping less, more stressed” – showed Americans report averaging 6.5 hours of sleep per night, comfortably below the recommended baseline of 7+ hours. Perhaps more tellingly, in a survey from 2024, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine found that nearly 70% of Americans report losing sleep due to anxiety.

Which brings me back to my original point. Anxiety-induced stress makes for the perfect bedfellow (pun intended) for a bad night’s sleep. The kind of anxiety-induced stress that can be both caused and amplified by obsessing over wearables data and a steady torrent of sleep-related content that aims to scaremonger rather than inform. 

Why Empathy Matters in Health Information – and in Sleep

In fairness to the UK Biobank study, it is unequivocally important and unique research steeped in actionable real-world data and peer-reviewed medical science. And yet we live in a world increasingly awash with AI-generated content, sporting sensationalist headlines and powered by algorithms designed with an inherent imbalance to engage rather than empower. In that environment, gold-standard scientific work like this is all too often reduced to the lowest common denominator. 

If you’ll indulge a minor tooting of our own horn here, this is precisely the inverse of how the team of wonderfully talented editorial misfits at Healthline Media thinks about and creates health information. We take research-backed science and our embedded clinical rigor and apply it to every aspect of our work. But we never lose sight of the fact that behind every study, there’s someone lying awake in the early hours running the math on their own risk factors, and that what they need in that moment is recognition that we are all at different stages on that journey, and a helping hand along the path to better health. 

Life isn’t linear, sleep isn’t linear, and health is certainly not linear. Some nights I’ll get my seven hours. Plenty of others I won’t. And that is ok. Because either way, my alarm clock has a favorite dinosaur and a nonnegotiable 6 a.m. start time, and no peer-reviewed study or scary headline is going to change that. So I’ll take the data and the empathy, leave the anxiety, and see you all again at dawn.

#4129. Ukraine’s military robots are getting tires that can’t go flat

Published Oct 16, 2026 11:02 AM EDT

Tough times can fuel innovation, and battle-hardened Ukraine has demonstrated that fact of life more than most places in recent years. Since Russia’s invasion in February 2022, the country has utilized drones and robotics in ways not before seen in large-scale warfare. More recently, Ukraine has introduced a variety of remotely controlled ground vehicles tasked with entering contested territory and resupplying areas or even evacuating wounded troops. But while those wheeled robots help keep soldiers off the front lines, they are also vulnerable. Their air-filled tires are particularly vulnerable, since they can be shot at or break when rolling over barbed wire, shrapnel or even rugged debris. To solve that issue, engineers from a pair of Ukrainian companies developed a new type of 3D-printable tire that doesn’t require any air at all. And it’s already being deployed on front lines.

The airless wheel is the result of a collaboration between two Print&Live UA, and iSCALE 3D. Their design is proposed to be built for Uncrewed Ground Vehicles (UGV) that come in various shapes and sizes depending on their task. Instead of a rubber casing filled with pressurized air, these new wheels are 3D printed around a lightweight internal lattice made from durable elastomers and composite polymers. The lattice flexes and springs back over rough terrain, essentially doing the cushioning work that air pressure does in conventional tires, but  without the risk of a  sudden debilitating puncture.

The tires also feature a rounded tread and enclosed sidewalls which are meant to keep mud, stones and ice from packing in. According to Print&Live UA and iSCALE 3D, the result is  a wheel set far lighter than Western airless alternatives and that keeps working even after taking some damage. Fewer blown out tires, they say, could mean more lives saved.

“The most important mission of a UGV is not spectacular—it is bringing a wounded person back, or water, medicine and supplies forward,” the companies write on their websites. “Every immobilized vehicle puts people at risk. That is the real purpose of this development.”

“One puncture and the mission ends where the machine stops,” they add.

In addition to the airless design, the 3D printing process makes the new wheels more adaptable. Each set can be tinkered with and adjusted to best suit the type of vehicle they will be equipped on, as well as for the specific mission type they might engage in. Stiffness, shock absorption, and overall geometry can all be tweaked on a computer. The process requires large, industrial-scale printers most hobbyists won’t have in their garage, but it’s still a much smaller, nimbler approach than an entire factory built to roll out one specific product.

“Because the geometry is digital, stiffness and damping are programmed for each UGV type and payload, and a design upgrade is nothing more than a new file,” the companies write.

The engineers also designed these wheels with the chaos of a battlefield in mind. Each two-piece rim set lets soldiers swap out a damaged tire without the tire-changing machines or presses a conventional repair would need. That flexibility means soldiers can repair or swap tires with simple hand tools in battlefield areas where full workshops aren’t available.

Airless UGV wheels are just the latest in a growing list of creative fixes forced by the Russia-Ukraine war. The constant need for better drones and robots has turbocharged the country’s tech sector, which has grown from a handful of defense companies before the invasion to hundreds today. Most of the companies building out the new hardware are Ukrainian, but the war has also attracted a number of American defense startups that have set up shop there, drawn in by the change to their designs in the real world. The country’s capital city  has attracted so much tech sector interest that some have even ascribed it the moniker: “Mil-Tech Valley.”

#4128. Inside the Lives of Lady Preppers

Women's Health may earn commission from the links on this page, but we only feature products we believe in. Why Trust Us?

They’re stockpiling food, packing bugout bags, and learning survival skills—but not necessarily because they think the world is ending.

THERE’S A WELL-KNOWN maxim among preppers—particularly the small but growing subset of female preppers—that you prep for Tuesday, not doomsday. On the day I interview Krystal C., a 37-year-old military vet turned prepping influencer in Utah, it is a Monday, and I am not prepared for that day or the one after it. That morning, I left my phone at home as I dropped my daughter off at daycare, and I haven’t seen my keys—both car and home—in weeks. (I’ve been stealing my husband’s set or simply going without.) Unlike me, Krystal has been prepping since childhood.

She learned prepping from her grandparents, although they didn’t use that term. “We called it being responsible and deliberate living,” Krystal says. It’s a sentiment she shares with Lisa Bedford, author of the Survival Mom book and blog and de facto founding mother of the female prepper movement. “Even to this day, 17 years later, I don’t really think of it as prepping,” the 50-something content creator says. “I really think of it as just taking care of my family and being proactive.”

Both women started seriously prepping around 2009, roughly two years before the term prepper entered mainstream consciousness. In 2011, Doomsday Preppers debuted on the National Geographic channel, and that image of preppers—predominantly white men in paramilitary garb, determined to protect their heavily armed bunkers at all costs—has endured. I spoke with 10 women for this story who all told me that while that type of prepper does exist, a more practical, family-focused approach to prepping has emerged in the 15 years since, and it’s a movement led by everyday women—often the mom next door.

Even though prepping is in Krystal’s DNA, she, like many others, got more serious about it after some kind of disaster. For her and Lisa, it was the Great Recession in 2008. For Zani Sunshine, a 45-year-old homesteading prepper—and one of the few highly visible Black female influencers in the prepping community—it was Hurricane Katrina and then the 2016 presidential election. Marcy R.,* another Black female prepper, started cramming her New York apartment with extra food and water after witnessing 9/11. Sue M., a 66-year-old entertainment paralegal in Los Angeles, started prepping due to the COVID-19 pandemic. For other women, like Ambrosia Luzius, a 41-year-old emergency management professional, it was not a once-in-a-lifetime disaster but the ordinary miracle of motherhood that pushed her into prepping.

Amy Edelman, 67, does not consider herself a prepper, but she did coauthor The Emergency Playbook, a how-to guide to everyday preparedness, and has a “zombie closet in the basement” filled with canned goods and vegetarian MREs (short for “meals, ready-to-eat,” originally developed by the U.S. military). When Superstorm Sandy knocked out the power in Amy’s house for a week, she decided to get a few more flashlights and stock some water in her basement. When inflation spiked the price of tomatoes, she went to her local garden center and bought what she needed to grow her own. She has a bugout bag (the colloquial term for the pack of essentials you prepare in order to flee your home) and knows how much it should weigh based on her body weight (15 to 20 percent). But she hasn’t road-tested it by walking three times around the park yet, which is something she recommends in her book.

Despite Amy’s aversion to the “prepper” title, she shares the same goal as pretty much all the other women I interviewed: “to change the reputation around prepping to something that’s more lifestyle-y.” She wants readers, particularly women, to realize that “you can do all this without losing your sense of normalcy.”

But here’s the thing: Female preppers already know that. They reject the notion that they are doing anything unusual. Instead, they see themselves as guided by ancestral common sense, rediscovering one lost art at a time, and their lives are richer—in some cases, literally—for it. The more I talked to these women, the more I realized…they may be right.

Prepping is defined as “the management of stockpiled household items in anticipation of market disruption,” according to a study published in the journal Sociology. In other words, it’s on us: Women are the primary shoppers for the vast majority (78 percent) of U.S. households, per Numerator, a consumer data and market research firm; and moms, in particular, do 80 percent of grocery shopping in households where a spouse or partner is present, according to Pew Research Center. They also take on more of the mental load of household management.

Women tend to be the organizers of diaper bags, the meal preppers, and the schedulers of doctor appointments, Krystal says: “This is all very natural. Women have always been caregivers and nurturers, and that sometimes gets swept to the side.” In fact, it’s the traits least associated with the “typical prepper” lifestyle that Krystal and other female preppers say make women arguably better suited to the task of prepping than camo-clad men doing Red Dawn cosplay. Male preppers “don’t have the same pressures and responsibilities of caring for everyone on a day-to-day micro level,” Zani, the homesteading prepper, says. “They may be the breadwinners, but they’re not usually the ones doing the family management.”

Historically, male preppers have been more visible for their focus on tactical preparedness, like combat and survivalist skills, but Lisa has been an early adopter of what’s now known as “practical preparedness.” This is essentially an extended version of household management that revolves around water and long-term food storage (which includes gardening, canning, and, on occasion, freeze-drying), first-aid supplies, and “everyday” activities, like practicing situational awareness. Lisa started out the way most women preppers do: buying extra shelf-stable groceries for her family. Her husband was in the construction industry during the housing crisis, and she wanted to make sure that if his business went under, their kids would still be able to eat.

The threat of food insecurity also drove Ambrosia to start prepping as a new mom. She’d just finished grad school but, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, had difficulty finding a job. So she and her family had to go on WIC for a few months. Wanting to take back control of caring for her family, she told her husband that she wanted to use 20 percent of their weekly food budget for long-term food storage. “When we did that, it changed so much for us,” she says. “Here we were a family that was struggling. We were going to food pantries, and now I’m giving away food to our neighbors.”

This approach continues to serve Ambrosia and her family well as they’ve been less affected by the supply chain issues, rising inflation, and food shortages of recent years. “If I buy flour today, it is X price. There’s no arguing that tomorrow is X plus one, and next year is X plus two. So if I buy it today and my brain is thinking long-term, I’m saving myself money today, but I’m saving future-me money, too,” Ambrosia says. “That’s the way I’ve told people like, ‘Hey, I’m not crazy.’ I’m not preparing for a shit-hits-the-fan [SHTF] situation. I’m just preparing for the regular future with hiccups.”

Black female preppers, however, are not necessarily prepping only for the occasional layoff. They’re focused on societal collapse, too, Zani says. Prepping is new to her majority Black, female, and urban audience (she currently has more than half a million Instagram followers) because “there’s a lot of things that we have to deal with and think about that white people do not.” When you’re just trying to survive amid the systemic racism and oppression of today, it’s difficult to think about, much less plan for, tomorrow. Still, Zani’s advice to her followers sits squarely in the practical preparedness realm. “I try to press upon them that this is not just for the end of the world,” she says, but that prepping for things like job loss and natural disasters can be helpful for anyone no matter how they feel about what’s going on. “If you do lose your job and you have food in your house, that’s going to help you get through it better.”

Long associated with Second Amendment–sponsored protection (Krystal refers to ammunition as “Freedom Seeds” in one Prepping 101 video), prepping—and most preppers—have been assumed to be politically conservative. Indeed, many are, but over the past 10 years Zani has seen a political shift among preppers. In fact, all the women I spoke to either identified as being on the liberal side of the spectrum or were careful not to share their political views. (For all of the prepping content creators, aside from Zani, it’s bad for business.)

Sue even sees prepping as a way to reach across the aisle. “There’s so much division and hate right now in the world, it’s nice to find common ground with people,” she says. “A lot of these prepper people, I suspect, have very different political views than I do, but that’s not what we talk about.”

Incidentally, the right to bear arms was the one political view shared by all of the women I spoke with, except for Amy, who actually advocates against gun ownership in her book. Another quality preppers tend to have in common is a general distrust of the government, Krystal says. They don’t believe that the government is coming to save them when disaster strikes—or worse, they’re worried that government agencies will seize their preps. “I’ve been doing this on social media for so long, I’ll see people that are like, ‘I’m prepping because Biden ran.’ ‘I’m prepping because Trump ran,’ ” she says. “I remain very apolitical.… If you’re worrying about a certain scenario, prep for it.”

Similarly, Des, a 30-something prepping content creator and single mom in the Northern California suburbs, doesn’t “share too much” of her political views online because the way she sees it, a power outage or flash flood doesn’t care who you voted for. Disaster doesn’t discriminate; it impacts everyone.

But a SHTF situation wouldn’t impact everyone equally. On average, Black Americans receive less Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) relief funding than white Americans, and their communities take longer to recover in the wake of natural disasters. That’s why Zani isn’t afraid to ruffle a few (a lot of) feathers with her political views; the stakes are too high. “As a Black woman, I can’t stay silent, especially since I’m just waking up my audience and this is something that might heavily affect them,” she says. Zani notes that her audience has not had the privilege to prepare because they’re forced to spend the majority of their time, energy, and money simply trying to survive systemic racism and oppression. “That’s why [Black] people aren’t thinking about job loss, natural disasters. It’s just, ‘Where is this country going?’ ”

The urge to prep stems from uncertainty about the future. For many women, including myself, that uncertainty only amplifies when they become moms. Morgan Rogue, 42, a prepping content creator and mom of three in the Texas suburbs, sums it up perfectly: “I’m going to be prepared for anything so they don’t have to go through anything.”

But while many moms are interested in prepping (and have the Costco receipts to prove it), Krystal often hears from these followers that they don’t know how to get started. She tells them, “Your diaper bag is already better than some guys’ bugout bags.” Then she encourages them to take that approach and apply it to other areas in their lives. That, in essence, is practical prepping and why women, particularly mothers, are natural-born preppers.

Morgan has involved her children with prepping “since birth” simply by doing everyday prepper activities, such as packing bugout bags, around them. Now that her kids are older, she gamifies preparedness drills, like seeing who can get out of the house with their bugout bag the fastest.

Des also uses games to pass down preparedness skills (like situational awareness) to her 7-year-old daughter. Her goal is to inspire and empower her daughter, not scare her, so she incorporates things like a prepper version of “I Spy” where she asks her daughter to spot anything that might look out of place when they’re in public. Other times, she encourages her daughter to help her figure out the safest place to park. The goal is to encourage kids to be more vigilant in everyday situations, Des says.

Ambrosia also weaves in preparedness lessons and uses her garden to help teach her 5-year-old daughter where food comes from, how to grow it, and how to prepare it to last longer. She also dresses her daughter in boys’ clothes because they’re more “durable” and “thicker” and makes sure she’s always wearing practical shoes. She teaches her children the importance of having an exit plan in place and a predetermined meeting spot to reconvene if there’s an emergency.

Former New York City resident Marcy now lives “on a family compound” in South Carolina with her four adult children, their spouses, and her grandchildren. Being a mother has always impacted how she’s prepped, but becoming a grandmother has recently changed her approach because she makes sure to stock extra diapers, baby formula, and infant medication. “I tried to get my children to join in, but they said I have enough food for everybody,” she says. “Sometimes, it’s in one ear, out the other.”

Meanwhile, Sue’s 31-year-old son is “not on board at all” with her prepping. “He doesn’t have the worrywart mentality,” she says, “so my son thinks I’m being ridiculous.” Even though he doesn’t live with his parents, Sue and her husband (who has gotten on board) make sure to prepare with him in mind.

“Most preppers feel lonely and isolated in their prepping because our families want nothing to do with it,” says Zani. For these preppers, community—either in person or online—can be a lifeline.

When Des started prepping in 2017, she looked for advice and, she hoped, community on social media. What she found left her feeling “a bit discouraged.” At the time, Des says, the online prepping community was “extremely male-dominated” and often tinged by that trademark “gloom and doom.” But the landscape is changing.

In 2022, a female-focused subreddit r/TwoXPreppers branched off from r/preppers, an ostensibly coed prepping platform where the majority of posts were nonetheless driven by male-centric concerns. Despite its biologically based name, the forum provides a trans-inclusive and pro-LGBTQIA “place where women can talk about prepping for their specific needs.” A month after its creation, it had 7,500 members while the original preppers subreddit boasted 292,000. Now, r/TwoXPreppers has grown in influence—up to 33,000 weekly visitors—while r/preppers has actually shrunk to 137,000.

But prepping platforms aren’t limited to Reddit. In April 2026, a small group of female preppers created the “Women Only Survival Preppers” group on Facebook. In just a few short months, more than 13,000 women (including myself) have joined. “We absolutely need a safe space, and I really think that this group is fostering that,” says Storm, a 57-year-old prepper in Wabash Valley, Indiana, who joined in May.

Given Ambrosia’s background in emergency management, she’d also joined prepper Facebook groups over the years. But she was especially drawn to the women-only group because it reminded her of her grandmother, who’d tried to teach her preparedness skills as a child. Ambrosia now admits she didn’t really listen. “I’m trying to gather all that knowledge, and that’s why I started networking with other women in prepping,” she says. She sees herself as part of this “underbelly movement” of women rediscovering—and, just as important, sharing—forgotten knowledge passed down by the women who came before them. “We’re kind of just relearning it together,” she says. “Women are unique [in that we] don’t learn something to keep it and to knowledge-hoard. I’m learning something, and that day or the next day, I’m teaching another woman.”

Sue joined the group for similar reasons: “I just thought I would really like to get to know other women who are doing this,” she says, adding that she admires how “self-sufficient” some of the other women are. When we speak, she’s been a member of the Facebook group for less than two weeks but has already learned a lot from other women’s posts, particularly about gardening and raising chickens.

Lest you think “Women Only Survival Preppers” is a virtual Lilith Fair at the end of the world, I should remind you that it is, after all, still a Facebook group, where the ordinary pitfalls of human interaction still apply. There have been controversies around LGBTQ issues, debates around abortion and vaccines; in July, one woman was even banned for posting the address of another (a.k.a. doxxing). Still, all the women I interviewed agreed that it was better than the alternative: dealing with men.

For Marcy, the women in the female prepper Facebook groups are more mature and confident and have more common sense than men in similar groups who seem to focus on only one thing. “Everything was about weapons, weapons, weapons,” she says. While firearms are part of prepping for Marcy, having a stable food supply, first-aid skills, and a strong community dynamic is more her focus.

In fact, Marcy left three coed prepper groups “because the men were out of control,” she says. She often read “very degrading and disgusting” comments, and even though they weren’t directed at her, she still felt attacked. Storm and Sue confirm that they’ve also seen no shortage of misogynistic comments in other coed online prepper spaces. Krystal doesn’t even use her last name due to threats she’s received online, and other content creators also talk about pushback from male followers who say that female preppers don’t know what they’re talking about or that they’re not “real” preppers.

The women-only groups also provide a safe space for female preppers to discuss one of the biggest threats to them in SHTF scenarios: sexual violence. Storm doesn’t mince words when she talks about the threat men pose to women. “Forget when shit hits the fan. Women are raped by men every day,” she says. “Do I think it’s going to get worse? Absolutely.” The threat of men is why Sue, after “being very anti-gun for many years,” recently bought a pistol and started taking shooting lessons. “I still don’t think anyone needs a semiautomatic weapon, but…I also think, unfortunately, that’s a skill [gun familiarity] you should have,” Sue says. “[I] hope I never have to use it, but I don’t want to be in a position where I wished I had learned.”

While all of the women I spoke with found their positive prepping community online, Ambrosia is one of a select few with an in-person prepper group, and she’s also encouraged all of her friends to prep. As a millennial, she’s witnessed so many “once-in-a-lifetime” events—terrorist attacks, multiple wars, a Great Recession, a global pandemic, and dozens of climate change–related natural disasters—that they’ve become normal. “When people say, ‘You’re doing all of this, and nothing’s going to happen,’ I’m like, ‘Whoa, where have you been the last 30 years?’ ” Krystal says. “It’s been a rough go.” As a fellow millennial, I have to agree.

To customize my approach to prepping, Lisa told me to begin tracking what I was already eating and how many everyday supplies (toilet paper, laundry detergent, etc.) I used for two to three weeks. Then to ask myself: What can I do to replicate that as long as possible, using food that I can store long-term?

I’ll be honest: I haven’t been that methodical. I’ve bought a few canned goods and cases of water, dabbled in the airtight-food-storage game, started assembling bugout bags for myself, my husband, and our daughter, and taken a class in CPR and first aid.

One weekday night, I noticed my husband opening up a can of black beans from my small but mighty prepping pantry. (I knew it was one of these cans because I’d taken to writing the expiration date on the top with a black Sharpie.) “Did you take this from my stash?” I asked him.

#4127. The Surprising Power of Mindset for Depression

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Devon Frye

You’ve probably heard about the power of mindset when it comes to education. As the developmental psychologist Carol Dweck famously showed, students with a growth mindset tend to do better in school than students with a fixed mindset because they're less likely to give up in the face of setbacks. What you might not know is that new research is showing mindset can have a powerful effect on dealing with negative emotions, or moods.

Suppose you wake up after a poor night of sleep, groggy and irritable. Do you see your mood as malleable, something you can change with a little effort? Or do you see it as fixed, something beyond your control?

Psychologists call these “emotion malleability beliefs.” It turns out that people who believe their emotions are malleable tend to have a greater sense of well-being, fewer symptoms of depression, and more confidence in their ability to manage their emotions.

The reason is that people who think their emotions are malleable tend to use superior cognitive strategies to deal with their moods. One such strategy is “cognitive reappraisal,” the ability to take a different perspective on your mood.

If you wake up groggy and annoyed, you might think, “Well, one bad night of sleep probably won’t destroy my day." In contrast, people with more fixed beliefs tend to use inferior strategies, such as rumination, where you obsess over how bad life is. “Why does this keep happening to me?” is the refrain of those with fixed mindsets about emotions.

I talked with Elizabeth Kneeland, psychology professor at Amherst College and a leader in the field of emotion malleability and depression, to find out more. As she put it, “if you think your emotions are outside your control, will you cope with them differently? Are you at risk for something like depression?”

In the 1990s, the dominant message about depression was that it’s a chemical imbalance in the brain, likely involving the neurotransmitter serotonin. That view went hand-in-hand with the idea that depression is largely outside of our personal control.

Despite the lack of consistent evidence that depressed people have lower serotonin than people who aren’t depressed, the idea quickly became widely accepted among the public.

About ten years ago, Dr. Kneeland began to wonder whether the chemical imbalance view could actually make depression worse. Perhaps it gives patients the mindset that depression is beyond their control—and thus robs them of the cognitive strategies that might turn it around.

Evidence already indicated that there was a correlation between more fixed emotion beliefs and depression. But Kneeland wondered: Does depression make people’s beliefs about their emotions more fixed? Or are people who have fixed beliefs about their emotions more likely to get depressed—or stay depressed?

In one study (Kneeland and Dovidio 2020), she tracked a group of people at high risk for depression: first-year college students. Those who started the semester with more malleable views of emotion had fewer symptoms of depression near the end of it than those who started out with more fixed views.

In another study (Kneeland and Simpson 2022), she worked directly with people who have some experience of depression. Half of the group read a short article about how emotions are malleable; the other half read an article on an unrelated subject. Then everybody wrote a short essay about something bad that happened to them.

Those who received the message that emotions are malleable coped better. They used significantly more cognitive reappraisal, and their bad mood lifted more quickly. “They still get upset,” Kneeland told me, “but they come out of it faster when they’ve gotten the message that emotions can change, that your emotions are under your control.”

Kneeland thinks more fixed beliefs keep people stuck. Depression is, in part, a matter of getting stuck in low moods. It seems plausible that changing your beliefs about emotion might help with depression itself.

When we tell people that their depression stems from a chemical imbalance, are we inadvertently giving them the wrong mindset? Kneeland teamed up with Hans Schroder, a psychologist in the psychiatry department at the University of Michigan, to find out.

In a study published this year in Cognition and Emotion, they recruited nearly 300 people and split them into two groups. Both groups read about a hypothetical patient, Alex, whose depression was caused by an “overactive amygdala,” and both groups were shown a brain scan.

The only difference was the framing. The first group was told that Alex’s amygdala “is not doing its job,” and that something is wrong with her brain. The second group was told that her amygdala “is doing its job,” by signaling that something in Alex’s life is wrong. Her depression, in other words, is a helpful signal.

The first group thought Alex’s depression would last longer, that she had less control over it, and that she would need long-term treatment. The second group thought that she had a better chance of recovering, that she had more personal control over her depression, and that she probably wouldn’t need long-term treatment.

Mindset is not a cure-all, of course. Kneeland emphasized that there may be some conditions in which more malleable beliefs about emotions might actually leave you worse off—perhaps those with a stronger biological basis, such as bipolar disorder or schizophrenia.

The belief that emotions can change might also tip into “I should be able to change my emotions, and I’m failing to,” which could feed depression or anxiety. “A lot of that is an open empirical question about context,” she said.

The work of Kneeland, Schroder, and others leads me to think that seeing depression as a functional signal, not a malfunction, can help alleviate it in more ways than one.

Not only does it lead us to try to find the source of our depression—what depression is trying to tell us about our lives. It also shifts our beliefs about emotions toward the malleable end of the scale, and thus leads us to adopt exactly the kinds of cognitive strategies that are most helpful for working through it.

#4123. Your eyes dilate depending on your thoughts

Published Oct 16, 2026 11:45 AM EDT

Your eyes really can betray your thoughts, but don’t get too self-conscious about it. Researchers in Japan believe that the subtle signs our eyeballs make are more useful to neuroscientists than the interrogation room. Based on their findings recently published in the journal iScience, examining the difference physiological responses from “inner” versus “overt” speech may help us better understand everything from linguistic development in children to the efficacy of silent pep talks.

To learn more, a team at the University of Tsukuba in Honshu enlisted dozens of volunteers for a series of simple counting exercises. After observing the participants at rest while thinking about nothing in particular for a control, researchers then asked them to count sequentially (1, 2, 3…), in number-subject combinations (one dog, two dogs…), and in specific intervals (1, 3, 5…). They also completed each task both silently and aloud. In each phase, the team carefully monitored their pupil dilation.

The results were subtle, but striking. Participants’ pupils dilated during each overt speech task, but this same muscle movement depended across each inner speech directive. Pupils constricted while volunteers silently counted sequentially, dilated for interval counting, and didn’t change at all for number-subject counting. But regardless of the inner speech task, pupil size changes were always smaller than they were during overt speech.

“The results of the present study do not support the initial assumption that inner and overt speech produce equivalent pupillary responses,” the team summarized. “Across the three experiments, overt speech consistently elicited pupil dilation, whereas inner speech did not.”

This collective data indicates that our inner speech isn’t simply the nonverbal counterpart to talking aloud. Instead, it’s a form of communication that appears to correspond to lower levels of brain arousal. If true, measuring pupil dilation may become a valuable tool when attempting to assess internal language and thinking. This could be particularly useful for studying language development in young children and infants who can’t properly describe their experiences to adults yet.

#4122. Cutting Back on Ultra-Processed Foods May Help Ease Depression Symptoms

  • A small study found that reducing ultra-processed food (UPF) intake was associated with improvements in depression symptoms.
  • The findings add to previous research linking high UPF consumption with an increased risk of depression.
  • Researchers are investigating how factors such as nutrition, inflammation, metabolic health, and the gut-brain axis may link UPFs with mood.

Emerging research has linked high consumption of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) with an increased risk of depression. Now, a small study published September 23 in JAMA Psychiatry suggests that reducing UPF intake may help ease depression symptoms.

There is no universally accepted definition of UPFs. However, a widely used classification system known as NOVA defines them as industrial formulations made primarily from substances extracted or derived from foods, often with additives not typically used in home cooking. Examples include soft drinks, packaged snacks, candy, sausages, hot dogs, and pastries. 

Previous research has linked UPFs to an increased risk of depression, anxiety, eating disorders, and other mental health conditions. In the new study, participants reduced their UPF intake by more than 85% and had significantly lower depression scores across three standard measures while following the low-UPF diet.

Lowering ultra-processed food intake may improve depression

This small study included 20 participants who:

  • were between 18 and 80 years old 
  • had a primary diagnosis of depression
  • had a Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) score of at least 5
  • consumed high amounts of UPFs
  • had at least one self-reported metabolic abnormality, such as overweight or obesity, high blood pressure, high cholesterol or triglycerides, or insulin resistance

The participants also had to agree not to change medications, therapy, exercise, or sleep during the study. 

Of the 20 participants enrolled in the study, 19 completed it. The average age was 44. Participants followed both their usual diet and a low-UPF diet during separate phases of the study, with the order randomly assigned.

Participants generally found the low-UPF diet acceptable and manageable. They also had lower depression and anxiety scores during the low-UPF diet phase compared with their usual diet.

On one commonly used depression measure, the PHQ-9, average scores declined from 8.68 during the usual diet phase to 5.53 during the low-UPF phase. 

The study had several limitations, including its small sample size, reliance on self-reported dietary data, and uncertainty about which aspects of reducing UPFs may have contributed to the changes in symptoms.

The researchers also said that larger studies are needed to determine whether reducing UPF intake can improve depression symptoms.

Michelle Routhenstein, Preventive Cardiology Dietitian at Entirely Nourished, who wasn’t involved in the study, agreed, noting that more research is needed to understand whether reducing UPFs directly improves depression symptoms or whether other aspects of the overall diet may be contributing to the observed benefit.

Dara Houp, PsyD, a licensed clinical psychologist with Pathways Psychological Services in NYC, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that it’s important to distinguish ultra-processed foods from processed foods more broadly.

Routhenstein agreed that the distinction is important. “Processing is not inherently negative. Many nutritious foods are processed in some way.” 

“I would not encourage people to simply label a food as ‘processed’ or ‘unprocessed’ and make decisions based on that label alone. There are various proposed mechanisms for how UPFs may affect mood.

An extensive new analysis further confirms the oldest known human footprints in North America date back to the last ice age (about 23,500–21,700 years). To pinpoint their origins, researchers at the University of Arizona relied on cutting-edge methods at the United States Geological Survey (USGS) Radiocarbon Laboratory to examine hundreds of thousands of prehistoric pollen grains extracted from the tracks. New Mexico’s White Sands National Park contains more than stunning vistas and desert dunes. It’s also home to one of the most controversial (and consequential) prehistoric discoveries of the last century. In 2006, archaeologists located footprints inside a dry lakebed that they later determined to be the earliest evidence of humans in North America. But there was a problem. #4110. Social Media is Obsessed With Creating Fat ‘Savings Accounts.’ Should You Be?

RIGHT NOW, there is something that aesthetic dermatologists consider more valuable than any synthetic compound you could inject into your face—more valuable, in fact, than gold.

Yes, dermal fillers like the ones crafted from hyaluronic acid (brand names: Juvederm and Restylane) still remain effective in helping to restructure and add volume to areas of the face that are sagging. But using your own fat to achieve the same effect offers benefits beyond merely replacing what time has taken. In fact, it’s become almost routine in many plastic surgeons offices to liposuction fat from one area of the body and transfer it directly to the face.

However, some patients aren’t using that adipose tissue immediately, opting to “bank” it, creating the equivalent of a fat savings account. “Surgeons will remove extra fat beyond what’s needed for an initial grafting procedure, store it, and save it to have on hand as an insurance policy in the future,” explains Krishna Vyas, MD, a board-certified plastic surgeon at Blechman Plastic Surgery in New York City. This is known as “fat banking” and it's been making waves on social media as patients’ fat deposits are rapidly dwindling thanks to GLP-1 use. It’s transformed what was once considered something to shed at any cost into an aesthetic hot commodity.

Meet the experts: Krishna Vyas, MD, is a board-certified plastic surgeon at Blechman Plastic Surgery in New York City. Lyle Leipziger, MD, is the chief of plastic surgery at North Shore University Hospital and LIJ Medical Center. Anthony Berlet, MD, is a board-certified plastic surgeon in New York.

Ahead, we spoke with several plastic surgeons about how fat banking is transforming facial aesthetics—and what to consider if you’re thinking about it.

“Fat banking refers to the process of having fat removed from your body through liposuction, which is then frozen and stored to be used later for cosmetic procedures that add volume to the face, breasts, or body,” explains Dr. Vyas. It’s typically sucked out of either the abdomen, hips, thighs, flanks, back, or arms, and is “processed to separate it from blood and fluid, placed into sterile containers, and frozen at very cold temperatures,” he adds. The fat is sent to a cryopreservation facility where it's stored in liquid nitrogen until you’re ready to use it, the fat is thawed and injected where you need it.

Fat banking is not a standalone surgery. Instead, it’s typically performed during a procedure that already involves liposuction, such as body contouring, a tummy tuck, or Brazilian butt lift (BBL). Rather than discard the extra fat that was removed, your surgeon will process and freeze it for future use. “The banking step adds minimal time to the surgery you’re already having,” Dr. Vyas assures.

None of this to say that doctors haven’t been using patients’ fat for other purposes before today. Fat grafting or transfer has been routinely used for decades in facial rejuvenation, breast reconstruction, correction of contour deformities, and body enhancement. It refers to a process by which fat is removed and then immediately re-injected into another area of the body during the same procedure. Soft tissue taken from the abdomen, for example, can be placed into the cheeks or lips. Fat grafting has become an increasingly viable alternative to traditional hyaluronic acid filler, as a way to “naturally” re-volumize areas of the face, improve skin quality, and in some cases, slightly lift the brows.

The concept of fat banking, on the other hand, is relatively new, but is becoming an increasingly popular tool for plastic surgeons. The key difference is timing. Here, you’re not using fresh tissue, rather fat that’s been stored for weeks, months, or even years, explains Lyle Leipziger, MD, chief of plastic surgery at North Shore University Hospital and LIJ Medical Center.

Like sushi, fresh fat is usually best, and comes with the highest opportunity of survival. But studies show that stored fat samples will remain sterile and usable if properly preserved for up to two years. That said, Dr. Vyas explains that surgeons who do offer fat banking will most likely use the stored tissue within 3 to 12 months. “How well the fat performs after thawing depends heavily on how it was harvested, frozen, and stored in the first place, which varies from provider to provider,” he adds.

Beauty brands, dermatologists, and plastic surgeons have done their part in helping to raise awareness of one of the biggest consequences of rapid weight loss from GLP-1s—volume loss and skin laxity—which can significantly speed up the aging process. It’s an interesting paradox: While fat may be undesirable in certain parts of the body, in the face, it can keep you looking youthful. And since you can’t pick and choose where you’ll shed fat when you lose weight, fat banking has gained significant traction on social media as an antidote to GLP-induced laxity and volume loss.

Trouble is, for these patients, it may be too late. “It turns out that the fat patients are rushing to bank may not be their best fat to begin with,” explains Dr. Vyas. There is literature suggesting that body weight affects the quality of adipose-derived stem cells, one of the cells responsible for whether a fat graft actually survives and integrates with its new tissue. “Fat harvested from patients at higher body weights contains stem cells that are biologically compromised, making it less able to form new blood vessels, slower to proliferate and migrate, lower in overall viability, and more inflammatory in their genetic profile,” says Dr. Vyas. Translation: Once you’ve lost a significant amount of weight, banking may not be your best bet.

That’s why some doctors recommend that patients bank their fat before they shed excess pounds. It’s not make-or-break, though. “In most patients, even those who have lost a significant amount of weight or are taking GLP-1 medications, there’s usually still enough fat available somewhere on the body for harvesting,” says Anthony Berlet, MD, a board-certified plastic surgeon in New York. The fat cells are there, they’re just smaller and more concentrated. But Dr. Vyas says it’s still worth having a conversation with your plastic surgeon well in advance of banking to make an informed decision.

For someone who loves the idea of being able to use their own fat without having to go through the process of surgery every time they need a touch up, fat banking can be an incredibly appealing option. Still, the process remains a niche service. “It’s an intriguing concept, but the science has not caught up to the marketing,” says Dr. Vyas. He explains that though the banked fat is viable for a certain amount of time when properly preserved, “the freezing process fundamentally changes the tissue since most of the fat cells do not survive, and the stem cells that make fat grafting work are significantly diminished." Additionally, with no standardized, widely accepted protocol for freezing and storing fat, banking remains outside common surgical practices.

And it ain’t cheap. Though there is no standard pricing, cost depends on the provider, the processing method, the volume of fat being banked, and ongoing storage fees. However, the American Society of Plastic Surgeons estimates that fat banking will set you back anywhere from $2,000 to $8,000, plus an additional $200 to $1,000 to keep the fat stored in a cryopreservation facility. Add to this the cost of reinjection down the line with your provider.

Plus, despite the trendy appeal, the overwhelming body of evidence suggests that fresh fat grafting, performed at the time of the desired procedure, remains the gold standard backed by decades of clinical evidence. “My preference is to use freshly harvested fat whenever possible because I believe it offers the best biologic activity, best revascularization potential, and ultimately the best long-term outcome for the patient,” Dr. Berlet says.

What truly excites our experts, is the possible future role for fat banking given that research and protocols are constantly evolving and improving. Dr. Vyas puts it best: “For now, patients should approach the service with realistic expectations, ask detailed questions, and understand that what comes out of the freezer is not the same tissue that went in.”
