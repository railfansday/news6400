#899. Ousmane Dembélé quietly becomes the main man after long journey to the top

The Frenchman, who has been named the best male footballer in the world by the Guardian, has benefitted from PSG’s focus on the team rather than individuals

What makes a good player great, and a great player the best? This question has been occupying me since 2014, when the Guardian first asked me to contribute to its inaugural Next Generation feature. My job was to look for a France-based talent born in 1997 who could go on to have a stellar career.

After a great deal of research, I narrowed it down from my shortlist of five by asking questions not about the players’ football ability, but about other attributes: resilience, adaptability, decision-making, creativity, work ethic, response to feedback and willingness to learn. Qualities we cannot see, and are harder to measure.

Based on those answers, one player stood out above all the others: a kid called Ousmane Dembélé, then a youth player yet to make a first-team appearance at Rennes. Eleven years after appearing in these pages as one to watch, Dembélé has been voted the best male player in the world by the Guardian’s 219-strong voting panel.

Those intangible qualities were in full effect on the night Dembélé made good on his years of promise. The defining image of Paris Saint-Germain’s 5-0 win over Inter in the 2025 Champions League final was not the trophy lift, or any one of the goal celebrations, but Dembélé, poised on the edge of the opposition penalty area, with hunched demeanour and furrowed brow, his face a picture of focus, ready to press. Just how did he get to this point?

One lesson to learn from the Dembélé story: the path to greatness is not always linear. He started off like a train, winning France’s young player of the year award in his first season as a professional. The following season, he helped Borussia Dortmund win the German Cup.

Around that time, in the course of research for my book, Edge, I spoke to Thomas Tuchel, his coach at Dortmund. Tuchel told me that Dembélé’s talent came with an obligation and responsibility to improve. He bracketed his players into one of three “ABC” categories, depending on their dominant motivation, and would change his man-management style accordingly.

The A category stood for “aggressive-motivated”, and referred to players motivated by individual glory and awards (in Tuchel’s eyes, this was not necessarily a negative: think Neymar at Paris).

B stood for “binding-motivated”, those players who love being part of the group and bringing people together (often captains, such as César Azpilicueta at Chelsea).

C stood for “curious-motivated”. These were the players capable of greatness, who needed to be trained in a slightly different way. Their talent could take them far; and on a good day, they were capable of anything. Tuchel loved coaching these players. Dembélé, he felt, was a C.

But the journey was not always smooth. In 2017, he moved to Barcelona, who were flush with money from selling Neymar for €222m (£194m). It was a sliding doors moment. That same summer, Barcelona changed coach: in came Ernesto Valverde; out went Luis Enrique. In six injury-laden years at Barcelona, Dembélé started only a third of the league games. He scored a total of 24 league goals. His most memorable moment might have been missing a last-minute one-on-one in the 2019 Champions League semi-final against Liverpool, when Barcelona were already 3-0 up (remember what happened next? They lost the second leg 4-0). When he did leave, amid mutterings of wasted talent and blackmail, it felt like a relief for all parties.

There is some symmetry to Dembélé’s exceptional 2025. Of the 30 goals he has scored this calendar year at the time of writing, the most decisive and emblematic was probably against Liverpool in the second leg of their Champions League last-16 tie. With PSG 1-0 down from the first leg, Dembélé drifted into his own half, losing his markers who didn’t want to follow him that far. He picked up the ball and spread it wide to the right-winger Bradley Barcola, then sprinted into the box and made it to the cross to score. (He also scored a penalty in PSG’s successful shootout after they won 1-0 at Anfield that night). Dembélé did something similar against Arsenal in the semi-final, dropping deep early in the game, switching the play to the left-sided Khvicha Kvaratskhelia and receiving the return ball, unmarked, on the edge of the area, before scoring. Even when teams knew it was coming, they couldn’t stop him.

At the start of last season, Luis Enrique’s plan was for the whole team to replace the goals of Kylian Mbappé, who had joined Real Madrid. After all, Dembélé had not scored more than 10 league goals in a season since his debut campaign at Rennes. Luis Enrique wanted the team to be the star. And so, with no star player to serve, Dembélé was liberated. This curious-motivated talent who once scored penalties with either foot, and even took corners with either foot, quietly became the main man. He starts the press; his vision, movement, pace, control and positioning set the tone; and his finishing, once considered wasteful, is ruthless.

I still don’t know exactly what makes a good player great but I am closer to an answer. Part of the equation remains what we cannot see: the chemistry within a team, the relationship with a specific coach, playing in a system that brings out the best in every player. But also those other qualities that Dembélé had at 17, and still has now. This well-deserved prize is as much for those attributes as it is for his goals and the trophies he helped Paris Saint-Germain win.

#897. FIFPRO report highlights growing pains in women's football

Expected global revenue of over $1 billion and new faces at the U17 World Cup show women's football is booming, but a recent report also highlights need for better structures globally.

At first glance, women's football is healthier than ever. A recent report by FIFPRO, football's global players' union, said worldwide revenue is expected to reach over $1bn (€894m) in 2027. The World Cup in Brazil, a first in South America, will be the last with 32 teams and is expected to be the most watched and biggest revenue-generating women's tournament ever.

The good news continues with the U17 Women's World Cup in Morocco, where the tournament is being held every year for five straight years. Germany, France, Spain, Brazil, the United States and defending champions North Korea are all at the 2026 edition of a competition whose reputation continues to grow.

"You can just see where the game is going, which is so exciting," US head coach Ciara Crinion told FIFA. "The level has just gone up. We have to continue to stay with that and compete as well. [The U-17 Women's World Cup] almost feels like that first stepping stone in a major tournament for young players, and now you see how serious it is taken."

Puerto Rico, Australia and Argentina are making their debuts. For Puerto Rico, qualification is historic, as no football team in the men's or women's game has ever reached a World Cup tournament before.

"We've embraced this idea of being Puerto Rico's first team to qualify for a World Cup," head coach Andres Mirabelli told FIFA. Alongside national development plans, player recruitment was aided by a diaspora of nearly four million in the United States, perhaps the strongest place for women's football in the world. According to FIFPRO, the regional football average salary in North America is $5,940 (€5,547) per month, which is far higher than any other continent.

But even for the handful of players who make it out of these youth tournaments to a professional career, creating a living out of the beautiful game remains a real challenge. The aforementioned FIFPRO report details that many professional female players across the world barely earn minimum wage, with some even falling below it.

The report, which sourced its data from player surveys across the world, found that only 66% of professional players are paid for 12 months a year. The global average salary is $2,600 (€2,300) a month, but the range varies drastically across the world. In Eastern Europe, for example, that average is $880 per month. In Africa, it is $432, but in North America it is just under $6,000.

"I think what you also find in the women's game is the gaps are not just between the top five leagues and the rest. There is a gap there, but even within leagues themselves [there is one]," FIFPRO head of research and strategy for women's football Frankie Brown told DW.

"In some of the other leagues you're dealing with teams that are operating professionally, so will be paying their players on a reasonable wage, and playing in the same league as them you'll have players that are on amateur contracts and not being paid at all."

With economic and job security an issue for many, the report reveals that psychological stress factors are now the second main reason many consider leaving football.

"This is one of the most concerning parts of the report," Alex Culvin, the director of women's football at FIFPRO, said in a statement.

"Of the 49% of women’s football players considering leaving the sport early, 46% cited their mental health as the reason. In interviews, players say the sport's growth brings new pressures — including extra workload and social media abuse — but that clubs do not have the necessary safeguards in place to support these changes. We want clubs and national associations to provide mandatory mental health support focused on wellbeing, which is independent of sporting performance objectives."

FIFPRO's report is asking FIFA to set a so-called floor for the women's game so that minimum standards in key areas such as contracts, pensions and health can be enforced. Perhaps inspiration can be taken from the labor deal the WNBA agreed in 2026. It was long-negotiated deal that ultimately sent a message to female athletes everywhere to push for better pay, conditions and a greater share of the revenue they help generate.

"It is positive that average salaries are increasing for some players and that more are earning a satisfactory wage, but it’s important to note that there is a huge disparity in pay across regions," said Culvin.

"There is also an imbalance in conditions, not only across the globe but also within regions; in Europe, for example, the difference in standards between the east and west is enormous. This is a gulf we need to work hard to narrow; the growth in the most advanced markets must not be completely isolated from the rest of the industry.

With high hopes for the 2027 World Cup in Brazil and recent moves to increase professional football domestically, Germany is aiming to become an example for structural improvement in women's football. Hosting the Euros in 2029 certainly gives them an incentive to improve standards.

While salaries are in the upper tier — in the 2023-24 season, a women's Bundesliga player earned an average base salary of around €48,000 per year — the process of structural development in the country is still in its infancy. A new German football association rule shows that even basic professional structures are still being established. Only since the 2025–26 season have teams in the Women's Bundesliga been required to employ assistant coaches, goalkeeper coaches and athletic coaches on a full-time, professional basis.

"The facilities and the staff members," Nicole Anyomi told DW, were a factor in her making a recent move to London City Lionesses. "We have 30-plus staff members here whereas in Frankfurt we had like seven or eight. You can learn from each person and there is something new to learn every day. I think that's how you develop as a player faster."

Ultimately, the faster the game grows, the greater need to close the gaps.

"Despite playing on different continents, players are connected by their precarity," Culvin said. "The redistribution systems in women's football need to be more effective, touching more players and supporting a more sustainable ecosystem."

#894. Scientists discover a surprising twist in Japan's 7,300-year-old volcanic apocalypse

A massive volcanic eruption that struck Japan about 7,300 years ago had a devastating impact on prehistoric communities, but its aftermath was far more complicated than scientists once believed. While some populations declined or disappeared from affected areas, others continued to grow despite the destruction.

An international research team led by Lund University investigated how the Kikai-Akahoya eruption changed population patterns across Japan. Their findings challenge the idea that the disaster either completely wiped out nearby communities or left them largely unaffected. Instead, the researchers uncovered a surprising mix of population decline, migration, adaptation, and growth.

A Catastrophic Volcanic Eruption in Ancient Japan

When the Kikai-Akahoya volcano erupted beneath the sea approximately 7,300 years ago, Japan was home to Jomon communities that depended on hunting and gathering for survival.

"This eruption must have been devastating, exploding out of the seabed with little warning. It battered the Japanese islands with powerful earthquakes and enormous tsunamis, and then blanketed the landscape with meters of volcanic ash," explains senior author Peter Jordan, professor in archaeology at Lund University.

For decades, archaeologists have disagreed about what happened to the people living near the eruption. One theory describes an almost complete apocalypse, with human populations abruptly disappearing from areas closest to the eruption. Another suggests that some communities managed to survive even relatively close to the disaster.

The new findings indicate that neither explanation fully captures what happened. The eruption severely damaged ecosystems, forced people from their homes, and made some regions uninhabitable. Yet evidence also points to remarkable adaptability among survivors, with certain populations actually increasing after the catastrophe.

"The disaster caused major loss of life. However, survivors migrated, forged new social relations, innovated new kinds of economy, and appear to have found creative ways to exist and later thrive in the new post-disaster conditions," says lead author Enrico Crema, professor at Cambridge University.

How the Volcanic Disaster Reshaped Japan's Population

The researchers identified striking differences in how communities across Japan responded to the eruption.

In southern Kyushu, Jomon populations had already been shrinking long before the volcano erupted. By the time the disaster occurred, relatively few people remained in the region, which was primarily visited by highly mobile groups of foragers.

Northern Kyushu followed a very different pattern. Its population had been increasing before the eruption, and that growth continued through the catastrophe and afterward, even though the surrounding ecosystems experienced extensive and lasting damage. The researchers suggest that people fleeing the most severely affected regions may have contributed to this unexpected population growth.

Evidence also indicates that survivors who had lived inland relocated toward the northern coast. Marine ecosystems in these areas recovered relatively quickly, providing valuable food resources. By developing new ways to take advantage of the sea's abundance, these communities may have supported further population growth.

Perhaps even more surprising, the researchers identified population changes in parts of Japan much farther from the eruption.

These distant regions had experienced population growth before the catastrophe, but afterward, that growth stalled or even reversed. Such widespread demographic consequences had not previously been recognized.

Several possible explanations could account for these changes. The eruption may have caused temporary climate disruptions that affected communities across Japan. Another possibility involves severe tooth wear among deer and wild boar, two important food sources for the Jomon (possibly a consequence of the animals grazing in ash-covered landscapes). Other volcanic effects on human health and living conditions may also have contributed.

Together, these findings suggest that the consequences of a massive natural disaster can extend well beyond the areas experiencing the most immediate destruction.

According to Jordan, the results demonstrate that the effects of catastrophes are rarely straightforward. Even when faced with overwhelming destruction, human communities can respond in different ways, depending on their circumstances and the choices available to them.

"This study helps in creating a deeper understanding of the contingent ways in which cultures and societies cope with sudden shocks and catastrophes. Even in the most impossible situations, survivors and communities have choices, however limited: they need to act and make decisions; they need to adjust, collaborate, and find new connections and social networks," he concludes.

People without German passports appear more frequently in German police crime statistics than German nationals. A new study explains why.

Every year, when the Federal Criminal Police Office publishes the police crime statistics, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) — a party that is, in part, right-wing extremist — pays particularly close attention. They are looking for crimes committed by refugees, migrants and foreigners living in Germany.

But studies by media scholars show that 95% of the AfD's press releases focus on non-German suspects, while crimes involving German suspects are almost completely ignored.

However, the common narrative of the AfD — that non-Germans are inherently much more violent — is simply not true.

In a new research paper, Susann Prätor, a sociologist, criminologist and professor at the Lower Saxony Police Academy, has taken a very close look at crime data in Germany, together with her colleagues from the University of Zurich and the Lower Saxony Criminological Research Institute.

She looked not only at officially reported crime — that is, all offenses that appeared in police crime statistics — but also at unreported crime: These are all offenses that are not reported or detected, and therefore do not appear in any official statistics but are identified through representative surveys.

"According to police crime statistics, people who do not have German citizenship commit crimes more frequently than German citizens. However, studies into unreported cases show that non-German nationals are twice as likely to be reported. Differences between the two groups are also evident in crimes that go unreported, but they are significantly less pronounced," Prätor told DW.

Crimes committed by foreign nationals are therefore twice as likely to appear in police crime statistics as crimes committed by Germans.

These statistics record all reported cases and do not specify whether the allegations prove to be true.

When the statistics are adjusted for the bias in the reporting of crimes, foreigners are no longer 2.6 times more likely to commit crimes than Germans, but only 1.7 times more likely. This figure is also consistent with the results of studies on unreported juvenile violent crime. According to these studies, foreign youths are about 1.6 times as likely to commit violent crimes as German youths.

Other factors affecting crime include age and gender. Young men make up a larger proportion of the foreign-born population, and young men are the group most prone to criminal behavior across all societies.

According to Prätor, "categories such as being young, male, poorly educated and having a history of violence are factors that increase the likelihood of violent behavior. A certain concept of masculinity also plays a role, wherever that may come from. But we do not see culture as a factor, contrary to the prejudice that masculinity and violence play a greater role in other cultures."

Prätor points to a survey on attitudes toward violence. According to the survey, 5% of German youths view violence positively. Among those without a German passport, the figure is 10%.

The criminologist has identified a crucial factor: "Family upbringing plays an important role in shaping attitudes toward violence, because these attitudes are formed in the home. If I experience violence in my own home, I will have a different relationship to violence than if I hadn't experienced it," said Prätor.

Germany is, however, on the right track, says Prätor, even if it doesn't always feel that way. "Fifty or sixty years ago, parental violence was much more common in child-rearing than it is today. Acts of violence committed by young people are also far less frequent than they were in the 1990s or the early 2000s."

Prätor emphasizes that it is important to bring objectivity to the debate. A good illustration of this is a look at police records of serious violent crimes — such as aggravated assault, manslaughter, or murder. "Of every 100 German citizens, 0.2% commit violent crimes; among non-German nationals, the figure is 0.6%."

However, Prätor points out that "more than 99% of the people living here in Germany — whether of German or non-German origin — the overwhelming majority in this country do not commit any serious violent crimes at all."

#878. A simple touch can bring dying corals back to life in just 7 weeks

For the first time, researchers have demonstrated that severely bleached corals can fully recover after spending just a few days in contact with healthy, genetically identical corals.

In laboratory experiments, corals that had turned white and were close to dying regained their health within seven weeks. According to a study published on October 14 in Science Advances, this represents the fastest documented recovery from coral bleaching. The discovery could lead to new techniques for restoring coral reefs, which some estimates suggest could disappear within decades as climate change fuels increasingly severe and frequent marine heat waves.

Coral bleaching occurs when rising ocean temperatures cause corals to lose the beneficial algae living inside their tissues. These algae supply much of the energy corals depend on for growth and survival. Without them, corals lose their vibrant colors, struggle to obtain enough nourishment, and become more susceptible to harmful algae. Even when they survive, regaining their health can take months or years.

"While bleached corals may be dying, they are not dead yet," said lead author Danny Collins, an oceans PhD student at the Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability. "The question we wanted to answer is, once a coral has bleached, what can we do?"

An Unexpected Discovery Reveals a Way to Rescue Dying Corals

Although they may resemble plants or rocks, corals are animals. In most coral species, individual animals reproduce by creating genetically identical copies of themselves. These clones grow together into large colonies that form the foundation of coral reefs.

The idea for the study emerged from an unexpected observation in 2025. Steve Palumbi, a study coauthor and the Jane and Marshall Steel, Jr. Professor in Marine Sciences, noticed something unusual in his laboratory. Two fragments taken from the same coral colony, one healthy and the other bleached, had fallen against each other in an aquarium.

Where the fragments touched, the bleached coral began regaining its color. The change suggested that beneficial algae were returning to its tissues.

At roughly the same time, Collins was investigating how coral diseases develop and spread. He was particularly interested in bacteria carried by plastic debris that lands on corals and can introduce harmful microorganisms through direct contact.

"Harmful bacteria enter corals through physical contact," said Collins, who is also a Knight-Hennessy Scholar. "We were curious about what would happen if we used contact to reintroduce something good - the beneficial algae from healthy corals."

To investigate, Collins designed an experiment using 10 pairs of coral fragments. Each pair consisted of one healthy fragment and one bleached fragment originating from the same coral animal.

Five pairs were positioned so that the fragments physically touched. The other five pairs were separated by gaps measuring only a few millimeters.

The results revealed a dramatic difference. Every bleached fragment that touched its healthy counterpart recovered completely within seven weeks. In contrast, the bleached fragments that were kept apart died.

How a Simple Touch Triggers Rapid Coral Recovery

The researchers discovered that the remarkable recovery depends on a special ability of genetically identical corals to connect their tissues.

"Genetically identical corals recognize one another when they come into contact," said Palumbi, who is a professor of oceans in the Doerr School of Sustainability and of biology in the School of Humanities and Sciences. "Their tissues fuse if they are from the same clone. We see something similar in humans. Organ transplants are often successful if the donor is your identical twin."

Using a powerful microscope, Collins and his colleagues examined what happened when the coral fragments touched. They found that tiny channels within their tissues quickly lined up, forming connections that allowed materials to move between the two fragments.

Through these connections, beneficial algae and the nutrients they produce could pass from the healthy coral into the bleached one. Within just three days, researchers observed algae transferring into the damaged fragments.

The team then investigated how long the corals actually needed to remain connected. By separating pairs after different periods of contact, they discovered that a single week was enough to transfer the algae required for complete recovery. In some cases, just five days of contact produced the same result.

Remarkably, the separated corals still returned to full health within seven weeks, the same amount of time required by corals that remained connected throughout the experiment.

This finding revealed that the rapid recovery was not primarily driven by a continuing supply of algae from the healthy coral. Instead, once enough algae entered the bleached fragment, they reproduced rapidly inside their new host and restored its energy supply.

"Once beneficial algae enter bleached corals, they don't just spread out," said Palumbi. "They flood in and start dividing at the fastest rates seen in corals."

During the second week of contact, the beneficial algae living inside the bleached fragments doubled in number approximately every four days. That growth rate was about ten times faster than what researchers have observed in wild corals.

A Promising Breakthrough for Coral Reef Restoration

Scientists have spent decades searching for ways to protect coral reefs from rising ocean temperatures, disease, increasingly acidic seawater, and other environmental pressures.

Their efforts have included selectively breeding corals that can withstand higher temperatures, raising young corals in nurseries to rebuild damaged reefs, and transferring communities of microorganisms between corals to improve their resilience.

The new findings suggest another possibility: helping bleached corals recover quickly enough to survive the dangerous period following a marine heat wave.

"Bleached corals are often considered a lost cause because recovery after a heatwave is very slow, if it happens at all," said Fiorenza Micheli, the David and Lucile Packard Professor in Marine Science and a faculty advisor of Collins. "We hope this new research will inspire solutions that keep corals healthy long enough to enable full recovery after heat waves through slower processes such as growth, reproduction, and recruitment of new colonies."

However, the researchers emphasize that their experimental approach cannot yet be used to restore reefs on a large scale. For the technique to work as demonstrated, every bleached coral would need access to a healthy, genetically identical counterpart. Finding and supplying those matches across entire reefs would be impractical.

Important questions also remain about how the recovery mechanism could be adapted for broader use.

Over the coming months, the team intends to investigate other ways to introduce beneficial algae into bleached corals without requiring them to remain physically connected to healthy fragments.

One possibility involves collecting beneficial algae from healthy corals well before marine heat waves occur. Researchers could then deliver large quantities of these algae to damaged corals after bleaching events, potentially allowing them to recover much faster.

"These new insights into how corals can naturally recover give us hope for the future," said Micheli, who also co-directs the Stanford Center for Ocean Solutions. "They open the door to what's possible and may lead to the breakthrough we need to accelerate reef restoration."

#868. Eating blueberries every day may have a surprising effect on your mood

A small pilot study suggests that eating blueberries every day may influence gut bacteria and potentially affect mood in older adults. Participants who consumed blueberries regularly experienced a greater and more consistent reduction in depressive symptoms, along with subtle changes in genes associated with gut microbial enzymes.

However, the improvements in depressive symptoms were not statistically significant when compared with the placebo group. Although the findings do not establish that blueberries can relieve depression, they offer preliminary clues about a possible connection between blueberry consumption, the gut microbiome, and mental health.

"Fiber and anthocyanins in blueberries have potential to modify the gut microbiome and metabolites that are relevant to depression in older adults," said lead author Courtney L. Millar, PhD, assistant scientist II at Hebrew SeniorLife's Hinda and Arthur Marcus Institute for Aging Research and assistant professor of medicine at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and Harvard Medical School. "Our objective was to preliminarily determine the effect of blueberry consumption on the gut microbiome, metabolites, and depressive symptoms."

Testing the Effects of Daily Blueberry Consumption

To investigate the potential connection, researchers recruited adults aged 65 and older who were physically inactive and experiencing mild depressive symptoms. The pilot study used a randomized, double-blind, parallel-arm, placebo-controlled design to evaluate whether consuming blueberries could influence gut microbes and mood.

Over three months, participants consumed either 48 grams of blueberry powder each day (approximately two cups of fresh berries) or a matching placebo powder.

Researchers examined the participants' gut microbiomes using metagenomic sequencing, which allowed them to measure the abundance of different microbial species and genes. They also used liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry to analyze gut-derived fecal short-chain fatty acids, compounds produced through microbial activity. Depressive symptoms were assessed using validated questionnaires administered before and after the three-month intervention.

Blueberries Linked to Subtle Changes in Gut Microbial Activity

A total of 18 participants completed the study. Most were women, and the group was predominantly white (placebo group, n = 8; mean age: 75 ± 6; blueberry group, n = 10; mean age: 71 ± 4).

Despite the encouraging pattern in depressive symptoms among participants consuming blueberries, the researchers found no statistically significant difference in mood improvements between the blueberry and placebo groups.

The gut microbiome results also revealed a mixed picture. The overall abundance of microbial species, MetaCyc metabolic pathways, and short-chain fatty acid metabolites remained unchanged.

However, researchers identified statistically significant changes within the blueberry group in the abundance of genes associated with several gut microbial enzymes. One of these was EC 3.6.3.31 Polyamine Transporting ATPase, which is involved in the production of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), a neurotransmitter that plays a role in brain signaling.

These findings suggest that blueberries may influence specific aspects of gut microbial function even without producing measurable changes in the overall composition of the microbiome. Larger studies will be needed to determine whether these changes are connected to meaningful improvements in mood or depressive symptoms.

The study, "A pilot study of daily blueberry intake modulates the gut microbiota enzyme commissions in older, sedentary adults with mild depressive symptoms," was published in the Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging.

#855. Scientists discover a natural compound that could protect the brain from too much salt

Consuming too much salt can affect more than blood pressure. Growing evidence suggests that excessive salt intake may also interfere with memory, thinking, and emotional well-being. High salt (HS) consumption is already a major public health concern because of its connection to hypertension, but its potential effects on the brain make finding ways to reduce the damage even more important.

Researchers in Japan have identified a promising possibility in LPC70, a compound derived from soy. Previous research found that this ingredient could reduce high blood pressure and cognitive problems associated with a high-salt diet (HSD) in mice. The latest study takes a closer look at how it works, revealing that the compound influences important chemical signals in the brain and kidneys.

How a Soy Compound May Counter the Effects of Excess Salt

The research centers on prostaglandins (PGs), signaling molecules made from a fatty acid called arachidonic acid (AA). These substances act much like local hormones, helping regulate essential functions throughout the body.

In the cardiovascular system, prostaglandins help control blood pressure by balancing the widening and narrowing of blood vessels. Their effects in the brain are more complicated. Depending on which receptors they activate, they can trigger signals that either protect nerve cells or contribute to harmful processes, potentially affecting memory, thinking, and emotional responses.

LPC70 is a form of soy lysolecithin in which lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) accounts for more than 70% of the phospholipids. Although earlier experiments showed that LPC70 could ease salt-related hypertension and cognitive difficulties, scientists had not fully understood the mechanisms responsible, particularly its influence on prostaglandin signaling.

To investigate, a research team led by Professor Akihiro Mouri and Dr. Hisayoshi Kubota, working with Dr. Kazuhiro Kagotani, examined how LPC70 affected these signaling pathways in mice consuming a high-salt diet.

Dr. Kubota, the study's first author, played a central role in the experiments. He assessed blood pressure, monitored behavioral changes, and analyzed molecular activity associated with prostaglandins. His work helped clarify how LPC70 produces different effects in the organs involved in salt-related cardiovascular and cognitive problems.

Prof. Mouri and Dr. Kubota are affiliated with the Department of Regulatory Science for Evaluation and Development of Pharmaceuticals & Devices at Fujita Health University Graduate School of Medical Science, as well as the International Center for Brain Science (ICBS) at Fujita Health University in Japan. Dr. Kagotani is affiliated with Tsuji Oil Mills Co., Ltd, in Japan and the Tsuji Health and Beauty Science Laboratory at Mie University, Japan.

The findings were released online on May 14, 2026, and published in Volume 197 of Neurochemistry International on July 1, 2026.

"We wanted to understand the role of LPC70 in coordinating changes in PG signaling across organ systems," mentioned Prof. Mouri. "Our study originated from a collaborative research project with Tsuji Oil Mills Co., Ltd, a company that develops naturally derived food ingredients," he added.

Soy Compound Improves Blood Pressure and Memory in Mice

As expected, mice that consumed a high-salt diet developed elevated blood pressure. However, the consequences extended beyond their cardiovascular health.

The animals also became less socially interactive and had difficulty recognizing objects they had encountered before, indicating problems with memory. Interestingly, their general movement and anxiety-like behavior showed no substantial changes. This pattern suggests that excessive salt selectively disrupted more complex cognitive and social functions rather than causing widespread behavioral impairment.

When researchers supplemented the animals' diets with LPC70, several of these effects were reduced. Blood pressure improved, and the mice performed better on tests of social interaction and object recognition memory.

These improvements prompted the researchers to examine what was happening inside the animals' kidneys and brains.

Different Protective Effects in the Kidneys and Brain

In the kidneys, a high-salt diet increased the expression of two components involved in prostaglandin signaling: cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and the PGE2 receptor EP3. Both may contribute to the development of high blood pressure associated with excessive salt consumption.

LPC70 reduced these salt-related increases, suggesting that the compound helps correct changes in kidney signaling that can interfere with normal blood pressure regulation.

The researchers found a different response in the prefrontal cortex, a brain region essential for thinking, memory, and social behavior.

Excess salt reduced the expression of the PGD2 receptor DP1, which plays a role in protective and anti-inflammatory signaling in the brain. LPC70 restored expression of this receptor without changing DP2, another receptor associated with potentially harmful effects on nerve cells.

This distinction is important because it suggests that LPC70 does not simply increase or suppress all prostaglandin activity. Instead, it may selectively favor signaling pathways that help protect the brain, potentially explaining the improvements in memory and social behavior observed in the mice.

A Surprising Change in Chemical Signaling

The team also discovered an unexpected change involving arachidonic acid, the fatty acid the body uses to produce prostaglandins.

Mice consuming excess salt had lower levels of circulating arachidonic acid. Yet when they received LPC70, levels of prostaglandins derived from this fatty acid, including PGE2 and PGD2, increased.

This seemingly contradictory result suggests that LPC70 may help the body use available arachidonic acid more efficiently to produce prostaglandins under high-salt conditions.

Taken together, the findings reveal a coordinated but highly selective response. In the kidneys, LPC70 appears to suppress signaling changes associated with hypertension. In the brain, it restores signaling linked to protection against inflammation and damage.

The compound's effects therefore depend on both the organ involved and the specific prostaglandin receptors being regulated.

Could Soy-Based Ingredients Help Protect Against Excess Salt?

The results suggest that LPC70 could eventually become a useful functional food ingredient for reducing some of the health consequences of excessive salt intake.

Its ability to improve blood pressure and cognitive performance in mice raises the possibility that it could support cardiovascular and brain health, particularly among people whose diets contain high amounts of salt.

However, the findings come from animal experiments, and further clinical studies are needed to determine whether the same benefits occur in humans.

"Our study elucidates LPC70's role in PG signaling, laying the groundwork for future clinical research and dietary interventions aimed at reducing hypertension-related cognitive decline and promoting healthy aging globally," concluded Prof. Mouri.

The research provides a clearer picture of how a soy-derived compound can influence the body's response to excessive salt, opening new possibilities for nutritional approaches to protecting long-term health.

First-division Paraguayan football club Sportivo Ameliano, ahead of its home match against Olimpiana on Sunday, requested reporters and photographers to follow a dress code 'in order to maintain... the club's institutional image'.

But photographer Adriana Caballero, 29, felt targeted by the club's request, which banned both men and women, but also banned 'low-cut blouses' and the 'exposure of the navel'.

On top of this, women were banned from wearing leggings that aren't black or dark blue.

The snapper, who has more than 200,000 followers on Instagram, said the furore began after a photo of her went viral.

She said: 'I always want to wear the most comfortable clothes possible for my job. Sooner or later it probably had to come to this.'

She added that the measure could also hinder her colleagues' work.

Following fierce criticism on social media, Sportivo Ameliano backtracked.
