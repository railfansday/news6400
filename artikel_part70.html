#2950. I Went From AI Prompt to 3D-Printed Armor in 18 Hours

What started off as a dare became a fully fledged app in less than a day

Tuesday morning, I received a notification from Google saying I could try out the new Gemini 4 Argon model. Normally, I just ignore it — I don’t really care which model it is, as long as it can help me with my day-to-day — but this time Google teased me with something close to my heart.

Gemini said it could “Create an app to model lampshades that I can 3D print.” My response, as CNET’s resident 3D printing expert, was, “I bet you can’t.”

I went from these first few screenshots to a fully 3D-printed object in under 18 hours, and I’m impressed by how easy it was.

I’ll say upfront that I don’t really like generative AI. I think it uses people’s art and creative work without fair compensation, and I want to pay artists for their work. I changed the project from making lampshades to making cosplay armor and deliberately limited it to things I can already make myself. I know how to model greaves, pauldrons and vambraces, so this takes away nothing.

In my mind, this is analogous to a blacksmith who gets an apprentice to make the blanks for steel armor. The fine detail and character of the work is done by one person, but the grunt work is done by another. I can’t make very good chestplates or helmets, so I removed them from the app to focus on what I can make.

Talking to Gemini 4 feels the same as talking to any of the Gemini models. It understands what I’m saying and, more importantly, my intent. The first app Gemini created looked terrible on mobile. There were too many windows and not enough visuals of the model we were making. Explaining that the app was too messy and that we needed to clear it up got the response I wanted, rather than a bunch more questions.

The difference with Gemini 4 Argon is its ability to accomplish complex tasks at speed. Things like making complex web apps seem easy, and the time it took to give me new iterations was far less than with the Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite model I’m used to using.

I have Gemini Plus because I have a Google One plan, and I hit the token limit several times as I developed the app, but waiting an hour for a reset wasn’t that big a deal. If you have the Pro version, you’ll likely be OK.

The first few versions of KeriForge (the name I settled on for my armor web app) were surprisingly usable right away. All the sliders for adjusting the model size worked. While they weren’t all intuitive, they had enough features to get me started. I kept going back, though. I removed some features, such as the helmet generator, and added new ones, including the strap hole marker and circumference calculator.

Gemini understood that these changes needed to reflect the physical nature of the end result. Instead of trying to cut holes in the digital model for the straps, it created marks on the model, so I could cut them out in the real world. 3D models need to be manifold — all their faces need to face the right way and connect to be “watertight” — otherwise, they can fail on the print bed. Making random holes in the model would most definitely cause it to fail.

At one point, I asked Gemini to let me “Download this onto my iPad Pro to make it easier to use.” Gemini understood that I didn’t want to make an iPad app, but that I wanted the web app to be available offline on my iPad. It made the app available as HTML and, once I got it up on Google Sites, told me how to download it using Safari.

It also has its own preview that lets me see the app running directly from the Gemini app on the iPad, so making changes was easy and instantaneous. It took around 10 iterations to reach KeriForge 8.2 and a state that I felt was good enough to produce a physical print.

Creating the app was a lot of fun, and I learned a lot about how Gemini works and ways to prompt it to get a better finished article, but my original skepticism wasn’t that Gemini couldn’t make the app, but that it couldn’t produce a fully 3D-printable design that I could make physically.

I spent some time measuring my arm and adjusting the blank vambrace to make sure it would fit if it did print, then I hit the export button. Because I used my iPad, I got to export it directly to Nomad Sculpt, a 3D sculpting tool that let me add some random squiggles and an approximation of my name on the side of the model.

From there, I exported the file once again, this time to a browser app called Layerling. This app lets me edit the STL file — STL is the format for plain 3D models without any color — to cut the holes the straps need to go through. It’s a much more comprehensive program than the one I just made, so I know it can handle cutting the model while keeping it 3D-printable. I’ve only used Layerling a few times, but the more I do, the more impressive it becomes.

Making the holes in the model was simple, and since I had already asked Gemini to ensure there were guide markers, finding the right location was painless. Once I had cut those holes, it was onto the final stage: printing.

Once I had the final STL ready for printing, it was a simple matter to export it over to Bambu Slicer and start the actual print. I chose to print it in TPU 75A, a flexible material I was already testing. TPU is a soft, rubbery material, so it can conform to my arm better than the more rigid plastics like PLA and ABS.

This was the moment when my bet with Gemini would come to fruition. After 7 hours of printing and about 11 hours of iterating and fiddling with Gemini, we would see whether the model actually worked or ended up as spaghetti all over my printer.

The screenshot clearly shows that it did, indeed, work. You can see that, despite some errors that weren’t caused by the model, it produced a fully manifold and usable 3D print. The dimensions were perfect, fitting my forearm length and wrist circumference exactly.

The errors you can see on the model stem from the TPU being too soft and the model being too tall. As the printer increased the model’s height, the tension relaxed, causing the model to wobble under the print head. In the future, I would need to support it better in the slicer or print it lying down to achieve the print quality I expect.

I’m not an AI power user. I like to use Gemini to organize my emails, help me sort out spreadsheets, and that’s about it. I assumed that creating a web app capable of producing a fully physical product, such as a 3D model, would be arduous; that I would have to constantly refine my prompts to get it to understand my needs and wants. That wasn’t the case, though. Gemini Argon seemed to understand the context of what I wanted far better than I gave it credit for.

The term “prompt engineering” has always made me shy away from using these products. I don’t want to spend time trying to get an AI to understand what I need when a person could do it much more quickly. I can see now that by using that term, gatekeepers can put AI behind a knowledge wall: to present it as a mysterious, complex thing. It simply isn’t the case with the latest models.

I still don’t like generative AI, but I can’t help but be impressed by something that can take me from an idea to a physical product in less than a day. I went into this challenge betting Gemini 4 couldn’t do what it said it could, and was humbled in the process. I’m not going to start using AI for everything I do, but I’ve already thought of some fun ways it can help me with upcoming projects. Something that inspires my creativity can’t be all bad, can it?

So-called balcony solar setups are becoming legal across the US. Here's the latest.

Plug-in solar systems are easy to set up and can save consumers money on their power bills, but they’re still illegal in many states. Utah was the first to legalize backyard and plug-in solar, but other states have since caught on, and more are working on their own pathway to legalization.

As we’ve covered before, these plug-in solar setups (also commonly called “backyard solar”) are plug-and-play systems consisting of one or more solar panels, an inverter, and a battery. The system connects to a home’s standard 120-volt outlet, automatically routing solar-generated electricity from the battery to the nearest running appliance.

These systems are restricted because most US household electrical systems aren’t designed to receive electricity through a standard wall outlet. Electrical codes, utility regulations, and equipment certification requirements in the US make them difficult to install legally.

Ten states have already legalized backyard and plug-in solar systems, so residents there can already purchase and install systems like the EcoFlow Stream in their homes. Here’s the current status of the states that have already successfully passed legislation, legalizing such devices for consumer use.

About two dozen states are still in the process of legalizing plug-in solar. Some bills have stalled until the next legislative session reconvenes in 2027, while others are going through committee hearings.

Balcony solar bills H.5175 and S3143 are advancing in Massachusetts, and the SUNNY Act is pending governor approval in New York. Bills in other states like Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, and Washington, DC have reached committee hearings.

Most states have stalled plug-in solar bills until the next legislative session in 2027, including Alaska, Arizona, Delaware, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Missouri, North Carolina, and Oklahoma.

Unfortunately, plug-in solar bills have failed in Georgia, New Mexico, Oregon, Washington, and Wyoming, so if you live in one of those states, these kinds of devices are technically illegal to operate. This doesn’t mean that it won’t eventually clear the legislative slate in these states, but lawmakers will need to file a new bill to reintroduce them after making necessary revisions.

Whether you can immediately set up your balcony solar panels or not after the systems are legalized in your state will depend on your home situation.

Generally speaking, the bills in question focus on preventing utility companies from blocking plug-in solar projects. These bills can’t preempt bans on these systems by landlords or HOAs, though supplementary bills have been introduced in some states to include protections for HOAs and landlords.

Plug-in solar is illegal because the electrical systems in traditional American homes aren’t designed to receive power from a standard wall outlet. Rooftop solar systems are professionally installed and designed so the energy harvested by the panels flows through a circuit breaker and into your outlets. Plug-in solar, in turn, introduces electricity directly into an outlet, bypassing the electrical panel.

In a plug-in solar setup, nearby appliances draw energy supplied by the breaker and the solar inverter, but the wiring could carry more current than the breaker alone would allow before it trips. But it’s not solely about safety.

Installing your own solar panels means less reliance on local utilities. These companies are the biggest regulatory obstacle for plug-in or balcony solar setups. Their rules and regulations on solar energy are designed for rooftop panels, not plug-and-play systems, so legislation should push utility companies to change that.

There’s also the elephant in the room: utilities would make less money off their services if plug-in solar becomes widely popular. When consumers purchase less electricity, they contribute less to grid costs.

Courtois, however, believes Mbappe's individual displays should be enough to earn him the accolade, despite Real Madrid's failure to win major silverware during the period under consideration.

The goalkeeper acknowledged that the process can be difficult for voters, particularly when leading candidates have excelled in different circumstances.

Okay, Pavi isn't physically in space, but her spirit winds up floating in a cosmic void. It's the same void Avatars Aang and Korra found themselves in before entering the Avatar State. In their series, they followed a glowing path to a massive cosmic projection of themselves holding a ball of white energy. Once they stepped into the ball, they'd be in the Avatar State. The entire cosmic set-up is a neat visual representation of the spiritual and mental components of being the Avatar. However, it's not what we see in Seven Havens.

Like Aang and Korra, Pavi follows a path through space in Seven Havens. But instead of finding a vision of herself, she sees a massive circular gate. Like the anomaly, it glows black and red. Unlike the anomaly's fragmented, angular geometry, this gate bears a beautiful, flowery design.

From there, Seven Havens reveals that beyond this circle lie six others, each ringed in their own glowing colors, although none bear a design yet.

The progression of these colors is a visual Avatar fans are familiar with, going all the way back to Avatar: The Last Airbender Season 2, episode 19, "The Guru." In that episode, Aang visits guru Pathik to learn how to enter and control the Avatar State. Pathik teaches him about unblocking his chakras, which are points of concentrated energy throughout the body, with real-world roots in Buddhist and Hindu practices.

The scenes in which Aang unblocks his chakras are each tinged with a specific color tied to each chakra, starting with red and moving up to orange, yellow, and so on. It's the exact same color progression as the gates Pavi encounters, highly implying that she is looking at a series of chakras. But they aren't hers. Instead, each of the seven chakras is almost certainly tied to one of the seven Havens.

Throughout Seven Havens' run, series fans have theorized that each Haven represents one of the seven chakras. After all, the show's opening credits show the Havens as glowing points of color on a map, each corresponding to one of the chakra colors. The same color scheme carries over to the costumes of the Havens in the play about the Cataclysm in the very first episode.

All of this is compelling evidence for the theory, but Pavi's vision in "The Envoys: Part Two" is the strongest confirmation of the theory so far. Let's start with the red gate in front of Pavi. It is the same color as the first chakra guru Pathik teaches Aang about: the Earth chakra, known in real-world practices as the root chakra. It sits at the base of the spine. According to Pathik, it deals with survival, but it's blocked by fear.

All these concepts of survival, fear, and the root chakra figure into Seven Havens in some way. Book 1 is titled "Survival," while the season finale is titled "The Root Chakra." As for fear, the city of Ellora is overrun with it. Whenever someone comes into contact with the anomaly, they become afflicted by fear.

With that in mind, it seems like the anomaly isn't truly some kind of spirit demon. Rather, it's a physical manifestation of the blocked energies of Ellora, which explains its appearance as a warped version of the design on the gate. In order to keep the world in balance, Pavi and her twin sister Nisha (voiced by Aishu Devan) will surely have to find a way to unblock Ellora — the root chakra — and likely the other havens as well.

Each of the seven Havens representing a chakra raises a massive question, though. What are they chakras of? They seem to be chakras of the entire world, but does that mean that the Earth in the Avatar universe is actually its own living being? Was the Cataclysm caused in part by blockage of these energy points?

All will surely become clear as Seven Havens continues to open its mystery box. For now, though, Pavi's vision is the clearest answer viewers have gotten to the many questions the show has raised about the world post-Cataclysm.

#2940. OpenAI signs AI safety research deal with Poland’s NASK institute

NASK’s AI Safety Research Center and OpenAI will study ways to test advanced models, find weaknesses and strengthen safeguards.

OpenAI has partnered with Poland’s NASK National Research Institute to research AI safety and security, as concerns grow over AI agents behaving in ways their developers did not intend.

The company announced the agreement today, saying the two organisations would work together to test increasingly powerful AI systems, identify vulnerabilities and develop stronger safeguards.

The partnership comes amid growing scrutiny of autonomous AI agents, as both OpenAI and Anthropic have disclosed cases in which their agents exhibited unexpected or unwanted behaviour.

In August, OpenAI said some of its test agents had escaped a controlled testing environment and coordinated an attack on Hugging Face, a platform for sharing AI models. The incident has since prompted a lawsuit against OpenAI in San Francisco.

Under the new agreement, OpenAI will work with NASK’s AI Safety Research Center, which serves as Poland’s AI safety institute. The collaboration will focus on evaluating advanced AI systems, finding weaknesses and improving the measures designed to keep them safe.

NASK is a state-owned research institute based in Warsaw. It also operates CERT Polska, the national team responsible for responding to cyberattacks on Polish networks.

“Collaboration with OpenAI is a natural step for the Institute and its AI Safety Research Center. […] By combining our research expertise with OpenAI’s technical capabilities, we can better anticipate risks, strengthen safeguards, and build public trust in these transformative technologies,” said Radosław Nielek, NASK’s director.

The organisations have worked together before. In July, CERT Polska gained access to OpenAI’s cybersecurity models through the company’s Daybreak programme, according to NASK.

OpenAI said the team used the models to help identify six vulnerabilities in MikroTik router software, all of which have since been fixed.

“Poland is home to exceptional scientists and technical talent, many of whom have helped lead OpenAI’s own research,” said Tom Duff Gordon, OpenAI’s head of EMEA policy.

The partnership also fits into OpenAI’s broader effort to shape how advanced AI is tested and governed internationally. The company has called on the US to work with other countries on shared technical standards for frontier AI, referring to the most advanced AI systems.

In September, OpenAI published a proposal for developing those standards. It includes human oversight of AI systems conducting their own research and guidance on how companies should report safety incidents.

OpenAI already works on AI safety with the UK AI Security Institute and a US organisation known as CAISSI.

Every planet in the solar system is easily seen at some point this month, with Mercury being the most difficult. That’s not unusual, thanks to its proximity to the sun. Here’s a quick rundown on the best times to view each planet in October.

Mercury: Mercury is in elongation (the farthest it can get from the sun) on Monday, making that day and the two or so days before and after the best possible time to view Mercury.

Venus: Venus will spend most of the month right next to the sun, making it virtually impossible to see. However, by the end of the month, it will begin to separate from the sun, making it visible just after sunset. The best view will be on Oct. 31, but the view will get better going into November.

Mars: Mars will be visible in the night sky every night in October. It rises from the eastern horizon right around 2 a.m. ET and stays there until the sun comes up.

Jupiter: Jupiter and Mars are going to be pretty close together all month, so most of the same rules apply, except that Jupiter rises about two hours later than Mars on most nights in October.

Saturn: Saturn is living its best life in October. It’s visible almost right at sunset ET and stays visible in the night sky until sunrise.

Neptune: Neptune is also visible every day in October and follows a very similar path to Saturn. It rises in the east, streaks across the sky and sets in the west right around sunrise. They’re actually pretty close together in the night sky every night during the month, so if you can find Saturn, Neptune isn’t far off, but you’ll definitely need magnification to see it.

Uranus: Uranus is visible the entire month and follows a very similar path through the sky as Saturn and Neptune, but its trek happens later at night. It rises on the eastern horizon about two hours after Saturn and follows it across the sky, but never really catches up, ending up in the high western sky when sunrise comes.

The Draconids meteor shower is a minor meteor shower that occurs every year around the first week of October. It officially started on Oct. 6 and ran until Oct. 10, making it one of the shortest meteor showers of the season. It was fed by the 21P/Giacobini-Zinner comet, which is part of the Jupiter family of comets.

Draconids meteors appear to originate from the Draco constellation. It sits high in the western sky in the northern hemisphere this time of year, with a slight lean to the north. If you’re using a sky map app, all you need to do is find Vega, and you’re already in the right neighborhood to catch Draconids.

The peak is a little tame, at around 10 meteors per hour most years, but it has a history of surprising astronomers. In 1933 and 1946, Draconids spat out thousands of meteors an hour in what were two of the most intense meteor showers of the 20th century.

#2928. How We Test Projectors

Our gifting experts are obsessively searching for holiday gifts that are unique, thoughtful and suitable for any budget.

Our expert, award-winning staff selects the products we cover and rigorously researches and tests our top picks. If you buy through our links, we may earn a commission.

From contrast to color to input lag, I measure and compare home theater and portable projectors using instruments and my own two eyes.

If you’re shopping for a projector, the specs and features can be very confusing. If one projector claims 2,500 lumens, does that mean it’s better than one that claims 2,000? If a projector boasts a contrast ratio of 1,000,000:1, is that many times better than one that has 10,000? What about lasers, LEDs, zooms and all the other specs? Frustratingly, too many of these specs are meaningless. Or at the very least, they’re inaccurate enough that they’re difficult, or even impossible, to compare across brands.

I’ve been testing and reviewing projectors for 25 years. I’ve learned that specs bear very little relation to how the projector looks in person. That’s why I test every projector I review for CNET with objective and subjective methods using my own eyes, my own specialized measurement gear and side-by-side comparisons on the same screen. Here’s how that works.

Naturally, this info can get a bit “into the weeds,” but hopefully it will give you an idea about the behind-the-scenes work that goes into my reviews.

First-time setup is important for any TV or projector. The out-of-the-box picture settings almost never let the display look as good as it can. With projectors, the ability to tweak is especially crucial since there’s a picture element the manufacturer can’t control: the screen.

One of the first things I do is adjust the contrast and brightness using test patterns. Sometimes color too, but this usually tends to be correct out of the box. I start in movie or cinema mode, but some models have a Filmmaker mode instead. Color temperature is usually the most accurate in movie mode as well, but if the image is noticeably cool or warm in color tone, I’ll adjust that too.

I place each projector as close as possible to the same location in my lab. This can vary a bit because every projector has a different lens. Generally, it’s around 10 feet from my 1.0 gain screen. The 16×9 portion of that screen is 102-inch diagonal. Its actual aspect ratio is 2.35:1, which makes big movies even more epic to watch. The screen’s size also makes comparing projectors side-by-side easier since it’s about 12 feet wide and a bit over 4 feet tall.

We recently started testing ultra-short throw, or UST, projectors. These projectors sit just a few inches from the screen and require more elaborate setup than their longer-throw cousins. Tiny adjustments to the position of the projector can result in significant changes to the size and shape of the projected image. I have an adjustable-height stand to get the vertical position correct, and then take an exacting amount of time making sure it’s exactly square to the screen. Because UST projectors project almost vertically, a lot more of their light bounces onto the ceiling. I take this into account when measuring brightness and contrast (more on that in the next section), but I also use a 103-inch ceiling-light rejecting screen for final subjective testing to see how they’d look with UST-friendly screen.

Once the projector is set up, I’ll watch a variety of content to see if I notice any issues that I should further check with test patterns.

A projector’s brightness, generally measured in lumens, is one of the most important aspects of its overall performance. However, as mentioned above, the specs claimed by a manufacturer are rarely accurate. A projector with a claimed 2,000 lumens is usually brighter than a projector with 1,000 claimed lumens, but it’s likely not twice as bright.

Part of the issue is that manufacturers typically come up with their claims based on a perfect theoretical number in one specific mode. That mode is almost always one that looks far worse than what the projector can manage in a dimmer mode. It might have a blueish color temperature (more on that in a moment), inaccurate colors and, increasingly commonly, egregiously loud fan noise.

I measure the projector’s light output in its most accurate mode. This is also the mode I do my subjective viewing and comparisons. I’ll also measure the projector in its brightest mode and describe the trade-offs it introduces.

When I say “measure,” I’m not talking about a ruler. I use specialized test equipment to objectively measure a projector’s output. In this case, a Minolta LS-100 meter gives me the projector’s luminance in candelas per square meter (cd/m2). Then, if you know the size and gain of the screen, you can do a bit of math to find the estimated lumens. Since I always use the same screen for testing, this is easy.

In recent years, projector brightness claims have, surprisingly, gotten more accurate. Most projectors I test now are within 10-20% of their claims. Given different measurement methodologies, unit-to-unit variation and other factors, that’s as close as one could reasonably expect. This carries over to other projector review sites. If they measure brightness at all, it will vary somewhat but should be fairly close. Occasionally, I’ll review a projector with specs greater than 20% off spec, which is a little suspect but not enough for pitchforks. It’s just worth noting, which I do.

Just like with TVs, contrast ratio is easily the most important aspect of a projector’s overall image quality. A projector with low contrast will look washed out, with grayish blacks and/or dimmer whites. Contrast can be challenging to measure correctly. How I measure contrast ratio is a little convoluted, but every step is to minimize measurement error and increase your ability to compare different projectors I’ve reviewed.

Using the most accurate picture settings and a Minolta LS-100 light meter, I measure a full black image and then a white window (100% white, but just in a small portion of the screen). I do this using whatever laser/LED and iris modes are available, though specifically without any auto-iris or “dynamic” laser/LED-intensity modes (more on these in a moment). Then, in whatever mode seems best, I measure again using an AEMC CA813 illuminance meter at a set distance from the lens. I average all these measurements together for the average overall contrast ratio.

My methodology is a slightly modified version of the one I learned at the Display Metrology Course at the National Institute of Standards and Technology, but which is sadly no longer offered. The main issue with contrast ratio measurements is that small variations can drastically change the overall measurement. For instance, if I measure 0.002 cd/m2 instead of 0.001, that changes the contrast ratio by half. Variations of 0.001 are quite easy and not detectable by the eye. For instance, objects in the room can reflect back on the screen, impacting the measurement. Light bouncing off the ceiling, back onto the screen and then into the light meter is another factor. So for consistency, I keep everything the same. The CA813, which measures the light directly from the lens, eliminates the room from the measurement, and acts as a sort of check against the luminance measurements from the LS-100. Averaging multiple measurements across different brightness levels is another way to average out slight errors in individual measurements. There are also ways to minimize any stray ambient light in the room, but I can’t share all my secrets.

AEMC CA813 illuminance and Minolta LS-100 luminance meters I use for projector measurements.

In my decades of projector testing, I’ve found that being careful with the room, averaging multiple measurements, using two types of meters and the other steps in my methodology results in a final result that’s very internally consistent. If I measured a projector I reviewed 5 years ago, I’d still get as close to the same result as is rationally feasible. That consistency is important to me since I want you to be able to compare the different projectors I’ve reviewed with as much accuracy as possible. As I’ve mentioned, you can’t do this accurately using manufacturers specs alone. It’d be great if you were able to do this across different review websites, but getting video reviewers to agree on a standard of reviewing and measuring is far beyond my purview. You can compare my reviews with my other reviews, and that’s all that’s really possible without some international projector reviewer consortium. Such a body would have meetings, and I hate meetings.

The method listed above gets us the projector’s “native” contrast ratio, which is what you see at any given moment on-screen. Many projectors also have the ability to adjust the contrast ratio dynamically. They can use an iris on the lens or adjust the power of the laser or LED light source. The projector “looks” at the video signal, and if the scene is supposed to be dark, it will close the iris or decrease the laser/LED power so the image appears darker.

While this does technically create darker black levels, in that the darkest parts of the image are physically darker, the trade-off is that the bright parts of the image are also darker. Everything is darker. Done well, dynamic contrast can help improve the projector’s overall image quality, but it’s less helpful than native contrast ratio for comparisons. It’s also somewhat misleading, since at any given moment the projector can’t look better than its native contrast ratio — even though its far higher dynamic contrast ratio is a more impressive number. So I measure both, but the native number is far more important.

Note the color differences between the three images.

Compared to brightness and contrast ratio, measuring color and color temperature is relatively easy. Using a C6 HDR5000 colorimeter, I measure the exact colors produced by the projector. How red is the red, how green is the green, and so on. This is more accurate than just saying “well, the grass looks very grassy.”

Beyond the red, green and blue primary colors — and the cyan, magenta and yellow secondary colors — the Portrait Displays Calman software also lets me test for a variety of in-between colors and shades, to get a broader idea of how well the projector creates color.

For the most part, modern home theater projectors in their movie or cinema mode are able to produce fairly accurate colors. Portable projectors tend to be more of a mixed bag, usually in an attempt to squeeze out as much light as possible.

Projectors have historically struggled with the wider range of colors possible with most HDR content. However, with the rise of laser and LED light sources, while they’re inching closer to the wide color gamut of TVs, they are still well behind. Most non-portable models can at least create colors beyond what’s possible with HD.

Objective measurements go a long way to telling me about a projector’s performance, but they have limits. Many projectors use the same internal components and could measure similarly, yet look different from each other in person. This can be due to a variety of factors, including specific settings chosen by the manufacturer, their video processing choices and more. That’s why I compare every projector I review side-by-side for CNET with other, similar projectors.

To do this, I connect two or more projectors to an HDMI splitter. A splitter takes a single HDMI source, like a streaming device or Ultra HD Blu-ray player, and splits it into multiple, identical signals. I then view the projectors side-by-side on my 12-foot-wide, 2.35:1, 1.0-gain screen. Usually, I’ll move the projectors closer to the screen so the image is smaller and each can display its full image. With some models, especially UST projectors, this isn’t possible, so I’ll block off a portion of the image so they all fit at the same time.

I then watch a mix of content, but always a few key selections that I watch on everything. For years, I used the opening of The Fifth Element on DVD (Aziz, LIGHT!), which should amuse anyone who remembers me from Home Theater Magazine. These days, my go-to clip is Thor and Loki meeting with Odin on the cliffside in Thor: Ragnarok. Lots of real and fantastical colors during this scene and the ones that follow it. I also like the test clips on the Spears & Munsil UHD HDR Benchmark. Because what would a video test be without slow-moving clips of nature? With most projectors, I’ll also have them as my main “TV” for days or sometimes weeks, watching a variety of TV, movie and gaming content.

One thing you might notice that’s missing from my tests is calibrating the projector. Calibration is the process of fine-tuning the color and color temperature to get the projector looking as good as possible. It goes far beyond the simple user-menu setup and requires specialized gear. I certainly have that gear, as well as the know-how. I’m ISF trained and have been calibrating displays for more than 25 years.

While calibrating a display can definitely improve how it looks, its use in a review is limited. If I find out that a $1,000 projector looks better if you spend $400 or more on a calibration, what value is that? I can’t assume most people would be willing to spend that money. Also, the only things calibration can improve are color and color temperature. While those are definitely important factors in a display’s overall performance, they are not nearly as important as brightness and contrast ratio. Also, most projectors these days are reasonably accurate in both aspects. Could they look better with careful calibration? Sure, but not by enough that I recommend most people spend the money. If I wouldn’t recommend it, I don’t think there’s much point in spending time on it in a review.

So if you want to get your projector calibrated, it can improve the image. Nearly every modern home theater projector can be calibrated. It’s not going to make a $1,000 projector look like a $3,000 projector, however. If you want to eke out every drop of performance and accuracy from your projector, and you don’t mind paying for it, it’s worth considering.
