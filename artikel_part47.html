#4098. Oral GLP-1 Orforglipron (Foundayo) Achieves Weight Loss for Women of All Ages

  • A new analysis of previous studies found that orforglipron promoted weight loss in women regardless of their menopausal status.
  • This is important since the decline in estrogen after menopause can make weight loss more difficult.
  • Menopausal women also tend to gain weight around their midsection, which is associated with health risks.
  • The fact that the new GLP-1 comes in a pill form offers a convenient option for many women.
  • However, doctors stress that it is also important to select the treatment that is the most effective for you.

A new GLP-1 pill called orforglipron (Foundayo) has been shown to help women lose weight, regardless of menopausal status, according to new research.

A group of researchers, including senior author Hunter Thomas Hoffman, PharmD, a clinical research scientist affiliated with the drug’s manufacturer, Eli Lilly, analyzed data from two prior trials, ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2, to arrive at this conclusion.

These studies reveal that this medication works equally well for women who have not yet gone through menopause, those who are currently experiencing it, and those who are postmenopausal. 

The findings were presented at the Annual Meeting of the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD), held in Milan, Italy, from September 28 to October 2.

This is important because menopause often causes weight gain due to lower estrogen levels, making weight loss more difficult for many women. 

Orforglipron offers a promising new option for managing weight in women with obesity or overweight, especially those with type 2 diabetes.

In April of this year, Eli Lilly’s Foundayo received FDA approval as a weight-loss drug, making it the second GLP-1 pill for weight loss to enter the U.S. market after Novo Nordisk’s Wegovy.

Examining menopause and weight loss with orforglipron

The ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2 studies involved women who were either obese or overweight and who had at least one medical condition related to their weight. 

Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or higher, and overweight as a BMI of 27 or higher. 

The participants were divided into groups based on their menopausal status: premenopausal (before menopause), perimenopausal (during the menopause transition), and postmenopausal (after menopause). 

Each group included between 142 and 225 females who either received orforglipron or placebo, an inactive pill with none of the drug in it.

The ATTAIN-1 study focused on people who were overweight or obese but did not have diabetes. ATTAIN-2 included participants who had both overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes. 

The purpose of analyzing these two groups separately was to understand how orforglipron works in people with different health conditions.

The researchers measured several outcomes over a period of 72 weeks. They examined changes in body weight, waist circumference (the distance around the waist), and the waist-to-height ratio, a measure used to assess body fat distribution. 

They also tracked how many women reached specific weight loss goals, such as losing at least 5%, 10%, 15%, or 20% of their body weight, compared to those who took the inactive pill.

By studying these factors, the researchers were able to determine not only whether orforglipron helped women lose weight but also how much weight loss was achieved and how it affected their body shape.

Up to 14% weight loss with orforglipron regardless of menopause status

The results showed that women who took orforglipron experienced significant weight loss regardless of their menopausal status. 

In the ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2 studies, premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women all lost an average of up to 14% of their body weight. 

This means that the medication was effective for women at different life stages, including those experiencing menopause, when weight gain is often a challenge.

In addition to losing body weight, women in the orforglipron groups also saw meaningful reductions in waist circumference. Waist measurements decreased by up to 12.5 centimeters (about 5 inches) across all menopausal groups in both studies. 

This reduction is important because greater visceral fat is associated with higher risks of heart disease and diabetes.

The waist-to-height ratio also improved for many women taking orforglipron. More participants treated with the medication moved into lower waist-to-height ratio categories than those who took the placebo. 

This indicates a healthier distribution of body fat and suggests a lower risk of heart disease.

When looking at the number of women who reached specific weight loss milestones, orforglipron performed much better than the placebo. Up to 83% of women on orforglipron lost at least 5% of their body weight, while 30% of women on placebo reached that same level of weight loss. 

Losing 5% or more of body weight is often considered clinically meaningful, as it can lead to improvements in blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol levels.

Overall, these findings suggest that orforglipron is a promising new medication for weight management in women with obesity or overweight, including those who also have type 2 diabetes. 

This can be especially helpful for those navigating the challenges of menopause while trying to manage their weight.

Oral GLP-1 offers convenient weight loss option for women

James J. Chao, MD, FACS, Chief Medical Officer and co-founder of VedaNu Wellness, wasn’t involved in the research, but said the significance of these findings for postmenopausal women.

“The 14% is what’s grabbing headlines,” he told Healthline, “but up to 12.5 cm from the waistline is what I’m interested in.”

According to Chao, as females enter postmenopause, they tend to store more fat around their midsection.

“This is the type of fat that can cause heart disease,” he said. “So a pill that can help a 38-year-old woman lose the same amount as a 62-year-old breaks that myth.”

Additionally, in contrast to most GLP-1 drugs, which are injectable, it’s a once-a-day pill. You can also take it any time, Chao explained, making it more convenient.

“No needle, no refrigerator, and up to 83% of participants lost at least 5%,” he said. “Sometimes it’s the simple things that make a difference.”

Luca Moretti, MD, a medical doctor at Sapienza University of Rome, who wasn’t involved in the studies, added that convenience isn’t the only thing to consider when deciding whether to use orforglipron.

Obesity management should begin with an individualized plan that includes nutrition, physical activity, and the management of other risk factors.

“When these measures are not sufficient, adding pharmacological treatment can be considered together with a physician,” he told Healthline.

Moretti further advised speaking with your physician about any potential side effects, your existing medical conditions, other medications you are taking, and your treatment preferences.

“So the most useful question to ask a doctor is not simply, ‘Is Foundayo more convenient?’ but rather: ‘Among the available treatments, which one offers the best balance of effectiveness, tolerability, and practicality for me?'” he said.

#4094. Castlevania: Belmont’s Curse review - a fantastic action game, a less than stellar Metroidvania

Castlevania: Belmont's Curse is a great action game with truly beautiful art, but its attempts at Metroidvania design leave something to be desired.

Rose Belmont is not her father. Rose is something new: the next generation of the legendary vampire hunters spending her life trying to step out of Trevor's shadow, to be seen as the self-reliant woman she is. Any monster that faces her is surprised to see a young girl, not the grizzled old man they've grown used to. But, despite these expectations, despite the long shadow her family tree casts, Rose tries to prove herself. She will not rely on, or be saved by, what came before. She will blossom into something all her own. This is her story, not his.

Castlevania: Belmont's Curse is, similarly, something new, learning from its predecessors. The first entry in the series in over a decade, and developed not internally by Konami but by the indie studios responsible for roguelike Dead Cells (including its Castlevania-themed DLC), Evil Empire and Motion Twin, it manages to stand on its own as a modern interpretation of a classic series. That said, it's far from a perfect evolution. And, just to get this out of the way: yes, it feels a little like Dead Cells in its movement, and no, since there has been some confusion online, it's not a roguelike.

I am consistently left stunned by Belmont's Curse. Still images really can't do justice to the ambient, ever-moving beauty of this world and those that inhabit it. Each area gifts you with sights beautiful and varied, from the crushing atmosphere of Dracula's castle (spoilers, Dracula's back, obviously) to the vibrant serenity of the cemetery. I wouldn't blame anyone you for taking a stray hit from a monster because it caught you staring at the background. The animations of Rose and her opponents are a wonder to look at, with Rose bearing an incredibly pretty animation set and specifically a backdash that rivals Alucard's own from Symphony of the Night.

The same praise can be given to Rose's movement and combat capabilities. Everything she does is quick and snappy, the highlight being the series-staple whip now doubles as a grappling hook for both traversal and enemy grappling alike. The whip-hook is the single best aspect of the game for me, completely opening up combat and exploration at once, allowing Rose to essentially fly across and between enemies at will, dodging and weaving projectiles while remaining on the offensive. Many of the game's platforming sequences are primarily built around careful use of the whip-hook, and they rarely felt any more than a tiny bit frustrating (this game does love its NES Mega Man-esque spikes, though they mercifully only reset Rose's progress through the platform sequence, rather than kill her outright).

Her weapons extend beyond just her base sword and the whip-hook though, as she goes on to discover both new weapons scattered around the map and new Arcana as rewards for beating certain bosses. Her arsenal goes on to include a lance and metal gauntlets among other options, granting a player real choice in how they wish to approach combat. Arcana, conversely, are cards that effectively function as spells, letting Rose equip one at a time and offering attacks such as the classic holy cross, a grounded shockwave and a petrifying glare. That last one might clue you in to the bosses here: Belmont's Curse draws from a variety of cultures for its spectacular boss fights, from the beautiful, fictional Medusa to the beautiful, very real Joan of Arc. The bosses are exceptional, each requiring several tries to work out the patterns of and rarely hitting you with something that feels truly unfair (while still hitting very hard). I adored the choice to have a few of these bosses stick around in Rose's head after their defeat as a sort of Greek chorus, offering commentary rarely asked for on whatever dire trouble she got herself into; their bickering and occasional agreement is where the writing shines.

Defeating said bosses and acquiring new abilities, as you might expect, is the aged skeleton of Belmont's Curse, as a new Metroidvania entry to the series. Best Joan in glorious combat and gain her holy light, used to dissipate barriers that would otherwise block Rose's path. Smelt the living armour Talos and acquire an empowered stomp that can shatter certain blocks. You know how these work. That said, while the combat is stellar in a vacuum for its call-and-response rhythm between Rose and her opponents, I feel it creates an unintended, sour dissonance with the search-action aspect.

However, while the boss fights aren't unfair – they're a challenge of skill and patience – that's not true of all combat encounters. It's not uncommon to die in rooms that lock behind you and flood the screen with a ton of enemies at once, in the sort of 'fuck you' design I suppose Castlevania used to be known for. Rose's sole form of healing is a very limited number of potions that can only be regained upon death or finding a save room, so if you run out mid-room, you're kind of doomed to drown under the flurry of projectiles and enemies flying at you at once. In many cases, save rooms are sparse across the map; I really hate to say it as I did with Silksong, but racing through the same rooms over and over after a defeat, ignoring enemies to save time and energy, does not instill joy or satisfaction, it's boring. Belmont's Curse doesn't offer traditional difficulty settings, but instead lets you modify stats individually, such as damage taken or dealt, how many potions Rose can carry at once, and if she respawns outside boss doors or not. These are a nice touch, and I'm sure will help people who struggle with the base difficulty design, but I feel that just handing control over difficulty to players is something of an admittance that the combat balance wasn't quite nailed.

There are other choices made within its Metroidvania-ness that baffle me and actively bring the rest of it down, too.

Rose finds new weapons across the map with varying levels of power, making stumbling upon a chest containing an exceptionally powerful sword or gauntlets feel a meaningful reward for exploration, but she also bears a standardised, empty-feeling RPG level-up system that incrementally increases her health and attack stats — a system that can be forgotten about until she levels up mid-fight and you remember it's there. Despite their wide variety in style, some of the weapons are simply worse than others; in all my playtime, I couldn't find a situation where using the immensely slow greatsword was ever a better idea than using the sword or lance, which are much faster and cover similar ranges.

Attempts to sequence break in any way or go off the implied golden path are met with inexplicable barriers that essentially tell the player "No, silly, you're not gonna do any of that Metroidvania shit here, you're going to play it how we intended."

And, in a choice that I cannot fully detail due to it containing story spoilers, there is a moment towards the end of the game in which you are presented with a boss room that seems of dire importance. Rose's voices in her head warn her that perhaps she is not yet strong enough to face this opponent, but she refutes it, insisting she can prove herself regardless of their doubts — the core of her arc. Yet, she is proven right, as the game allows you to enter the boss room and start a fakeout phase in which the boss 'dies', only to then suddenly switch over to a cutscene in which Rose dies, without a single button pressed by the player. They call her a foolish girl, and god did I feel foolish for thinking I could fight a boss that the game allowed me to face, only for a voice to politely tell me that perhaps I should gain more arcana and abilities before trying again (not being at all specific as to what is actually needed to unlock the true fight, of course). A progression-locked boss that doesn't tell you it's locked until after you automatically fail. Fascinating, ridiculous stuff. What, did you think a Metroidvania would let you fight a boss when you feel ready, not when the game decides you are behind the scenes? Foolish girl, indeed. I closed the game out of utter bewilderment following this experience, if that tells you anything.

Ultimately, this strikes me as an action game that really doesn't want to be a Metroidvania, and is fighting itself each time it leans too far in that non-linear direction. This is a decent action platformer with fun combat, I can't deny that, but I feel this could have been so much more if it had just been linear as they seem to have wanted it to be. I am getting a little tired of seemingly every Metroidvania needing to make its combat the focus over the exploration, and making the 'search' part of search-action suffer as a result. Where Rose is weighed down by the expectations of proving herself as a Belmont, Castlevania: Belmont's Curse is weighed down by seemingly needing to prove itself as a Castlevania game in more than name.

#4092. Switching from GLP-1 Injections to Wegovy Pill Shows Additional Weight Loss of 4.1%

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

People with overweight or obesity who switch from an injectable GLP-1 medication to the Wegovy (semaglutide) pill may continue to lose weight, according to findings from the OCTANE study presented at the 2026 Meeting of the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) in Milan, Italy.

The study is among the first real-world analyses to examine weight loss and treatment experiences among people who switched from an injectable weight loss medication to oral semaglutide (Wegovy).

However, while real-world studies like this can provide useful insights into how treatments work in everyday life, these findings do not prove that switching to the Wegovy pill caused the weight loss.

The results have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal.

This was a retrospective cohort analysis evaluating weight change, treatment adherence, and patient satisfaction after people switched from injectable semaglutide (Wegovy) or tirzepatide (Zepbound) to oral semaglutide for weight management.

The study included a random sample of 194 adults with overweight or obesity and at least one obesity-related complication, such as hypertension, cardiovascular disease, obstructive sleep apnea, and dyslipidemia.

Participants had no history of diabetes or bariatric surgery and had an average baseline weight of 221.6 pounds (100.5 kg) and an average body mass index (BMI) of 34.5 kg/m2.

Participants switched from an injectable GLP-1 medication to oral semaglutide between January 5 and February 7, 2026. There was no requirement for how long they had used an injectable medication or how much weight they had lost before switching.

Participants were required to use oral semaglutide regularly for 3 months and self-report their weight within 30 days of starting treatment and again at the end of the study period.

After 3 months of taking oral semaglutide, participants had lost an average of 4.1% of their body weight, or 8.8 pounds.

At the 3-month mark, 2 in 5 participants (40.7%) had lost 5% or more of their body weight.

The proportion of participants with a BMI of 30 or greater also decreased by 21.6 percentage points, from 87.1% at baseline to 65.5% after 3 months.

Most participants (82.4%) reported at least one treatment improvement. Around 69% reported better-fitting clothes, and around 51% reported eating healthier.

Betul Hatipoglu, MD, Medical Director, Diabetes & Metabolic Center at University Hospitals, Cleveland, OH, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that, “These findings are encouraging because they suggest that some people can continue losing weight after switching to oral treatment.”

However, she also noted, “I would view this as promising early evidence supporting another treatment option, rather than proof that switching improves results.”

Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, noted that because the oral medication is absorbed through the gastrointestinal tract, less of it reaches the bloodstream compared to the injectable form.

However, oral semaglutide can still be effective for weight loss.

“While studies show that the injectable form is more efficacious, current findings demonstrate that the oral version is also effective for weight loss,” said Ali.

Some people may prefer the oral form for ease of use and transportation because it does not require refrigeration. However, it must be taken in the morning on an empty stomach, followed by a 30-minute wait before eating, Ali told Healthline.

“Both formulations can cause gastrointestinal side effects, including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation. Taking a pill does not eliminate those risks,” Hatipoglu said.

Hatipoglu also said factors to consider include how well your current treatment is working, side effects, your comfort with injections, your daily routine, cost, and insurance coverage.

She added that the decision should be made with a prescribing clinician, who can help plan the transition and monitor your response.

“Both options can be effective, so patients should discuss the risks and benefits of each with their provider. Ultimately, these medications are tools to support a healthier diet and lifestyle, and patients who make these fundamental changes achieve the best results,” said Ali.

#4090. How Shame Gets in the Way of Anxiety Recovery

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Shame—the feeling that we are somehow “bad” or broken—gets in the way of many facets of our lives, and one of these that I have both experienced personally and observed in my clinical practice is our ability to cope with and recover from anxiety. Anxiety, although commonly experienced and not necessarily indicative of a diagnosable mental disorder, can often feel like a problem we are suffering from in isolation, while those around us seem to function without any trouble whatsoever. The problem with this way of thinking is that it incorrectly positions anxiety as something that is shameful and embarrassing, and that indicates a personal failing.

The sheer prevalence of anxiety challenges the notion that it is a “rare” or “uncommon” condition, let alone one that should be viewed with shame. In fact, the latest statistics show that more than 30 percent of U.S. adults experience an anxiety disorder at some point in their lives (NAMI, 2024), while a 2024 survey revealed that nearly 20 percent of adults reported experiencing anxiety symptoms in the past two weeks (Terlizzi & Zablotsky, 2024). To frame anxiety as an outlying, neurotic condition is to ignore its omnipresence. Normalization of the experience of anxiety is vital to challenging the belief that it deserves to be treated with contempt.

Beyond the statistics that highlight anxiety’s prevalence, there are also numerous studies and scholarly articles linking anxiety to shame and indicating “a growing body of investigations showing that shame and guilt are important features of various psychological problems including anxiety disorders” (Candea & Szentagotai, 2018). A 2021 meta-analysis found that, in “thirty-seven articles included in the current review [which] empirically examined the direct association between shame and [anxiety],…35 reported significant positive correlations between shame and social anxiety” (Swee et al., 2021). The link between anxiety and shame cannot be denied.

But what does this link mean in terms of anxiety coping and recovery? Shame, as defined by June Tangney, a distinguished university professor in the psychology department at George Mason University, is when “we feel bad about ourselves. We are fundamentally flawed because we did something. It reflects on who we are as a person. I’m a bad person for having done that” (APA.org, 2023). Now consider the harshness of this type of self-assessment: I felt anxious; therefore, I am fundamentally flawed and am a bad person. This is hardly motivating language for someone who is trying to cope with a problem or navigate a challenge. Yet, it is the way many anxious people (myself included) speak to themselves. It is common to assign self-negative assessments to our experience with anxiety, which often manifest in the form of shame and humiliation.

The irony here is that one of the main things we look for when we are anxious is reassurance; for example, we long to be assured that we are safe and that all is well when we feel an intense wave of worry or panic. Yet, the inner language of shame is anything but reassuring. Rather, it perpetuates an inner narrative of being, as Tangney describes, “flawed” and “bad.” It is difficult to find reassurance in that type of message. In addition to not being reassuring, shame also keeps us stuck and works against motivation to change. Imagine wanting to learn a new skill but each time you attempt it, you say to yourself, “I am so bad at this.” The chances of sharpening that skill are slim in the face of such self-recrimination. In essence, shame does nothing for motivation or ability to move forward.

So, is it as simple as just speaking to ourselves more kindly? Yes and no—while a little positive, supportive self-talk would go a long way toward helping feel less anxiety-based shame, it is not that simple. A larger reframing, redefining, and redescription of the experience of anxiety is necessary. Rather than framing it as “disordered,” “dramatic,” “histrionic,” or “irrational,” anxiety can be seen as an uncomfortable yet commonly experienced phenomenon that affects many individuals. If we can accept anxiety as a part of the human experience, we can fight the unhelpful shame narrative. After all, if shame makes us feel alone, then acceptance can cultivate commonality and mutuality, which can combat the idea that anxiety is an experience that should be hidden from the world and dealt with in shameful solitude.

My own experience, as well as my years of clinical work with individuals experiencing anxiety and panic, reminds me daily that anxiety, when viewed as pathological and purely diagnostic, brings feelings of shame and humiliation, isolates those experiencing it, and paints a picture of the person as broken. When, however, anxiety is viewed as a normal, albeit uncomfortable and challenging, part of being human, shame reduces, and real healing begins.

In one 2025 observational study, semaglutide users were more likely to get a new erectile-dysfunction diagnosis or prescription. But that’s correlational, not causational.

Other studies have found that taking dulaglutide for four weeks had no effect on desire, and long-term dulaglutide use is associated with a slightly lower risk of moderate to severe erectile dysfunction. “They turn down cravings for food,” he explains. “So, it’s fair to ask whether they could dial down sexual interest as well.”

Animal studies suggest that these medications can alter reward-related behavior. But as Dr. Luthar points out, it’s still unclear whether those changes translate to sexual desire in humans.

“Those studies often use doses and delivery methods that don’t match how people take these drugs, and you can’t ask a mouse how his libido is,” he adds.

“We also want to rule out things like thyroid problems, alcohol, stress, and relationship issues,” he says.

The good news is there may be a relatively simple fix that doesn’t require giving up the medication—and its benefits. For example, Dr. Luthar says that may mean slowing down the dose increases, checking for nutrient deficiencies, and treating underlying causes like low testosterone.

“I have patients in this category who have improved with dose reduction,” says Dr. Appelbaum. “I have others where we have had to weigh the metabolic benefits of continuing the medication against the quality-of-life impact of persistent libido blunting.”

Whatever you do, don’t assume your ability to get an erection—or finish—isn’t meant to be taken seriously.

“Your sex life is part of your health,” says Dr. Appelbaum. “Your physician needs to know.”

#4081. Our Slow Development Is Key to Human Uniqueness

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Updated October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

The baby giraffe, weighing as much as 150 lbs, can stand and walk within the first hour of its life. The baby pig can start walking just minutes after birth. The baby iguana? Moments after hatching, it can leap into a full-out sprint to avoid predators.

The abilities of young animals can be awe-inspiring. How do the skills of the young human stack up?

If you've spent any time around human newborns, you'll be hugely underwhelmed. It's a lot of crying, a lot of diaper changes, and very little resemblance to the abilities of adult humans. They can barely lift their own heads, let alone walk.

Compared to other animals, human nature has some very humble beginnings.

But from this humble starting point eventually comes the incredible sophistication, talent, and mastery that defines the human condition. All human exceptionalism, in all of its richness and diversity, stems from the same modest starting point.

How do we get there? When we examine the question of human nature through the lens of neuroscience, a compelling picture emerges. It suggests that the often incredible abilities of adult humans are inextricably linked to this "incompleteness" at birth.

How does the developing brain ultimately take on these skills and abilities? And what can this neuroscientific perspective teach us about the essence of human nature?

The first thing to note is fairly obvious: Compared to newborns, who are all relatively similar, adult humans differ tremendously. We come to hold a range of incredibly specialized skills and aptitudes. Some become astronauts, while others become rappers, athletes, etc. Some speak Japanese, while others speak sign language, or English, or speak many languages. You get the idea.

This basic observation poses a difficult question: How can we ultimately come to take on such incredible specialization, when our starting place, as newborns, is so unspecialized?

This brings us front and center to the age-old debate in developmental neuroscience about nature versus nurture.

On one extreme view, we're born as blank slates: everything we come to understand in this world, from emotions to concepts to language. The shape and growth of our bodies may be genetically determined, but the contents of our minds are not. Nothing is predetermined. Here, culture, experience, and socialization play a complete and deterministic role in cultivating our inner lives.

At the other end of the spectrum, it's all innate. The essential features of our adult self are already genetically specified at birth. This includes our abilities and aptitudes, intelligence, and personality. Even a predilection toward being a musician, as opposed to being an accountant or some other profession, is ultimately an element of our genetic endowment. In this view, development is merely the process where these inborn features are polished and expressed.

While most neuroscientists and developmental psychologists will agree that the answer lies somewhere in between, the debate—especially about contentious topics like intelligence—rages on.

However, from the perspective of human nature, these two forces may be much more complementary than the debate typically poses. To whatever extent that the environment can shape features of our adult selves (e.g., nurture), it's fundamental nature of the human brain.

Humans, as a species, didn't evolve many specific traits or tendencies. Unlike the Iguana, which can rely on pure, genetically imprinted instinct to sprint away from deadly predators moments after it has hatched, human beings have very few specific behaviors, pieces of information, and tendencies that are present at birth.

What we are born with is maximal flexibility. We're born with an openness, unlike any other in the animal kingdom, to be shaped by the specific features and experiences presented to us in our environment. Our specialization is the sheer capacity for learning and malleability.

In this view, the biggest contribution of our nature is that we are shaped so profoundly by our nurture.

One interesting line of evidence for this view comes not from modern neuroscience but from anthropological investigations of ancient humans during a critical period in our evolution. Research spearheaded by Rick Potts of The Smithsonian's Human Origins Program finds that early humans faced unique and intense evolutionary pressures.

Food resources were scarce and highly variable, the climate swung rapidly from season to season, and a wide range of large predators was an ever-present feature of life. The throughline of all of these challenges was variability in the environment: Early humans, during this time, could only afford to rest on their laurels for a short time. One season, they would need to catch fish to get by, but the next season or two, they'd need to hunt or forage for berries.

Coupled with the ever-changing weather and the dynamic threats predators face, there was no one lifestyle solution to settle into. Thus, forged through these environmental pressures, our distant ancestors slowly evolved this incredible capacity for change. Instead of developing a specific set of characteristics suited for a particular environment, these early humans evolved the capacity to adapt to whatever came their way.

The human brain evolved for flexibility, itself.

Humans are born incomplete and, of any other creature, molded most significantly by their specific environment and set of experiences. But what precisely about the brain—especially the developing brain— makes human beings so open and uniquely adaptable? What is it about the human brain that enables this?

This comes down to neuroplasticity, which is the brain's incredible ability to be shaped by experiences and adapt following injury or ailment. It is the flexibility of the physical brain that enables it to change and evolve as we go through life.

Neuroplasticity is the basis for all human learning. Without this malleability, the brain would be unable to retain new information, create new memories, or improve our skills.

While neuroplasticity persists throughout the lifetime, it is a much more robust feature of the young, developing brain. This is why learning, especially for implicit skills, is much easier early in life than later. Young children learn languages, for example, far better and more quickly than their adult counterparts, and they do so with little apparent effort. Such is the power of the young developing brain.

By just two and a half years old, toddlers know up to 1,000 words in their native language, and can communicate substantial ideas. Moreover, children growing up in bilingual or multilingual environments will learn each language relatively easily. In contrast, if you've ever tried to learn a new language after your 20s, you know it's much more arduous.

Language learning is but one of many examples of developmental exceptionalism. Consider learning a new skill, such as a sport or instrument. These are undoubtedly much easier to master as a child than to try and pick up as an adult. The same 1,000 hours of learning are much more fruitful as a child than as an adult.

Our ability to learn by leaps and bounds as a young child is owed to the plasticity of the early, developing brain.

The researchers found that women consistently reported a higher prevalence of pain across all 11 sites than men.

When zoology professor Daniel Baldassarre traveled to the remote Galápagos Islands of Wolf and Darwin with two other researchers, getting there proved quite a challenge. With no beaches to land a boat on, they had to approach the islands’ cliffs in a small dinghy and wait for a lull in the waves before jumping onto sharp, black lava rocks.

But their efforts paid off. On that trip, they saw a bird that very few people have ever set eyes on in the wild: the vampire ground finch. These finches are only found on Wolf and Darwin, and feed on the blood of much larger birds called Nazca boobies that also live on the islands.

Up close, Baldassarre and his colleagues noted the vampire finches’ blood-stained bills and their throats visibly engorged with blood. They also saw them in action.

“We observed scores of vampire finches clamouring all over the backs, tails, and wings of boobies, opening up substantial wounds with their sharp beaks, and drinking their fill of blood,” they wrote in an article for The Conversation.

“Blood is a notoriously difficult food source to process and extract nutrients from, which probably partly explains why so few animals have evolved to feed on it,” Baldassarre tells Popular Science. He suspects that on these islands where freshwater is extremely rare, blood might serve as a water source for the finches.

It’s not the only component of the vampire finches’ diet, however. Based on unpublished data collected by Baldassarre and his colleagues, blood makes up about one-tenth of the vampire finches’ diet.

“These finches are quite opportunistic, so they will eat just about anything they can find,” Baldassarre says. “We observed them feeding on everything from seeds to insects to broken seabird eggs.”

One theory is that vampire finches rely more on blood feeding in periods of extreme drought, when other resources are scarce. But more research is needed to see if this idea holds up in practice.

Vampire finches weren’t always vampires, scientists think. The relationship between vampire finches and boobies probably started off with the finches eating parasites found on the skin and feathers of the boobies. This would have been mutually beneficial because it provided a cleaning service for the boobies and food for the finches.

But at some point in evolutionary history, in the process of picking off parasites, the finches started to create lesions on the boobies’ skin, and eventually began feeding on their blood.

Over time, their bills became more scalpel-like, perfect for making skin incisions. Their digestive system also changed. When Baldassarre and his colleagues studied the microbes found in vampire finches’ guts, they found that their gut microbiome was very different from that of other, non-blood-consuming finches on the Galápagos Islands, something they attribute to their unique diet.

Until recently, vampire finches were considered a subspecies of the sharp-beaked ground finch (Geospiza difficilis).

“But they have now been designated as their own species [Geospiza septentrionalis] because of the differences they have evolved because of blood feeding,” Baldassarre notes.

Baldassarre and his colleagues have seen vampire finches gang up on and kill nestling boobies,“but that was quite rare,” he points out.

“In most cases, the finches seem to inflict just enough damage to break the skin and extract some blood, but not too much to harm the boobies significantly.” This is similar to many parasite-host relationships, such as fleas, ticks, and mosquitoes on mammals. “You can kind of think of the vampire finches as giant mosquitoes,” Baldassarre says.

As seen in a segment on vampire finches in David Attenborough’s documentary A Perfect Planet, the boobies seem to tolerate the vampire finches rather than chasing them away. There are likely a few reasons for this, Baldassarre says.

“For one, as in large animals being bitten by mosquitoes, often the damage is minimal and not noticeable, so it doesn’t elicit a strong reaction. Also, the boobies sometimes have a vested interest in staying put and guarding their spot in the colony, or perhaps nest or nestlings, so may not want to fly away. Finally, the finches seem to target hard-to-reach areas on the boobies like the base of tail feathers, so it’s likely difficult for the boobies to deter them.”

On Sept. 30, NASA formally concluded OSIRIS-REx, the historic first U.S. mission to pluck a pristine piece of an asteroid and deliver it safely back to Earth.

The van-sized OSIRIS-REx spacecraft — whose name is short for Origins, Spectral Interpretation, Resource Identification, Security-Regolith Explorer — visited and sampled Bennu, an ancient space rock holding pristine remnants from the dawn of our solar system more than 4.5 billion years ago. Among those cosmic relics are raw organic building blocks, the kind that may have seeded life on Earth.

"I have known this day was coming for a long time, but it is still difficult to see it arrive," OSIRIS-REx Principal Investigator Dante Lauretta, who's based at the University of Arizona, said in a post on LinkedIn. "I feel enormous pride in what we accomplished together, and real sadness that this journey has come to an end."

For the mission team, the farewell capped a decade of high-stakes engineering, on-the-fly troubleshooting and nail-biting close calls that ultimately delivered to Earth the largest asteroid sample ever collected in space, one that held secrets from the early solar system.

Long before launch, the team faced obstacles that nearly thwarted the endeavor — chiefly, two rejections from NASA — before finally securing the green light for the $1 billion project under the New Frontiers Program in 2011.

When OSIRIS-REx launched on Sept. 8, 2016, on a two-year journey across the inner solar system, Bennu was little more than a point of light in telescopes. Scientists expected the asteroid’s surface to be a smooth, sandy sphere where OSIRIS-REx could seamlessly touch down. Much to their surprise, however, when the probe arrived at the asteroid in late 2018, it revealed Bennu to be a treacherous, loosely bound amalgamation of jagged boulders.

"Our expectations about the asteroid's surface were completely wrong," Lauretta said in an Oct. 7 statement from the University of Arizona. "There was no obvious place to collect a sample anywhere."

When OSIRIS-REx touched down on a small crater named Nightingale, Bennu offered so little structural resistance that scientists said its surface reacted more like fluid than a hard, rocky shell. The spacecraft's sampling head sank directly into the asteroid, stopping after burrowing 20 inches (51 centimeters) deep by firing its thrusters at the right time to pull away, with the precious sample in hand.

Then came another nail-biting moment: The sample collector lid got jammed in a partially open position because so much material was scooped up, allowing fragments of the precious cargo to escape. In a race against time to prevent further loss, the mission team quickly stowed the sampler head directly inside the return capsule, securing the cosmic treasure for its journey back to Earth.

Even the final return to Earth, on Sept. 24, 2023, kept the team on edge: A parachute failed to deploy properly during reentry, forcing a tense but ultimately safe landing in the Utah desert.

"Successful exploration missions are not those that avoid surprises; they are those that are deliberately designed to adapt to them," Lauretta said in the statement, quoting a line from the mission's final report to NASA.

"That certainly describes OSIRIS-REx," he said. "Again and again, the mission presented us with circumstances we had not expected, and again and again, this team found a way forward."

The mission's triumphs began long before the samples reached Earth. For example, the probe set a Guinness World Record when it slid into orbit around the 1,610-foot-wide (490 meters) Bennu, which became the smallest object ever circled by a spacecraft.

The scientific payoff that followed included remote-sensing work that confirmed Bennu's surface is coated in carbon-bearing compounds. Those observations also revealed that Bennu was once part of a much larger, water-rich parent asteroid that shattered in a catastrophic collision in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.

The returned sample — which weighs 4.29 ounces (121.6 grams) here on Earth, more than twice the mission's requirement — continues to thrill and occupy scientists.

A wave of research papers, now topping 200, continues to piece together the composition and history of the ancient space rock. Among other findings, Bennu's pristine material has revealed amino acids and sugars that, on Earth, are essential for life, alongside a history of exposure to liquid water that could have served as the "broth" for these compounds to interact.

More discoveries are likely to come. Most of the returned Bennu sample is being preserved for future generations of scientists, who will study it "with questions and instruments we cannot yet anticipate," Lauretta said in the statement.

Though its OSIRIS-REx chapter is closed, the spacecraft itself is far from retired. Renamed OSIRIS-APEX (OSIRIS-APophis EXplorer), the probe is using its remaining propellant to head toward Apophis, another near-Earth asteroid and a different type of space rock from Bennu. It is scheduled to arrive in 2029.

#4069. Taking Blood Pressure Meds? DASH Diet Likely Safe for Potassium Levels

  • A new study reports that the DASH diet does not significantly increase potassium levels in people taking high blood pressure medication.
  • Researchers said that the diet doesn’t appear to cause significant changes in kidney function.
  • Experts say the DASH diet offers many health benefits, but they recommend that people gradually integrate it into their daily lives.

People who adhere to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet do not experience significant changes in potassium levels while taking blood pressure medications or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) drugs, according to a new study.

Researchers also reported that people taking these medications do not experience significant changes in their kidney function.

Both types of medications are known to increase potassium levels. High potassium levels can lead to hyperkalemia, a condition that can cause issues with the heart rhythm. People with impaired kidney function due to chronic kidney disease are at increased risk of hyperkalemia.

“The DASH diet is a well-known, proven nutrition pattern that is known to improve blood pressure,” said Marian Budu, MD, a clinical research coordinator on the GoFresh Trials at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston and the presenting author of the study, in a news release.

“Knowing that the DASH diet did not increase blood potassium levels in this population with normal baseline potassium levels provides assurance that the DASH diet can be prescribed to a wide range of people,” she continued.

The preliminary findings are being presented at the American Heart Association’s Hypertension Scientific Sessions 2026 meeting this week in Virginia. The research has not been published yet in a peer-reviewed journal.

Experts not involved in the study told Healthline that the research provides medical professionals with important information.

“While we have traditionally exercised caution before recommending the DASH diet in patients who are prescribed ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or potassium-sparing diuretics, these results show that those patients could likely adopt the DASH diet without significant concern for harm,” said Cheng-Han Chen, MD, an interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA.

“DASH diets do not appear to increase potassium levels to any significant degree in patients taking medications that are known to potentially raise serum potassium,” added Kevin Volpp, MD, the founding director of the Penn Center for Health Incentives and Behavioral Economics in Pennsylvania and the scientific lead of the American Heart Association’s Health Care by Food initiative.

“This is important because it is a theoretical concern that diets rich in potassium could cause high levels in potassium in some patients.”

DASH diet didn’t raise potassium levels with blood pressure meds

The new study was a secondary analysis of the GoFresh and GoFresh Rx randomized trials that enrolled Black adults in the greater Boston area with high blood pressure.

Some of the participants were taking medications to lower their blood pressure, and others were not taking any hypertension drugs.

The study focused on four classes of blood pressure medications: angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), and potassium-sparing diuretics and beta-blockers, as well as NSAIDs.

The analysis took place in 2026 and included 356 Black adults with an average age of 53. About 70% were women.

At the start of the study, the participants had systolic blood pressure readings between 120 mm Hg and less than 150 mm Hg. They also had a diastolic blood pressure reading of less than 100 mm Hg.

The participants also had varying degrees of kidney function. In addition, they had limited access to grocery stores.

Half of the participants received three months of weekly DASH-patterned groceries and weekly nutrition counseling sessions. The other half received a cash stipend every four weeks to buy groceries, with no nutrition counseling.

The participants’ blood potassium levels were measured at the beginning of the study and again after 12 weeks.

The researchers reported that eating more potassium-rich foods in the DASH diet did not increase potassium levels to dangerous levels among participants taking blood pressure medications.

They noted no significant increase in potassium levels, regardless of whether participants were assigned to the dietary intervention or the monetary stipend group.

In a news release, the researchers acknowledged that their study did not include people with stage 4 or stage 5 kidney disease or those with higher-than-normal potassium levels.

They added that the study population comprised only Black individuals living in Boston, so the results may not generalize to the broader population.

The study authors said that future research should focus on participants with suboptimal kidney function and those with higher potassium levels.

Volpp agreed that more research is needed.

“This study was conducted in patients with non-elevated potassium levels at baseline and does not answer the question of whether they are safe in patients with elevated potassium levels or significant impairments in renal function,” he told Healthline.

Chen noted that potassium levels should be taken seriously.

“Certain classes of blood pressure medications can potentially cause high potassium levels, especially in people with kidney disease,” he told Healthline. “If potassium levels become too high, this can lead to symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, and muscle pain, or even serious heart problems such as arrhythmias.”

Get started with the DASH diet

The DASH diet is a dietary plan that emphasizes fruits, vegetables, and lean proteins such as chicken, fish, and beans.

The plan also includes whole grains, low fat dairy products, and cooking with vegetable oils. The diet discourages the intake of red meat, salt, fat, and added sugars.

Previous research has indicated that the DASH diet can provide numerous health benefits.

A preliminary 2022 study concluded that the dietary plan can help lower blood pressure and improve heart health, but those results haven’t been published in a scientific journal. Still, the findings were consistent with a 2019 study that found the DASH diet can help lower the risk of heart disease.

In addition, a 2020 study reported that adults aged 65 and older with obesity lost body fat while following the DASH Diet. A 2023 study suggested the diet can help reduce belly fat.

A 2019 meta-analysis suggested that the DASH diet may also help lower the risk of certain cancers.

Many of these protective effects are attributed to the diet’s high fruit and vegetable content.

Chen said the DASH diet has many benefits, but the eating plan might not be for everybody.

“The DASH diet has been shown to be beneficial in lowering blood pressure, losing weight, and controlling blood sugar and cholesterol levels,” he said. “However, the amount of planning and portion tracking required to follow the diet may be too much for some people.”

Chen recommended that people ease into the DASH diet.

“The best way to start on the DASH diet is to slowly introduce the changes to your meals,” he said. “This can be as simple as including a few extra servings of fruits and vegetables each day as well as swapping some whole grain products for refined grains. Over time, more of the diet can be incorporated.”

Volpp agreed that introducing the DASH diet in increments is a good strategy.

“For many people, it is easiest to make small incremental changes sequentially,” he said. “One way to get started is to increase fruit by a couple of servings a day. Another is to pay attention to the amount of sodium in packaged foods and consciously choose products lower in sodium. A third is to make a conscious effort to eat more vegetables.”

“Make changes first that are easiest for you to make; it will be easier to make those changes stick,” Volpp added.

#4064. Letter from the Editor-in-Chief: Lung Cancer Can Look Like Anyone

  • Up to 1 in 5 lung cancer cases in the United States occur in people who’ve never smoked, but our picture of the disease hasn’t caught up.
  • Healthline Editor-in-Chief Robin Hough talks with Gini Harrison, a psychologist and his friend of nearly 30 years, whose only symptom was a sore shoulder blamed on breastfeeding. Her stage 4 EGFR-positive lung cancer diagnosis took nearly nine months.
  • They discuss testing, catching lung cancer sooner, and the documentary film, Our Lungs: Time to Breathe, which follows filmmaker Sarah Li and the family of Alex, a 24-year-old who died weeks after diagnosis.

Picture someone with lung cancer. Now picture a 24-year-old soccer player in the prime of his life. A filmmaker in her early 40s with a camera in her hand, doing what she loves best. A busy mom of two, with a shoulder so sore she can’t pick up her newborn, yet struggling to get a diagnosis. 

The gap between the picture in our heads and the people actually being diagnosed is costing time. And time is the one thing with lung cancer you cannot get back. 

We know that lung cancer is the most common cancer worldwide and the leading cause of cancer death. And while lung cancer incidence rates are falling in many countries as fewer people smoke, lung cancer in people who have never smoked is on the rise globally, both as a proportion and in absolute numbers. 

According to the CDC, as many as 20% of lung cancers diagnosed in the US are in people who have never smoked. Data suggests that non-smoking lung cancer is now among the most common causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide.

The numbers are stark. Lung cancer can affect anyone. And that includes the young – like Alex, one of the subjects of a powerful new short documentary commissioned by the charity EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK for their Our Lungs: Time to Breathe campaign. 

Alex was a 24-year-old soccer player who died just weeks after receiving a diagnosis of Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) positive lung cancer, following several misdiagnoses born of the fact that no one suspected lung cancer in one so young, fit, and with no smoking history. The film was made by filmmaker Sarah Li, who herself was diagnosed with Stage 4 EGFR+ lung cancer in April 2024, just a month after Alex died from the same mutation of the disease, and charts her journey with survivor’s guilt, what an earlier diagnosis may have meant for Alex, and what that fact means for her own future. 

Ahead of Lung Cancer Awareness Month in November, I sat down with my friend, Gini Harrison, to talk about the film, her own journey, and why catching lung cancer sooner matters so much. Gini is a Professor of Psychology at The Open University, a trustee of EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK, and is living with stage 4 EGFR-positive lung cancer. She’s also the mom at the top of this piece, whose sore shoulder was initially attributed to breastfeeding posture.

This interview has been lightly edited for clarity and length.

EGFR-positive lung cancer: Warning signs

Take me back to the beginning. What were your initial symptoms, and what were you told?

Gini Harrison: I’d had my son, Michael, in February 2021 and was on maternity leave. My daughter, Emily, was five. I think the shoulder pain started around March, but that was it. No cough, no breathlessness… nothing that made me think about my lungs.

Initially, they gave me painkillers, which helped a bit. But the pain kept getting worse. Eventually, I started losing movement and strength in my arm, and it got to the point where I couldn’t pick up my son anymore.

This was all happening during COVID, so my appointments were over the telephone. Eventually, after months without being examined face-to-face, I paid to see a shoulder specialist. As soon as he touched my shoulder blade and I practically leaped out of the chair, he knew something wasn’t right. He ordered an MRI, which showed a tumor in my shoulder blade and another in my upper right lung. In December 2021, I was diagnosed with stage IV lung cancer.

What do you remember about that period, and how did it feel to be reassured when your instinct said otherwise?

Gini Harrison: I wasn’t thinking, “This must be cancer.” I was just thinking, “Why isn’t this getting better?”

And I think that distinction matters. Patients shouldn’t have to identify their own disease before they can get help. You should be able to say that something is wrong and be listened to.

What went through your head when you heard “lung cancer”? Did you have any of the assumptions you now push back against?

Gini Harrison: It was devastating. So many of my first questions were about the children: “How are we going to tell them?” “Am I going to be here to see them grow up?” “Will they remember me?” Suddenly, there was a question mark over a future I’d taken for granted.

I was also completely shocked by the type of cancer. If someone had said breast cancer, I don’t think I would have been anywhere near as surprised. But lung cancer?! I’d always associated it with smoking, and I’d never smoked.

I hadn’t imagined that this particular cancer could ever happen to me. So yes, I had some assumptions that I now challenge. I understand how deeply rooted they are because I had them too.

What lung cancer ‘looks like’

When people learn you have lung cancer, what do they say? What’s the first question you get asked?

Gini Harrison: People are often shocked, and one of the first questions is whether I smoked.

And they often look healthy. That doesn’t mean everything is easy, or that there aren’t side effects. It just means you can’t tell what someone is dealing with by looking at them.

I think we’ve given lung cancer such a narrow public image that people outside it can become almost invisible.

Has anyone ever asked if you smoked? How do you answer, and how should it land?

Gini Harrison: Oh, yes! Once, I spoke to a doctor about a breathing problem after radiotherapy. He asked, “How much did you smoke?” When I said I’d never smoked, he replied, “Well, how did you get lung cancer then?”

On another occasion, I went to a hospice for help managing side effects, and someone there asked, “How are you still alive?” That’s quite a thing to hear when you’ve come looking for support!

When people ask about smoking, I try to explain that I’ve never smoked and I’m certainly not alone. Never smoking doesn’t make you immune. But I’m also very clear that nobody deserves lung cancer, whether they’ve smoked or not. We shouldn’t be creating a hierarchy of who deserves compassion.

Smoking history matters medically. But in a personal conversation, “How are you doing?” is a much kinder place to start.

Genetics and biomarker testing 

Explain biomarker or genetic testing as if I’m a friend (which I am!) who’s never heard of it. Why does it matter?

Gini Harrison: Lung cancer isn’t one single disease. Two people can both have lung cancer, but different changes in their cells may be driving it.

Genes contain the instructions that tell our cells how to work, including when to grow and divide. Changes in those genes (called mutations) can cause cells to keep dividing when they shouldn’t, forming a tumor. In my case, there’s a mutation in a gene called EGFR.

EGFR-positive lung cancer is a type of non-small cell lung cancer caused by a genetic mutation in the EGFR gene, which keeps the EGFR receptor switched “on,” leading to uncontrolled cell growth and enhanced cancer cell survival.

Biomarker testing looks for these changes, as well as other features of the cancer that can guide treatment. If we know what’s helping the cancer grow, there may be a medication that specifically targets it.

The word “genetic” can sound scary, particularly when you have children. But these changes usually develop in cancer cells over a person’s lifetime. They aren’t necessarily inherited.

Why early diagnosis is so important 

What would you say to someone reading this who has a symptom that won’t go away?

Gini Harrison: Please get it checked. And if it persists, worsens or doesn’t improve with treatment, go back. Explain what’s changed and how it’s affecting your life. Ask, “What happens next if this doesn’t get better?”

The symptoms don’t always look like lung symptoms. Mine was shoulder pain.

Most symptoms will turn out to be something other than cancer. But when a symptom continues to worsen, the original explanation needs to be reconsidered. Lower risk doesn’t mean no risk.

And I don’t want all the responsibility to fall on patients to keep pushing. Reassurance should come with a next step.

Our Lungs: Time to Breathe – Alex and Sarah’s stories 

How did you get involved in advocacy, and what was the hardest thing about being part of it?

Gini Harrison: Once I had recovered from my initial treatment, I realized that I wanted to help others in my situation, so I became a trustee of the charity EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK. Through this work, we produced a film, Our Lungs: Time to Breathe. At the heart of it, it challenges the perception of what someone with lung cancer looks like.

The hardest part is how close to home it feels. Alex was so young, and that worked against him. Watching his family talk about what happened is heartbreaking. It’s just so unfair.

What did you hope people would see in Sarah’s and Alex’s stories?

Gini Harrison: Sarah’s story shows life continuing alongside lung cancer. She’s a filmmaker with ambitions, people she loves, and things she wants to do.

Through Alex’s family, you see the enormity of the loss. I hope people feel that, rather than simply hearing another statistic.

Lung cancer and the bigger picture

What’s changed since you were diagnosed, and what hasn’t?

Gini Harrison: For me, the biggest thing is that my children are growing up and I’m here to see it. Michael is now five (the age Emily was when I was diagnosed), and Emily is about to turn ten. I can’t tell you how grateful I am to be here with them.

It wasn’t about big bucket-list things. It was about being here every day. Seeing Michael start school. Seeing Emily reach double figures. Those milestones are enormous when you’ve wondered whether you’d get to see them.

After chemotherapy and radiotherapy, I’ve had no evidence of disease since May 2022, and my scans remain stable. But a big change has been seeing the progress in available treatment. It gives me more hope about what might be available if I need treatment again.

The public image of lung cancer still needs to catch up. We need better recognition of symptoms, access to appropriate testing and treatment, and support for the whole person. As a psychologist as well as a patient, I’m particularly aware that psychological well-being and family life need attention alongside the cancer itself.

What do you want people to do after reading this?

Gini Harrison: I’d love for people to share the film and help get it in front of those who wouldn’t normally think lung cancer has anything to do with them. And take something from it into everyday life: book the appointment you’ve been putting off, ask your doctor about testing, or respond with kindness when someone tells you about their diagnosis.

Just being willing to rethink your assumptions could make a difference.

What do you wish more people would say to someone in your position?

Gini Harrison: “You don’t have to put a brave face on with me. How are you really doing?”

When you look well, or your scans are good, people can assume you’re “fine now”. But I’m not sure the fear ever completely goes away. Every scan is terrifying. It feels like there’s a sword of Damocles hanging over my head, and part of me is waiting for the bad scan. I also still have lasting side effects from treatment, and probably always will.

My life has fundamentally changed. Some of that has brought good things, but it doesn’t make the difficult parts disappear. It helps when someone gives you room to be honest about both, without expecting you to reassure them or turn everything into a positive story.

Gini and I have been friends for nearly 30 years, and, while she’d probably cringe to hear me say it, I remain in awe of the way she approaches life with cancer. I am so grateful for her sharing her experiences with me for this column. What Gini asks of us is simple: look again, ask the question, listen, and act on your instincts. Our Lungs: Time to Breathe is where that starts for her. The study included 39 adults with an average age of 50, an average weight of 189 pounds, and an average body mass index (BMI) of 31.4.

All participants had lost more than 10% of their body weight while taking GLP-1 medications and had stopped treatment within the previous 30 days.

All participants also completed questionnaires at the beginning and end of the study and used Bluetooth scales to track their weight at home throughout the four-month period.

Participants continued to receive care from their healthcare providers, which may include nutrition, exercise, and lifestyle counseling from a clinician or registered dietitian. Participants received a subscription to Noom, an app that uses behavioral strategies to support weight loss and maintenance. The app also provides tools to help users recognize and manage triggers that may lead to overeating. Participants were encouraged to use the app for at least 15 minutes daily, including completing lessons, logging food, and tracking their weight.

After four months, participants who received either medically tailored meals or access to the digital weight management app experienced significantly less weight regain than those receiving usual care.

The estimated weight regain was 10.75% of body weight in the usual-care group, compared with 3.6% in the Noom group and 3.58% in the medically tailored meals group.

Although participants in all three groups regained weight, the findings suggest that structured nutritional or behavioral support may help limit weight regain after stopping GLP-1 medications.

“These findings highlight the benefits of ongoing support for patients on a weight loss journey,” said Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA. Ali wasn’t involved in the study.

He added that tracking apps and regular follow-up appointments with a healthcare professional or registered dietitian may also help people maintain their progress.

#4051. Real or Not? Teaching Children About Generative AI

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

This post was coauthored by Lydia Rowley, a student at Brigham Young University.

Imagine this: You unlock your phone and open Instagram. The first post you see is a video of a popular public figure making rude, racist comments. You’re stunned. You never thought they would endorse those viewpoints. As your finger hovers over the unfollow button, you watch the video again—and notice a few little details that change everything. The hair and skin are a little too shiny. The motions are too smooth. A few people in the background disappear between shots. Clicking on the comments confirms your suspicions: This video is AI-generated.

You close Instagram, relieved to realize the truth. But the fear lingers: Would you notice next time? Even more concerning, would your children notice?

Generative AI becomes more realistic with every update, making it ever more difficult to differentiate AI-generated from real content. Some studies claim children cannot differentiate AI from real content as well as adults, who are not particularly successful themselves. However, new data from our under-review paper shows that 9-year-old children did better than guessing when asked to identify AI-generated videos, though less than half (46.5 percent) identified all the videos correctly. Children’s understanding of AI is related to their ability to identify it. The 54.7 percent of children in our study who said they knew what AI was correctly identified more videos than their less familiar peers.

Parents are often a child’s first source of information, so in-home education about AI-generated content is essential. But what do we teach children about how to identify this new technology? In our research, currently under review, children most readily identified an AI-generated video involving babies riding animals in a fashion show, explaining that this could never happen in real life. Some children noticed when the AI made errors with lighting, skin, or hair, but surprisingly few made note of a caption stating that the video was AI-generated. These observations show us what we can do to help children learn to identify AI-generated videos.

How can we teach children to identify AI-generated videos? The following suggestions build on our findings.

As AI-generated videos and images become increasingly common on the internet, parents can prepare their children to thoughtfully and critically evaluate this content. By educating children with age-appropriate definitions and relevant examples, discussing the implausibility of some AI-generated content, and explaining specific “tells” to look for while evaluating online content, parents can help children develop the critical thinking skills necessary to differentiate real and AI-generated content. AI-generated content is not going away anytime soon, but by educating their children, parents don’t need to be quite so afraid of its deception.
