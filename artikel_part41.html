StarkWare expects to have a whitepaper ready for public review in November, following the week-long community poll.

Starknet StarkNet (STRK) holders have overwhelmingly backed a proposal to research transitioning from an Ethereum Layer 2 network into an independent, post-quantum Layer 1, according to an initial seven-day Snapshot poll which closed on Thursday morning.

More than 93.5% of the voting power supported the proposal, with over 2,800 wallets participating, according to StarkWare. The development company behind Starknet did not vote in the poll, leaving the decision to the community. Following the results, StarkWare and the Starknet Foundation will further research the potential transition. Still, STRK holders would have to vote again on any future proposal to actually move the network.

"We're beginning work on a whitepaper even before we start the weekend, and hope to release a draft for public scrutiny in November," StarkWare CEO Eli Ben-Sasson told The Block. "Starknet's community has chosen to lead on the biggest security challenge crypto has ever faced. AI agents are accelerating the quantum threat in ways many don't realise. The race is on."

Starknet is built on hash-based cryptography, and its accounts can adopt post-quantum signatures without changes to the protocol. Still, it inherits its security from Ethereum, so it is only as quantum-safe as the underlying blockchain, the team explained.

"Ethereum's target of 2029 to become quantum-safe is too late, and deadlines slip. Ask anyone who waited for the Merge," Ben-Sasson added. He has set a 2027 target for Starknet to become quantum-safe, although no date has been agreed for a potential transition to Layer 1.

Ben-Sasson first floated the Layer 1 idea on Oct. 8, and the seven-day community poll opened on Oct. 15. The issue centers on Starknet's continued use of Ethereum for its bridge and data availability, as those parts cannot become quantum-safe independently of Ethereum, according to the proposal. StarkWare previously set out a three-phase post-quantum roadmap in June.

Regardless of the path chosen by the Starknet community, STRK will remain its native token, no new token will be introduced, and Ethereum bridges to Starknet will remain open, according to the Starknet Foundation.

#4397. American Bankers Association: OCC’s GENIUS rules ‘undermine’ US Congress

Banking advocacy organizations are pushing back against the OCC’s finalized rules on a stablecoin law, arguing against changes to provisions on yield.

A lobbying group for US banks has weighed in on the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency’s (OCC’s) recently finalized rules on payment stablecoin issuance and custody, arguing that the regulator’s interpretation went against Congress’ intent.

In a Wednesday notice, the American Bankers Association (ABA) said that while the OCC’s final rules implementing the GENIUS Act included “several positive steps,” the banking group remained critical of provisions related to stablecoin yield. The regulator dropped a provision that would have established a presumption that certain third-party arrangements violated the law’s ban on yield payments.

“The final rule removes the OCC’s originally proposed safeguards that would have addressed indirect arrangements involving affiliates, exchanges and other distribution partners,” said ABA. “As a result, payment stablecoin issuers can provide economically equivalent yield while technically avoiding the statutory prohibition. The OCC also declined to adopt a broader presumption against arrangements designed to circumvent the ban.”

Stablecoin rewards were one of the key issues lawmakers and interest groups debated before and after GENIUS passed Congress in July 2025, and they continue to face scrutiny amid discussions of the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act, a crypto market structure bill currently in limbo after a failed procedure vote in the Senate last month.

Notably, the Blockchain Association approved of the change to yield prohibition, arguing that the OCC “[brought] the final rule more closely in line with the statutory text.” The Bank Policy Institute, another banking industry advocacy group, largely sided with ABA, saying that “the decision to omit the yield presumption replaces clarity with ambiguity and puts the banking system and its customers at risk of deposit flight.”

The OCC finalized its rules ahead of the GENIUS Act’s implementation in January 2027. Other government departments are expected to release rules on GENIUS related to their agencies’ purview over stablecoins, including the Federal Reserve and Treasury.

Fireblocks has denied a report that Visa is in late-stage talks to buy the institutional crypto custody and stablecoin infrastructure firm for around $2.5 billion.

“We are aware of the rumors about a possible acquisition of Fireblocks by Visa. This is news to us, and not true at present time,” the company told The Block on Thursday.

The denial came hours after Axios reported, citing multiple sources, that the two sides were close to a deal. It would have been Visa’s first acquisition in the stablecoin sector and one of the largest in crypto’s history, behind Coinbase’s $2.9 billion purchase of Deribit in 2025 and Naver Financial’s pending $10.3 billion deal for Upbit parent Dunamu.

The reported price would have been a steep markdown from the $8 billion valuation Fireblocks commanded in January 2022, when it raised $550 million in a Series E round at the peak of the venture cycle. Founded in 2018 by Michael Shaulov, Pavel Berengoltz and Idan Ofrat, three veterans of the Israeli cybersecurity scene, the company sells the plumbing that banks, exchanges and fintechs use to hold and move digital assets.

Its customers include BNY Mellon, Worldpay, Revolut and Galaxy, and it says it has secured trillions of dollars in transfers across more than 150 blockchains.

Fireblocks powers FIS’s institutional bitcoin trading, underpins BNP Paribas’s digital asset custody, and sits among the founding members of the Botanix federation, a bitcoin sidechain project.

Whatever the status of the talks, the report landed on fertile ground. Payment incumbents spent 2026 buying or courting the companies that handle stablecoin and crypto rails rather than building them from scratch.

Zerohash, for its part, is seeking a US national trust bank charter.

Visa partnered with Zerohash in August to add stablecoin funding and payouts to Visa Direct, and in September said its stablecoin settlement volume had passed a $20 billion annualized run rate, more than 15 times the prior year’s level.

Both card networks joined a coalition of more than 140 companies behind Open USD, a dollar stablecoin designed to spread the economics of a $300 billion market beyond Tether and Circle.

#4351. This Isn’t Your Dad’s Prostate Test

The PSA screening process got an upgrade. Here’s why it’s worth another look.

IF GETTING A screening test for prostate cancer isn’t on your radar, it’s probably because the government guidelines essentially told you not to bother back in 2012. But prostate cancer rates have continued to rise. It’s now the second leading cause of cancer deaths in men, and it’s particularly lethal for Black men, who are more than twice as likely to die from it as white men. The guidelines have evolved.

The reason to consider the prostate-specific antigen (PSA) test now isn’t any big, headline-grabbing news that there’s a whole new way to test for it (there isn’t yet). It’s not even that PSA testing is now associated with about a 13 percent reduction in deaths from cancer and a 35 percent reduction in metastatic cancer diagnoses. It’s that the entire landscape of what happens after a troublesome result has shifted.

A high PSA number used to fast-track you to an uncomfortable or potentially infection-causing biopsy and hustle you to radiation or surgery. “A lot of men were being exposed to treatments and side effects without necessarily extending their lives,” says Kelvin Moses, MD, PhD, a professor at Vanderbilt University Medical Center who just helped revise the prostate-screening guidelines for the American Urological Association (AUA) and the Society of Urologic Oncology (SUO). Because many prostate cancers grow slowly—which is why doctors say men are more likely to die with prostate cancer than from it—lots of guys underwent procedures that they wouldn’t have really needed.

But now, MRIs and high-tech monitoring can help ID which prostate cancers need to be acted on and which can be left safely alone. “We really need to change the narrative on PSA,” says Yaw Nyame, MD, of the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center at the University of Washington Medical Center. “It’s not whether it works or not. It’s how we can use it as part of a smart screening pathway.”

THE PSA TEST, which measures a protein produced by the prostate, has never been straightforward. High levels indicate something’s off in that walnut-size gland, but they don’t tell you if it’s cancer; it might just be an enlarged prostate or a urinary tract infection.

When the FDA approved the test for cancer screening more than 30 years ago, the message was clear: Get the test! Then in 2012, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force didn’t recommend it on grounds that there wasn’t enough evidence that the benefits outweighed the harms. Now, the tide is turning back to “get it.” Start at age 40 to 45 if you’re Black, have a family history of prostate cancer (father, brothers, uncles), or if breast or ovarian cancer runs in your family. Start around age 50 if you don’t have a family history or other risk factors. (When to get it again? If your PSA is in a healthy range, then screen every two to four years after that.)

And there’s one test you don’t have to take. If fear of the digital rectal exam (DRE) is keeping you from getting tested, relax. The new guidelines reinforce what people have been hoping for all along: It’s an inefficient way to screen for cancer.

NOW, PSA TEST results are only a starting point. If your PSA is high, you’ll want another test in a few months to confirm that it’s not due to something else—inflammation, irritation, or recent ejaculation can drive up the numbers. In 25 to 40 percent of cases, the second PSA test will be normal. About that “normal”: It shifts with age. “A PSA of 4 has always been a cutoff. If your PSA is 4 and you’re 53 years old, that’s very high. But if you’re 75 or 80, that’s pretty reasonable,” says Dr. Moses.

If the follow-up confirms a high number, doctors dig deeper. You may—depending on your doctor and how clear your case is—be eligible for one of what Dr. Nyame calls a “whole entourage” of urinary and blood biomarkers with names like the Prostate Health Index (PHI), 4K, IsoPSA, and MyProstateScore 2.0 that can help clarify if you’re pointing down a biopsy path.

Even then, the next move should be imaging before anybody comes at your prostate with a biopsy needle. “A prostate MRI can reduce the need for a prostate biopsy by about 20 to 30 percent,” says Dr. Nyame. Yet “only 30 to 40 percent of patients with an elevated PSA are getting an MRI before biopsy,” he says. Part of the issue is access to MRIs, combined with the fact that it’s a relatively new recommendation that some physicians aren’t yet accustomed to following.

If all these checkpoints suggest you do need a biopsy, ask your doc which technique they’re comfortable with and good at doing. Traditionally, these have been done through the wall of the rectum, which leaves up to 7 percent of guys with infections that can land them in the hospital. Research on a procedure that goes through the perineum suggests it may cut the risk of infection. It also requires specific equipment and training, so not every doctor offers it yet. Don’t go off a cliff trying to find this; the AUA/SUO guidelines say that either way is still okay.

ONE OF THE biggest shifts in the new rules of prostate care is what to do if cancer is detected. Often, “keep an eye on it” is the ultimate strategy. “We think that something like 50 percent of cancers don’t need treatment,” says Dr. Nyame. Wait, what?!

“The C-word short-circuits people. There’s this rush to get scheduled for treatment,” says Heather Goltz, PhD, professor of social work at the University of Houston-Downtown and health educator for people with cancer. Skipping right to surgery or radiation carries risks, including urinary incontinence and erectile dysfunction, issues you can avoid when the cancer was never likely to harm you.

Enter active surveillance, a treatment strategy of closely monitoring the prostate with, yes, more PSA tests, as well as with MRIs and biopsies. A multicenter study published in JAMA found that 10 years after guys chose active surveillance, less than 1 percent died of their disease; less than 2 percent developed metastatic cancer; and nearly half saw no progression in their disease at all. “The adoption of active surveillance has increased in the U.S. and has really reduced the harm of PSA-based screening,” says Dr. Nyame.

IF THE WHOLE PSA test thing feels antiquated in this age of cancer blood tests (like Galleri) and whole-body MRIs (like Prenuvo), it’s because these new technologies are still the “someone’s texting you” dots in the screening conversation. They’re a signal, but it’s not clear yet what they’re saying.

Cancer blood tests look for tumor DNA floating around in your circulatory system. “Not every cancer shows up very well in the bloodstream, and prostate is one of them,” says Dr. Moses. That’s because prostate cancer, at early stages, doesn’t shed a lot of DNA into the bloodstream.

But what about MRIs? If you’re going to need one anyway when your PSA is high, why not just start there? Studies are looking at that very thing right now, but even if they are found to be useful, cost and access will still keep them from the front lines, at least for a while.

That means the new tests won’t take over yet, but they don’t have to. The PSA might not have changed, but the protocols around it have made screening easier to opt into, giving the test a better chance to save lives.
