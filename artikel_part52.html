#3848. Nintendo Switch Sports Resort Preview - A Return Visit To Wuhu Island

The 2006 release of Wii Sports was a cultural phenomenon. People fought each other in the aisles of Best Buy to secure their Wii consoles before the holidays, primarily because of the Wii's pack-in motion-control tech demo. Family gatherings often devolved into bowling or tennis matches, regardless of whose house we were at since everyone owned a Wii. Three years later, Wii Sports Resort didn't have the same cultural impact, but it introduced the Wii Motion Plus, giving fans a reason to upgrade.

With Nintendo Switch Sports in 2022, the novelty came from the series' long absence and the franchise arriving on the wildly popular and far more advanced Nintendo Switch. But with this month's release of Nintendo Switch Sports Resort, the cultural significance has faded substantially with each and every entry. However, after spending an hour touring the newly renovated Wuhu Island, I'm not concerned much with how much of a mainstream impact it will have. Instead, I'm more focused on the several hours of lighthearted fun it will deliver to my friends and me.

Nintendo Switch Sports Resort delivers a heavily upgraded version of Wuhu Island, which most famously appeared in Wii Sports Resort. The resort island is explorable on land via Skateboard, air via Prop Plane, and sea via Power Cruiser. I had the opportunity explore by all three of these extremely intuitive methods.

Gliding across the ground on your Skateboard is as simple as holding one Joy-Con in Mouse Mode and mimicking the kick-push motion, with tilting the Joy-Con to steer and lifting it off the surface (in my case, my leg) to jump and perform tricks. The Prop Plane has you hold the Joy-Con like a paper airplane you're ready to throw, with tilt controls and the gyroscope controlling your pitch and speed. Power Cruising has you jump into the seat of a jet ski by holding two Joy-Cons like handle bars, tilting to steer and twisting to rev the engine faster. Exploring the island is enjoyable, as you search for touristy photo areas that explain a bit about the history of Wuhu Island, as well as abundant collectibles that reward you with points to customize your Mii or avatar.

I enjoyed the exploration element of Nintendo Switch Sports Resort, and it will almost certainly add a new layer of replayability to the overall package, but the main attraction is the individual sports. Thumb Wrestling is a goofy minigame where you hold the Joy-Con upright and try to pin your opponent's thumb, while they furiously shake their Joy-Con to escape your grasp. The Archery game's special mode using two upright Joy-Cons lets four people aim at different parts of the same target simultaneously (with points given to your opponent if you accidentally hit their section).

Boxing has you defending from incoming attacks and then picking your shots when you see an opening. Table Tennis has you holding the Joy-Con like a paddle and trying to get your opponent to make a mistake so they either miss the table or you smash their weak return shot. And, of course, bowling is still as fun and straightforward as ever; if you haven't played since Wii Sports, you'll notice it's no longer about release timing and more about spin and aim.

Each sport I played controlled well, even if the Boxing motion controls struggled if I threw a punch the correct way and I miss being able to cheese the release timing of the Wii Sports iteration of Bowling. You can play them in a variety of different formats, including special variants, like Bowling with a hundred pins, as well as the Island Crown mode, where you compete against 20 CPU characters in nine sports with the mission to climb the ranks and take on the Island Legend boss of that sport. Island Crown, in particular, sounds like a mode that will steal away hours of my time when the final game arrives.

Regardless of how big a splash Nintendo Switch Sports Resort is poised to make, I got what I needed from the hour I spent wailing and waggling my Joy-Cons: a fun, casual sports experience full of bite-sized minigames that will deliver enjoyment whether I'm alone or in a party setting. Thankfully, it won't be long before I can have Nintendo Switch Sports Resort on my personal console, as it arrives on Switch 2 on October 22.

NASA has revealed new details about its plans to return astronauts to the surface of the moon, outlining a series of upcoming milestones for SpaceX and Blue Origin as both companies race to ready their lunar landers for the agency's Artemis program.

During an Oct. 5 presentation at the Astronautical Congress (IAC) in Antalya, Türkiye, Steve Creech, acting manager of NASA's Human Landing System (HLS) Program, laid out development timelines for both spacecraft, with key demonstrations planned through early 2028 that include orbital refueling and uncrewed lunar landing tests to qualify the vehicles to fly astronauts to the surface of the moon.

A more pressing deadline, however, is NASA's Artemis III mission, which is targeted for the third quarter (Q3) of 2027. Artemis III will launch four astronauts aboard NASA's Orion spacecraft to low Earth orbit (LEO), where they will rendezvous and dock with test versions of both companies' landers for interoperability checkouts. Those demonstrations will help inform NASA's determination about which lander the agency will choose to carry astronauts to the moon's surface on Artemis IV, targeted for early 2028.

SpaceX and Blue Origin were both awarded NASA contracts to develop HLS vehicles — Starship and Blue Moon, respectively — and have spent the past several years refining their respective lunar lander designs and dealing with various delays along the way. In the face of those delays, and in an effort to better structure its mission progression for success, NASA overhauled its Artemis program architecture in February to maintain its goal of landing astronauts on the moon by the end of 2028 and establish a more regular cadence of uncrewed landings in the meantime.

As part of that shakeup, Artemis III, previously the program's first crewed lunar landing mission, was moved up a year and repurposed for the lunar lander vehicle tests the agency is preparing to fly to LEO next year. The program's first moon landing remains scheduled for 2028, but was shifted to the manifest for Artemis IV.

Between now and then, both SpaceX and Blue Origin have more than a dozen test demonstrations and vehicle milestones to achieve, including success during Artemis III and flights afterward. This will all lead up to a key decision NASA must make: Which vehicle will carry out the first crewed landing on the surface of the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972?

Starship reached for the first time on its most recent test flight, which occurred on Sept. 28 from SpaceX's Starbase site in South Texas. NASA lists several other important goals for the vehicle to achieve in 2026, including (in the order listed during the IAC presentation) the first return and catch of the rocket's upgraded Version 3 (V3) booster at its launch pad; the first-ever return and catch of a Ship upper stage; the first Starship launch from one of SpaceX's launch pads still under construction on Florida's Space Coast; and, possibly, the first V3 booster reflight.

NASA also expects SpaceX to complete the initial build of the crew cabin for its lunar lander Starship variant. SpaceX released renders of Starship's interior last year, as well as images of the test elevator astronauts are using to train for Starship egress on the lunar surface, but little else has been made public about the progress the company has made on the cabin's design.

Should the cabin's initial build wrap up by the end of the year, as NASA's presentation suggested, SpaceX will use Q1 2027 to complete integration of the systems that Starship will need to communicate with Orion during Artemis III, and deliver a cabin simulator for astronauts to train on leading up to the mission. SpaceX plans to continue Starship test flights through Q2 of next year, complete a landing leg demonstration and begin crew cabin airlock tests.

For Artemis III, SpaceX plans to fly one of its existing V3 Starship upper stages, with some slight modifications. Starship and Orion will dock as part of Artemis III but the SpaceX vehicle won't be equipped with a crew cabin for the mission's astronauts to enter. Between the end of this year and the beginning of the next, SpaceX is expected to complete the addition of a docking system and nosecone cover to the Artemis III Starship vehicle, which, NASA documents suggest, has already completed its mechanical fit checks.

Other notches on SpaceX's checklist leading up to Artemis III include the first propellant transfer demonstration with Starship in orbit. As the first-ever launch vehicle and spacecraft designed for full reusability, Starship's fuel constraints require that its tanks be topped off in order to fly beyond LEO. For a moon landing mission, this could mean up to a dozen launches of SpaceX's Starship "tanker" spacecraft to an orbiting fuel depot, which the moon-bound Starship will visit. To pull that off, though, SpaceX has to show it can successfully transfer those cryogenic fuels on orbit, and also store them effectively enough to prevent too much boil-off.

Starship won't require any refueling flights to complete its objectives for Artemis III, but SpaceX is expected to launch its first Starship tanker vehicle within a few months of that mission as it gears up for the Artemis IV moon landing mission. To that end, a launch of an uncrewed Starship lunar lander variant is targeted sometime toward the end of Q3/beginning of Q4, with an uncrewed lunar landing mission carried out before the end of 2027.

Blue Origin, meanwhile, has its own set of milestones to meet as it develops its Blue Moon Mark 2 (MK2) lander — a 52-foot-tall (16 meters) spacecraft powered by three BE-7 engines. Unlike the bare-bones V3 Starship that SpaceX will launch on Artemis III, Blue Origin plans to fly a completed test vehicle, incorporating the crew cabin, partial life-support, flight software and other systems needed to allow astronauts to enter the lander from Orion during their time docked in LEO. Astronauts will be able to enter Blue Moon from Orion to get a feel for the lander in zero-g and test some of its systems.

NASA's timeline shows the company working to complete a series of manufacturing and testing milestones in the last few months of 2026, including continued BE-7 testing and qualification of Blue Moon's BT-7 maneuvering thrusters. The company also faces its Artemis III interim design review and a critical design review for an upcoming landing demonstration mission in 2028.

Blue Origin is also working to restore its pad at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station's Launch Complex-36 (LC-36) to operation and return its New Glenn rocket to flight, following an explosion of the rocket in May that caused extensive damage to the facility. NASA's presentation places that milestone in Q4 2026, a timeline the company has also been very vocal about maintaining. A recent Reuters report, however, suggests that may not happen until early 2027.

By early 2027, the company expects to complete the spacecraft's communications systems and finalize its flight software, with assembly, integration and testing continuing into Q2. Q1 2027 is also when Blue Origin's Blue Moon MK1 lander (a smaller, uncrewed version of its MK2 successor) will launch to the lunar surface as a part of NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program of robotic moon missions.

During Q2 2027, as the Artemis III mission approaches, Blue Origin is expected to complete integration of its test lander with New Glenn and conclude qualification of its BE-7 engine. Throughout next year, the company plans to continue construction and refurbishments at its launch facilities and hopes to have a new launch pad ready to support the upgraded, more powerful New Glenn 9x4 rocket l by the end of Q3. The first flight of that rocket variant is scheduled for Q4 2027, alongside a second MK1 lunar mission and the launch of an uncrewed MK2 demonstration lander.

That spacecraft is expected to attempt its own uncrewed lunar landing in Q1 2028, around the same time Blue Origin has scheduled a third MK1 flight. Like SpaceX, Blue Origin will need to demonstrate that its lander can safely reach the moon's surface,operate there and return to lunar orbit before NASA clears it to carry astronauts on a future Artemis mission.

NASA's overall timeline remains the same as it has been since the February revamp, with Artemis III expected to launch in mid-2027, followed by a crewed lunar landing on Artemis IV in 2028, and possibly another on Artemis V that same year. There may be changes on the horizon, though. NASA will host a press conference on Friday (Oct. 23) with Administrator Jared Isaacman and Associate Administrator Amit Kshatriya, as well as Artemis program director Jeremy Parsons. During the event, which you'll be able to watch here at Space.com, they will discuss the status of the upcoming moon missions and an "Artemis acceleration."

SpaceX and Blue Origin still face tight schedules to complete their respective uncrewed landing demonstrations before Artemis IV. With SpaceX targeting Q4 2027 and Blue Origin Q1 2028, both are pushing the margins against NASA's hopes to return astronauts to the lunar surface that same year. Which company ultimately gets the green light for that first landing mission will depend on the progress each makes between now and then, and whether their landers can demonstrate the readiness that NASA requires to safely carry astronauts to the surface of the moon and back again.

#3841. Gears of War: E-Day Review - A Grand Reemergence

It’s been nearly seven years since the last mainline Gears of War game, and you’d be forgiven for thinking the franchise is little more than a past-expiration relic from the Xbox 360 era. Gears of War: E-Day may take the series back to its early days, but it is far from The Coalition compromising on innovation. Instead, the studio delivers its finest work since taking over the franchise, giving fans ample reason to be excited for the chainsaw-riddled cover shooter once again.

Reprising their roles as the leads, Marcus and Dom visit the city of Kalona to be honored as heroes of the recently concluded Pendulum Wars. Unfortunately, the peace is short-lived, as an unknown threat known as the Locusts emerge from beneath the surface, wreaking havoc and introducing a new threat to humanity. Emergence Day is perhaps the most pivotal moment in Gears of War lore, and watching it play out is nothing short of horrifying and awe-inspiring.

Right off the bat, Gears of War: E-Day feels like a horror game; monsters of all shapes and sizes rise from the ground, tearing fleeing civilians apart and decimating humanity’s militaristic response. Over the course of three days, you watch the human race scramble to respond to the surprise attack, completely overwhelmed by the ferocity and abundance of the Locust forces. Watching the city of Kalona fall in real time, as each of humanity's Hail Mary attempts is thwarted by the invading force, establishes the Locusts as a true threat. Nothing can be done to save the city; no matter how many enemies I mow down, Kalona’s fate is sealed. That feeling of helplessness flies in the face of the fact that you’re controlling characters who are essentially armed-to-the-teeth walking tanks, but it works extraordinarily well.

Marcus and Dom are joined by newcomers Mags and Lucas, who remain your squadmates throughout the entire game, whether you play solo or co-op. Despite their utility in combat, they’re even more important to the story. Marcus and Dom are dealing with the loss of Dom’s brother Carlos, while Mags is a strong-willed former soldier who reluctantly gets sucked back into service the moment the Locusts emerge. Lucas stakes his personality on being the by-the-book new guy who knows a lot of useless facts. For too much of the story, Lucas is underdeveloped, but a late-game story involving the newbie elevated him to stand shoulder-to-shoulder with Marcus, Dom, and Mags; I just wish it happened sooner.

Gears of War: E-Day delivers an astonishing collection of shootouts, supported by improved traversal and tactical abilities, masterfully arranged battlefields, stunning visuals, and a strong, diverse arsenal of weapons and equipment. E-Day smartly evolves how Gears of War missions play out; rather than the straightforward shooting galleries of the early games or the wide-open areas of Gears 5, Kalona presents city streets full of collectibles, upgrades, and side missions. I delighted in scouring each abandoned side street, debris-filled alleyway, or burnt-out corner shop for collectibles, and I actively went out of my way to take on side missions to help wayward survivors and find hard cases full of weapon mods.

Those mods let you customize each gun (as well as your armor and tactical ability) to fully suit your playstyle. I loved popping heads with a Longshot sniper rifle with a mod that instantly reloads on headshots, and I relished melting those who dared to rush me with the Incinerator shotgun, modded to narrow its spread in favor of distance and damage. Then, stacking an armor mod that rewards perfect reloads with added damage and close-range kills with healing, plus tactical abilities that heal your teammates or mark enemies, made me feel unstoppable, even against insurmountable odds.

After wrapping E-Day’s incredible campaign, I couldn’t help but want more. Thankfully, the multiplayer suite will undoubtedly provide plenty of enjoyment going forward. Versus remains fairly loyal to the franchise’s pedigree; whether playing team deathmatch, zone control, or the new power core-delivery Crucible mode, it aptly brings the same level of chaos fans have come to expect. I enjoy jumping into the currently available social playlists to test my skills against players worldwide, but the matchmaking too often disregards player and class levels; I lost count of how many times I was outmatched by players way above my level. Hopefully, as player counts fill out and ranked play begins, the matchmaking improves.

Horde Siege is where I plan to spend most of my time going forward. In this fresh evolution of the classic wave-based defense mode, Gears of War: E-Day borrows liberally from the extraction shooter genre. You use the open streets of Kalona to complete PvE objectives as a squad. You can beeline toward your main objective and call for extraction, but I more often enjoyed completing side objectives with my squad to earn upgrade points for my character class. With friendly squads in the same session, Horde Siege takes the formula to new heights, opening the door for emergent storytelling as you push back against the Locust threat through distinct missions that sometimes intersect. At times, I miss the more intricate, strategic wave-based Horde iterations of past Gears games, but a standard 10-wave Horde mode is enough to whet my appetite.

Gears of War: E-Day is as close to a complete package as it gets, effectively rivaling the series’ heyday. Minor quibbles with the story or matchmaking pale in comparison to the frightening tale of humanity’s darkest hours and the intense fallout of the all-out invasion. Seeing Marcus and Dom back in action and in their prime is an exciting prospect for a longtime fan like me, but it would be meaningless if it were just a nostalgia play; instead, Gears of War: E-Day innovates in all the right ways while keeping the core and heart of the franchise intact, stamping the series’ return with one of, if not its best, entries to date.

"The reality is that the bigger your team, the more vulnerable you typically are to disruption in terms of time," Darrah says. "If you have a 500-person team and you go one month late, that's probably adding five, ten million to your budget right there. It doesn't take much. And it's very easy for a 500-person team to go one month late, just because of the sand in the gears of the machine."

To put that in another context, consider Grand Theft Auto VI. As Jason Schreier detailed in his Making Of feature for Bloomberg, where Rockstar's head of publishing Jennifer Kolbe had to go to Take-Two Chief Executive Officer Strauss Zelnick and tell him they needed to delay their mega game from a 2025 release until May 2026 and then go back to him a few months later to say it had to be delayed until November. "I think he said, 'You’re kidding. Please tell me you're kidding,'" she said. Now, bear in mind that there aren't 500 people working GTA 6, there are more than 4,700. Using Darrah and Willit's back of the napkin maths, that delay may have cost Rockstar more than $500m.

Darrah speculates that the growing complexity may also be encouraging larger studios to stay in the prototyping phase because "prototyping is the one place where the devs are still allowed to be creative, so they don't want to leave that phase because they don't want to get down to just executing lists." Though, as Willits told me, having a fully-staffed team working on a prototype is terrible news for a studio's bottom line – "you do not need 200 people before you have a vertical slice, you're burning money."

Even if a game does come out at the end of that long, expensive production, it now has to earn back significantly more to break even. But, more and more, we're seeing executives get scared of the mounting costs of an in-development game and cutting bait while it's still in the pre-production phase. Just look at Perfect Dark and Everwild.

Darrah touches on the risk of that long, hard-to-leave-behind prototyping phase, because while you want developers to add things to the game "you don't want them to be able to generate work for other people. And as people have gotten more specialised, the ability for people to add things to the game that doesn't generate work for other people is basically vanishingly small."

Threaded through the interview with Darrah is his prediction that the games industry is headed toward a "bifurcation", in which several games will tear away from the rest of the market and become an unreachable target for other publishers, forcing them to scale their expectations down. "GTA 6 is going to rip itself off and take five or six games with it, up into the upper echelon of 'we can spend any amount of money on our games and it's going to be all right,'" Darrah says. Along with Rockstar's crime 'em up, he suggests Assassin's Creed, Call of Duty, and Battlefield could join it. He says there are just too many games that have $100m budgets that aren't making that money back.

Darrah points to his old studio Bioware as an example of one of the victims of this split. "BioWare sat right at the point where it doesn't quite work." Shortly after Dragon Age: Veilguard's launch, EA CEO Andrew Wilson said the RPG had significantly underperformed, reaching only 1.5m players, less than the 3m the company had planned for. (Also, worth noting, that's not 1.5m sales, as the game had a free trial available and was available through EA's subscription service.)

As much as this may sound like something out of Bioshock, with all of the top-selling games breaking away and making a home under the sea where they just eat all of the money for the rest of time, Darrah paints a surprisingly optimistic picture. "If that is happening, then I think you're going to see a lot more studios the size of Sandfall Interactive's Expedition 33. These are teams that are pretty small, pretty focused – Clair Obscur was very small with unusually massive outsourcing – a roughly 40-person team."

As Nic (RPS in peace) explained in the past, the claim that Clair Obscur was made by 30 people hides the sheer number of people involved in its production. Darrah explains that the difference between Sandfall and so many other teams in the industry is that typically a studio has a representative of all the disciplines it needs in-house – designers, artists, programmers, testers, audio people – and outsourcing is used to expand the capacity of the team, providing artists to work with the art director, for instance. But, Darrah says, Sandfall is different. "It looks to me like what Sandfall did was to keep entire holes in their structure – they don't appear to have any internal animators at all, and they outsourced 100% of their animation." The nature of the game, a turn-based RPG, helps with that, but even then that's a case of the developer recognising a significant way to reduce the team's overhead.

(Another example of a studio seemingly outsourcing smartly to keep their headcount low, look at what Neon Giant's achieving with immersive sim No Law.)

I will also point out that, while outsourcing can reduce costs for a studio, for the recipients of the work it can mean low wages and a figurative straitjacket. South American developers told James earlier this year how, despite their evident expertise, it was still difficult to convince publishers they were perfectly capable to leading projects. All to often, they are only contracted to assist American studios on theirs.

If that great schism happens, with publishers giving up their dreams of making the next GTA or the next Call of Duty, it could, Darrah says, reshape the industry. "If that all goes away, then you'll get a bunch of room to make it work with titles that need to sell 3-4 million units," he says. "That's much more attractive. I don't want to be working on something like GTA, where if this thing doesn't sell 50 million units, it's going to lose money. That's insane."

Though, within this prediction there is something a smidge worrying: the future of CD Projekt. Darrah says the Witcher maker has entered that rarefied space of the mega game – "Their games now need to sell 15, 20 million units to make money." And with that capacity for success but also that necessity for success, well, Darrah says, "that's a really dangerous place to be."

Darrah doesn't offer any predictions for The Witcher 4 or Cyberpunk 2's prospects, but I can't help but think of the news in May that there are already twice as many people working on The Witcher 4 than the 240 who worked on The Witcher 3.

As I say, none of what Darrah is saying is new exactly, but when you look at what's been taking place in the last year, it does sound like a move away from the mega budgets of the early 2020s has to be on the cards for all but the very biggest studios, right?

A combat encounter while waiting for an elevator to arrive failed to recognize that I killed everyone, forcing me to reload a save and do it again.

After watching Hellraiser: Revival’s finale stumble through the same visual and audio potholes plaguing the rest of the journey, I was angry. But upon taking time to cool down and reflect, my ire shifted to disappointment and relief that I didn't have to play more. During the rare moments Hellraiser: Revival isn't on fire in some way, it's a halfway respectable take on the popular horror franchise. I just wish playing it didn't so often feel like enduring the twisted machinations of Pinhead himself.

#3801. I Tested Google’s New AI Image Detector, and It’s Difficult (but Not Impossible) to Trick

David Nield is a technology journalist from Manchester in the U.K. who has been writing about gadgets and apps for more than 20 years.

He has a bachelor's degree in English Literature from Durham University, where he also spent a term as editor of the award-winning student newspaper Palatinate. His journalism career started in print media, where he contributed to and edited several technology magazines and bookazines sold in the U.K. and internationally. More recently, he has worked as a freelancer for some of the biggest technology publications on the web, covering everything from on-the-ground reporting about product launches, to detailed explainers and how-to guides on apps, gadgets, and platforms. His expertise covers broad areas of consumer tech, including smartphones, laptops, wearables, and AI.

An abundance of AI imagery is something we all have to live with these days—from school posters to restaurant menus—and while there are still certain tells that indicate something was made with AI, they're not always present. In a lot of cases, it can be almost impossible to distinguish an AI image from a real one.

This is a problem for all kinds of reasons, and, as such, the AI companies are attempting to address it. Images produced by Gemini and ChatGPT come with an invisible watermark called SynthID. That way, AI-generated images don't need visible branding in order to identify their origins. Of course, you need access to a detector to know whether this watermark is present. As it happens, Google just launched a standalone SynthID checker tool on the web, free for anyone to use. I gave "SynthID Detector" a shot, and while it largely works as expected, it isn't perfect.

For a while now, Gemini has let you upload an image and ask if it is AI-generated. With the new online portal, it's even easier to check—though you do still need to sign in with your Google account. Once in, you can check a wide variety of files. This checker supports JPG, JPEG, PNG, BMP, WEBP, AVIF, HEIC, HEIF, TIFF, TIF, and GIF image formats; MP4, MOV, and WEBM for videos, and WAV, MP3, OGG, FLAC, AAC, and M4A for audio. As long as a file was made by a SynthID-supporting AI, it should have a watermark. (The SynthID watermark is a standard supported by Nvidia, OpenAI, Kakao, and Google, though OpenAI has its own detection tool.)

Google doesn't say exactly how SynthID works, presumably to make it more difficult to get around, but does confirm the watermark is "robust against common transformations." It also says the watermark is "woven into the content itself", which means pixels for images and video, and frequency components for audio. The company also admits its detection tool isn't infallible. What it calls "heavy modifications" can degrade the watermark sign, as can multiple "transformations" applied to the same image. The recommendation is to use files that are as high quality as possible, so it seems to me that more data makes detection more reliable (similar to AI text detectors).

Google also says the SynthID watermarks are "imperceptible" to humans, so you don't need to look or listen out for them—they're only revealed by an AI detector tool like this. If you're generating images, video, or audio with AI, the watermarking shouldn't impact the quality of the end result in any way.

I started with some easy tests. The detector correctly spotted that images created in Gemini and ChatGPT, and downloaded directly to disk, were indeed fake. It only took a few seconds to confirm, and it also identified the AI tool that had been used to make them.

It also correctly marked one of my own photos as AI-free, although it did hedge its answer a little, saying that it was "unlikely" that the media was made with AI. All the detector could say with confidence was that there was no SynthID watermark, showing that it's this watermark in particular that it's checking for. It's not looking for any other signs of AI.

I then tried screengrabs of the same AI images, and the detector correctly flagged that they came from Google and Anthropic's AI tools. This shows there's something buried deep in the pixels, rather than in the file metadata itself, revealing the SynthID watermark. You can't just copy and paste to get around it. Applying an oil painting filter in Photoshop to an AI image didn't trip up the detector, nor did adding a slight blur. Adjusting color, contrast, and tone made no difference, and the detector still spotted SynthID when I cropped and combined two different AI images: one from Gemini, one from ChatGPT.

Eventually, I did beat the detector. Once, when I heavily pixelated an AI image—at which point I rather suspect my disinformation campaign would be pretty ineffective anyway—and once when I created a composite image, which was 75% real, 25% AI-generated. With less AI to work with, the detector can falter.

On the back of that result, I used ChatGPT to add an extra person into an otherwise genuine group photo, though this time the tool detected SynthID—even though less of the overall image was AI. There might be some work to do in images that are only partly AI, which is understandable as the detector has less data to work with.

There is a quota limit here, which seems to be around 10 files per day—but 10 each for images, videos, and audio files. Once you've hit your limit, you need to wait 24 hours to try again. As far as I could tell, the SynthID Detector is exactly what Google says it is: a useful check to have, but far from a guarantee one way or the other.
