Some of the richest people in history are using charitable donations to explore fresh approaches to funding scientific research. Their ranks could soon grow: in the next year or so, initial public offerings by some of the biggest artificial-intelligence companies, OpenAI and Anthropic, both in San Francisco, California, could see the companies valued at more than US$1 trillion apiece, making hundreds of their employees millionaires in the process.

Many of the new philanthropists are adopting an engineering mindset, focusing their donations on technological approaches to solving societal problems. As Nature reports in a News feature, they are also bringing many of their own ideas about how this research should be prioritized, funded and executed.

These donors speak of high-risk, long-term and interdisciplinary research. Their goals include finding ways to make research faster and more efficient by encouraging data sharing and speeding up grant approvals, with solutions that include incorporating AI into the processes.

But there’s a disparity when you compare how these funders and those in the public sector operate. Like much of the research that takes place in companies, the results of projects funded by philanthropy won’t necessarily be published. Private organizations are not always obliged to collect and report data on how well their projects have worked — by publishing in peer-reviewed journals, for example — or to make other details about their grants public. At Nature, we think they should be more transparent. Transparency in science funding is a component of transparency in science itself: a non-negotiable principle. Research that is not transparent cannot be verified or built on by others, who might then improve it or find new applications.

An increasing number of examples of science philanthropy can be found around the world. In China, tech entrepreneurs have launched awards such as the Xplorer Prize for young researchers, which is funded mainly by the company Tencent in Shenzhen. New philanthropic foundations are emerging, including the Beijing-based Xiaomi Foundation, which supports universities and provides grants to teachers and researchers. And in Brazil, the Serrapilheira Institute in Rio de Janeiro was established in 2017 by private donors to fund fundamental research, which is usually supported by governments.

But it is in the United States that philanthropists have one of the longest histories of shaping the direction and structure of the scientific enterprise, from the industrialists of the early twentieth century to today’s tech entrepreneurs.

In 2015, Facebook founder Mark Zuckerberg and his wife, paediatrician Priscilla Chan, pledged to donate 99% of Facebook shares, then valued at US$45 billion, to the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative. One of the initiative’s ambitions is to build interdisciplinary research infrastructure to allow the study of complex biology, to take research beyond the study of individual diseases. By 2025, Bill Gates and Melinda French Gates had donated nearly $64 billion of their wealth to the Gates Foundation (formerly the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation) in Seattle, Washington, which is focused on projects to improve public health and alleviate poverty in some 140 countries.

These and newer donors are keen to fill some of the perceived gaps in publicly funded research. Some, such as those financing the Arc Institute in Palo Alto, California, have created funding schemes to allow investigators to rapidly change direction in response to research results — something that is difficult, if not impossible, to do with conventional grants. Others, including former Google chief executive Eric Schmidt, are investing in technologies to speed up research. The Astera Institute, co-founded by cryptocurrency entrepreneur Jed McCaleb and based in the San Francisco Bay Area, is focused on using AI and developing infrastructure to tackle what it describes as “neglected ideas that aren’t supported well through existing institutions”.

Philanthropic foundations do not have the same reporting requirements as government funding agencies, which must publicly document their investments and the outcomes of their spending choices. It can be argued that freedom from such requirements is liberating. Without the fear of being called before lawmakers to explain the value of specific research projects, philanthropists are free to be more adventurous with their money. They can invest in long-term and high-risk projects. And with fewer bureaucratic obligations, charities can be better positioned to pivot rapidly when new technologies emerge, and to bridge disciplinary divisions to assemble teams with diverse expertise.

That said, science supported by philanthropy still relies on a foundation of public funding. Privately funded science and technology builds on published findings, many of which have been achieved through research funded by the public purse (M. Mazzucato and G. Semieniuk Oxford Rev. Econ. Policy 33, 24–48; 2017). If privately funded science is to achieve its ambition to benefit the world, philanthropists need to coordinate with and support the public sector. There are pragmatic reasons for doing so. Any new technologies that tech philanthropists want to roll out to the public will need to be compatible with existing ethical and safety frameworks.

Philanthropists should also bear in mind that in science, the line between an idea and a result is rarely straight or predictable. Those still relatively new to philanthropy would be wise to study the experiences of the Gates Foundation. In 2003, the organization, working with academic researchers, announced that it would fund up to 14 Grand Challenges in Global Health (H. Varmus et al. Science 302, 398–399; 2003). This was a set of ambitious goals to harness science to improve public health, ranging from developing vaccines that require no refrigeration to creating new treatments for infectious diseases. Some of the work, such as the use of gene editing in mosquitoes to control malaria, is paying off. But it has taken time. A decade after the launch of the challenges, Gates acknowledged that he had been “pretty naive” about how quickly the objectives could be achieved — a salutary lesson for his successors.

In countries around the world, public funding for scientific research is being cut or is at risk of being cut. That makes philanthropy an even more important source of finance. Without greater transparency about the results of philanthropic grant-making, however, there is no way of knowing what approaches have been successful or which might be instructive for guiding future science funding.

The engineer’s drive for efficiency and constant improvement, combined with the philanthropist’s freedom from constraint promise to bring fresh perspectives to science and innovation. Open science offers the best bet for ensuring that the resulting research delivers on its lofty goals.

#5997. 9 Ways to Remix Your Tux Right Now

The tuxedo is sacred. It's been around since the 19th century, which is a long time to keep wearing roughly the same thing to a nice dinner. It's remained relatively unchanged in all those years: black jacket, white shirt, bow tie. There are consecrated instructions about how much cuff should show and what should go around your waist. And naturally, you can spend quite a lot of cash getting all of this exactly right.

At this weekend's Academy Museum Gala in Hollywood, though, some of the best menswear came from guys who switched things up. The trousers got wider. The necklines got lower. And Alexander Skarsgård apparently decided there wasn't nearly enough shirt.

Let's take a gander at the 9 biggest tux swerves that you can keep in mind for your next big black-tie evening.

Alexander Skarsgård's white Valentino shirt had a stand collar, enormous cuffs, and sleeves that folded into big, stepped pleats. With no dinner jacket to distract, you could see the whole construction; it looked almost like armor, particularly around the neck and wrists. Normally, you're supposed to see a small strip of shirt cuff below your jacket sleeve, and that's it—but Skarsgård's cuffs were a substantial part of the fit, which is quite a change for something usually measured in fractions of an inch. Don't want to wear a jacket? Take his lead and pick a shirt that can play the main character.

Connor Storrie's Saint Laurent look swerved in the opposite direction, nixing the shirt and tie altogether. He replaced the familiar white-and-black combo with soft, scarf-like silk at the neck (scarf ties are big right now), with some bare chest on show underneath. The shine of the fabric kept it dressed up, while also giving the sharply cut jacket something softer to sit up against.

Charles Melton's ERL-made look put the volume below the waist: a cropped-front tailcoat, then very wide trousers with an almost tracksuit-like fabric. The gold embellishments made it considerably dressier than anything you'd wear to the gym. Keeping the jacket shorter also meant you could see where those enormous trousers started, giving the fit some shape around the waist.

A$AP Rocky stuck with a more standard tux but added a giant brooch and a small Chanel clutch shaped like a silver cloud, which popped against the plain black fabric. That's a fairly useful argument for keeping the tailoring simple when you've got accessories you actually want people to notice.

Colman Domingo took things a step further, as he is wont to do, with a black sequined Valentino jacket featuring an open neckline, and flared trousers. The shape mattered as much as the sequins: There was nothing tight or buttoned-up about it. Keeping everything black also meant he could add quite a lot of shine without having to think about another color.

Drew Starkey wore his Dior tux underneath a long, black-and-white polka-dot, nearly rain jacket-like coat. There was still a formal dinner jacket in the fit; he'd just put something more interesting over it. It's an option worth remembering (especially for wintertime functions) before buying another complete suit for your next black-tie invitation.

Bill Skarsgård's Dolce & Gabbana tux was as close to classic as you can get, with the standard white shirt and black bow tie. But the tie sat in at slight angle, making the otherwise very neat fit into something a little less precise. (And a bit unnerving—fit for a guy who perpetually plays horror villains.) It's a tiny detail, but a useful one if you've spent the whole evening checking that your tie is straight.

Jacob Elordi's custom Bottega Veneta tux was long and roomy, making an already very tall guy look even taller. The jacket wasn't overly cropped to compensate for his height, and the trousers had room to move. It's worth looking at if your idea of “good tailoring” still involves getting everything taken all the way in.

Taylor Zakhar Perez changed up the usual color palette altogether. His Amiri suit was chocolate brown, with broad black lapels and flared trousers. The sheeny satin lapels kept the connection to evening wear, while the brown made everything look considerably less stuffy.

For people with chronic back pain, everyday movements like bending down to tie their shoes or picking up a grocery bag can trigger painful flare-ups. Now, scientists have made a surprising discovery: These episodes may activate a brain network that's associated with our sense of self.

In a new study, the researchers found that movement-induced chronic back pain was tied to increased activity in the brain's default mode network, a group of connected brain regions that lights up during self-reflection and mind wandering. This finding was unexpected because the network typically becomes less active during acute pain, such as transient pain triggered from touching a hot stove or banging your knee on a table.

The new study, published Oct. 5 in the British Journal of Anaesthesia, suggests that chronic-pain flare-ups may involve brain processes beyond those responsible for detecting painful sensations.

Chronic lower back pain, defined as lasting at least three months, is a leading cause of disability worldwide. It's thought that when chronic pain first begins, a network of regions in the brain called the "pain neuromatrix" becomes sensitized so that the pain persists even after the painful stimulus is gone. Although scientists know that chronic pain is associated with changes in brain activity, they don't fully understand what happens when movements trigger sudden increases in that ongoing pain.

To investigate, researchers recruited 114 adults with chronic lower back pain and used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to examine their brain activity. fMRI measures changes in blood flow, which acts as an indirect measure of brain activity.

After an initial scan, a researcher helped lift each participant's leg until their back pain increased, held it in position for 10 seconds, and then lowered it. The researchers scanned the participants' brains immediately after this pain-inducing leg movement and compared the results with scans collected beforehand. On average, the test increased the participants' reported pain intensity by 23%.

As expected, during the leg movement, the researchers observed increased blood flow in brain regions involved in processing pain and movement. But they also detected increased activity in key regions of the default mode network, which typically becomes more active when a person is thinking about themselves or their autobiographical memories or just letting their mind wander.

Acute pain lowers activity in the default mode network, studies show, presumably because it induces an awareness of something separate from yourself.

"When we saw just the first round of analysis, we're like, 'Did we do it right?'" study co-author Morgan Gianola, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of California, San Diego, told Live Science.

"We did not expect [the default mode network] to be involved at all," said study co-author Fadel Zeidan, a neuroscientist at UC San Diego, told Live Science. "We expected it to be deactivated to the point that we had to triple-check our work to make sure that the results were legit."

The researchers also exposed participants' calves to painful heat ‪—‬ a common method of studying brain activity during acute pain. Unlike the movement-induced back pain, the heat was associated with reduced activity in part of the default mode network, suggesting a different mechanism was at play.

Participants who reported more severe chronic back pain had stronger connectivity between the default mode network and brain regions that process sensations from the back. This suggests that chronic pain may become intertwined with how people perceive themselves and their bodies, the study authors concluded.

"This pain flare has almost become a part of who they are from an egocentric perspective," Zeidan said.

Katherine Martucci, a neuroscientist at Duke University who wasn't involved in the study, praised the experimental design, noting that researchers typically avoid movement during brain scans because it can interfere with the clarity of the scan. This study "nicely gets around" that problem by taking scans before and after the painful motion, Martucci told Live Science in an email.

However, she noted that the observed brain activity could also reflect lingering pain or anxiety following the painful leg raise. In addition, the study couldn't determine whether the altered brain activity observed contributes to chronic pain or develops in response to it.

Nonetheless, Zeidan and colleagues think the findings could eventually inform future treatments for chronic pain. The therapies might address how people relate to their pain, rather than solely targeting the painful sensations themselves. Zeidan, who coincidentally developed chronic back pain while conducting the study, believes that people's past experiences and memories with chronic pain have influenced their brain processes to a point where the pain itself becomes part of who they are.

Zeidan's team is now investigating whether existing approaches, such as mindfulness meditation or cognitive behavioral therapy, can influence the default mode network and help relieve chronic pain. (Cognitive behavior therapy for chronic pain, or CBT-CP, is already a well-established treatment option.)

Martucci cautioned that it's too early to draw conclusions about how these results might be applied in clinical practice. But she said the current findings provide important evidence of how the brain responds to chronic pain flare-ups.

"They do provide solid data on how people with chronic low back pain show important changes in their brain responses after a condition provoking their pain," she said. "The brain imaging data validate their [the participants'] experience of pain in an important and very visible way."

#5995. 4 Unconventional Wedding Guest Outfit Ideas for the Non-Traditionalist

If there’s one thing I’m not, it’s a traditionalist; an ethos that extends from my personal style and interests right into my disinterest in weddings. I never played bridal dress-up as a little girl, instead riding around on a Fisher-Price motorcycle. So while I’m always sincerely happy for my loved ones getting married, I’d be even happier to hear they eloped. Nuptials are possibly my least favorite gathering; the prospect of putting together a note-perfect outfit often only adds to my aversion.

One thing I’ve learned over my years in the guest seat: I may not be able to control my social battery draining by the time the reception rolls around, but I can control what I wear so I still feel like myself. And if you, like me, aren’t interested in throwing on any black suit or floor-length pastel slip dress in the name of easy formality, I’ve put together a few unconventional wedding guest outfit formulas I rely on whenever the occasion calls for it.

Instead of purchasing (or renting) a slew of dresses destined to be worn once, I’m keen on a slowly acquired capsule wardrobe of formal and semi-formal attire I can reach for at a moment’s notice. Even if my edgier-leaning aesthetic isn’t exactly your cup of tea, there is a method to my stylistic madness: The secret, I’ve found, is to discover your favorite silhouettes, stick with them, and get creative with accessories.

Scroll on to discover four adaptable outfit ideas for the wedding non-traditionalist.

I often find myself asking—what even is black tie? In lay terms, it’s a floor-length gown over a cocktail dress, or a tux over a suit. That said, black-tie dressing doesn’t have to be stodgy, nor does it have to feel like a prom. I gravitate towards dresses with draped and ruched details for their figure-flattering cuts. Then, I use accessories to add personality—this is where I like to lean into what one of my favorite designers, Simone Rocha, calls “hardcore femininity.”

Say you don’t want to splurge on a look for a one-time occasion—I often don’t, either. I feel a bit more justified (and less rueful) about buying separate pieces I’m sure I’ll wear again; they don’t necessarily need to be from the same brand, either. These are the items you can dress up or down with ease, like a structured, bodice-like top and a flowing maxi skirt. A brooch and fun shoes can zhuzh up any otherwise plain ensemble.

A little black dress is the most classic wardrobe staple—and I’ve found that a cocktail-length option is especially versatile. (Plus, depending on the belt or brooch you pair with your LBD, it’ll feel like a new look each time.) One of my favorite dresses is from Geel; it easily takes me from drinks out and about in Brooklyn on warm nights to a semi-formal wedding when styled with a dressier heel-and-clutch combination.

My style is heavily ’90s and ’00s-inspired—not in the minimalist, CBK-inflected way; more like Nicole Kidman in Practical Magic. I’m a big Anna Sui enthusiast, so who better to turn to for a ’90s-inspired set than the queen of whimisgoth style herself? After all, cocktail attire leaves room for play, and if I can find a way to sport an animal print, I always will. Again, I follow my rule: Will I ever wear this again, and can I do so in different settings? If yes, I’m clicking add to cart.

If you're approaching menopause, you are likely being bombarded by online ads for treatments promising relief for everything from hot flashes to brain fog. Some of these therapies have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and have strong evidence to support them — but one hormone-based treatment that's gaining in popularity has much less science to back its use for menopause.

More doctors are writing their menopausal patients prescriptions for testosterone therapy. A 2026 study conducted in the U.S. recorded over 90,000 off-label prescriptions for testosterone to women in 2025, mainly among women ages 45 to 64 and those with diagnoses of menopause. This marked a 58.7% increase from 2024.

These prescriptions are "off-label" because the FDA has not yet approved testosterone as a treatment for menopause, perimenopause (the transition into menopause) or postmenopause (the time period afterward).

So, does scientific evidence support the use of testosterone to alleviate menopause symptoms? Or is it too soon to say?

While testosterone treatment for menopause has gained popularity, there's limited evidence to back up its use, experts say.

Although often called a "male" hormone, testosterone is also produced by females, albeit at lower levels than in males, on average.

There's a common misconception that females experience "androgen deficiency" — a marked decline in testosterone and related hormones — leading up to menopause, said Susan Davis, a professor of endocrinology and the head of the Women's Health Research Program at Monash University in Australia. This is not the case.

Rather, in females, testosterone levels tend to drop slowly between ages 20 and 60, and there's a slight rebound from age 60 onward. Menopause, which generally occurs around age 51 to 52, is not linked to a marked decline in testosterone. Estrogen and progesterone fluctuate during perimenopause and then decline sharply, and these changes clearly contribute to the telltale symptoms of perimenopause and menopause.

Placebo-controlled trials have not been able to show that testosterone treatment helps to alleviate brain fog or improve well-being, mood, bone density or muscle function in menopausal patients, said Davis, who has conducted a number of these trials herself.

"Telling women that fatigue, low mood, brain fog are symptoms of androgen deficiency is completely untrue," she emphasized.

On Sept. 17, the FDA held a public workshop to evaluate the evidence for testosterone therapy for menopause, concluding that more evidence is needed to fully assess the use of the hormone as a menopause treatment.

There is some evidence to suggest that testosterone might help to treat a condition called hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) in postmenopausal women. HSDD describes when a person has little to no sex drive and it causes them distress. Men and women of many ages experience it, so the condition is not unique to menopause. But it can sometimes develop in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women.

A review of 36 randomized trials involving 8,480 participants found that testosterone treatment delivered with skin patches was associated with 0.85 additional satisfying sexual events per month in postmenopausal women, compared to a placebo. This observed effect is not huge, Dr. Nanette Santoro, a professor of obstetrics and gynecology at the University of Colorado School of Medicine, told Live Science in an email.

Of all the studies done to date, if there was a screaming benefit, you think you would have seen it.

Lately, anecdotal accounts of postmenopausal women having "fantastic sexual awakenings" after taking testosterone have been floating around, Santoro said. But these anecdotes haven't been backed by hard data yet. "Patients are being bombarded with a tsunami of information providing them with the most sky-high expectations imaginable," she said.

Notably, testosterone is not FDA approved as a treatment for HSDD in menopause yet. A drug called flibanserin, which affects serotonin in the brain, is approved to treat HSDD for pre- and postmenopausal women under age 65.

The placebo effect — when patients perceive an improvement in their health or well-being despite receiving a treatment with no active ingredient — can also complicate testosterone trials.

Davis said that in her patient groups, it's common for about 1 in 3 trial participants to report an improvement of their symptoms even though they are taking a placebo. Other trials have reported similar findings in their placebo groups, with about 30% to 40% experiencing some improvement in sexual desire and satisfaction, for example.

"I wouldn't say that there is definitely not a benefit," Davis said. "It's just that a benefit has not been shown."

One study trying to address this gap is the U.K. National Institute for Health and Care Research's ESTEEM trial. This large-scale, gold-standard trial aims to recruit hundreds of menopausal women and test whether using testosterone cream can reliably improve their quality of life. The cream will deliver testosterone through the skin and be applied to the lower abdomen and thighs. (Doctors often advise women to apply the creams in this way because those body parts are easy to hide if the treatment causes localized hair growth that the patients would prefer to conceal.)

The trial runners will track participants' symptoms linked to menopause, looking at sleep quality, memory performance, feelings of anxiousness, migraines, body mass, and overall well-being. The results of that trial are currently expected by 2028.

In the meantime, though, there isn't strong evidence that testosterone directly improves symptoms of menopause beyond HSDD. "Of all the studies done to date, if there was a screaming benefit, you think you would have seen it," Davis said.

Females' testosterone levels generally decline throughout adulthood, with a slight increase after age 60. However, there isn't a sharp dip in testosterone around the time of menopause.

Testosterone treatments available in most countries are not necessarily dosed for use in female patients. Only four countries — Australia, South Africa, New Zealand and the United Kingdom — have authorized a testosterone cream that's specifically formulated and dosed for women. These products differ from testosterone therapies for men in that they contain lower doses of the hormone. They are available to postmenopausal women with HSDD, specifically.

In other countries, such as the U.S., doctors are left trying to adapt medication dosed for males for their female patients, which can lead to a wide variation in dosing. And the effects of these different dosing regimens are poorly understood for female patients. "It is not necessarily unsafe to use male preparations for women, it's just that much, much less must be used," Santoro said.

Excessive doses raise concern about unwanted masculinizing effects, such as clitoral enlargement, voice deepening, hair thinning, baldness, and rapid growth of facial and body hair, Santoro said. (These masculinizing effects might be sought after in the context of gender-affirming care for transgender men and transmasculine people, but would often be undesirable for cisgender women.)

Additionally, the potential impacts of extra testosterone on cardiovascular health and breast cancer risk are poorly understood, Santoro noted. At lower doses, testosterone can cause mild acne, oily skin, and some increase in facial and body hair growth in postmenopausal women, trials have found; if these symptoms remain mild, they are generally considered acceptable.

Testosterone is being used off-label in the U.S., which means the "safety, efficacy and appropriate dosing for that use have not been evaluated and approved by the FDA," a spokesperson for the agency told Live Science in an email. The dosages and formulations approved for males "may differ substantially from what might be appropriate for women, whose testosterone levels and metabolic profiles are clinically distinct," the spokesperson said.

Anecdotally, Santoro encountered one female patient who had received very high levels of testosterone through an implant placed under the skin, which had been prescribed to her by a different doctor. She started losing her hair and seeing sizable growth in her clitoris, while her breasts started "shrinking away."

"Women are being prescribed some very big doses in North America, and no one studied the safety of these," Santoro said.

At the end of the day, Davis argues that women deserve to be given accurate information, given that navigating menopause can already feel like "swimming through wet cement." Good menopause care not only involves discussing potential pharmacological treatments, but also evaluating a patient's lifestyle, such as diet and exercise, and discussing societal pressures that often affect menopausal people. For instance, they may face pressure to maintain an active sex life and younger-looking physique.

When it comes to hyped therapies like testosterone, she said, "just be cautious about people who tell you they've got the answer that no one else has got."

If you’re looking to take a nostalgic trip to a bygone era, you can’t beat a big bowl of Watergate salad. Made with canned pineapple, pistachio pudding, and Cool Whip, this retro crowd-pleasing dessert “salad” will take you straight back to the ’70s with just one bite. Watergate salad has just five ingredients and can be assembled in a single bowl, so it’s the perfect recipe to throw together if you don’t have a ton of time, but you need to take something to that potluck or family gathering.

In a large bowl, combine the drained crushed pineapple, reserved pineapple liquid, and pistachio pudding mix. Whisk everything together until thickened—this shouldn’t take more than a minute.

Next, add the marshmallows and Cool Whip to the bowl. Fold them into the pudding mixture, using a flexible rubber spatula to scoop around the sides of the bowl and down through the middle, until everything is combined and no streaks of Cool Whip are left.

Dollop the Watergate salad into a serving bowl and cover loosely with plastic wrap, then refrigerate until the marshmallows have softened and the salad is set; this will take at least 4 hours, but overnight is better.

When you’re ready to serve, uncover the bowl and top with pistachios. If you’re feeling especially retro, you can add a few cherries too!

#5986. On Sleeping Lion, Lennon Stella Cuts Through the Noise

The singer opts for pared-back instrumentation and radical honesty on her sophomore album.

I was 14 years old when I first encountered Lennon Stella. I was on the family computer watching the singer, then aged 12, and her 8-year-old sister Maisy perform a cover of Robyn’s “Call Your Girlfriend” on YouTube with an empty butter container and a brown paper backdrop. You might have had a similar experience if you’re one of the 31 million viewers who have seen the video.

Shortly after their viral breakout in 2012, the Stella sisters were booked for the ABC hit music drama Nashville, which Lennon starred in from ages 12 to 18. Before the show ended, she had a record deal. She dropped her debut EP in 2018, followed by her 2020 full-length album, Three. Two. One. Then the pandemic hit, and she, like all of us, retreated home. But long after most emerged, Stella stayed in her cocoon for years, leaving fans to wonder if she would ever drop new music again. Now she’s finally releasing her sophomore album, Sleeping Lion, on October 23.

Stella, 27, is warm when I meet her at Bar Oliver in New York City’s Chinatown on a September afternoon. She has that same raspy yet melodic voice she had as a kid. Wrapped in a coat and scarf, she sips a coffee, and we talk about where the hell she’s been for the last six years.

Home, mostly, it turns out. She’s been hanging out with friends and watching “a shit ton of movies.” It was the first time she realized she could call a career time-out. “I was going pretty consistently since I was a kid,” Stella says, referring to her early acting days.

But at the same time, Stella is trying to re-create a feeling she had while working on Nashville. “There was a purity about it,” she says. “I would say that’s the thing I loved the most and the thing I want to keep with me now.” As she tells it, she and Maisy were shielded from much of the industry’s influence. “For the first five seasons, they were like, ‘No acting coach. No one touch them.’ And they let both my sister and I do whatever felt natural.” She’s missed that feeling.

“I just felt like I knew what I was doing. Nobody was questioning me, and I wasn’t questioning myself.” It was around this time that she got into the music industry, which changed all of that. The litany of influences and opinions around her muddied the water, and Stella says she got lost and confused. The result, she admits, was a gap between the music she was making and the music inside her. “Now I’ve come back to the place of remembering what I felt really sure of on Nashville. And if you go back to what feels genuinely true and honest, that’s where the good stuff is.”

It’s hard to bring up Nashville without mentioning the August passing of Hayden Panettiere, who played Stella’s on-screen mentor. It’s too fresh to discuss when I meet Stella, but in a recent Rolling Stone interview, she alternated between talking about Panettiere in the past and present tenses, belying a tender truth: to Stella, her absence doesn’t feel real, not yet. I ask if, when she is ready to talk about the loss, she might do it through music. “I don’t know yet,” Stella says. “But maybe.”

If we’re comparing this album to her previous one, Sleeping Lion sounds more folk than pop and reads more like a diary entry and less like a script. It’s clear that this music is purer and truer to Lennon’s nature. “This record is a return to what I have loved since I was a kid,” she says. Her vocals still coo like a salve for your nervous system, only now they are accompanied by pared-back instrumentals rather than upbeat synths. These tracks feel more aligned with what Stella listens to when she’s alone, artists like Nick Drake, Connie Converse, and Canadian singer-songwriter Andy Shauf, who’s both a major inspiration for her and now a producer on Sleeping Lion. Working with Shauf, Stella says, was incredible. “It felt like when you’re a kid in your bedroom and nobody’s listening and you’re just playing.”

Stella practiced “being terribly honest” on this album, and her longtime collaborator Simon Wilcox urged her to write without fear of how her songs’ subjects might react. “At times in the past, I’ve avoided [being too honest in lyrics] because I’ve been trying to be conscientious of people’s opinions—and airing someone else’s dirty laundry,” she says. Of course, when I hear this, my mind goes to potential lovers, but Stella assures me this isn’t about a boyfriend. Her boyfriend, in fact, has the opposite effect. “He’s the safe one,” she says of Jegor Venned, her partner for the last seven years. “He’s like, ‘I want you to write from your deepest, truest place and never worry.’ It’s been a very healing thing for me, having a consistent, loving twin soul in my life.” For once, we can’t blame the boy.

“A big part of all this was learning how to make room for myself to have my own experience and perspective and not be so aware of what everyone else needs or wants from me,” she says. This is, in part, where the album title comes from. The Sleeping Lion was a tarot card she pulled. “There was a lot of this time in the six years that was about me awakening the sleeping lion within, of finding my sense of self and going back to what I was before I was so concerned about other people’s perception of me,” she explains.

Stella tells me she’s interested in acting again eventually, but for now, she’s given us a cozy fall album to soothe our souls. She’s back onstage, too, performing shows for her Up Close & Personal tour in Nashville, New York, Toronto, and Los Angeles throughout October and November. She’s excited to be face-to-face with listeners again. “I feel like I’ve been in a bit of a vacuum for the past six years, just writing the songs and not having them go anywhere,” she says. “I need the conversation part.”

She’s good at the conversation part. Maybe it’s her kindhearted disposition, or our proximity in age, or the fact that I’ve been privy to her star power for exactly half my life, but Stella seems to cast an easygoing glow around her—one that I’m happy to bask in as we chat. In many ways, her earnest explorations of life and love make her feel like a mirror for late 20-somethings like me. I’ll continue rooting for her for the next 14 years, or however long she chooses to continue making music.

#5985. If You Loved Klara and the Sun, You Should Read These 9 Books

From science-fiction robot stories to dystopian explorations of what it means to be human

When Nobel Prize-winning author Kazuo Ishiguro first published Klara and the Sun, artificial intelligence was at the precipice of mainstream consumption. It was 2021, only five years ago, but AI technology was still pretty much confined to the worlds of healthcare and software engineering. ChatGPT wouldn’t launch for another 19 months, and it would be another couple of years before chatbots would turn into “AI girlfriends” and “AI therapists,” able to converse freely with users in language that felt authentic and human. But conversations surrounding life-like robots, and their place in the not-so-far-away future, have been around for decades, even centuries.

From Mary Shelley’s classic Frankenstein to Philip K. Dick’s dystopian Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?, which served as the basis for The Blade Runner film, authors have blown through ink and paper exploring what it means to be human, what separates us from synthetic creations, and the consequences we must face from introducing pseudo-sentient beings into the world for our own gain. More recent offerings dive even deeper into the implications behind society’s goals and intentions in the use of this technology, from heartbreaking efforts to assuage grief or combat loneliness to power-hungry strives to feel control over another being or “manly” in a relationship. In many, there’s a bit of both.

Klara and the Sun is told from the perspective of a solar-powered “Artificial Friend,” a humanoid purchased as a companion for a sick teen, Josie. The two protagonists exist in a world divided by the wealthy, “lifted” teens—kids, such as Josie, who go through life-threatening genetic enhancements to increase their intelligence—and their “unlifted” peers. The risky process leaves Josie ill, and while Klara becomes fixated on curing her friend, Josie’s mother starts secretly training the robo-girl to be her daughter’s replacement. The book reads quickly, but packs in conversations on friendship, humanity, class systems, and grief, just to name a few.

In a similar vein, we see titles such as Annie Bot and The Echo Wife that touch on the idea of using technology to replace a loved one (though here, it’s because the real person becomes too “difficult,” or human, to deal with). Both of these novels, as well as Roe Anderson’s The Hierarchies, center around the phenomenon of companion and sex robots for men, who prefer the company of an obedient, agreeable, and passive fake-wives to a real women. For something different, there’s Adrian Tchaikovsky’s satirical Service Model, which follows a high-end robot valet who accidentally kills his human master, or Silvia Park’s 2025 Luminous, which will have you wondering what it might be like to have a robot as a sibling. Then there’s Fallow by Sarah Anderson, a Severance-esque book that dives into the ways that technology can create dystopian class divides.

Ahead, here are nine hand-selected books to read if you devoured Klara and the Sun (the book or the new Jenna Ortega-starred movie).

Regardless of whether or not you’ve already seen the new Taika Waititi-directed film adaptation of Klara and the Sun, the book is a must-read. Like the movie, it follows the relationship between Klara, a solar-powered “Artificial Friend,” and Josie, a young sick teen who picks the robot pal out at the store. As the story goes, dark truths behind Josie’s illness, the alternative universe the family lives in, and the girl’s mother’s intentions start to surface. I’ve cried each time I’ve re-read it.

I first read Never Let Me Go in 2020, per the suggestion of my sister who never fails with book recommendations. Almost instantly, I read it again. (And then I started a countdown to the release of Klara and the Sun.) Similar to the 2021 work, Ishiguro’s 2005 book takes place in a world that almost looks like one we know. It follows boarding-school children as they navigate friendship dynamics, bullies, and crushes. But over time, the reader learns that there’s something a bit more sinister going on. If you want your heart ripped out in the best way possible, or to read something that will make you think of nothing else for weeks, this one’s for you.

A feminist sci-fi novel told from the perspective of a companion robot (or, lets be real, a sex robot), Sierra Geer’s Annie Bot (2024) is a quick but, at times, challenging read. Annie is a bot designed with one mission in life: to please a man. Her owner can adjust her libido at any given moment, lock her in a closet if he’s unhappy with her, and of course holds the power to get rid of her altogether. As the story unravels she begins to understand her position in the world, questioning her purpose.

If you thought extramarital affairs were hard to stomach, try a book about an extramarital robot affair. Sarah Gailey’s The Echo Wife (2021) follows an award-winning scientist who discovers that her husband stole her research on human cloning... to create a (more obedient) genetically-engineered clone of her. Eerie.

If the part of Klara and the Sun that caught your attention was the way in which technology created dystopian class divides (or if you count Severance as a favorite show), Sarah Anderson’s 2026 Fallows is for you. The newly-released book follows Natalie, a young woman with no dental insurance and lots of student loans who takes a job as a company’s in-house surrogate, delivering seven babies in ten years to allow executives to become mothers without maternity leave. As she’s coming to an end of her contract, however, she is pulled into a different kind of social experiment.

While there are many examples of robot girlfriends and a prominent one about robot friends, robot siblings is a less frequently explored sub-genre. Silvia Park’s 2025 speculative fiction book, Luminous, is set in a near-future, reunified Korea, where three estranged siblings—two human, one robot—reconnect. This one’s another one that will have you contemplating what it means to be human.

A satirical sci-fi novel, Adrian Tchaikovsky’s 2024 Service Model follows a high-end robot valet who accidentally kills his human master. If that’s not enough to entice you, perhaps the world the author built will. After the death of his owner, the robot embarks on a journey to see the country for the first time—and discovers a post-apocalyptic landscape with broken institutions and a whole community of robots built to serve a long-gone human race.

S.B. Divya’s 2021 Machinehood is set in 2095, at a time when humans are fed pills that a) keep them alive, and b) keep them competitive with AI. Already an interesting premise, it takes a turn with the introduction of the “Machinehood,” a mysterious part-human, part-machine terrorist group that seeks to rid the world of the pills.

If you loved Klara for her acute observations on human life and the way she represented the all-too-familiar experience of feeling othered, or “alien” in a society, Beautyland fits right in. The novel follows a girl who believes she is an extraterrestrial being, sent from her home planet to report on the findings of human life. It is humorous and full of heart.

Olga Ravn’s 2023 novel The Employees, is told through a log of statements from the human and humanoid crew aboard the Six-Thousand Ship, which is hurtling through space. The passengers aboard the ship start have recently become strangely attached to the objects they’ve just picked up on a recent visit to the planet New Dimensions, eliciting feelings of longing, a desire to belong, and even anger to revolt. Some of the entries are one sentence long while others are much longer, and they can be funny, poignant, or sad. Crucially, it is never revealed who has written each statement.

#5984. Nine Movies to Watch if Nathan Fielder Is Your Jam

You Can See Everything, the three-hour documentary co-directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim, is mind-warping in a number of ways. Chief among them is its form-breaking structure, which calls into question the inherent artifice of documentary itself.

The film takes aim at a subject that ostensibly could not be more well-tread: the disgraced Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, whose rise and fall has been chronicled in countless articles, podcasts, films, and television series. Justifiably, one might wonder what more we could possibly need to see or say about Holmes. But consider everything we know about Fielder, himself an enigmatic pop figure whose trickery, unlike that of his subject, is celebrated (and laid bare in absurdist-comic projects like Nathan For You, The Rehearsal, and The Curse). Oppenheim, meanwhile, has built a burgeoning directorial career through richly cinematic portraits of American vulgarity: Spermworld, Ren Faire, and, more recently, his narrative debut, Primetime.

What begins as a relatively straightforward glimpse into Holmes’ home life during the weeks leading up to her imprisonment shapeshifts once she begins her sentence. Reckoning with Holmes’ absence, Fielder and Oppenheim (and the other surprising collaborators they enlist) make bold choices that knowingly distort the line between reality and fiction—and forces the viewer to reconsider whether there was ever a line to begin with. By the end, once we’ve Seen Everything, we’re left questioning whether any of it was actually real or trustworthy.

This is what will keep us talking about Everything long after the initial froth of surprise guests and full-frontal nudity has subsided. The film asks us to question the machinations of narrative and authorship—not just the means by which stories are told, but the power and the motives of those involved. These themes help the film feel extremely timely, but also position Fielder and Oppenheim in a robust lineage of filmmakers that have similarly dared to deconstruct genre and form.

In keeping with Everything’s obfuscations, we’ve compiled a genre-spanning list of several other films that challenge our ideas about performance, perception, and truth.

This may be an obvious choice for longtime Fielder fans. But for the uninitiated, the feature-length series finale of Nathan For You is a prerequisite to understanding his creative journey from deadpan prankster to performance artist-cum-investigative reporter-cum-anthropologist. Fielder facilitates and documents an elderly man’s search for his long-lost high school sweetheart, a search that takes shocking turns as Fielder uncovers murky truths about the relationship. Fielder eventually puts himself under the microscope, effectively laying the groundwork for the rabbit hole down which he’d descend in The Rehearsal. Finding Frances even features what might be the first instance of Fielder’s Rehearsal method at work, when he stages an opportunity for the film’s subject to practice reuniting with his old flame in advance of the real event. Watching it in hindsight, it feels as if you’re seeing Fielder have a real-time epiphany that would set the next decade of his career in motion.

McElwee’s plan to shoot a documentary about Union army General William Tecumseh Sherman’s campaign during the Civil War is quickly derailed when the filmmaker, reeling from a breakup, instead embarks on a meandering, navel-gazing exploration of his own romantic life and the women who captivate him. Just as Fielder frequently uses his work as a vehicle to analyze his personal relationships, McElwee scrutinizes his shortcomings even as he repeats them.

Welles sets the stage for his seminal docu-drama by quoting illusionist Jean-Eugene Robert-Houdin: “The magician is an actor, playing the part of a magician.” From there, he spins a yarn connecting the dots between a prolific art forger, a hoax biographer, and Welles himself—questioning, throughout, the slippery parameters of authenticity and authorship. Is a lie still a lie if everyone believes it?

The oldest film on this list by half a decade, Medium Cool sets an important precedent not only in breaking formal conventions but by raising questions about the ethics of media production. On its face, the film is a work of fiction, following Robert Forster as a steely news reporter who discovers his station is seeding information to the FBI. But filmmaker Haskell Wexler—best known as a documentarian and the Oscar-winning cinematographer of films like Who’s Afraid of Virginia Woolf?—integrates the reality of the times in staggering ways. In the film’s climax, the actors and crew are caught in the real-life violent protest that took place at the 1968 Democratic National Convention in Chicago. “There are fictional characters in real situations [and] there are real characters in fictional situations,” Roger Ebert wrote in his 1969 review. “The mistake would be to separate the real things from the fictional. They are all significant in exactly the same way.”

Vanguard of Iranian cinema Abbas Kiarostami became an international sensation with the release of Close-Up, which remains the most celebrated and influential of his neorealist dramas. Using a cast comprised entirely of non-actors playing themselves, the film alternately documents and reenacts the real-life story of a cinephile whose impersonation of a well-known Iranian filmmaker lands him on trial for fraud. (It is worth noting that Kiarostami’s own artistic integrity was called into serious question in 2020, when Ten actress Mania Akbari came forward with allegations that Kiarostami plagiarized and sexually assaulted her.)

A protégé of Kiarostami, Panahi is similarly known for neorealist docufiction, but often plays a prominent role in the films himself. It’s a pretty nice arrangement.

We were enjoying some of our neighbors’ delicious hot cider recently when my son asked me what makes apple cider different from apple juice. It’s a good question, considering both are made by pressing apples, and the answer isn’t quite as straightforward as the labels might suggest.

If you’ve ever had cider fresh from the orchard, you’ve probably noticed that it looks and tastes different from the apple juice you buy at the grocery store. Apple cider is usually cloudy, with a fuller apple flavor and sometimes a little tartness. Depending on the types of apples used, one batch can taste quite different from the next, with some leaning sweeter and others having a sharper, more tart flavor.

The difference generally comes down to processing. Both drinks begin with apples that are crushed and pressed for their juice, but apple cider is typically left unfiltered or only lightly filtered. Apple juice usually goes through additional filtering, which removes the fine apple particles responsible for cider’s cloudy appearance. That’s why most commercial apple juice is clear and golden, while fresh cider tends to look as though it came straight from the press. The additional processing gives commercial apple juice its familiar clear appearance and helps manufacturers produce a consistent product year-round.

The distinction isn’t quite as clear-cut as it might seem, though. While apple cider is commonly less filtered than apple juice, there’s no legal requirement that one must be cloudy and the other clear. That’s why you’ll occasionally come across unfiltered apple juice—or cider that’s been processed much like ordinary juice. And outside the United States, the terminology changes again. Order cider in England, for instance, and you’ll be served the alcoholic version that Americans call hard cider.

There’s one other difference worth checking before you buy fresh apple cider: whether it’s been pasteurized. Some orchard-pressed ciders are sold unpasteurized, which means they haven’t been heat-treated to kill potentially harmful bacteria. The FDA advises that children, pregnant people, older adults and people with weakened immune systems avoid unpasteurized juice and cider. If you’re buying directly from a farm or orchard, it’s worth asking how the cider was pasteurized.

Generally speaking, yes. In most recipes, you can substitute apple juice for apple cider, or vice versa, without making any other adjustments. Both bring apple flavor, sweetness and a little acidity, though the proportions can vary depending on the apples and how the drink was made.

I’d pay closer attention to recipes that depend on a pronounced apple flavor. Apple cider doughnuts are a good example. Many recipes call for simmering apple cider until it’s reduced, which concentrates the flavor before it goes into the batter. You can do the same thing with apple juice, although the flavor of the finished doughnuts will depend on what you started with. The same applies to apple cakes, muffins and other baked goods that call for apple cider. In most cases, either will work, but when apple flavor is the point, I’d reach for cider.

I also love cooking with apple cider, especially when pork is involved. A splash in a roasting pan or a slow braise adds sweetness and acidity, both of which work beautifully with richer cuts of pork. Apple juice can do the same job, though I’d be sure to taste the sauce before serving. If it’s sweeter than you’d like, a little lemon juice or apple cider vinegar can bring it back into balance.

If you’re making a drink like mulled cider, the difference isn’t quite as important. Apple juice takes perfectly well to cinnamon, cloves, orange peel and a gentle simmer on the stove. Fresh cider will generally give you a fuller apple flavor, but either makes a perfectly cozy fall drink.

Ultimately, you should choose based on what you’re making and what’s available. For everyday cooking and baking, there’s rarely a compelling reason to make a special trip for one if you already have the other. But when fresh cider is in season, it’s definitely worth enjoying. Especially when someone has been generous enough to do all the pressing for you.

It’s boom time for billionaires. In the next few months, the world could see a fresh crew of technology entrepreneurs join that club, and the numbers of multi-millionaires would swell even more.

Two giants of artificial intelligence, OpenAI and Anthropic, plan to go public in the next year or so, floating stocks that are expected to rocket some of their employees into the ranks of the ultra-wealthy. Some analysts have projected that Anthropic’s initial public offering (IPO) might bring in US$2 trillion, the largest in history. OpenAI is reportedly targeting $1.4 trillion for its IPO, although the company delayed going public until 2027 amid renewed concerns about AI safety.

If these IPOs go as planned, the effects might extend well beyond sales of superyachts, mansions and private jets. The windfall would probably also add to the flurry of private investments and philanthropic donations from tech-savvy entrepreneurs who are eager to jump-start their favoured areas of biomedical research, such as cancer biology, longevity and reproductive technologies. “It’s a very exciting time,” says Andrew Lo, who studies entrepreneurship at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in Cambridge. “We’re going to see the creative energy of the tech sector unlocked.”

Wealthy technology entrepreneurs have long put their cash and creative energy into medical research. And as their fortunes have grown, so has the scale of their investments, and the variety of ways in which their funds are doled out. One major player is Altos Labs, a longevity-focused company in Redwood City, California, which launched in 2022 with $3 billion in funds raised from tech titans Yuri Milner and Jeff Bezos, among others — an unprecedented sum for a fledgling biotechnology company with no drug candidates. The Chan Zuckerberg Initiative (CZI) , founded by Facebook co-founder Mark Zuckerberg and his wife, physician Priscilla Chan, has spent about $4 billion on basic scientific research and other causes, and plans to spend up to $10 billion more over the next decade.

Ultra-wealthy research investors follow a different strategy than do government funding agencies such as the US National Institutes of Health (NIH). Organizations such as the CZI and Altos Labs boast a commitment to high-risk, long-term research. Tech entrepreneurs are also often keen to invest in new technology, and to harness the power of AI for biomedicine. In April, for example, the CZI announced that it would spend $500 million on funding predictive AI models of human cells. Last year, OpenAI Foundation, the non-profit arm of OpenAI in San Francisco, California, pledged to spend $25 billion on health, disease cures and AI resilience. The foundation has announced more than $100 million for Alzheimer’s disease research and another $125 million for life-sciences data projects this year.

But this investment comes at a time when the public is suspicious of the tech elite and its political influence. It is difficult to assess the success of philanthropic programmes, and many are geared towards encouraging the use of the same technology the philanthropists profited from developing. Meanwhile, high-profile private investment in firms that study longevity or controversial reproductive technologies, such as gene editing in human embryos, raises questions about whether tech entrepreneurs will skew research towards applications that benefit the wealthy, rather than helping a broader swathe of society. “In philanthropy, no one gets elected,” says Rob Reich, a political scientist at Stanford University in California. “There’s no way of holding them accountable.”

US philanthropists have a long history of influencing what research gets done, and how. In the early twentieth century, grants from industrialists such as Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller helped to transform higher education and solidify the role of US universities in scientific research. Their giving focused on ensuring that the institutions they supported succeeded; today’s philanthropists are more likely to seek a particular outcome rather than support the long-term health of a university, says Eric John Abrahamson, a historian at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, Maryland. In 2024, non-profit organizations, including philanthropies, supported an estimated $25 billion in research and development — about 11% of total research funding in the United States, according to the Science Philanthropy Alliance (see ‘Support sources’).

It is difficult to track all donations from technology magnates comprehensively, but individual examples show that their influence has been sizeable. In 2000, Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates established the Gates Foundation (formerly called the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation), which made massive investments in vaccine development and the study of infectious disease, among other fields. It remains an influential behemoth: last year, Gates announced that he would donate about US$200 billion over the next two decades.

Other large tech donors include Sergey Brin, a co-founder of Google, who has donated more than $1 billion to Parkinson’s disease research. Sean Parker, an early executive at Facebook, has given hundreds of millions to cancer-immunotherapy research. In 2021, several philanthropists, including tech entrepreneurs, donated $650 million to found the Arc Institute in Palo Alto, California, in the heart of Silicon Valley, to understand the root causes of complex diseases. And US software magnate Larry Ellison announced that he will spend billions of pounds over ten years on a new campus near the University of Oxford, UK. The campus will focus on health care, as well as agriculture, climate change and AI.

In addition to charitable donations, tech entrepreneurs have been fuelling biomedical innovation through private-sector investments, such as funding Altos Labs. Another company, Calico Life Sciences, was founded in 2013 with backing from Google. With a focus on combating ageing and curing disease, Calico, in South San Francisco, California, was conceived as a long-term ‘moonshot’ programme. Sam Altman, a co-founder and chief executive of OpenAI, has invested about $180 million in Retro Biosciences, another longevity-focused company, in Redwood City, California.

In some cases, the line between for-profit investments and philanthropic donations are becoming more blurred, says Louis Shekhtman, a network scientist at Bar-Ilan University in Ramat Gan, Israel. The CZI, for example, was set up as a limited liability company (LLC), rather than as a tax-exempt foundation. Doing so comes at a cost: depending on how they are constructed, LLCs might not be eligible for the tax breaks that more-conventional charities enjoy. But an LLC can be more flexible in how it spends its money, and has fewer requirements to publicly disclose its activities, says Joan Heminway, a business-law scholar at the University of Tennessee in Knoxville.

In a 2015 statement shortly after the launch of the CZI, Zuckerberg said the LLC structure would give the initiative “the flexibility to give to the organizations that do the best work — regardless of how they’re structured”. He also said that any net profits from CZI investments would be put towards furthering its philanthropic mission.

Favourable US tax policies and a high concentration of billionaires — many of them in the technology industry — mean that the United States is unmatched among wealthy nations for charitable giving, says Shekhtman. It is also a major hub for biomedical research, and home to nearly half of the world’s biopharmaceutical companies.

And US tech entrepreneurs have a penchant for experimentation in how they structure their investments, says Keith Yamamoto, vice-chancellor of science policy and strategy at the University of California, San Francisco. When it comes to philanthropy, private donors are more able to take risks with their investments than are government funding agencies, which are beholden to taxpayers to ensure that their money is spent wisely. “Public money limits your activities,” says Harold Varmus, a former director of the NIH, who has also been on advisory boards for the Gates Foundation and the CZI. “There is a freedom of motion that is associated with private money.”

Some tech titans have experimented with various ways of structuring their grants. The Arc Institute, for example, boasts a “no-strings-attached” funding system that gives its investigators more freedom than do standard government grants awarded to biomedical researchers.

Philanthropists are also able to fund long-term, interdisciplinary projects that can be difficult for government grants to take on. Convergent Research, a non-profit organization funded in part by Eric Schmidt, the former chief executive of Google, is forming ‘focused research organizations’ that bring teams together to solve important scientific or engineering challenges that could benefit a wide swathe of research. “We’re looking for the pieces of critical, fundamental infrastructure missing from a particular field,” says Anastasia Gamick, a co-founder and president of Convergent Research, which is headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts.

Projects include developing cheap ways to map the neurons in mammalian brains, and large-scale screening to catalogue the off-target effects of small-molecule drugs.

Meanwhile, Yamamoto points to the CZI’s Institute for Advanced Biological Imaging as another example of a long-term, high-risk project that aims to develop pivotal technology. He hopes that the team there — which has brought engineers, physicists and computer scientists together with biologists — will succeed in refining a difficult technique called cryo-electron tomography so that it can image processes in a cell at the level of individual proteins.

Tolerance for risk is also evident in some tech entrepreneurs’ investments in for-profit companies. Some investors hope to reshape how fundamental research is conducted. Arcadia Science in Emeryville, California, for example, was launched with backing from Altman and from Jed McCaleb, a cryptocurrency entrepreneur in the United States, and pledges to “rethink the entire research cycle” by branching out from the usual laboratory model systems, such as mice and the bacterium Escherichia coli, to study more diverse organisms. Like Calico Life Sciences, the company warned investors from the start: this will take time.

Arcadia has also taken an unusual step for a for-profit company and embraced open science to a degree not typically found in industry, says Yamamoto. Its lab protocols, computer code and data sets are published directly online.

Silicon Valley investors are also sometimes willing to wade into controversial territory. Last year, Brian Armstrong, chief executive of the cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase, helped to fund a company dedicated to developing methods to edit the genome in human embryos to prevent disease. The company, named Preventive and based in San Francisco, will first have to grapple with the fundamental science of heritable gene editing to learn whether and how it can be done safely, says Lucas Harrington, a co-founder of Preventive. Private investment will enable the company to do that research, which US legal restrictions prohibit government grants from funding.

A Neanderthal who was buried 75,000 years ago in Shanidar Cave in Iraq died from a wooden spear straight through the chest, archaeologists have revealed.

While it's impossible to know exactly what happened, the violence may have been a targeted hit.

"The position of the wound and the force required to push it right through the Neanderthal's body bear the hallmarks of an intended kill shot directed towards the heart," study first author Emma Pomeroy, a paleoanthropologist at the University of Cambridge, said in a statement.

In a study published Thursday (Oct. 15) in the journal Scientific Reports, Pomeroy and co-authors analyzed the bones of Shanidar A, a Neanderthal who died violently at about age 45 and was then buried in Shanidar Cave.

The archaeological site at Shanidar is famous for its evidence of purposeful burial, which experts once thought was only done by modern humans. Although the Neanderthal "flower burial" at Shanidar has since been debunked, the cave holds other evidence of repeated Neanderthal burials, which shows that these extinct humans placed specific meaning on that location, the authors wrote in the study.

The skeleton of Shanidar A was incomplete when it was discovered in 2019, consisting of a mostly complete right hand, three ribs, six teeth and a left scapula or shoulder blade, which meant they could not figure out the sex of the Neanderthal.

Near the edge of the shoulder blade, archaeologists noticed a round hole about 0.6 inches (1.5 centimeters) in diameter and a round piece of bone that had been punched out of the shoulder blade. The researchers ruled out post-mortem damage to the bone, including animal gnawing and insect burrowing.

An example of a Paleolithic thrusting spear found in Germany. This may have been the kind of weapon that killed the Shanidar A Neanderthal.

"The perforation is best explained as resulting from perimortem [around the time of death] penetrating trauma, such as that caused by a projectile or a thrusting implement such as a spear," the researchers wrote in the study. Specifically, the Neanderthal was killed by a spear that entered their chest from the front at a high velocity and pierced their shoulder blade.

The shape of the wound in the shoulder blade appears to match experimental evidence of injuries made by wooden spears rather than stone-tipped weapons, the researchers wrote in the study. Neanderthals would have used wooden spears for both thrusting and throwing. But it is unclear if this individual died because of a violent interaction with another Neanderthal or by accident, such as a hunting incident.

"All we can ever know about the end of this Neanderthal's life is that it was violent, and came at the end of a wooden spear," study co-author Graeme Barker, an archaeologist at the University of Cambridge, said in the statement.

Even though this Neanderthal had an untimely demise, evidence on the teeth suggested that the individual was cared for during life.

The six teeth from Shanidar A were heavily worn-down in an unusual manner, the researchers wrote. One of them — a front tooth with a large quantity of tartar on it — had been split in half during the Neanderthal's life, and the individual continued to use the damaged tooth for a considerable amount of time. The collection of teeth suggests the Neanderthal sustained a major trauma to the face that fractured the lower front tooth and may have caused additional injuries to the jaw.

The Neanderthal's lower front tooth was fractured during life.

"There’s no doubt that Shanidar A would have had difficulty communicating and been in absolute agony for a long period of time," Pomeroy said in the statement. "Surviving a blow that splits a tooth in this fashion, then continuing to live and eat for a period of at least a year and maybe more, would have required care and support from other Neanderthals."

Rebecca Wragg Sykes, an archaeologist and author of "Kindred: Neanderthal Life, Love, Death and Art" (Bloomsbury, 2020) who was not involved in the study, told Live Science in an email that the apparently fatal chest injury is an important addition to our understanding of Neanderthals. While it is plausible the Neanderthal was stabbed by a wooden spear, Wragg Sykes said, "it would be interesting to also compare whether a wild goat horn, the most intensely hunted species at Shanidar, might have been responsible."

Shanidar A is not the first mortally wounded Neanderthal to be found at the site. In 2009, researchers analyzed the skeleton of Shanidar 3, an older adult male who died 50,000 years ago, and found a deep slice in one of his ribs. The researchers suggested that the Neanderthal may have been killed by a stone-tipped spear possibly thrown by a modern human. Although modern humans first reached Europe around 54,000 years ago, experts aren't sure exactly how they interacted with Neanderthals.

Pomeroy told Live Science in an email that the discovery at Shanidar of two stabbed Neanderthals 25,000 years apart is interesting but possibly just a coincidence.

"We know from the archaeology that Neanderthals were repeatedly coming back to Shanidar and using it for short periods at a time, both around 75,000 years ago and around 45-50,000 years ago, as well as leaving their dead there," Pomeroy said.

The traumatic injuries, purposeful burial and indirect evidence of wooden spears all show that 75,000 years ago, Neanderthal life — and death — were complex, the researchers wrote in the study.

#5967. Baked Potatoes on the Grill

Making baked potatoes on the grill means fluffy, tender spuds with buttery skins—all without turning on the oven.

When you already have your grill fired up for steaks or chicken, there’s no need to turn on the oven for a baked potato side. These grilled baked potatoes take just a few minutes to prep, and turn out fluffy and tender inside with buttery skins. Once they’re on the grill, you’re free to focus on the rest of the meal. Serve this grilled side dish with classic toppings like sour cream and chives, or set out a few options and let everyone build their perfect potato.

Making baked potatoes on the grill is just as easy as cooking them in the oven. Scrub and pierce the potatoes, then brush them with melted butter, season with salt and pepper (or any other seasoning you like), and wrap them in foil. Place the foil-wrapped potatoes over indirect medium heat, cover the grill and cook the potatoes until they’re tender.

Set up the grill for indirect cooking. For a gas grill, preheat the burners on one side to medium and leave the other side off. For a charcoal grill, arrange the coals on one side, leaving the other side empty. Close the lid and let the grill preheat to about 350°F.

The year is 2050, and as the sun sets, the night's first constellations emerge in perfect rows. But these amber-glowing arrays aren't stars at all; they're satellite clusters, harvesting the day's last rays before beaming their power back to Earth.

If it reads like science fiction, that's because it started that way. Called space-based solar power (SBSP), the concept featured prominently in the techno-utopian imaginations of 20th-century visionaries and writers. But for decades, it was dismissed — a pie in the sky proposal.

Now, as a new space race heats up, governments and private companies in China, the U.S., the U.K. and Japan are developing several projects to reassess the idea's feasibility. These include China's ZhuRi ("Chase the Sun") program, Japan's OHISAMA project, the U.K.'s Space Solar, and power-beaming experiments by Caltech and the Air Force Research Laboratory in the U.S., all of which are targeting test launches in the next five years.

Proponents say the technology has the potential to meet Earth's energy needs many times over while causing minimal harm to the environment. But first it needs to get off the ground: In the eyes of critics, space-based solar power is exorbitantly expensive, riddled with untested assumptions, and far too complex to be deployed in time for an urgent green-energy transition.

The challenges include getting the massive solar-panel-covered satellites into space; assembling them; getting those solar panels to constantly face our star; and then finding ways to convert, concentrate and transmit their collected power back to Earth.

Despite these hurdles, researchers keep returning to the idea. Perhaps because the most crucial step — generating energy in the first place — is already being done by the sun. Every second, our star fuses hydrogen atoms to expel the same energy as nine trillion atomic bombs. All that's left to do is catch it.

"We're not a competitor to fusion. We are fusion," Peter Garretson, the U.S. Air Force's former chief of future technology and an SBSP expert, told Live Science.

The first glimmers of SBSP as a concept came from the Russian rocket scientist and astronautics pioneer Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, who, in 1923, suggested deploying space-based mirrors that could concentrate sunlight to Earth with enough intensity to boil 10 cups of coffee.

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky inside his workshop in Kaluga, Russia. Long before he became globally celebrated for his pioneering formulas on rocket design and space travel, Tsiolkovsky spent decades obsessively working on designs for a steerable, all-metal cargo airship.

Tsiolkovsky's fanciful proposal soon entered science fiction, appearing in the British writer Olaf Stapledon's 1937 novel "Star Maker" as a "gauze of light traps" that an advanced alien civilization used to focus "the escaping solar energy for intelligent use" — an idea later formalized by theoretical physicist Freeman Dyson as a Dyson sphere.

Then, in 1941, Isaac Asimov made the clearest, most famous early sketch of SBSP in his short story "Reason," in which a robot colony toils aboard a power station that collects solar energy and fires it back to Earth in the form of microwaves. Asimov provided few details on the fictional station's engineering, instead focusing on the robots' religious worship of the vessel.

But the transformation from science fiction to a concrete proposal didn't take long. In 1968, aerospace engineer Peter Glaser, a consultant and project manager for the Apollo 11 mission's distance-measuring laser reflectors, drew up an engineering design for a "solar power satellite" consisting of three key elements: a ray-collecting device, a converter and a transmitter.

Glaser's collector was a disk of photovoltaic panels that would be lifted some 22,400 miles (36,000 kilometers) off the ground. Locked in a geostationary orbit above Earth and bathed in near-constant sunlight, the satellite would harvest solar rays before converting them into microwaves to be beamed, largely unimpeded through the atmosphere, to receiving antennae on the ground. Once there, the microwaves would be converted into electricity and distributed across the grid.

A 1976 artist's illustration of a solar power satellite based upon Peter Glaser's original design.The image shows the assembly of the satellite's microwave transmission antenna.

NASA awarded Glaser a contract to study the concept further, with both domestic and international energy crises (such as the 1973 oil crisis and the 1979 Three-Mile Island nuclear accident) adding wind to his solar sails.

But Glaser's design hit technical and financial roadblocks, the most forbidding being the project's sheer size. By 1981, when the U.S. Department of Energy's four-year study of space-based solar power was released, it estimated that a "meaningful" contribution of 300 gigawatts (sufficient to power hundreds of millions of homes) would require 60 satellites, each spanning 21 square miles (55 square kilometers) and weighing 50,000 tons (45,000 metric tons). The plan was deemed unaffordable.

Later reassessments, such as a 1997 report by NASA physicist John Mankins, pointed to advances such as improved solar cells that made the concept more realistic. Yet the overall consensus remained hostile. A 2015 report released by the Strategic Studies Institute (SSI) of the U.S. Army War College stated that while SBSP systems are "technically feasible," there was "no compelling evidence that such systems will be economically competitive" with terrestrial power systems. It concluded that "the reality is that such systems always seem to be seen as just 10 years away by their advocates."

But a lot can change in a decade, including the cost of firing rockets. Since the SSI report, SpaceX's reusable Falcon 9 rocket has sent launch costs plummeting to around 30 times less than those of NASA's Space Shuttle, with satellites also getting cheaper due to mass production and advances to the clean rooms where they're made. And SpaceX's even bigger Starship rocket promises to more than quintuple Falcon's payload capacity, lowering prices further.

SpaceX's Starship Flight 14 launches from Boca Chica Beach, Texas on September 28 as part of its first orbital test flight.

"What you have is a cadre of people in the space community that have grown up before Starlink and SpaceX," Garretson said. "They literally could not believe that, as your launch rate goes up and you amortize your costs, [the launch cost] approaches the cost of fuel." Some took notice. A 2021 assessment commissioned by the U.K. government estimated that a 2-gigawatt, mile-wide space-based solar power array would cost 17.3 billion British pounds ($23.4 billion) and generate energy at 50 British pounds ($68) per megawatt hour over its century lifetime.

That's the equivalent output of a large power station in the U.K., with the initial costs being 39% to 49% cheaper than biomass, nuclear or the most efficient gas energy sources, according to the assessment. And while it's still 14% to 52% more expensive than current terrestrial wind and solar energy, the technology could be beamed anywhere, even to remote locations.

Based on these findings, in 2023, the U.K. government awarded 4.3 million British pounds ($5.8 million) in seed funding to a handful of companies and universities to develop SBSP further.

The U.K. report chose one satellite design as its reference architecture: CASSIOPeiA. Belonging to the U.K. SBSP company Space Solar, the satellite's design is an attempt to crack a significant challenge faced by early solar satellite blueprints: how to keep solar panels constantly aligned with the sun to maximize radiation collection.

Unlike other satellite designs, such as China's Multi-Rotary Joint Solar Power Satellite or Mankins' SPS-ALPHA, which rotate the panels or use a system of mirrors to constantly redirect sunlight onto them, CASSIOPeiA is shaped like a gigantic DNA strand.

This design, alongside a sun-synchronous orbit and a slow spin, enables CASSIOPeiA to constantly receive and transmit solar energy without any heavy or failure-prone moving parts. Space Solar claims that a single CASSIOPeiA satellite, which would be about 1 mile (1.6 km) in diameter, could microwave-beam as much as 2 gigawatts of constant power to a fixed point on the ground — enough to supply a city of 2 million people.

Space Solar says that once the satellite is in space, it will be far more efficient and economical than gathering sunlight on the ground could ever be. That's because the light hitting the space-based power station isn't deflected or attenuated by the atmosphere.

"You use a lot less materials compared with terrestrial solar, and you utilize them more," Sam Adlen, co-founder and co-CEO of Space Solar, one of the funding recipients, told Live Science. "So it's 100% utilized in space rather than 10% if you're on the ground — and you've got 13 times more instant energy, so you're getting much more out of the materials."

However, the next step — beaming gigawatts of energy down and across tens of miles of atmosphere and through potential cloud cover — remains a major technical hurdle.

Proposed power-beaming methods are currently split between two types: lasers and microwaves. California-based startup Aetherflux, which has plans to beam power from satellites in low Earth orbit, has developed an infrared laser transmission system that it will test using a demonstration satellite launched on October 1. The microwave energy will be transmitted at around one kilowatt (enough to supply the basic electrical demands of a household).

Meanwhile, Caltech and Overview Energy, another U.S. startup, have both demonstrated respective microwave and laser-power beaming from orbit and from a plane, respectively, albeit in negligible amounts ( less than a kilowatt).

Despite their efficiency and proven ability to travel vast distances, optical beams in the infrared and near-infrared ranges are easily scattered by water droplets in the atmosphere, preventing power from being transferred on cloudy days. Persisting efficiency issues with optical power transmission also worsen at gigawatt power levels, meaning lasers are less feasible for use at the utility scale.

Microwaves, on the other hand, can be beamed at frequencies with much higher power and suffer relatively little atmospheric attenuation. That's why Space Solar, Japan's OHISAMA project and China's ZhuRi program all plan to use microwave-beaming systems. Nonetheless, microwaves have comparatively longer wavelengths, so their beams diffract more widely across longer distances. This means that even if beams are sent by transmitters hundreds of meters wide, they would need Earth-based receivers spanning dozens of square miles.

An aerial view of China's Five hundred meter Aperture Spherical Telescope, the world's largest single dish. With current technology, transmitters of this scale would need to be constructed in space in order to successfully beam microwaves to magnitudes bigger receivers.

These limitations mean that "it's going to take many years" before gigawatt-scale power beaming is achieved, Remo Proietti Zaccaria, head of the DELTA Lab at the Italian Institute of Technology, told Live Science.

Improvements in power-beaming efficiency will bring their own challenges. Even battery-powered laser pointers can blind or burn, so prototypes by laser-beaming SBSP companies like Aetherflux must be designed with that possibility in mind. They promise safety mechanisms that switch off their lasers if anything flies between the transmitter and the receiver.

"Definitely for the weapon side, this technology now can be used efficiently," Proietti Zaccaria said.

If you're imagining an orbital weapon devised by a Bond villain, you may be disappointed — even fully developed power-beaming lasers and microwaves would take a while to cook an egg, or boil Tsiolkovsky's coffees. But beaming technology could still have potent military use: Scrambling communications systems on Earth to create havoc with relatively little effort.

"If you throw even that amount of intensity of power to our antennas, you're going to freeze the electronics; that's for sure," Proietti Zaccaria said. "It's not necessary to destroy them. As long as you freeze the electronics, you've done the job."

The challenges associated with power beaming have pushed some engineers to simpler concepts. One (going full circle back to Tsiolkovsky's original proposal) envisions the deployment of space-based mirrors that reflect sunlight back to Earth after sunset, with a single reflector extending the daylight delivered onto solar panels by around 20 minutes.

This may not seem like much on its own, "but then you can increase this with multiple reflectors," Onur Celik, a researcher in space technology at the University of Glasgow, told Live Science. "If you have 10 or more of them, you can arrange them in space in such a way that you can extend this to up to two hours or so at that altitude."

"The sunlight that you deliver, if it all turns into electricity, could power thousands of homes," he concluded.

The trillion-dollar question is whether that power will be cheaper and more efficient than that generated on the ground. The median cost of the electricity generated by SBSP as estimated by the U.K. government is 50 British pounds per megawatt hour ($68), putting it roughly in line with the costs of offshore wind and terrestrial solar power.

However, this estimate is orders of magnitude too sunny for the 2024 NASA cost report, with its projections suggesting SBSP's baseline electricity costs fall between $610 and $1,590 per megawatt hour, depending on the array design. NASA concluded that the U.K.'s median estimate was achievable only after drastic future reductions in launch and manufacturing costs.

Independent studies are also divided over feasibility. One assessment suggests that SBSP could reduce Europe's need for land-based renewable energy by up to 80%, while another counters that the costs could be 12 to 80 times more expensive than those for terrestrial alternatives. This variability exists within the studies themselves, with research published in the journal Acta Astronautica estimating that SBSP could contribute to meeting anywhere from 3% to 31% of global electricity demand, depending on possible constraints in how many satellites can safely fit into an orbit, the receiver size, and where power is beamed to.

"For the numbers we found, I don't believe that this technology could actually be employed to generate energy for Earth," said Proietti Zaccaria, a co-author of the Acta Astronautica study. If humanity had used up all available space for solar panels on Earth, "then I will tell you it would make sense, because at that point, 3%, 5%, 10% could make a difference."

Proietti Zaccaria does see use cases for SBSP schemes for future moon or Mars missions, but otherwise, "the cost for building technology of this kind is really very, very high," he said. "Vice versa, the cost nowadays of buying and installing a solar panel on the roof of any building has become extremely cheap."

Among the challenges would be ferrying the raw materials for the systems into space and then assembling them. Such a feat would make the construction of the International Space Station — currently the most complex structure built in orbit — seem like child's play. Maintaining arrays and preventing them from crashing into other satellites in Earth's increasingly crowded orbits would also take significant international coordination.

Exposures taken over just 30 minutes on a night in June 2024 show the growing crowdedness of Earth's skies.There are now roughly 20,000 active and inoperational artificial satellites in Earth's orbit, with the number expected to reach around 60,000 by 2030.

Then there are the unweighed environmental costs. All modern rockets use methane, a potent greenhouse gas, to propel them into space, and they release much of it into Earth's upper atmosphere, where its effects are the strongest.

"And so clearly, there is a question there on how launching 100,000 of these superheavy vehicles into the atmosphere will actually impact the atmosphere itself, as opposed to the benefit that we get in the next 20 years or so from having solar power from space," Matteo Ceriotti, a senior lecturer in space systems engineering at the University of Glasgow, told Live Science. "It's not entirely clear."

Ceriotti said the likeliest route for overcoming all of these difficulties come from smaller companies running, and then streamlining, proof-of-concept tests in space.

"Obviously, it's not something that we have ever done," he said. "So clearly, we need to start somewhere, and we need to start small."

The hopes of SBSP proponents remain unclouded by gloomy estimates. They cite several technologies, from personal computers to reusable rockets, that were once thought to be impossibly expensive, even fictitious, that are now ubiquitous and cheap. Their eyes remain fixed on demonstration missions to be launched over the next four years by the likes of Space Solar, Reflect Orbital, Aetherflux and Virtus Solis. If these missions succeed, another once-impossible idea could appear much brighter.

"The private sector is good at this, because it has to be," Sanjay Vijendran, an SBSP consultant who led the European Space Agency's now-shuttered SOLARIS project to test the feasibility of SBSP, told Live Science. "It can break the problem down to quite a small level and start to do things with today's technology and today's way of doing things, and start to show that this can be valuable."

#5957. Slow-Cooker Birria Tacos

Recipe by Ericka Sanchez, La Habra, California

Nothing tops slow-cooker birria tacos. This comfort food is cheesy, crispy, crunchy and dunked into a warm bowl of rich, umami-packed consommé.

If you’re new to the concept of birria, allow me to introduce you to the Mexican gift that is quesabirria, or cheesy birria tacos. Half taco, half quesadilla and 100% delicious, they’re the kind of midnight snack or after-work meal that will quickly earn repeat status in your recipe repertoire.

The only problem? Making traditional birria can be quite labor-intensive because the beef needs to simmer for hours on end. That’s where this slow-cooker version comes in. If you don’t have the time or patience for the traditional hours-long process, this recipe delivers all that comforting goodness in a fraction of the time.

With a few store-bought spices, a handful of fresh ingredients, and about 30 minutes of prep time, you’ll have the best-ever birria ready to dominate taco night. The only decision left is whether you’ll serve them with cervezas or margaritas.

In a large bowl, combine the chiles and hot water, and let them stand for 30 minutes.

Editor’s Tip: Rehydrating the peppers breathes new life into the dried chiles. You’ll know they’re ready when they’ve softened and plumped up.

Add the chiles and the soaking liquid to a blender, along with half an onion, the tomatoes, garlic, bouillon granules, peppercorns, thyme, oregano and cumin. Puree the mixture until smooth, then strain it through a fine-mesh sieve to eliminate any chunky pieces.

Editor’s Tip: You’re going for a silky consommé, not a chunky salsa, so the sieve is important. But if you don’t have one, a regular mesh strainer will also work. Just use the back of a spoon to press as much of the sauce through as possible. No sauce spared on our watch!

In a large skillet, heat the oil over medium-high heat, then add the chuck roast. Sear it until a nice golden brown crust has formed, about two to three minutes per side.

Editor’s Tip: Searing is the handy step that turns making birria beef from an all-day affair into a slow-cooker cinch—without sacrificing flavor.

Transfer the roast to the slow cooker. Add the bay leaves and remaining onion half. Add the strained sauce, and stir to combine. Cook the beef, covered, on low until it’s tender, five to six hours.

Editor’s Tip: You’ll know your beef is ready when your kitchen smells like a taqueria and the chuck pulls apart easily with a fork.

Remove and discard the onion pieces and bay leaves. Transfer the meat to a cutting board. Shred it with two forks before returning it to the slow cooker.

Working one at a time, dip a tortilla into the cooking liquid, coating it thoroughly. Place the tortilla onto a griddle, comal or cast-iron skillet over medium heat. Sprinkle it with cheese if desired, and cook it until the cheese melts. Top it with beef, and fold the tortilla over to form a taco. Serve tacos with your desired toppings and a side of the cooking liquid.

Editor’s Tip: For authentic texture, cook the folded tortilla until crisp and golden on both sides.

Store the birria beef filling in an airtight container in the fridge for up to four days. You can store the beef in the consommé cooking liquid, but keep it separate from the tortillas and toppings.

If you’re prepping birria tacos the day before a party or game, make the slow-cooked beef as directed and return the shredded beef to the cooking liquid. Keep it in the fridge until about an hour before you’re ready to fry up the tacos. Reheat the filling in the slow cooker until it reaches 165°F, or reheat it in the microwave or on the stovetop. Keep the tortillas in a sealed bag so they don’t go stale, and prep any toppings and herbs in individual containers so you can set up your taco bar at the very last minute.

You can freeze slow-cooked birria beef for up to three months. Transfer the beef and some of the consommé to a freezer-safe container and seal it tightly. Be sure to label it; a frozen pint of taco meat can look an awful lot like Bolognese or chili.

We wouldn’t recommend freezing fully assembled, cooked tacos, which can turn soggy and lose their signature crispness. Your best bet is to keep the beef and cooking liquid separate from the tortillas, cheese and toppings, then assemble and sear your tacos when you’re ready for round two.

We like to make slow-cooker birria with boneless chuck roast. It produces plenty of flavor-packed fat for the cooking liquid, and saves you the hassle of removing the meat from the bones. Plus, it becomes fork-tender after a few hours in the slow cooker.

That said, you can absolutely use a bone-in chuck roast or beef short ribs. The only cuts we wouldn’t suggest are especially lean ones like sirloin or tenderloin. They can dry out during the low-and-slow cooking process.

For 3 to 4 pounds of meat, plan on roughly six to eight hours on the low setting on your slow cooker. It may be ready closer to the six-hour mark since you’ll be quartering the chuck roast first. Either way, we highly recommend giving your meat the ol’ fork test toward the end to see how it’s doing. Once the beef pulls apart easily, you’re good to go. Slow-cooker birria tacos will save you a ton of time, but the last thing you want to do is overcook your meat and dry it out.

Most people are strictly divided when it comes to flour or corn tortillas for tacos, but in the case of birria tacos, you really want to lean into corn. They’re the traditional choice, and they crisp up beautifully once dipped in the rich, chile-stained fat from the m. Flour tortillas don’t have the same texture—and trust us, that crispy exterior is the whole point.

#5956. The Best Apples for Baking

Not every apple is cut out for the oven. The best apples for baking—whether you’re making BA’s Best Apple Pie, a Buttery German Apple Cake, or Suspiciously Cheesy Apple Crumb Bars—have firm flesh that softens when cooked without turning to mush. A good balance of sweetness and acidity helps too. Granny Smith, Pink Lady, Braeburn, Honeycrisp, Golden Delicious, Jonagold, Mutsu, and Winesap are among our favorites.

But say you’re at the farmers market and none of those varieties is in sight. Don’t panic. Cookbook author and former BA food editor Claire Saffitz has a simple trick for finding a worthy substitute: Press your thumb against the apple. If it’s firm enough that you don’t leave an indent, you’re probably in good shape.

Softer apples—those that yield to the pressure of your thumb—are higher in water content and more likely to lose their shape or get soft and mushy when cooked. Save them for applesauce or apple butter, where a little breakdown is exactly the point. For more on why those apples listed above top our list of apple picks, read on.

Whether you’re making a classic apple pie or one with a cheddar-laced crust, the fruit you pick matters.

Granny Smith apples have long been a go-to for baking thanks to their pronounced tartness, firm texture, and year-round availability. Their bright, citrus-like acidity balances the sugar in pies and other desserts, like Gourmet’s classic Apple Strudel, and has made them a favorite among professional bakers for decades.

But they’re not everyone’s first choice anymore. During recipe development for BA Bake Club, senior Test Kitchen editors Jesse Szewczyk and Shilpa Uskokovic found that Granny Smiths repeatedly turned mushy in baked desserts, including their Apple-Cranberry Tarte Tatin and Caramel Apple Monkey Bread. Switching to Honeycrisp produced desserts in which the fruit held its shape—and offered more complex flavor. Granny Smith remains a classic, but if you want distinct pieces of tender fruit, another apple may be a better bet.

Pink Lady apples strike an ideal balance of sweet, tart, and tannic notes. They hold their shape in the oven and are relatively slow to oxidize, so the slices won’t brown as quickly while you’re assembling your dessert. Their bright flavor and relatively wide availability make them our first choice for BA’s Best Apple Pie.

Pink Lady is also a favorite of Christa Campbell of Rainbow Orchards in Camino, California, where they bake plenty of pies. “They keep that divine sweet-tart flavor,” she says, “and have the perfect oh-so-soft crunch and juiciness when baked.”

This superbly crisp apple has a concentrated taste and bakes up juicy but not mushy. Braeburns have pronounced sweetness balanced by tartness, and their flavor becomes richer, almost spicy, in the oven.

They release relatively little liquid during baking, which makes them particularly good for a crumb-topped apple pie: Less excess moisture means less risk of a runny filling or soggy crust. They’re equally at home beneath the streusel topping of Saffitz’s Yeasted Apple Coffee Cake, where their concentrated flavor can stand up to plenty of cinnamon.

Use a mix of apples with the skins on for this colorful Fall Fruit Galette.

Honeycrisps are the Bon Appétit Test Kitchen’s desert island apple. With their shatteringly crisp texture, juicy sweetness, and ability to hold their shape, they are as appealing baked as they are eaten out of hand.

The catch? Honeycrisps can be expensive. Shilpa suggests saving them for recipes that showcase the fruit, like Apple Dumplings, an Apple-Walnut Upside-Down Cake, or old-fashioned baked apples. For recipes where the apples are sliced or chopped and cooked down with sugar and spices, she recommends sticking to “something cheaper and just as effective.”

Golden Delicious apples might not be the most exciting choice for snacking, but their mild flavor becomes sweet, tart, and honeyed in the oven. They soften more than some of the other varieties on this list, making them a good option when you want a tender filling rather than distinct, firm slices.

Freshness is key: Older Golden Delicious apples can become mealy. They’re also less acidic than other varieties on this list, so pair them with a tarter variety or add an extra tablespoon of lemon juice to your pie filling. Their relatively thin skins make them a good candidate for leaving unpeeled in a rustic apple galette.

Tart with a honeyed sweetness, Jonagolds hold up exceptionally well in the oven. Related to Golden Delicious, they have a similarly mellow flavor but a firmer texture, making them excellent for pies and other baked desserts where you want recognizable pieces of apple.

The downside is their short window of peak quality. Jonagolds don’t store particularly well, so look for them early in the fall, especially at farmers markets and orchards. If you find good ones, put them to work in a Naked Apple Tart when they’re at their best.

Firm apples stay suspended in batter better than softer, wetter apples. Try your favorites in this Dutch Apple Snacking Cake.

Also known as Crispin, Mutsu apples are juicy, crunchy, and on the sweeter side. A cross between Golden Delicious and Indo apples, they have a similar flavor to Golden Delicious but a firmer texture that holds up particularly well during cooking.

They’re a great choice for pies, tarts, and other desserts, like Shilpa’s Glazed Apple Pie Fritters, where you want tender apple pieces that don’t disappear.

Winesap apples bring intense, full-bodied flavor with deep cider-like notes, and are equally suited to cooking, eating fresh, and making cider. They resist breaking down during cooking, adding complexity to baked desserts while keeping their shape.

Their assertive flavor makes them especially good in recipes with other fruits like an Apple-Rhubarb Crumble, or strongly flavored ingredients like a puff-pastry apple tart with almonds, maple syrup, and brandy.

Can you use the same apples for pie, cake, and crisp?

Generally, yes. Firm, flavorful apples work well in a range of baked desserts, but the ideal variety depends on the texture you want. For pies and tarts with distinct slices, prioritize apples that hold their shape. In cakes, crisps, and cobblers, a slightly softer apple can work well too.

Which apples should you avoid for baking?

Soft or mealy apples are less suited to desserts where you want the fruit to retain its shape. McIntosh, for example, tends to break down readily when cooked, making it better suited to applesauce or apple butter. But if you’re making a dessert where a softer filling is welcome, that tendency isn’t necessarily a drawback.

Do you need to peel apples before baking?

Not always. Leaving the skins on can add color and texture, especially in rustic desserts. For a more uniform filling, peeling is usually preferable. It ultimately depends on the recipe and whether you enjoy the texture of cooked apple skins.

#5952. The Expert Guide to Choosing the Best Hair-Straightening Treatment

From curl preservation to frizz control, here’s how to find the right smoothing service for your hair goals.

Whether you were born with pin-straight hair and often need a flyaway fix, or want a smoothing solution for curly-to-straight flexibility, there’s likely a straightening treatment that meets your specific needs. Straightening treatments won’t necessarily eliminate the need for straightening tools like a flat iron or blow dryer; instead, they alter the hair from within, either permanently, with the help of heat, or at a surface level to smooth the cuticle and eliminate frizz. Professional hairstylists break down how different hair-straightening treatments work to help you decide which is best for your texture and styling goals.

Straightening treatments vary by permanency, with the most temporary options being a professional blowout or silk press. Elba Rodriguez, a stylist and educator with John Frieda, explains that there are two main categories of hair-straightening treatments: relaxers, also known as retexturizers, and Keratin treatments, also known as smoothing systems. “What separates them are the active smoothing ingredients, how they’re applied, the processing time, and the overall procedure of the service,” Rodriguez explains to ELLE. More permanent options like keratin, chemical relaxers, and Japanese straightening alter the structure of each strand by either reverting hair to its original form after some time or keeping the strands permanently straight.

Relaxers/retexturizersPermanent hair-straightening treatments like relaxers and retexturizers change the composition and pattern of curly and coily hair. They restructure the hair to create a smoother, straighter pattern, and that texture stays smooth until the roots grow in, at which point the new growth can be touched up.

Since the chemical process of a relaxer keeps hair straight, you don’t need a flat iron unless you’re creating a specific style. Rodriguez says you also typically need to wait two weeks before or after coloring. Retouching grown-out roots with a relaxer is generally needed every three to four months.

Rodriguez explains that temporary hair-smoothing systems, like keratin, fill the hair cuticle, creating a frizz-free, smooth, and shiny result that gradually washes away and reverts hair to its natural texture over time.

Keratin treatments require a flat iron to activate and seal the smoothing keratin-based solution. “How the hair is flat ironed, and the temperature used are both part of achieving the desired result,” says Rodriguez. Unlike a relaxer, you can color your hair immediately before or after a keratin treatment. Results generally last 10 to 12 weeks.

Keratin treatment formulas for different hair types and desired results:

Straightening treatments are designed to be safe when performed by an experienced professional and when the right formula is used for your hair. “I always consider the hair’s condition, previous color or chemical treatments, and the ingredients in the product before recommending a service,” says hairstylist and Great Lengths global ambassador Dimitris Giannetos. “I would never recommend doing a chemical straightening treatment yourself at home,” he cautions. “There are too many variables, including processing time and the hair's previous treatments, to do it yourself without risk.”

Rodriguez adds that the service professional or stylist should be knowledgeable about how to properly care for the hair, skin, and surrounding environment throughout the process. She also stresses that the chemicals in these formulas can cause hair breakage and burns to the skin or scalp, and therefore should be done only by professionals.

Instead of choosing a treatment based on the hair texture you’re starting with, use your desired look as your guide. For example, you have curly or coily hair and want to maintain a curl pattern but eliminate frizz; or you want to loosen the curl for better manageability. “Hair condition, hair color, at-home care, budget, and overall hair goals all play a part in choosing the right treatment,” says Rodriguez.

Keep in mind that hair-straightening products can disrupt the natural curl pattern, so consult your stylist on your longer-term hair goals before choosing one. “Heat styling is temporary, while smoothing treatments can loosen the curl and permanent chemical treatments can significantly alter the natural texture. If you love your curls, I recommend choosing a temporary option rather than permanently changing the pattern,” says Giannetos.

Pair your goals with current texture using Giannetos’s guide to the right treatment:

Curly: A smoothing treatment can reduce frizz while still maintaining some of your natural curl.

Dense curls: “I prefer a customized professional treatment that makes the hair more manageable without unnecessarily compromising the curl pattern,” says Giannetos.

Wavy: Wavy hair usually responds beautifully to a professional blowout or smoothing treatment, which can create a sleek finish while maintaining movement.

Fine: With fine hair, Giannetos prefers a gentler approach so you don’t lose body or volume. For additional length and volume, he suggests adding in extensions like Great Lengths, which can be styled seamlessly with your own hair.

For Rodriguez’s clients with fine or wavy, less-resistant textures, she recommends keratin treatments to help maintain density and body while smoothing the hair.

Thick: A smoothing treatment can be great for thick hair because it reduces frizz and makes hair much easier to style, but choose the formula based on the condition of your hair.

After a straightening treatment, at-home maintenance is fairly simple. “Hydration and heat protection are key. Use products formulated for treated hair, incorporate a weekly mask, minimize unnecessary heat, and be gentle when brushing—especially when the hair is wet,” says Giannetos.

For keratin treatments, use a sulfate-free shampoo and conditioner to help maintain the treatment and to help it last longer.

For permanent straightening treatments, use a moisturizing shampoo and conditioner to help care for the hair.

For both treatments, Rodriguez also recommends leave-in conditioners like John Frieda Frizz Ease Daily Nourishment Leave-In Conditioner and heat protection like John Frieda Frizz Ease Extra Strength Serum to keep hair moisturized and for additional smoothing benefits.

“You should also comb the hair gently, avoid aggressively towel-drying, and thoroughly dry the hair with a blow dryer when wearing straight styles. Satin pillowcases or bonnets can also help protect the hair overnight,” Rodriguez concludes.
