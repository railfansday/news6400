#1249. Manchester City Premier League charges: What do they mean?

Since being bought by the UAE in 2008, Manchester City has become one of the world's most successful football clubs. A raft of serious financial charges threatens to undermine this and bring chaos to the Premier League.

Manchester City has won the English Premier League eight times over the last 14 years. Whether those titles will remain is one of a plethora of issues at stake after England's top division charged one of its most high-profile clubs with more than 100 breaches of its financial regulations.

On Tuesday, the Premier League announced that an independent commission had found City had drawn up "sham contracts" as well as "relying on sham agreements to artificially inflate the club's revenues and reduce its costs." These contracts, dubious sponsorships and a number of other methods were, according to the commission's report, used to inflate the club's revenue to the tune of about £900 million (€1.05 billion) over the period between the 2009-10 and 2017-18 seasons.

The case has been ongoing for eight years after initial revelations from Der Spiegel, a German magazine, and Rui Pinto of the 'Football Leaks' website. City continues to deny any wrongdoing.

"The Club is innocent of the accusations made by the Premier League and a comprehensive body of irrefutable evidence exists in support of all of its positions, relating to this case," read a club statement. "Manchester City FC will now pursue the appeal avenues open to it, on the basis that the opinion contains clear material errors, of law, principle and fact, and is unsafe."

On Friday, the club issued a further statement saying it had lodged a "comprehensive Appeal."

The 750-page report was damning, accusing City of inflating contracts and sponsorship deals, running secondary contracts off the books, making "concerted efforts to stop and frustrate the Premier League investigation" and signing off inflated image rights deals to artificially boost their earnings. The report says all this enabled them to pass both Premier League and UEFA financial spending limits.

The Premier League's chief ­executive, Richard Masters, described the case "as the most significant in Premier League history," adding that the club "systematically broke Premier League Rules for nearly a decade."

Although the Premier League, the world's most commercially successful football league, has drawn some praise for its actions, others point out that it took Pinto leaking emails and media reports to take action. The report shows that Manchester City, which had won the league once in 71 years before being taken over by the United Arab Emirates (UAE) in 2008, were consistently posting revenues way higher than any of their more established and successful rivals.

In this context, its the owners. The Abu Dhabi United Group, essentially an investment vehicle for the UAE royal family, bought the club in 2008. Mansour bin Zayed Al Nahyan, know simply as Sheikh Mansour, fronted the takeover. He is a member of the ruling Al Nahyan family and the UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister. Khaldoon Al Mubarak, who also holds several government positions in the UAE, was brought in as chairman.

Nation-state ownership of this type is controversial in European football. Manchester City's owners have long been criticized for the country's human rights record and using the club for sportswashing. Similar criticisms have been made of European champions Paris Saint-Germain, which Qatar owned and City's Premier League rivals Newcastle United, which is owned 85% by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund.

City's CEO, Ferran Soriano, joined in 2012 as City expanded their ownership into foreign leagues, buying up clubs in the US, Japan and Australia. He replaced former head coach Roberto Mancini with fellow Spaniard Manuel Pellegrini before former Bayern Munich and Barcelona coach Pep Guardiola took over in 2016, ushering in the club's most successful period.

The club immediately started splashing out on big-money transfers after the UAE investment arrived. At the time, neither UEFA nor the Premier League had the tight financial rules they now have. UEFA introduced its Financial Fair Play (FFP) rules for the 2011-12 season, while the Premier League followed with its Profit and Sustainability Rules (PSR) in 2013-14.

City's free spending and relatively low commercial income meant they were likely to fall foul of such rules. The club was handed a conditional €60 million UEFA fine for losses in the 2011-12 and 2012-13 seasons. They would later be banned from the Champions League on similar grounds in 2020 before being reinstated on appeal.

In an attempt to try and comply with these financial rules, City set up a system they called "Project Longbow" in 2012. The commission said much of this was genuine, but one arm of it was an agreement with a third party called Fordham that was, according to the ruling, "little more than a front." Essentially, it reads, the club's UAE owners "would be used (and were used) to enable Fordham to acquire from the club (at a sizeable, artificially inflated price) the club's entitlement to benefit financially from its players' image rights."

In other words, City's owners were pumping money into their own club through vastly inflated sponsorship and image rights deals so their income looked higher and they could spend more, particularly on player transfers and wages. The report also claims that City paid three individuals, whose names are redacted, through a third party to keep them off the books.

That's the question on everyone's lips. So far, no sanctions have been imposed on City, and the appeal will likely drag proceedings out even longer. Meanwhile, the club, which is top of the Premier League, faces Liverpool on October 11, after the international break.

While there is no precedent for such a tranche of charges, clubs have previously fallen foul of the Premier League's PSR regulations. Everton were handed a 10-point penalty for a £19.5 million breach over a period ending with the 2021-22 season. This was based on a Premier League tariff of six points for the initial breach, plus an additional point for every £5 million the club overspent. Given that City's overspending is close to a billion pounds, applying that would certainly relegate them, though the Premier League does not operate England's second-tier league, the Championship.

For now, it looks like City and the league will play on under a cloud. But if the verdict remains after appeal, clubs and individuals whose careers and honors stand to be impacted will likely come forward and litigate. City themselves could be relegated, docked points over successive seasons, face transfer bans or any combination of those things. The titles won in the relevant period could also be stripped.

#1245. Climate leaders called for solar energy for 'everyone, everywhere' by 2030, but with three years to go, is that goal attainable?

Prominent climate leaders have called on governments, businesses, institutions and the public to make a push for solar power before the decade is out, repowering the world with cheap, clean energy. Is that possible?

In a world "set ablaze by war and  warming," with ever-worsening climate disasters such as wildfires in Europe and collapsing glaciers in Nepal, solar energy provides a solution in the form of clean, affordable power, prominent climate leaders wrote in an open letter.

Signed by author Bill McKibben, former UN Climate Secretary Christiana Figueres and Canada's former Minister of Environment and Climate Change Catherine McKenna, the letter makes a clear call. It urges governments, businesses, institutions and the public to come together to provide clean, affordable energy to "everyone, everywhere by 2030" — from the close to one billion people who still lack access to reliable electricity, to the billions more who pay too much for dirty fossil fuels.

With just a little over three years to go until the decade is out, how realistic is this push?

"It's an ambitious target, but we've blown away in terms of where we thought we would be on solar and renewables," McKenna told DW. "So let's just keep on going."

According to global energy think tank Ember,solar power has grown at its highest rate in eight years. In 2025, it increased by a record 30% over the previous year and has risen more than tenfold since 2015.

Solar is the cheapest form of electricity in the history of the world, said McKenna, who founded the climate advisory firm Climate and Nature Solutions, and the global coalition Women Leading on Climate.

"It is scaling way faster than anyone ever imagined."

As is battery technology used to store excess renewable energy.

"The cost of batteries have as well come down as the cost of solar modules," said Eicke Weber, a physicist and former director of Germany's Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems who went on to found the European Solar Manufacturing Council, among other things.

"We have the technologies available and we have enough money to finance it," he said, of a transition to widespread solar access.

In 2025, for the first time in 100 years, renewable energy sources overtook coal power in the global electricity mix, Ember's most recent analysis showed.

At the moment, installed solar capacity around the world stands at 3 terawatts. 1TW equals 1000 gigawatts, and a single nuclear power plant has a capacity of 1GW.

Currently, the world produces about 1TW of new photovoltaic capacity each year, most of it in China. Weber says in order to fully change global energy supply to renewables, we would need about 50TW, adding that "we have more production capacities than we are selling."

Roughly 655 million people still lack accessto electricity.

"The pace of electrification in Sub-Saharan Africa has to triple to achieve universal access by 2030," McKenna said, pointing out how quickly this gap could be closed with solar.

Areas without electricity have the opportunity to leapfrog straight into clean technologies, she says, bypassing the fossil fuels that are heating the planet. Rising global temperatures are fueling extreme weather events such as drought, storms and flooding around the world.

"And there's a misunderstanding, which is often propagated intentionally by fossil fuel companies, that it's expensive, it's hard, it's unreliable. And that just isn't the case anymore," she said. "To be honest, the most unreliable thing is if you're trying to pass fossil fuels through the Strait of Hormuz."

Hans-Josef Fell is used to the argument that certain things cannot be done. Widely known as a pioneer of renewable energy, he drafted Germany's Renewable Energy Sources Act, which established feed-in tariffs that catalyzed clean energy growth at home and beyond.

"The Renewable Energy Sources Act unleashed exponential growth that shattered every assumption about what was achievable. It overturned the scenarios and projections that policymakers and experts had considered realistic. In the end, reality outperformed all expectations," said Fell, who now heads the think tank Energy Watch Group.

Fell, who has lived off grid for five years, said achieving a global energy transition requires political and societal will that is "currently lacking around the world because public debate is dominated by fossil fuel interests."

According to calculations by his think tank, if global energy demand were met entirely by renewables, solar energy would account for roughly 60-70% of the supply. The rest would come from wind power, and other sources, such as bioenergy and hydropower.

Countries like China and Pakistan have already pushed ahead.

"It is in our hands right now in the year 2026 to say we drop everything, we concentrate on this problem. We don't need to wait for new research," Weber said.

"It is possible to achieve this, but it would require a very kind of moonshot program," he added, saying it would need serious political backing.

He says while it might be possible for Germany to pull off such as transition in two or three years, if the political will were there, it would likely take longer to achieve on a global scale. "We would need probably till 2040 to say that we have a goal of a worldwide 80%, 90% electricity from renewables."

#1211. Nobel Prize in literature: How the winner is secretly chosen

Can Xue is considered one of this year's favorites; Margaret Atwood and Salman Rushdie are other strong contenders. But what goes on behind the scenes in the selection of the Nobel Prize in literature?

On Thursday, October 8, at around 1:00 p.m. CEST, all eyes in the literary world will be set on Stockholm.

That's when the new permanent secretary of the Swedish Academy, Ingrid Carlberg, will hold a press conference to announce the winner of the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature, marking the end of a lengthy selection process that officially began a year earlier.

The Swedish Academy is the body responsible for awarding the prize. Its 18 members consist of writers, professors and intellectuals.

In September of the previous year, the Academy's Nobel Committee sends forms to hundreds of individuals and organizations eligible to submit nominations for the Nobel Prize in literature.

According to the Nobel Prize website, these include professors of literature and linguistics at universities and colleges, previous Nobel laureates in literature and the heads of writers' associations that represent the literary community in their respective countries. Anyone from one of these groups who has not received a form may also submit a nomination.

"The list can reach 200 names per year of nominated authors," explains Gisele Sapiro, research director at France's National Centre for Scientific Research. She studies how the Nobel Prize, as a global institution, influences the international circulation of literature, power dynamics in the literary world and translation markets.

"The committee can also add names itself, such as authors who were nominated in previous years," she says.

In the spring, the Nobel Committee then selects five names through a multistage process and presents them to the Academy. Over the summer, Academy members read the nominees' works and discuss them. The Academy always announces the decision in early October.

As for the criteria the Academy uses to make its decision, it prefers to keep them to itself.

The public doesn't learn which nominees missed out until 50 years later — that's how long the Academy waits before releasing the list.

Sometimes decades pass between an author's first nomination and actually receiving the prize. Danish writer Johannes V. Jensen, who won the Nobel Prize in literature in 1944, waited 40 years. Doris Lessing received the award in 2007 after 35 years, while Thomas Mann, who won in 1929, had to wait 27 years.

In his will, Alfred Nobel, the prize's founder, stipulated that it should be awarded to the person who had produced "the most outstanding work in an idealistic direction." That leaves plenty of room for interpretation.

According to Gisele Sapiro, the Academy aims to honor ambitious, influential literature that has, for example, given rise to a new literary movement. "At the same time, the work has to be universal, being able to transcend its own culture," she says.

Translations can be one indication of this. "I noted in my research, however, that all the laureates since 1990 had been translated into English, French and German — except for Abdulrazak Gurnah [the Tanzanian-British 2021 Nobel laureate], who had not been translated into German before winning — meaning that I think these three languages are the most important," Sapiro explains.

Sapiro says it is also a major advantage if nominees have already won other major literary awards, such as the International Man Booker Prize, the Cervantes Prize or the Büchner Prize.

Politics can also influence the Swedish Academy's decisions.

On several occasions, the prize has gone to writers who openly opposed the regimes in their home countries, such as Belarusian writer Svetlana Alexievich in 2015. She is an outspoken critic of authoritarian President Alexander Lukashenko.

Genre, on the other hand, does not matter. In the past, laureates have been honored not only for novels but also for poetry and plays. In 2016, singer-songwriter Bob Dylan received the prize for his song lyrics.

So far, only 33 of the 122 laureates have come from outside Europe. There is also a clear gender imbalance: only in the past eight years have men and women alternated as winners, significantly increasing women's share of the total number of laureates to 14.9%.

#1207. Paramount and Warner Bros merge into one Hollywood giant

Paramount has completed an $81 billion purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery, as its owner Skydance creates a new entertainment behemoth. Billionaire David Ellison's hostile takeover bid defeated rival efforts from Netflix.

Paramount on Tuesday finalized its takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, completing a hostile acquisition that was months in the making and almost faltered at several hurdles.

The takeover cost a total of around $81 billion (roughly €72 billion), or almost $111 billion including the Warner Bros. debts passed on as part of the deal.

The merger brings together two of the oldest US filmmaking studios and will form a new giant called Skydance Corporation.

The new name Skydance hails from billionaire investor David Ellison, who first bought out Paramount and its subsidiaries and then moved on to target the much larger fish that is Warner Bros.

The bid faced stiff competition in a bidding war with Netflix, and later scrutiny from US regulators and politicians, but ultimately went through.

"Today is a historic day, not just for Skydance but for our entire industry," said Ellison, whose family has ties to US President Donald Trump.

Skydance shares began trading on the New York Stock Exchange on Tuesday under the ticker SKYD, while Warner Bros. Discovery shareholders will receive about $31 per share.

The deal closed less than a week after a federal judge in California approved a settlement between Paramount and 12 US states that had sued to block the transaction on antitrust grounds.

As well as the two giant film studios, Paramount Pictures and Warner Bros. Pictures, the deal also brings together the CBS News and CNN news networks, and the HBO and CBS television brands. Ellison says he plans to merge the subscription streaming platforms Paramount+ and HBO Max.

Ellison will serve as chairman and CEO of the new company. Former Mattel boss Ynon Kreiz is operating as co-CEO in charge of day-to-day operations.

Skydance Corp. looks set to have a total revenue in the region of $70 billion, based on the performance to date of its component parts. It says it's targeting cost savings of around $6 billion within three years as it takes advantage of overlaps created by the merger.

#1202. Munich up against at least six competitors for 2036 Olympics

The International Olympic Committee has revealed the six other countries intending to bid for the 2036 Olympic Games. This comes after Germany selected Munich as its Olympic candidate.

Munich's bid for the 2036 Summer Olympics will be up against at least six candidates from Europe, Asia and Africa. On Tuesday, the International Olympic Committee (IOC) announced that, in addition to Germany, Hungary, Turkey, India, Qatar, South Korea and South Africa all intend to bid for the Games. All are currently engaged in the Continuous Dialogue stage of the bidding process with the IOC.

However, there could be further applicants, as the deadline for interested parties to submit a bid is October 31. The candidates are to present their plans virtually to the Future Host Commission in November. In March 2027, the IOC is to deliberate, analyze, and narrow down the field of applicants to a group selected to enter into the Strategic Dialogue stage.

A subsequent Targeted Dialogue stage with one or more bidders is planned for 2028, with the final decision on the 2036 Olympic host expected at the IOC Session in the summer of 2029.

However, the IOC did not disclose exactly which cities, alongside Munich, are in the running – although Ahmedabad, Doha, Budapest and Istanbul are seen as possible hopefuls.

Munich secured its bid late last month after beating the Cologne/Rhine-Ruhr region in a vote by delegates of the German Olympic Sports Confederation. The city last hosted the Games in 1972. Berlin had withdrawn its bid in the days leading up to the vote.

"Wherever the Games take place, one thing must never change: Athletes have to be at the heart of everything we do," IOC President Kirsty Coventry said.

"As an athlete, you spend years working to make your Olympic dream come true. The experience you have when you finally reach the Olympic Games stays with you for the rest of your life," added the former swimmer who won gold in the 200-meter backstroke for Zimbabwe at the 2004 Athens and 2008 Beijing Summer Games.

"That is why, for every future host, delivering the best possible experience for the athletes must always be the starting point."

The IOC is to evaluate the bidders' proposals based on "technical factors." These include a master plan for competition venues and the athlete experience, the financing strategy, political and public support, the capacity to deliver the event, and a sustainability strategy. The German Olympic Sports Confederation believes Munich is well-positioned in all these areas.

The Munich bid is not focused solely on 2036 but was also designed with 2040 and 2044 in mind. If it misses out on 2036, Munich can expect to face a similarly large pool of competitors for the following two Summer Games. According to an IOC statement, there are National Olympic Committees "taking part in noncommittal talks with the IOC about hosting future Olympic Games beyond 2036."
