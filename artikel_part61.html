#3400. Xbox reportedly planning three more rounds of layoffs, with the first wave coming as early as this month

As Sharma outlines Xbox's future with Helix.

Xbox is reportedly gearing up for three more rounds of layoffs as the Microsoft gaming division continues CEO Asha Sharma's plan to reset the business. According to a new report, the first of these is taking place this month, followed by another in November and one in January 2027.

That's according to "people familiar with the plans", who revealed that to Fortune Magazine in a new report which also featured a profile of Sharma. Plans could change, however, the people said.

If true, this would be the second round in as many months, following September's round which saw the elimination of 268 roles alongside organisational changes that moved Obsidian under Bethesda, with Rare and World's Edge joining Activision.

Those layoffs are part of what's considered the second major wave in Xbox's larger plan to cut costs and return the division to profitability. The first wave was the largest by far. It took place in July and saw over 1,600 job cuts. The remaining 1,600 are planned to be distributed across future quarters, culminating in the early months of 2027.

Thanks to this latest report, we now know when those remaining cuts will occur. The report does not offer any information on which teams are in Sharma's crosshairs, considering the massive cuts that already took place at its largest and most iconic studios.

One of the biggest remaining question marks, however, surrounds the fate of Gears of War: E-Day studio The Coalition. The Canadian first-party studio just released the third-person shooter to what appears to be softer than anticipated sales.

Eurogamer spoke to members of The Coalition's development team as they were preparing to release E-Day, and it's clear there's widespread worry Xbox will reduce staff at the studio now that their latest project has shipped. Many also fear the cuts could be timed in such a way to prevent those potentially getting laid off from receiving promised bonuses.

We're well over halfway through October, so if the information is accurate, the bad news is likely to be announced this week or next. The same report also revealed that Xbox is internally estimating next-gen Helix consoles to cost between $1,200 and $1,800.

#3397. The Difference Between Suunto's Race, Run, and Vertical Series Smartwatches

This week, Suunto launched its newest addition to its Race line of performance watches, the Race 3S. At $599, it’s lightweight with a premium feel, and to me, its most interesting upgrade is the new silicon-carbon battery, which Suunto says offers up to 38 hours of dual-band GPS, up to 45 hours in Endurance mode, 10 days of training use, and up to 12 days in daily mode. While I usually stick to my Garmin Forerunners, I’ve been personally testing the Suunto 3S and loving it on first impression.

Suunto may be one of the lesser-known fitness watch brands, but its offerings, aimed at hikers and outdoorsy people, have long been well regarded. The company sorts its sports watches into families: Run, Race, and Vertical. Three families of watches, all with several generations of models and a lot of overlapping specs, are enough to leave you staring at a product page, wracked with decision fatigue. This week’s launch of the Suunto Race 3S, the newest member of the Race family, is a good enough reason for me to help you sort it out.

Best for: Road runners, 5K-to-marathon trainers, and anyone who wants a watch they forget they're wearing, including overnight. It's also a good first step into Suunto's ecosystem.

In her review of the Suunto Run my colleague Beth Skwarecki calls this watch on of her favorite running watches, pointing to features that not only rival, but maybe even outshine Garmin and Coros watches at a similar price point. Both watches in the Run series, the Run and the Run 2, are known for being easy to wear and simple to use, but still with all the core features of a reliable running watch.

Best for: Trail runners, triathletes, and runners who want serious training tools and navigation.

The Race S is the compact option, while the Race 2 has since replaced the original Race and has a bigger screen and larger case. It might best suit people with bigger wrists who want a big display and don't mind the weight.

As of today, the Race 3S is the lightweight premium pick. It combines the Race S's compact size with a titanium and sapphire build, a bigger battery, a brighter screen, and double the storage. Suunto says its battery capacity is 46 percent larger than the Race S's, and its display is 233 percent brighter.

When in doubt, the Race series is the middle of the lineup, and it's a natural sweet spot to land on. If you can't decide between the Run and Race options, Beth's got you covered with an in-depth comparison.

Best for: Ultrarunners, mountaineers, backpackers, and anyone doing multi-day trips where charging isn't an option. It's also a good fit if you want a watch that holds up to rough use.

The Vertical 2 is marketed as an expedition-grade watch. It weighs about 87 grams and measures 49 mm, and it's available in stainless steel or titanium. It's water-resistant to 100 meters. It has a built-in LED flashlight that the lighter Race and Run watches lack, a redesigned heart rate sensor, and more storage and processing power than the original. Its main selling point is battery life—allegedly up to 65 hours of GPS in Performance mode and up to 250 hours in Tour mode.

The original Vertical is the older model, with a solar-assisted MIP display. It's still sold and often discounted, but the Vertical 2's AMOLED screen is a major step up in clarity.

The Suunto Run series is for runners who want a light, simple watch. The Race series is for performance athletes who want a premium build and strong all-around training and navigation tools. The Vertical series is for people who go far from civilization and want the longest battery life and the toughest build.

The Suunto lineup also includes Core outdoor watches and dive computers like the Ocean, but those are a different conversation. For runners and trail athletes, the choice comes down to Run, Race, or Vertical, and which of them fits how far, how long, and how rough you like to get:

If you mostly run on roads and want a lightweight entry-point watch, look at the Run.

If you race, train across several sports, or run trails and want a premium build, look at the Race 3S, or the Race if you're sticking to a smaller budget. Or you can choose the Race 2 if you prefer a bigger case and screen.

If your adventures last days rather than hours, choose the Vertical 2.

#3393. Mass Effect deserves way better than being unceremoniously chewed up and spit out in EA’s latest reshuffling

BioWare is absolutely not without blame, but it did not need to end this way.

Just like that, one of gaming’s previously most respected and beloved developers, BioWare, is gone.

The studio that brought us Mass Effect, Dragon Age, Baldur’s Gate, Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic, and more has been shuttered by EA with a quietly released midday announcement ahead of the upcoming N7 Day in a few weeks, when news on the Mass Effect sequel was expected. It’s a very sad day for the studio, those who are now without a job, and fans of multiple franchises and games.

I’m tremendously sad for those who’ve been let go today, as always. But with all due respect to Dragon Age et al., I’m especially mourning my favorite space-faring sci-fi franchise; both what it was, and what it could’ve been.

Amid the studio’s closure, EA revealed that the next Mass Effect game, announced in 2020 and presumably slow-going development-wise at BioWare, has also been canceled. We may never know what was to come of it, but the teases had me excited and hopeful. Liara T’Soni was back, and that was enough to trigger excitement and nostalgia. But alas, it’s over. That’s it. No more.

EA owns the Mass Effect IP after acquiring BioWare way back in 2007, which also was the beginning of the end in the eyes of many. That end was expedited by the company’s $55 billion acquisition this year by Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund, Silver Lake, and Donald Trump son-in-law Jared Kushner’s Affinity Partners. Once this deal was closed, everybody knew substantial changes were coming, and BioWare ended up first on the chopping block as part of around 400 layoffs, according to Bloomberg.

But leaving Mass Effect behind entirely would be a massive mistake. For my money, it’s one of the best sci-fi universes ever made, rich in lore and even more so in potential. I’ve loved it from the moment I first played the original game on Xbox 360, survived the suicide mission in Mass Effect 2, read through all of Drew Karpyshyn’s books, enjoyed all of the comics, closed things out and saved Earth in Mass Effect 3, and even had fun with Mass Effect: Andromeda. Yes, I enjoyed Andromeda, and I was hoping to learn more about the Quarian Ark that went missing in its canceled DLC.

There is so much potential for more stories in that universe, and EA at least agrees as much in some ways, as it began working on a Mass Effect TV series at Amazon’s Prime Video. Prequels, sequels, spin-offs. The strength of the series was in its cast, its characters and their interactions, the worldbuilding of its politics involving multiple alien races all trying to co-exist, and the stories told within the mainline games and its overarching plots elsewhere.

I can’t help but feel like there may have been ways to properly use the IP that could’ve made money for EA, since that’s all that really matters (EA CEO Andrew Wilson made $38 million this year and $30 million in 2025, by the way).

I spent countless hours in ME3’s co-op multiplayer. They could’ve tried going free-to-play with a shooter, or MMO, or ARPG, they could’ve made strategy games, or licensed out the IP to other studios to give it a fresh look with a traditional RPG style that you’d expect. They still could. I hope they do, or better yet, I hope EA sells off the IP to a publisher that will give a shit enough to let a good game be made rather than letting it die. I think the fans would follow.

The writing’s been on the wall for a long while at BioWare, unfortunately, and it’s felt like this day would come for a long time. It’s easy to blame EA for everything, but the studio had also become a shell of what it once was, after years of talent leaving in exodus for one reason or another. It had only released three games (plus the Mass Effect Legendary Edition remasters) in the past decade plus, and none of them performed or reviewed particularly well.

Starting with Andromeda in 2017, the studio’s been on a downturn. I still think that game was nowhere near as bad as some made it out to be, but also not what players had come to expect from the franchise. Then Anthem happened. Yeah, it’s been bad. That game had a great basis, but forced attempts at making it a live-service game failed miserably (among other issues), leaving it to be remembered as an all-time flop.

After the Legendary Edition in 2021, BioWare’s next release was Dragon Age: The Veilguard in 2024, which had mixed reviews from critics but did not land well with many fans, and didn’t sell anywhere near what EA was expecting (shocker, I know). So while today’s news is not a surprise, it’s still a massive disappointment. Especially when it comes to my Mass Effect.

I’m sure we may learn more about what happened with Mass Effect and BioWare in the future, but it’s apparent that the studio and its leadership mishandled the games, EA totally botched the franchise’s potential, and now those of us looking forward to more stories in that universe are left wondering what would have happened.

I’m sad today, but also angry, disappointed, and unsurprised. I wish BioWare ever even got the chance to really show us what it was working on. For now, it all just feels like a great loss for the industry, and gamers especially. And not to be too dramatic, but this song is all I hear in my head as I think about the franchise we may never see or hear from again:

#3377. Whatever Happened to Daylight Saving Time Going Away?

It will soon be time, once again, for our clocks to “fall back.” Nov. 1 is the date most places in the U.S. quit daylight saving time for 2026 and go back to standard time. We're still on this clock-changing treadmill, despite legislation that was supposed to eliminate it a few years ago, and despite medical professionals warning that these clock changes are bad for our health.

For example, a study out of the Stanford School of Medicine found that switching the time back and forth every year probably contributes to increased rates of stroke and obesity. Permanent daylight saving time would be an improvement on that, and permanent standard time would be even better, health-wise. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine published a position paper a few years ago stating that scientific evidence supports the idea "that the United States should eliminate seasonal time changes in favor of permanent standard time, which aligns best with human circadian biology."

But despite all this, we’ll still have to turn the clocks ahead again in spring. So whatever happened to those bills in Congress aiming to abolish the time changes?

The Sunshine Protection Act was a federal bill that would have made Daylight Saving Time permanent. If enacted, it would have taken effect on Nov. 5, 2023, the date that we would otherwise have changed the clocks. The result would be that our winter mornings would stay dark an hour longer than they currently do, but we’d get an hour more of daylight in the winter evenings.

The bill passed the Senate in March of 2022, but it wasn't taken up by the House of Representatives. In July of 2026, a similar law passed the House of Representatives, and President Trump wrote on Truth Social this month that "Republicans in the Senate have to get moving on what I call the 'No More Changing of Clocks Act.'" The Senate Majority Leader said it doesn't have the support to pass, since it would mean dark mornings for commuters and for children waiting for morning buses.

We operate on two different times throughout the year: "standard time" and "daylight saving time." These even get their own time zones! For example, if a time is marked as "EST," that means eastern standard time, which is different from eastern daylight time.

Standard time is the one we use in winter, and daylight saving time is the one we use in summer. The bill mentioned above would have done away with standard time and kept us on daylight saving time year-round. Meanwhile, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine has taken the position that we should stay on standard time (that is, winter time) year-round, since screwing with our circadian rhythms twice a year has detrimental effects on our health and safety. If they got their way, we would fall back some year and then never spring forward again.

States have their own ideas about what time it should be. Currently, states are not allowed to switch to permanent daylight saving time, but they are allowed to stay on standard time year-round if they prefer. Two states do so: Hawaii and Arizona (minus the parts of Arizona in the Navajo nation).

Nineteen states have passed laws or resolutions that would allow them to switch to permanent daylight saving time if the federal government ever allows them to do so. Those states, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures, are Alabama, Colorado, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Louisiana, Maine, Minnesota, Mississippi, Montana, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Washington, and Wyoming. Three other states have made attempts to join the list: Similar bills made it partway through the Kentucky and Ohio legislatures but were not signed into law. California voters have authorized a law that isn’t yet officially on the books.

Until a federal law passes, those states are stuck with their current time zones. Current federal law, as passed in 1966, allows states to opt out of daylight saving time (as Hawaii and Arizona have) but does not offer a way for states to make daylight saving time permanent or to choose their own dates for changing the clocks. If you’re sick of time changes, you can always move to one of the territories that don’t observe daylight saving, which include the U.S. Virgin Islands and Puerto Rico.
