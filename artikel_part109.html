#996. How green is Europe's urban public transport? A data-driven reality check

Greener buses and cleaner fleets are reshaping public transport across Europe. But are cities moving fast enough to meet their zero-emission goals?

An electric plug icon on their windows makes them easy to spot in cities like Brussels and Madrid. Quietly navigating the streets, electric buses have become an increasingly common sight on Europe's streets in recent years.

Michela Le Pira, assistant professor of transportation at Italy's University of Catania, says removing tailpipe emissions — the gases and particles generated when a combustion vehicle is running — might be "the easiest strategy to improve public transport."

Most Europeans live in urban settings, and road transport harms air quality. In 2023, almost 300,000 deaths across the EU were caused by air pollution levels exceeding the World Health Organization's (WHO) recommended thresholds.

Of all fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations in European urban areas, about 14% come from road transport, including public, private and freight transport. Additionally, towns and cities account for nearly a quarter of the European Union's transport greenhouse gas emissions (GHG).

DW and the European Data Journalism Network evaluated more than a decade's worth of data from more than 100 reports and sources on public transit and the emissions it causes, and identified 148 sustainability commitments by local authorities and transport operators in 33 cities across Europe. The analysis focuses on motorized mass transit, leaving out bikes and pedestrians as well as taxis, shared vehicles and e-scooters.

The EU wants to become the first climate-neutral continent by 2050. A key part of that plan is cutting transport-related GHG emissions by 90% from 1990 levels.

As DW's analysis shows, Copenhagen is the only EU capital that has almost electrified its entire bus fleet — its vehicles do not have combustion engines and therefore do not produce tailpipe emissions. In cities like Rome, Lisbon and Nicosia, more than 90% of buses still run on diesel, and metros and trams account for less than 10% of their public transport fleet.

Although cars still dominate European streets, "electric buses are one way to reduce local air pollution," said Martin Baierl, data analyst with Transport & Environment, an advocacy non-profit for clean transport.

EU funds enabled many cities to increase their electric and alternative fuel bus fleets from 2021 on.

Electric buses comprise 20% of the total fleet in EU capitals and selected cities. Their number increased by 55% between 2021 and 2025 — equivalent to 1,234 more such vehicles on the road. The number of diesel-only buses has been declining since then, although they still outnumber those running on alternative fuel.

In addition to decarbonizing the bus fleet and helping to reduce air pollution, embracing electric models can help to lower noise and public transport operating costs. All the more so if the city produces its own electricity, explains Baierl. However, the outlay for e-buses is higher than for diesel models. In 20 out of the 31 cities with data to analyze, over 50% of the bus fleet is made up of traditional vehicles.

Brussels, for example, was an early adopter of moving away from traditional diesel-powered buses, switching to hybrids instead. Now the Belgian capital is in the process of electrifying its fleet, but at a slower pace. "It's not easy to know what the right technology will be in the end," Baierl said.

Another example is hydrogen-powered buses, which also produce zero emissions but are more costly to operate than their electric counterparts.

Transportation is the only major sector where greenhouse gas emissions remain above 1990 levels, despite the EU's ambition of a 90% reduction by 2050.

On average, public transport — including buses, trams and metros — is used for more than a quarter of trips in the 10 largest EU capitals, according to recent data from the non-profit group CDP (formerly the Carbon Disclosure Project). In Stockholm, the share is 49%, while in the smaller Slovenian capital of Ljubljana, it's 11%. DW analysis of transport sustainability pledges reveals that only four cities have explicitly committed to achieving zero emissions in buses or all public transport.

Yannis Papadimitriou lives 85 kilometers (about 53 miles) away from Athens, the EU capital with the highest air pollution mortality risk. He travels to work by commuter rail, a means of transport he'd like to see promoted more.

"For me it is a very conscious choice to use electric public transport, especially trains, which are the safest, most environmentally friendly and quickest way of transport."

Though trains, trams and metros run on electricity, there are more buses within city boundaries, and in many urban areas the majority still run on diesel.

In Northern Europe, 15 kilometers away from Copenhagen, Poul Kattler also used to commute to work. He is now retired and takes the bus more frequently with his grandchildren. Despite Copenhagen having an almost completely fossil-fuel-free public transport system (only one bus runs on biomethane), the metro expansion, which is responding to the city's growth, comes with its own set of concerns.

"The whole construction phase [of the new metro line] is extremely environmentally demanding," Kattler said. If the construction emissions are considered, "the CO2 balance would be very poor for the metro now."

But in the long run, the benefits of a new metro line in reducing CO2 emissions outweigh its construction-related emissions, Le Pira said.

Combustion or exhaust emissions are not the only way transport pollutes the air and the environment. Indirect emissions, like those coming from electricity generation or fuel production, add to transport's CO2 footprint. Brakes and wheels release non-exhaust particle matter when wagons or vehicles slow down.

Since 1992, buses and any vehicle running on fossil fuels in the EU have been subject to stricter emissions standards. Fine particle emissions from buses and heavy-duty vehicles had dropped by 92% in 2022 according to the European Environment Agency. Yet overall road transport non-exhaust emissions from tire wear have increased 58% in two decades, as cars have become heavier.

With a few exceptions, EU member states are not close to cutting greenhouse gas transport emissions by 90%, as envisioned by the EU's 2050 target.

Zero-emission public transport is one step toward decarbonizing transport in cities and meeting GHG reduction goals. The next challenge is to get people out of their cars.

"If we talk about sustainable mobility in general and not only clean public transport, the main problem is the modal shift from private cars to public transport," Le Pira said.

On average, private automobiles account for more than a third of transport in the largest EU capitals, according to CDP data.

Cheaper ticket prices for groups, like students, and disincentives for car use, such as low-emission zones, are two complementary ways of attracting more passengers while reducing private transport.

In her research work, Le Pira investigates how public transport can be more efficient and appealing for city dwellers, for example, by combining transport modes where people step off their bikes and onto a train. This kind of solution could be particularly relevant for small cities "where cars are still the main transport mode."

According to Baierl, places with good public transport draw people in. "Cities where not everybody has to use a car are just more livable," he said.

#993. Fatty liver disease is actually five different conditions, scientists discover

A common liver disease affecting nearly 30% of adults worldwide is far more complex than previously understood. Researchers at Mayo Clinic have identified five biologically distinct forms of the condition, each associated with different risks of serious health complications, including heart disease, liver failure, cancer, and the need for a liver transplant.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, come from a collaboration between Mayo Clinic and Virginia Tech. Scientists discovered that metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, or MASLD, can develop through several different biological pathways. Some are closely connected to obesity and diabetes, while others appear to be driven by inherited genetic differences.

One of the most important findings involves the genetic forms of the disease. These subtypes are associated with a greater likelihood of progressing to advanced liver damage, even in people who do not have the metabolic risk factors typically linked to fatty liver disease.

By identifying these differences, doctors may eventually be able to recognize vulnerable patients sooner, improve screening, and select treatments based on the specific biological processes responsible for their illness.

"When clinical and genomic data are analyzed together at this scale, you begin to see patterns of disease progression that would otherwise remain hidden," says Shulan Tian, Ph.D., co-senior author and a bioinformatician at Mayo Clinic. "Once you separate these subtypes, you can start to match treatments to the biology that's actually driving the disease."

MASLD, previously known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, develops when excess fat accumulates in the liver. Many people experience no noticeable symptoms, allowing the condition to go undetected for years.

Although fatty liver disease does not always cause serious complications, it can progress to inflammation, scarring, and permanent liver damage. Over time, severe cases may develop into cirrhosis, a condition in which extensive scarring prevents the liver from functioning properly.

MASLD is a major contributor to cirrhosis, liver cancer, and liver transplantation worldwide. Understanding why some patients experience severe complications while others do not has therefore become an important goal for researchers.

Genetic Analysis Reveals Five Distinct Liver Disease Subtypes

To investigate these differences, the research team examined genetic sequencing results alongside extensive medical information from more than 4,600 patients diagnosed with MASLD.

Their analysis included liver enzyme measurements, body mass index, blood lipid levels, and other indicators of metabolic health. The researchers also considered additional medical conditions, including diabetes, depression, and sleep apnea.

Using advanced computer models, they searched for patterns connecting patients with similar genetic characteristics and clinical findings. This approach revealed five distinct groups, each with its own underlying biological features.

The results suggest that patients who receive the same fatty liver disease diagnosis may actually have substantially different forms of the condition, with different risks of developing serious complications.

"What's emerging here is a way to systematically identify meaningful subgroups within complex disease," says Eric Klee, Ph.D., co-senior author and the Everett J. and Jane M. Hauck Midwest Associate Director of Research and Innovation. "It helps us map complex disease with such precision that we can begin to anticipate its course and intervene before the most serious damage occurs."

How Large-Scale DNA Research Made the Discovery Possible

The researchers relied on Mayo Clinic's Research Data Atlas, a system designed to connect patients' genetic information with detailed medical records. By bringing these large collections of data together, scientists can detect relationships that might be difficult to recognize when studying individual patients.

Dr. Klee helped develop the platform, which draws on resources including the Tapestry Study.

That project has produced Mayo Clinic's largest collection of exome data, involving more than 100,000 participants. An exome is the portion of a person's DNA that contains instructions for making proteins, which carry out many of the body's essential functions. Studying this genetic information can reveal inherited differences that influence how diseases begin and progress.

The combination of genetic data and medical histories allows researchers to investigate diseases in much greater detail, potentially uncovering important differences that conventional diagnostic methods cannot detect.

"This is exactly the kind of insight large-scale genomic research was built to deliver," says Konstantinos Lazaridis, M.D., the Carlson and Nelson Endowed Executive Director for the Center for Individualized Medicine who led the Tapestry Study and is a co-author of the research. "When you connect genetic data with detailed clinical information across large populations, you can start to redefine diseases in ways that directly impact patient care."

Surprising Links to Depression, Sleep Apnea, and Migraines

The findings also suggest that the differences between fatty liver disease subtypes extend beyond the liver itself.

For the first time, researchers identified associations between particular MASLD subtypes and conditions such as depression, sleep apnea, and migraine.

These connections suggest that the biological processes underlying certain forms of fatty liver disease may be linked to health problems affecting multiple parts of the body.

Although the findings do not establish that fatty liver disease directly causes these conditions, they highlight how closely liver health may be connected to other biological systems.

Could Personalized Treatments Change Fatty Liver Disease Care?

The discovery could eventually change how doctors assess and treat MASLD. Rather than approaching every patient as though they have the same disease, clinicians may be able to use genetic and clinical information to determine which subtype is present and which complications are most likely to develop.

Such an approach could be particularly valuable for identifying patients with genetic forms associated with severe liver damage, including those who appear to have relatively few conventional risk factors.

The research team now plans to evaluate its findings in larger and more diverse patient populations. Scientists also want to determine whether different subtypes respond differently to available treatments, including GLP-1 receptor agonists, a class of medications used to treat conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity.

If future studies confirm these differences, the findings could help move fatty liver disease care toward more precise screening, earlier intervention, and treatments tailored to each patient's underlying biology.

Salah may not quite be the explosive force of old who lit it up during a glittering nine-year stint at Anfield, but he is still a player of serious pedigree.

When he joined Trabzonspor a couple of months ago, thousands of fans mobbed him at the airport before making their way to the stadium for his unveiling.

In this fanatical hotbed of Turkish football, the Egyptian King reigned supreme against Hearts and was worshipped by an adoring public when he took his leave from the field midway through the second half.

For Hearts, it was in some ways quite a strange night. They did not play poorly. Without wishing to glorify defeat or patronise them, there were positives to be taken.

Mo Salah celebrates in muted fashion after handing Trabzonspor the lead from the penalty spot

The performance was vastly improved on what they delivered against Celtic last weekend when they suffered a 7-1 mauling in Glasgow.

There were fears that a similar trouncing in Trabzon could be on the cards for Wouter Vrancken’s side but they emerged with credit and respectability intact.

The main problem for Vrancken is now an ever-lengthening injury list. Harry Milne suffered a knee injury in training on Wednesday night and could be set for a lengthy spell on the sidelines.

Vrancken already had several key players out of action. Indeed, Hearts have enough players injured at the moment to fill a small hospital wing.

On the back of taking a hammering at Parkhead last weekend, the Belgian made four changes to his Hearts team for this trip to Turkey. It was a case of dealing with an injury crisis which seems to worsen by the week, whilst also preserving others such as Stephen Kingsley and Laurent Mendy who were carrying slight issues

Josh McPake and Oisin McEntee also dropped to the bench, while in came Christian Borchgrevink, Tomas Magnusson – effectively the only fit central midfielder – Rogers Mato and James Wilson in what was a depleted and makeshift XI in a lot of positions.

Especially in the case of Mato. Signed as a forward player who can play out wide, he was asked to play as an emergency left-back last night – and his direct opponent was Salah.

Former Liverpool star Salah was a constant menace for a Hearts side riddled by injury

Jordi Altena, normally a right-back, was also asked to play in central midfield alongside Magnusson. In a stadium famed for its noise and hostility, this always looked like a tough assignment, all the more so with a lot of square pegs in round holes.

But credit to Hearts. They started the match fairly well and did not appear to be suffering from any sort of hangover after such an appalling display last Sunday.

They should have taken the lead after only six minutes when Malachi Fagan-Walcott rose to meet a Calvin Miller corner. The Englishman leapt with power and authority, but his header came back off the post when he really ought to have scored.

Even so early in the match, it was a huge chance. Especially against this calibre of opposition, Hearts could ill afford to pass up gilt-edged opportunities.

Hearts were pressing the game far better than they did against Celtic. There was more cohesion to what they were doing, more intelligence in how they went about it. But they were undone by a clumsy foul by Magnusson on the half-hour when he tangled with Trabzonspor striker Franculino inside the box.

Belgian referee Lawrence Visser, who has some history with Vrancken back in their homeland, pointed to the spot and would also book the Hearts boss shortly after for some protestations.

Salah stepped up and calmly slotted the penalty straight down the middle past Hearts keeper Beau Reus. In the stands, it was clear that the Trabzonspor fans have a new hero.

The referee was proving to be fussy and card-happy, dishing out five bookings in total across the first half. There was also a very strong claim that Hearts should have had a penalty on the cusp of half-time.

Trabzonspor striker Franculino celebrates after scoring the second following a Salah one-two

Claudio Braga tangled with Trabzonspor right-back Melih Kabasakal and it did appear like there was contact, yet Braga did not make any great appeal for it to be given.

Already down to the bare bones in midfield and in defence, Hearts saw another body head for the treatment room when Borchgrevink pulled up with what appeared to be a hamstring strain, with Kingsley replacing him.

Trabzonspor went close to making it 2-0 when a clever dummy from Salah teed up Benjamin Bouchouari, but his shot was well saved by Reus.

For all Vrancken’s side trailed 1-0 at half-time, they were by no means out of the game and had enjoyed some really tidy spells of possession.

James Wilson linked well with Braga up front. Out wide, Miller also looked capable of causing problems. But any hopes of a fightback were scuppered ten minutes after the restart when the combination of Salah and Franculino sparked into life in devastating fashion.

They played a clever one-two on the edge of the box, with Salah’s lay-off allowing Franculino to steer a brilliant left-foot finish into the top corner past Reus.

It was a really classy finish from the Guinea-Bissau forward, who joined Trabzonspor for a fee of around £15million from Midtjylland at the start of the season.

It was now difficult to see a way back for Hearts. There was no denying the performance had been much-improved on what they delivered at Celtic Park. Yet, as the game approached the hour-mark, and for all their spells of possession, the reality was that Hearts hadn’t tested Trabzonspor goalkeeper Andre Onana anywhere near often enough.

The game fizzled out thereafter. Hearts looked leggy and like a team who are now reeling from the loss of so many key players to injuries.

Trabzonspor had Sidney Cabral sent off late on but the game was long since won at that point

Vrancken could ask no more from them. Especially on the back of what happened last weekend, this was a performance which could at least be passed as credible and competitive.

The Hearts boss did not have a huge amount of options on the bench to change things and was probably of a mind to try and save some legs, with a game away at Motherwell to come on Sunday.

Trabzonspor were reduced to ten men in stoppage-time when Sidny Cabral was sent off for a late challenge on Sabah Kerjota, but there was no time – or energy – left for Hearts to try and take advantage.

#985. Germany's women: Ena Mahmutovic makes her case to become Germany's next No.1 goalkeeper

Germany's win against Australia saw Ena Mahmutovic excel in goal. The 22-year-old Bayern Munich goalkeeper is considered the successor to Ann-Katrin Berger and is making a name for herself.

With determination, pace, and four beautifully crafted goals, Germany's women won its friendly against Australia, thrilling not only the fans but also head coach Christian Wück.

"You can see how fired up the girls are, how well they're already playing together, and that we have an incredible amount of talent on the field and on the bench," said a delighted Wück after his team's well-deserved 4-2 victory.

Since there were several notable absences, including captain Giulia Gwinn and top scorer Lea Schüller, others had to step up and fill the gaps. And they did so with flying colors. Melissa Kössler made a strong case for herself as a striker by scoring her first two international goals. Center back Camilla Küver and Sjoeke Nüsken were also on the scoresheet.

Much like it was with the men's team during Jürgen Klopp's first international break as Germany head coach, the question as to who will emerge as the next number one goalkeeper for the women was also a hot topic.

The difference is that unlike Manuel Neuer, who has retired from international football, Ann-Katrin Berger is sticking around for at least one more tournament cycle.

However, with just three matches to go before the start of the playoffs in the National Women's Soccer League (NWSL), where she plays for Gotham FC, Berger is giving these two Germany friendlies a miss.

This means 22-year-old Bayern Munich goalkeeper Ena Mahmutovic, who is seen as a potential successor to Berger, took her her chance.

"Ena deserves to play the next match," Wück said ahead of the game. "We must not forget that she already kept a clean sheet in Slovenia (Germany's last World Cup qualifier). And I hope she can repeat that tomorrow."

Some will draw parallels to Jonas Urbig, who many see as a future number one for Germany's men. Both he and Mahmutovic were born in 2003 in the western state of North Rhine-Westphalia, and both are seen as up-and-coming stars for their club and country.

The difference is that while Urbig remains an understudy to Neuer at Bayern, Mahmutovic is already the first-choice keeper at her club, having won the starting job over Maria Luisa Grohs prior to the start of the season.

For Germany, though, Mahmutovic still has to bide her time, while there are already strong indications that Klopp intends to make Urbig his number one.

"I'm obviously delighted," Mahmutovic said ahead of the games.

"It's always something special, of course – especially playing against a team from another continent. I think testing ourselves against them is very helpful as we look ahead to the World Cup."

Bayern director Bianca Rech recently described Mahmutovic as "the future of the national team," calling on Wück not only to give her a callup, but also regular playing time.

Wück still sees Berger as slightly better than Mahmutovic, but he is also full of praise for the young keeper.

"Ena's performances have been very good," he said. "She will get her playing time."

The match against Slovenia was just Mahmutovic's second appearances for Germany. Her first, which came almost two years ago, was memorable for the wrong reason. In the dying minutes of a friendly in December 2024, she lost control of the ball after a back pass, allowing an Italian forward to score the winner in a 2-1 defeat for Germany. Still, Mahmutovic was included in Germany's squad for the 2025 Euros.

Had it been up to her father – who is from Montenegro – Mahmutovic would never have become a goalkeeper.

"He still says I would have been better as an outfield player because I'm good with both feet," she said.

That's a quality that evidently wasn't lost on Thomas Gerstner, when her coach at her then-club Duisburg, gave her her Women's Bundesliga debut in May 2020. With the team coping with several absences, the then-16-year-old, who had been on the bench as backup goalkeeper, came on as an outfield player.

"The coach brought me on during stoppage time," Mahmutovic recalled. "I got to make two sprints – that was my debut."

That, however, was a one-off, and nobody questions Mahmutovic's strengths as a goalkeeper.

"She is one of the most talented goalkeepers in the world," said Bayern coach Jose Barcala, noting that she is the kind of player who can decide a match.

Mahmutovic will be hoping she gets many more opportunities to do just that for Germany.

#981. Archaeologists discover 1,200-year-old mummified hairless dog in Peru

Archaeologists working at an ancient burial complex in Peru have uncovered remarkable evidence of hairless dogs that lived more than 1,200 years ago, long before the rise of the Inca Empire. The discoveries include naturally mummified remains, unusual skeletal features, and clues about what the animals ate and how they were treated by the people around them.

Peruvian hairless dogs are a distinctive Indigenous breed known for their slender bodies, pointed ears, and lack of fur. They frequently appear in pottery created by ancient societies along the Andean coast. Today, a variation of the breed is recognized by the American Kennel Club as the Peruvian Inca Orchid. The dogs are also an important national symbol, having been officially designated part of Peru's cultural heritage in 2000.

A study published in the Journal of Anthropological Archaeology reports the first physical evidence of Peruvian hairless dogs at Castillo de Huarmey, the only known Wari Empire site discovered so far on Peru's northern coast.

Ancient Dog Remains Discovered at a Wari Empire Site

Located approximately 190 miles north of Lima, Castillo de Huarmey covers 110 acres and once served as an administrative and burial center for the Wari Empire. The complex was occupied between 600 and 1050 CE, roughly 400 years before the Inca rose to power.

The site has already produced several extraordinary archaeological discoveries. In 2012, researchers uncovered the first undisturbed elite Wari tomb, containing the remains of 58 high-status women, six human sacrifices, and approximately 1,300 artifacts. Another important discovery followed in 2022, when archaeologists identified nearby burials of elite craftspeople accompanied by gold, silver, and bronze tools. Together, these findings have helped researchers understand how the Wari Empire governed its territories.

The newly examined dog remains offer a different perspective on daily life at the site, particularly the complicated relationships between people and animals.

"Our findings indicate that humans and dogs coexisted at this Wari site, but reconstructing their bond is challenging, as past emotions are difficult to capture through archaeological methods," says first author Weronika Tomczyk, a research associate in Dartmouth's Department of Anthropology and a postdoctoral fellow in its Ecology, Evolution, Environment and Society Program.

"The goal of our study was to not only present data on Castillo de Huarmey's dog burials, but also emphasize that people in the past had often contradictory relationships with the animals, just as they do today: a pet for one group of people could at the same time be considered a pest by their closest neighbors."

Remarkably Preserved Dog Mummies Found in the Peruvian Desert

Castillo de Huarmey lies in an extremely dry region of Peru's Pacific coast. The desert's exceptionally arid conditions have helped preserve materials that would normally deteriorate over time, including hair, bones, clothing, leather, pigments, and decorated pottery.

Among the artifacts recovered from the site is an unusual ceramic vessel depicting a seated Peruvian hairless dog with humanlike features. The animal appears to be holding an object resembling a musical instrument.

Between 2010 and 2025, excavations also uncovered numerous remains of South American camelids, probably domesticated llamas and alpacas. Some may have been deposited as offerings during funerary ceremonies, while others appear to have been discarded as waste. Archaeologists also recovered the skeletal remains of dogs.

However, interpreting these discoveries has been complicated by extensive damage and looting. A powerful earthquake in 1970 caused part of Castillo de Huarmey to collapse, exposing burial chambers containing pottery and other valuable objects. Looters subsequently disturbed large sections of the complex.

The deeper archaeological layers remained untouched and retained their original Wari context. In contrast, the upper layers, where most of the animal remains were discovered, contained materials that had become mixed together.

During excavations in the northern section of the main ceremonial area, three sets of dog remains stood out.

One was a naturally mummified skull that still retained visible hairless skin and attached ears. The skull had also been colored with cinnabar, a bright red pigment commonly used in ancient Peruvian funerary traditions.

Researchers discovered a second, larger dog skull, along with the naturally mummified skeleton of a male dog buried in a shallow pit. The male skeleton was almost complete, missing only the lower portions of its front limbs.

At first, the researchers suspected that the mummified dog might have been buried relatively recently, perhaps around 50 years earlier during the period of looting. But Tomczyk noticed two important details: the animal was hairless, and all three dogs lacked their first premolars.

Missing Teeth Reveal the Dogs' Ancient Identity

The missing teeth provided an unexpected clue that helped researchers identify the animals.

"What I noticed is that some of the dog skeletons were missing teeth, and not that they lost them during their lifespan; some teeth, especially first premolars and sometimes last molars, had just never erupted," said Tomczyk. "And then I found in the literature that the same gene which is responsible for hairlessness in dogs is responsible for a reduced number of teeth, indicating that these were Peruvian hairless dogs."

In other words, the animals were not necessarily missing teeth because of injury, disease, or old age. Their reduced number of teeth was linked to the same genetic trait responsible for their hairlessness.

This distinctive biological connection offered evidence that the remains belonged to Peruvian hairless dogs rather than other ancient dog populations.

To investigate further, the researchers applied standard zooarchaeological techniques, which involve studying animal remains recovered from archaeological sites. These methods helped them estimate the ages of the dogs and examine their physical characteristics.

The team also analyzed chemical signatures preserved in the animals' bones and teeth. Known as isotopic analysis, this technique can reveal information about what an animal ate and drank, as well as where it may have lived.

The researchers examined isotopes of carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and strontium. These are different forms of chemical elements that can preserve clues about diet and environmental conditions. Strontium is particularly useful because its composition varies with local geology and can help scientists trace an animal's geographic origins or movements.

The results were compared with similar measurements from human and camelid remains recovered at Castillo de Huarmey.

"Isotopic analyses of a dog's tooth give a signal of its life as a puppy because dog teeth erupt so fast, and the bone gives a signal of more or less the last year of its life," said Tomczyk. "Such sampling strategy aimed to provide signals from distinct stages of a dog's life."

Ancient Puppies Buried Alongside Elite Members of Society

Altogether, the excavations produced 341 dog bone specimens representing at least 19 individual dogs, in addition to the complete mummified male dog.

Some of the remains were found in particularly intriguing burial contexts.

For example, the bones of a puppy estimated to have been just 6 to 8 weeks old were discovered alongside an elite craftsperson nicknamed the "Master Basketmaker." Other finds included the bones of an adult dog buried within the site's palace and part of a puppy skeleton buried with a male individual identified as XY, who was probably sacrificed to serve as a tomb guardian.

Most of the dogs were adults at the time of burial. However, their remains suggest that the animals did not all receive the same treatment.

Some appear to have been deliberately buried, potentially reflecting a special role in Wari society. Others were found among discarded materials. Only a small number of bones displayed butchery marks, suggesting that dogs were not commonly consumed as food.

Radiocarbon dating provided another important discovery. The complete mummified male dog was determined to be at least 1,200 years old, making it the oldest radiocarbon-dated specimen recovered from Castillo de Huarmey so far.

This result has important implications for understanding the site's chronology because it provides an additional dating reference for the ancient complex.

Ancient Hairless Dogs May Have Eaten Like Humans

The isotopic evidence also revealed surprising similarities between the diets of dogs and people living at Castillo de Huarmey.

Most of the dogs had consumed at least some maize, or corn, which was a major food source throughout the pre-Hispanic Andes. Their diets therefore overlapped with those of the human population.

An especially interesting pattern emerged among the dogs believed to have been hairless.

When these animals were puppies, their diets appeared more similar to those of human children. As they grew older, however, their food sources became more varied.

The findings raise the possibility that some puppies received particular attention or were raised under controlled conditions, potentially as part of an organized breeding practice.

"We do not know if the dogs were intentionally fed maize or if perhaps, they were just eating leftovers or trash," said Tomczyk. "Yet, the distinct dietary patterns of hairless puppies provide insights into what may have been organized breeding."

The researchers emphasize that the archaeological evidence cannot establish whether these dogs were cherished pets, working animals, or animals with other roles in Wari society.

Nevertheless, the differences in burial practices, physical characteristics, and dietary patterns suggest that the hairless dogs held a distinctive place within the community.

"While we will never know if any of the three dogs were pets or how people treated them, it is evident they received different treatment than other dogs," said Tomczyk.

The discoveries provide a rare glimpse into the lives of dogs and humans more than a millennium ago, showing that relationships with animals could be just as varied and complicated in ancient societies as they are today.

The study's co-authors include Miłosz Giersz, Wiesław Więckowski, and Roberto Pimentel Nita of the University of Warsaw, along with Claire Ebert of the University of Pittsburgh.

#973. Scientists find a youth protein that helps aging brain cells act young again

A protein associated with youth may help aging brain cells regain some of their lost abilities. Scientists at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai have discovered that TIMP2 plays an important role in keeping the brain's immune cells healthy, offering new clues about how the brain changes with age.

In a study published in Nature Communications, researchers found that removing TIMP2 caused brain immune cells, known as microglia, to develop characteristics linked to aging and neurodegenerative disease. When the scientists restored TIMP2 in the bloodstream of older mice, these cells became better at removing cellular debris and showed fewer molecular signs of inflammation and other potentially harmful changes.

The discovery suggests that proteins associated with youth may help preserve the brain's natural defenses as it ages. It also identifies TIMP2 as a potentially important factor in maintaining healthy immune activity in the brain.

Why the Brain's Immune Cells Become Less Effective With Age

Getting older is the greatest known risk factor for Alzheimer's disease and other neurodegenerative disorders. However, scientists still do not fully understand the biological processes that make the aging brain increasingly vulnerable to these conditions.

One important piece of the puzzle involves microglia, specialized immune cells that serve as the brain's maintenance and defense system. They remove cellular waste, help maintain the networks through which neurons communicate, and respond when brain tissue is injured.

As the brain ages, these cells can lose some of their ability to perform these essential tasks. They may also shift into states that promote inflammation and interfere with normal brain function.

"TIMP2 facilitates healthy function for the brain's immune cells," said Joseph M. Castellano, PhD, Associate Professor of Neuroscience at the Ronald M. Loeb Center for Alzheimer's Disease and The Friedman Brain Institute at The Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, and corresponding author of the study. "By supporting the ability of microglia to clear debris and limit maladaptive responses, TIMP2 may help restore aspects of microglial function that become compromised with age. Since our previous work identified TIMP2 as a regulator of synaptic plasticity through the extracellular matrix, these findings suggest that this factor sits at the intersection of several processes that are critical for normal brain function."

Synaptic plasticity refers to the brain's ability to strengthen or weaken connections between neurons, a process essential for learning and memory. The extracellular matrix is a network of molecules surrounding cells that helps support and regulate their activity.

To investigate how TIMP2 affects brain immune cells, the research team studied several groups of mice representing different biological conditions.

The study included Brittany Hemmer, PhD, the first author, who was a graduate student in Castellano's laboratory during the research.

Some of the mice were genetically modified to lack TIMP2 throughout their bodies. In others, the researchers removed the protein specifically from either microglia or neurons. Comparing these models allowed the team to examine how TIMP2 influences immune cell behavior in both healthy and aging brains.

The scientists used several advanced techniques to study the changes. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing allowed them to examine patterns of gene activity in individual cell nuclei. They also used detailed imaging, functional tests, and a technique called in vivo microdialysis, which collects and measures substances from the fluid surrounding cells in living brain tissue.

The results revealed striking changes when TIMP2 was absent.

Microglia developed several features typically associated with aging and brain injury. Their cellular activation markers changed, their ability to remove debris declined, and they showed molecular signatures linked to cellular senescence, a biological state associated with aging and cellular stress.

The effects extended beyond the immune cells themselves. Using in vivo microdialysis, the researchers also detected higher levels of proteins associated with inflammation and stress in the extracellular environment surrounding brain cells.

Together, these findings indicated that TIMP2 helps prevent microglia from shifting toward potentially damaging states.

Restoring a Youth-Associated Protein Improves Aging Brain Cells

The researchers next explored whether supplying TIMP2 could counteract some of the changes observed with age.

They administered TIMP2 through injections to older mice, allowing the protein to circulate throughout the body. The treatment produced encouraging results.

Microglia became more effective at clearing cellular debris and showed a reduction in molecular markers associated with inflammatory states. In other words, restoring the youth-associated protein helped the aging immune cells recover some of the functions that normally deteriorate over time.

These improvements suggest that TIMP2 influences how microglia respond to conditions within the brain. By supporting their protective activities and limiting potentially harmful responses, the protein may help maintain a healthier environment for neurons.

The results also point to a possible biological connection between factors circulating in the body that are associated with youth and the activity of the brain's innate immune system. This system provides a first line of defense against injury and other threats.

A Potential New Direction for Brain Aging and Alzheimer's Research

The discovery adds to growing scientific interest in whether biological factors associated with youth can influence the aging process.

Because microglia help maintain brain health and can contribute to harmful inflammation when their behavior changes, finding ways to preserve their normal function could have important implications for understanding age-related neurological disorders.

"While additional studies are needed, this work provides new insight into how youth-associated factors influence pathways involved in brain aging and age-related neurological disorders that may ultimately inform therapeutic strategies," Dr. Castellano added.

However, the researchers caution that these findings come from experiments in mice. It remains unknown whether TIMP2 produces similar effects in humans or whether manipulating the protein could eventually become an effective treatment.

Additional studies will be necessary to determine whether TIMP2 itself, or the biological pathways it controls, can be targeted to reduce harmful changes associated with brain aging.

For now, the findings provide a promising new explanation for how a protein linked to youth helps maintain the brain's immune defenses, and how restoring it may recover some functions that weaken with age.

#969. Pope lung nodule: Could it be cancer?

The case of Pope Leo shows how small lung nodules are often found during routine health checks. Quick treatment is generally advised before nodules can grow to become larger life-threatening masses.

It often takes a chest X-ray or CT scan to spot small lung (or pulmonary) nodules, especially when a patient is asymptomatic.

At less than 3 centimeters (1.1 inches), lung nodules are less likely to press against your airway and cause any chest pain or shortness of breath or wheezing.

In the case of Pope Leo XIV, who told a gathering at the Vatican on October 11, 2026, doctors had found a nodule on his lung, it is unclear exactly how large that nodule is.

All we know (at time of writing on October 12, 2026) is that it was "detected at a very early stage," and that it was "well localized."

That suggests it has not spread or metastasized, although its full status is not publicly known. Doctors advised the pontiff to have his lung nodule removed.

Whether his was a chance finding — or whether the pope had presented with symptoms — we do not know.

But "incidental findings of lung nodule(s) in asymptomatic individuals" is considered an "increasingly common clinical dilemma," according to a 2019 paper published in the Annals of Thoracic Medicine.

This "dilemma" rests in part on the fact that biopsies — removing a small sample of tissue from the spot or nodule to determine whether it's malignant (cancerous) — are difficult to perform on small nodules.

The risks involved in a surgical diagnosis are considered "excessive compared to the relatively low prevalence of malignancy in the small nodules."

That's according to a 2017 paper published in the European Respiratory Review. And that may also be the dilemma that Pope Leo's doctors have faced.

Fortunately many — if not most — early-stage lung nodules are benign. It is also possible to have more than one nodule at a time, and they can all be benign.

It's said the pope will need a week to recover after his operation to remove the lung nodule. He is due to travel to Uruguay, Argentina and Peru between November 6-17.

Lung nodules are generally defined as a white spot or lesion on the lung. They have a rounded or irregular shape.

Radiologists refer to their "opacity" because, compared to the surrounding air-filled lung tissue, lung nodules appear as white or gray spots on a CT scan — that is, they have a certain density.

Lung nodules can be caused by old lung infections, foreign particles that are inhaled and get lodged in the lung, inflammation, scar tissue and indeed the onset of cancer.

At more than 3 centimeters, the nodule is defined as a mass. The bigger it gets, the greater the chance that it will obstruct your airway and the lung's ability to function.

Symptoms may start with chest pain, shortness of breath and wheezing. They may then progress to a chronic cough or coughing up of blood, fatigue, hoarseness, loss of appetite, unexplained weight loss and recurring respiratory infections like bronchitis or pneumonia.

It is hard to determine when lung nodules become life-threatening cancer. But one indicator of a lung nodule's malignancy is its size.

At 2 milimeters or smaller, the risk of cancer is very small and remains so up to a nodule size of about 6-7 milimeters.

The risk of cancer becomes 9%-10% at about 8 to 10 milimeters. And at larger than 1 centimeter, the risk of cancer is about 15%.

The density of a lung nodule is also a vital indicator of malignancy.

There are two main categories of lung nodule density: solid and subsolid. Subsolid nodules are further divided into pure ground-glass (nonsolid) and part-solid nodules.

According to a 2026 review article published in the New England Journal of Medicine, subsolid nodules are less prevalent than solid nodules, but they have "a higher likelihood of being malignant."

#960. Two monster black holes could collide in just 100 years

Supermassive black holes are thought to exist at the centers of nearly all large galaxies. These enormous objects can weigh millions or even billions of times as much as the Sun, but scientists still do not fully understand how they grow to such extraordinary sizes. Pulling in (accreting) gas from their surroundings is one way they gain mass, although this process alone may be too slow to explain the largest examples. Merging with other massive black holes could provide the missing explanation.

Galaxy collisions are common throughout the universe, making it likely that their central black holes also eventually come together. As two galaxies merge, their black holes are expected to move closer, begin orbiting one another, and gradually spiral inward until they combine into a single object.

Despite this expectation, scientists still struggle to model the final stages of these mergers. Even more puzzling, astronomers have not reliably identified a pair of supermassive black holes orbiting extremely close together, despite abundant evidence of colliding galaxies.

An international research team led by Silke Britzen of the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy (MPIfR) in Bonn has now uncovered direct observational evidence suggesting that such a pair exists at the center of the galaxy Mrk 501. The findings have been accepted for publication in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society and are scheduled to appear in an upcoming issue.

Astronomers have long known that the central black hole in Mrk 501 produces an intense jet of particles that shoots into space at nearly the speed of light. These jets arise from the extreme conditions surrounding black holes, where powerful magnetic fields help accelerate material outward.

To investigate the system, researchers examined detailed radio observations collected at multiple frequencies on dozens of separate days over approximately 23 years. The extensive dataset revealed something remarkable: alongside the familiar jet was a second jet whose movement could also be tracked.

The observations provide what the researchers describe as the first direct imaging evidence of such a system at the center of a galaxy, strongly suggesting that a second supermassive black hole is present. The black holes themselves, however, remain too close together in the sky to be individually resolved.

"We searched for it for so long, and then it came as a complete surprise that we could not only see a second jet, but even track its movement," reports Silke Britzen.

Two Supermassive Black Holes in a Cosmic Dance

The previously known jet is aimed toward Earth, making it appear exceptionally bright and relatively easy to observe. The newly identified jet points in a different direction, which helps explain why it remained hidden for so long.

Over just a few weeks of observations, astronomers noticed dramatic changes in the second jet's position. It appeared to emerge from behind the larger black hole and sweep counterclockwise around it. The movement repeated, suggesting that the entire system was undergoing a regular orbital motion.

"Evaluating the data felt like being on a ship. The entire jet system is in motion. A system of two black holes can explain this: The orbital plane sways," explains Silke Britzen.

The researchers also identified an unusual event in June 2022, when radiation from the system appeared to form a ring. This phenomenon, known as an Einstein ring, occurs when gravity bends light in a way that creates a ringlike appearance.

One possible explanation is that the two black holes and Earth were briefly aligned in just the right way. In that configuration, the gravity of the foreground black hole could have acted like a lens, bending light from the second jet behind it. This effect, called gravitational lensing, offers another potential clue to the nature of the system.

Two Black Holes Could Merge in Just 100 Years

By examining changes in the jets and repeating patterns in their brightness, the team calculated that the two black holes appear to complete an orbit around each other approximately every 121 days.

Their estimated separation is just 250 to 540 times the distance between Earth and the Sun. Although that might sound enormous, it is remarkably small for objects with masses ranging from 100 million to a billion times that of our Sun.

Such a tight orbit could have dramatic consequences. Depending on the black holes' actual masses, their separation may shrink rapidly enough for them to merge in as little as 100 years.

That would be an exceptionally short countdown by astronomical standards, potentially giving researchers an extraordinary opportunity to investigate how supermassive black holes approach their final collision.

Gravitational Waves Could Reveal an Approaching Merger

Unfortunately, Mrk 501 is so distant that even today's most powerful instruments cannot distinguish the two black holes as separate objects. The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT), which produced the first images of black holes in 2019 and 2022, also lacks the resolution needed to capture this particular pair individually.

As a result, astronomers will not be able to directly watch the distance between the black holes decrease. However, another method could reveal their approach.

As massive objects orbit one another, they generate gravitational waves, tiny distortions in spacetime that travel outward through the universe. The proposed black hole pair in Mrk 501 should produce extremely low-frequency gravitational waves that could be detected using pulsar timing arrays (PTAs).

These arrays use the remarkably regular radio pulses emitted by rapidly rotating neutron stars, known as pulsars, as precise cosmic clocks. By measuring subtle changes in when those pulses arrive at Earth, scientists can search for the influence of passing gravitational waves.

Supermassive black hole binaries (SMBHBs) are already considered the leading explanation for the gravitational wave background, a persistent signal thought to arise from many distant sources across the universe. In 2023, the European Pulsar Timing Array and other research collaborations reported evidence of this background.

The discovery in Mrk 501 now makes the galaxy a particularly promising target for connecting a gravitational wave signal to an individual pair of supermassive black holes.

Such a detection could do more than confirm the system's existence. It might allow scientists to measure how the pair's orbit evolves and determine whether the black holes are approaching their final merger.

"If gravitational waves are detected, we may even see their frequency steadily rise as the two giants spiral toward collision, offering a rare chance to watch a supermassive black hole merger unfold," notes co-author Héctor Olivares.

Mistrust between the coalition partners has increased significantly, with SPD lawmakers speaking of an increasingly toxic political climate.

For months, the CDU and SPD have been grappling with low poll numbers, disappointing election results, and disputes over key reform projects. Plans for changes to the electricity tax, the introduction of a sugar tax, the pension and the labor market reform — hardly any initiative has been able to proceed without public controversy.

Adding to this is the crisis within the chancellor's party, the CDU, which saw a historic election defeat in the eastern state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania in September, when it even lost representation in a state parliament for the first time in history.

On Sunday and Monday, the party leadership met for a closed-door session to discuss the way forward.

They produced a four-page document which indicated a shift in priorities, putting more emphasis on social issues: affordable housing, lower electricity prices, better health care, and greater respect for citizens' lifetime achievements are mentioned in the paper. This is seen as a response to criticism that the CDU had been focusing too much on economic and regulatory issues.

The chancellor made an effort to emphasize his intention to continue with the current coalition government: "The personnel changes will not call into question the basis of the coalition agreement." He then repeated what he had recently said during a visit to Ukraine: "We need a stable federal government right now in order to remain capable of acting on the international stage."

Both CDU/CSU and SPD know that calling early elections could primarily benefit the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD), which is leading in the polls nationwide. So despite the deep rifts, Klingbeil and Merz currently see no alternative to their coalition.
