#5849. The 5 Best Snow Blowers for Digging Yourself Out This Winter

These tested snow blowers will help make quick work of clearing driveways and walkways this winter.

Winter is almost here, and for many of us, that means the chance of snow in the forecast is going up. Will you be ready for whatever weather is headed your way? Now's the perfect time to check out what you've got in the garage or shed and prepare for the months ahead. The Old Farmer's Almanac is predicting widespread wintry weather with dramatic swings.

If you’re tired of shoveling, need to replace an aging snow blower, or are looking for something bigger and better, a new snow blower could take some of the chill out of snow removal and might be the best way to manage the cold, snow-filled months ahead. Our recommendations run the gamut of sizes and include both battery- and gas-powered models. Read on to find the best machine to help you clear snow more quickly and efficiently.

We are gearing up for our 2026 snow blower testing, and the models below are several we’ll be evaluating. These represent the breadth of options available right now and are some of the most reliable and effective you can buy.

When we test snow blowers ahead of snowfall season, we use wet sawdust to evaluate how these machines perform. We get a load delivered to our office and mix it with water until we arrive at a snow-like consistency. When we first conducted this test, we weighed 1 cubic foot of our imitation snow and found it was exactly 21 pounds. Average wet snow weighs about 20 pounds per cubic foot, so we were in the right ballpark.

Once the test area is set up, we run the machines through the sawdust just like we would if we were blowing snow off our driveways. It’s important to note that wet sawdust, while it is similar enough for testing purposes, is not snow. It’s heavier than most snow and less slippery. So our test is a bit tougher on these machines than most of the snow you’ll encounter. We should also note that cordless models are more sensitive to the heavier material than the gas machines. We fully expect these machines to perform better when used in actual snow.

The steel-framed Power+ is very much like gas-powered units of its size, except for the electric motor and two 56-volt, 7.5-Ah batteries. Those two things give the Power+ some distinct advantages in the noise and weight departments, being both quieter and lighter than its gas-fueled counterparts. Using it won’t hurt your hearing or wake the neighbors, and you won’t be laboring to move it around in the garage to store.

Ego claims a run time long enough to clear an 18-car driveway—that’s an area about 130 by 12 feet—of 8 inches of snow. This may be true with light, dry powder, but keep in mind that the heavier and wetter the snow, the shorter the run time. Clearing our test area of sawdust—significantly heavier than all but the wettest snow—used up between 25 and 30 percent of the battery. It’s important to note that we operated the auger in turbo mode to throw the sawdust as far as possible, a distance of 31 feet, but it does have a variable-speed control. So you can adjust the auger as needed for the conditions and to preserve battery.

Using the Power+ is a breeze. With a differential in the axle and its relatively light weight, we found it very easy to maneuver. We like how freely the discharge chute moves through its 200-degree arc. But with a separate control for the deflector, it’s not as quick to adjust on the fly. Other features include four LEDs, adjustable plastic skid shoes that won’t mar garage floor coatings or walkway pavers, and adjustable handle height. The Power+ is one of very few cordless snow blowers with two stages, and overall, we were impressed with its capabilities. It threw wet sawdust well, which means it will have no problems in real snow.

Snow blowers are an investment, but whereas many two-stage snow blowers are priced over $1,000, this model from Champion Power Equipment lists at $899. Even at this lower price, it carries a number of features others have, including push-button electric start, a self-propelled drive system, remote chute control, and an LED headlight.

With a 24-inch width and a 20.5-inch height, it can handle deep snowfall, and the height is adjustable, so you can set it to handle gravel paths without damaging the blades. Plus, 180-degree turning wheels help make tighter turns. The 209-cc engine is slightly less powerful than some of the two-stage gas snow blowers we’ve reviewed, but that still should be enough to tackle deep snow banks.

Ryobi power tools do a good job of making things a bit more affordable, and this single-stage cordless snow blower is no exception. With a list price of $849, it’s a few hundred dollars less than comparable blowers, but it’s sized to handle moderate snowfall. It offers a clearing path of 21 inches wide and 11.5 inches high and can throw the snow up to 35 feet away.

This model comes with two 40-volt, 8-amp-hour batteries, which should keep things running for up to 2.5 hours, though that can decrease depending on how deep and wet the snow gets. The other benefit of this model is that you can use its batteries with any other Ryobi 40-volt power tools, making them useful for other work you have around the house in just about any season.

Ego claims its 21-inch Power+ snow blower can throw snow up to 40 feet with a 180-degree, adjustable chute, which is pretty far for a cordless machine. In the wet sawdust test, we were able to throw material 24 feet—longer distances are certainly achievable with lighter, drier snow. It took the Power+ 4 minutes 16 seconds to clear the sawdust down to the pavement in our area test.

Like most single-stage blowers, the Power+ is not self-propelled. In our dense, heavy sawdust, pushing the machine required some effort to make forward progress. It’s not a bad thing, though, as it makes it easier to prevent the machine from digging in too deeply and bogging down. In wet or ice-packed snow, we expect you’ll have to put in a similar effort.

The Power+ has a variable-speed auger, so we were able to adjust speed for the conditions and conserve battery when less power was needed. There isn’t too much to worry about in the battery department, since it comes with two 5-Ah batteries, enough to clear 8 inches of snow on a 12-car driveway—that’s six cars long by two cars wide.

The Power+ comes with a dual-port charger, so the two batteries can be charged simultaneously to get you ready for the next round faster. Ego rounds things out with front-mounted LEDs for low-light and dark conditions, plus an easy folding handle for stowing the unit when not in use.

This single-stage snow blower from Toro is well-suited for medium-sized properties that get guaranteed snowfall each year, but don’t present a ton of ground to cover—just a driveway and some sidewalks. Its 212-cc engine should cut through even wet snow and it can throw it up to 40 feet away. Plus, it’s self-propelled to help you push through the snow.

It also simplifies things with some smart controls like a trigger and slide that can change the direction of the discharge chute and push-button electric start. This blower is aimed at clearing snowfall up to 9 inches, so northern regions that see more severe snowstorms may want to opt for a two-stage model, but this model will work well if you only have a few moderate snow events each winter.

The big limiting factor for cordless battery-powered snow blowers is how long they’ll run. If the battery dies, there are two options: Put a second battery in, if you have one, or put it on the charger and wait. Battery options do have their advantages, though. They produce no fumes, emit very little noise, are simple to operate, and can be stored anywhere in your home. There is also very little maintenance required with them.

Large gas snow blowers can easily handle snow 20 inches deep or more and, with enough gas, run for hours on end. And if you do run out of gas, just add fuel and keep going. The downsides with gas models are that they're noisier, do produce fumes, and require routine engine maintenance.

The choice comes down to how much snow you typically get, and how much space you have to clear.

Much like the choice between gas or battery power, choosing a single- or two-stage machine has a lot to do with snow volume and the area to be cleared. Single-stage blowers are so called because they have one curved paddle that collects the snow and ejects it through the chute. This is the limiting factor in how far the snow can be thrown, as well as the volume that can pass through the machine.

Single-stage units are simple to operate, lightweight, easy to maneuver, and typically less expensive than two-stage machines. Two-stage units have a horizontal auger that collects snow and pushes it to a separate, rotating impeller that ejects it. Two-stage machines can throw snow 30 to 60 feet, which makes it easier to clear large areas without piling snow that you’ll just have to move again.

The main purpose of a drive system is to adjust and convert engine revolutions per minute to an appropriate speed to drive the machine forward in various conditions. There are two basic types of transmissions: friction disc and hydrostatic.

Friction-disc transmissions are simple, mechanical devices that employ a rubber-edged wheel that presses on the face of a large pulley. By repositioning the wheel on the face of the pulley, the machine will speed up or slow down—closer to the center for slower, closer to the outside edge for faster. Snow blowers with friction discs will often have six forward speeds and two reverse, controlled by a lever on the dashboard. And these machines typically have a live axle, which means both wheels are attached to a solid axle and will always spin at the same rate.

Hydrostatic transmissions pump hydraulic fluid through hydraulic motors, which convert the flow to mechanical rotation. On these machines, drive speed is independent of engine speed and there is no fixed number of “speeds.” The lever controlling speed can be positioned at any point in its range. “Hydro”-drive machines are capable of slower speeds than those with friction disc drives, which is good for very deep or heavy snow. Because of the complexity of hydrostatic transmissions, they can be significantly more expensive than friction-disc-drive units.

There are two features that help improve steering. The first uses triggers under each handle to control clutches that disengage the wheel on the corresponding side. The opposite wheel continues to drive, turning the machine in the direction of the stopped wheel. The other feature incorporates a differential gear on the axle, which allows the wheels to spin at different speeds.

Tracks provide greater grip and stability, which make them very good on slopes, loose surfaces like gravel, or areas that tend to get icy snowpack. They are often easier to keep going nice and straight. Wheels work well in most situations but can often slip on hills and small patches of ice. It takes more parts to manufacture a track drive than a wheeled drive, so tracked units tend to be more expensive. In the end, the decision comes down to budget and terrain.

Under no circumstances, reach into a running machine to clear snow jammed in the auger or chute. That's the biggest one. If you are using a gas-powered machine, we strongly suggest hearing protection be worn.

If the machine has pneumatic tires, be sure to pump them up to the proper pressure before using— low tire pressure can make them more difficult to maneuver. Also, for those machines with grease fittings on auger bearings or gearbox, be sure to lube each season with low temp grease.

Run the auger at the lowest possible speed that will clear the snow, and turn the auger off anytime you aren't actually blowing snow.

When temperatures spike, California redwoods slow down photosynthesis, new research has found. This finding has serious implications for the long-term survival of the iconic trees in a warming world, and their role in storing carbon from the atmosphere.

California redwood (Sequoia sempervirens) forests are among the largest aboveground carbon stores on the planet. These towering trees are native to central and northern California, and evolved to take advantage of the region's historically mild weather. In recent decades, their range has contracted significantly. But climate change is threatening the health and function of these forests.

The region has seen significant warming in recent decades, and faces more frequent extreme heat days, with temperatures spiking above 100 degrees Fahrenheit (37 degrees Celsius). Now, in a new study, published Sept. 22 in the journal JGR Biogeosciences, a team of researchers has shown that California's renowned redwoods are struggling to photosynthesize in the heat.

Photosynthesis is the process used by plants, algae and some bacteria to turn sunlight into energy. It chemically converts carbon dioxide and water into sugars and oxygen.

"These trees adapted to grow in really temperate environments, so no one had really looked at the impact of heat on photosynthesis," study first author Lily Klinek, a doctoral candidate in the Plant Optics Lab at the University of California, Davis, told Live Science.

From 2022 to 2024, Klinek and colleagues measured how the leaves of redwoods in Garcia River Forest in Mendocino County responded to the heat. Once or twice a month, the researchers took three different California redwood leaf samples and measured their fluorescence and temperature.

A plant's molecules can use absorbed light in three ways: for photosynthesis, dissipated as heat, or reflected as light (florescence). Fluorescence is a proxy measurement to understand photosynthesis in the plant, Klinek said.

The researchers wrapped a fourth leaf in tinfoil for 20 minutes to put it in a state of "rest" before taking the same measurements. "When they're in a state of rest, we expose them to a pulse of light that’s in the wavelength they would use for photosynthesis to see what would be their maximum photosynthetic rate," Klinek said.

They found that on hot days, the leaves exposed to sunlight were up to 18 degrees Fahrenheit (10 degrees Celsius) hotter than the air temperature. "That's a huge difference and indicates that the leaves are stressed to the point where they can't thermoregulate effectively," Klinek said.

Many plants' leaves manage their temperature and photosynthesis by opening or closing their stomata, which are tiny pores on their surface. These stomata (from the Greek word for mouth) regulate the amount of carbon dioxide entering the plant, and how much oxygen and water vapor it releases. Lower carbon dioxide intake leads to reduced photosynthesis, meaning the plant has less energy.

The large differences between air and redwood leaf temperatures suggest that the leaves had closed their stomata to avoid losing water from evaporation during hot conditions and consequently overheated, Klinek said.

To further investigate the impact of heat on leaf physiology, the researchers took California redwood leaf samples back to the laboratory and exposed them to a range of temperatures while they were in water. Despite their access to water, the leaves still photosynthesized less as they got hotter, even though their stomata were open, Klinek said.

This shows that heat imposes direct limitations on redwood photosynthetical capacity, rather than indirectly decreasing that capacity by causing stomatal closure, the researchers wrote. The finding indicates that redwoods' ability to photosynthesise and store carbon will decrease as temperatures rise.

​​"It is not only stomatal closure that is causing reduced photosynthesis," Lucy Rowland, an ecologist at the University of Exeter in the U.K. who was not involved in the study, told Live Science in an email. "These trees are already suffering from the direct effects of heat on their photosynthetic systems."

The finding has serious implications for the future of redwoods and similar plants. "The points at which this damage becomes irreversible remains really poorly understood, but is something which is really essential to understand if we want to predict how our future climate is going to impact plants," she said.

The next step in Klinek's research is to move beyond leaf physiology to understand these thresholds on the trees and their ecosystems as a whole.

"We're not sure to what extent the effect that we saw is a reversible stress response and if they are sustaining damage from this constant stress," she said. Researchers need more data to "really get a better sense, beyond the leaf scale, how these ecosystems are responding to heat and drought," she added.

#5844. Easy Homemade Cranberry Sauce

This easy cranberry sauce recipe has been a fixture on my family’s Thanksgiving table for years, served alongside my mother’s elaborate Indian-inspired holiday dishes. With just 10 minutes on the stove and a handful of ingredients—fresh or frozen cranberries, sugar, orange zest, and water—it delivers the bright, tangy flavor that cuts through all the richness of the holiday meal.

My mom started making cranberry sauce after tasting a homemade version at a friend’s house. Over time, she made it her own, dialing back the sugar for extra tang and adding orange zest for brightness. We’ve tried fancier versions with maple syrup, chiles, and warm spices, but always come back to this stripped-down sauce. Anything more dulls the sharp cranberry bite we love.

A splash of orange liqueur is entirely optional, and you can swap some or all of the white sugar for light brown sugar. Best of all, you can make the sauce up to five days ahead, leaving one less thing to think about on Thanksgiving Day.

Can I use frozen cranberries to make cranberry sauce?

Absolutely. Fresh and frozen cranberries both work, and there's no need to thaw frozen berries before cooking. They may take a little longer to burst and become jammy.

Yes. The liqueur adds another layer of citrus flavor, but it's entirely optional. Brandy-based Grand Marnier brings warm, vanilla-like notes, while Cointreau offers a cleaner, brighter orange flavor. You can also simply leave it out; the orange zest provides fruity brightness on its own.

Cranberries contain natural pectin, which helps the sauce thicken as it cools. If yours still seems thin once cooled, return it to a simmer and cook until it coats the back of a spoon and holds a line when you run your finger through it.

What can I do with leftover cranberry sauce?

Cook 1 (12-oz.) bag fresh or frozen cranberries, ¾ cup granulated sugar, 1–2 Tbsp. Grand Marnier, Cointreau, or other orange liqueur (if using), 1 Tbsp. finely grated orange zest, a pinch of kosher salt, and 1 cup water in a medium saucepan over medium heat, stirring often to prevent scorching, until cranberries burst and sauce becomes jammy and coats a spoon, about 10 minutes.Let cool before serving.

Do ahead: Cranberry sauce can be made 5 days ahead. Transfer to an airtight container; cover and refrigerate until well chilled.

#5843. Where Do Trends Come From Now?

A few years ago, during a fit of self-actualization, I got my ears pierced. I don’t have any tattoos, and I’ve never even dyed my hair, so the decision to pierce my ears felt both exciting and personally out of the ordinary. I still don’t quite understand why I did it. Men have been piercing their ears for centuries, but I suspect, as with so many things we guys also do, particularly where clothes and accessories are concerned, my decision had something to do with tiny hoops being trendy.

Trends are as old as civilization itself, but the speed at which they move in the age of TikTok and AI-driven algorithms has never been faster. Likewise, with a good portion of the global population on some kind of social media, a trend’s reach has never been farther. These changes—exemplified by my sudden, inexplicable desire for hoops—begged a couple of questions: Where do trends come from now? And how do we know when they’ve run their course? For answers, I turned to the people who study trends for a living.

Nick Paget, the senior menswear strategist at trend forecasting company WGSN, believes that “it will be increasingly hard to look back on this decade and say, ‘Ooh, it was all about this kind of styling, this look’ in the way that we used to be able to say about the ’90s, ’80s, ’70s or ’60s.” Nowadays, it’s like every era-based trend has been chucked into a blender and blitzed into oblivion. Institutional tastemakers, including high-fashion designers and magazines such as this one, seemingly have less say than ever.

To understand what consumers want and where they are headed, WGSN has its own methods of analyzing data. According to Paget, the company uses TikTok metrics to monitor various algorithms (a platform they aptly call “TikTok Dashboard”) to get a more holistic view of what users are interested in. “We neutralize the algorithm bias that someone might have if they just use their own account,” he explains. Via this mechanism, they can effectively watch a fashion trend be born on the runway and transform, grow, and die across social platforms.

Take, for example, the recent resurgence of skinny jeans. In this case, Paget casts a wide net about how we got there: “A darker aesthetic has come out of the geopolitical mood,” he says. “And then something happens like designers start talking about skinny jeans again, and we start thinking, well, could the Hedi Slimane kind of dark, grungy, but slightly glamorous rock star be relevant again?”

As any dogged menswear aficionado knows, slim silhouettes and skintight jeans defined Slimane’s tenure at Dior Homme in the early aughts. Slimane’s punky aesthetic, which helped define the decade’s look, is now a key reference in the indie sleaze revival trending on TikTok; their return is also a reaction to the big-pants trend of the last five years. Slimane’s skinny jeans gave way to over a decade of slim denim being embraced by the general population, but given the pace at which the app’s algorithms cycle through trends now, it seems unlikely to last nearly as long the second time around.

Ziad Ahmed, who is senior vice president of United Talent Agency’s marketing division, UTA Advisory, spends a lot of time thinking about Gen Z culture the pace of trends online. Brands and creators often hire Ahmed to help them connect with a younger audience; his recent work includes a Coach campaign that gave the 85-year-old fashion brand a boost from the likes of pop star Charli xcx and activist Malala Yousafzai. “It’s often the things that middle-school girls are rapturously consuming that [become trends],” Ahmed says. “And once everybody knows it, it may not be that niche trend-setting phenomenon anymore.”

Since Ahmed began working with creators and brands a handful of years ago, he has watched the ecosystem shift in favor of influencers as public personas. “A critical mass of young people are making revenue based on social media, and the algorithm is constantly changing, and we have no visibility into those changes,” he says. When algorithms change, whether on YouTube, Instagram, or TikTok, they directly shape the trends that appear in one’s feed, and they impact creators. The architecture of the algorithm, Ahmed suggests, has an enormous say on what we see or covet on any given day. The job of the influencer, then, is to interpret those trends and advise users on how to adopt them (including, but not limited to, by making relevant purchases via affiliate links) into their own lives.

But despite the mysterious hand of the algorithm, there are still some believers that actual human beings can influence a trend’s lifecycle. “The way we know that trends are changing is internal,” says Avery Trufelman, host of the fashion history podcast Articles of Interest. “I think it’s that feeling when you look at your closet, and you think, ‘I have nothing to wear,’ even though you’re looking at a bunch of clothes; it means you’ve changed, times have changed, the need for clothing has changed. That’s been the biggest thing about learning about how trends work. I really did always think it was this dictum on high from [the media] or influencers being like, ‘This is out. Everybody get rid of this and go buy the new thing.’ But I don’t think that’s how it works for most people. I think it really is this little voice in your head that’s like, ‘It’s time to go shopping.’”

The genesis of today’s disparate, granular, and highly self-referential trends—quiet luxury, gorpcore, dressing like JFK Jr.—may be harder to pinpoint than the trends of decades past, but Trufelman has a different theory. She tells me about Chronophoto, an online game that shows you an image and you have to guess what year it was taken. “You realize what you’re looking at to place people in their time [is] mostly the clothes. To me, this is just the most irrefutable proof to anyone who’s like, ‘I don’t care about fashion. I don’t care about trends.’ It’s like, this is how time marks you. This is how we place ourselves in time.”

She continues: “If you read some of the new trend guides from the 1960s, the first big heyday of vintage, they’re like, ‘There are no trends anymore. Everyone’s wearing things from other eras. Trends are over.’ But we can look back at a picture and be like, ‘Yeah, that’s from the ’60s.’”

The difference now is that trends no longer primarily flow from designers at the top and rebellious teens at the bottom, but from many angles, including artificial intelligence, and the whims of Big Tech. “The fashion world used to be able to dictate a lot more easily what came and went, and it’s lost control of that to a degree,” Paget says. “The way that high-fashion houses have to court influencers and entertainment stars either as ambassadors or by putting them front row at the shows, or both.” In that sense, “the relationship between those brands and magazines has radically changed.”

After a few years of wearing my tiny hoops, I’m beginning to think about taking them out for good—but I can’t help wondering if my waning enthusiasm for them is the product of the same forces that made me want them in the first place: a subtle yet undeniable shift in the zeitgeist driven by fashion designers, influencers, legacy media, and algorithms. Maybe I’ll just bleach my hair instead.

Meet Fifi’s Custom Wigs.

The French Quarter wig shop was founded in 1977 by Marcy Hesseling, eventually taken over by Thomas O’Donnell and his husband Neil Ard back in 2023. The couple had just relocated from New York City—where O’Donnell worked in theater, and Ard in fashion—and both saw opportunity as a natural next step for their combined careers.

“I'd taken a part-time job at Fifi's, just for fun,” says O’Donnell. “Then Marcy Willy Wonka-ed us. We bought the business with the intent of carrying the torch for the foreseeable future, and so far, so good!”

Today, Fifi’s has maintained its legacy as a must-visit shop located in the heart of the French Quarter. The shop’s creative director, Bridget Broyles, leads all of the wig designs, though the co-owners also make wigs there as well. “Fifi’s exists for fun,” says O’Donnell. “Walk in and you're surrounded by stacks of wigs in every color, headpieces and fascinators on every surface, and glitter in every nook and cranny. Nothing is quiet about it—and that’s the point.”

The store offers both ready-to-buy and custom-made wig options to fulfill any look or vision. “We have hundreds of wigs in every style, color, and length, piled high and ready to go out of the box,” says O’Donnell. “Anyone can walk in off the street and walk out looking like Dolly.”

The custom wigs are what the shop are known for best. Year-round, clients will walk in with a specific character they want to embody—and the shop will create the perfect wig to complete the look. The sky is the limit when it comes to creativity: They have fashioned wigs to evoke butterfly wings, disco balls, tropical gardens, and have even designed a Friday the 13th-themed slasher wig, too. “We love meeting people where they are, bringing their vision to life, and making them feel beautiful, and we work to make that experience accessible to everyone,” says O’Donnell. “They might have $60 to spend or $600.”

The design team can create a wig in just a few hours—or more involved styles may take several days or weeks. “I love any wig where I get to craft the elements that go into it, especially the ones with entire scenes on top,” says Broyles. “I've done pirate ships being taken down by tentacles, and the Popeyes Chicken wig, where I had to figure out how to make fried chicken. The demands, obviously, ramp up every Halloween season, when they are busier than ever. Well, almost busier than ever: It’s their second-busiest season after Mardi Gras. “We start taking custom orders in the summer, so by October, the back room is a full creative machine,” says O’Donnell. “Up front, you can feel the excitement build as Halloween gets closer: Every chair is filled, and people wait patiently for their turn at a new look. It's chaotic in the best way.”

This Halloween season alone, the custom orders have begun rolling in. Though the shop can never quite predict what customers will ask for, the team says there are some annual styles that always pop up. “The perennial favorites are always in demand: bloody prom queens, sexy Medusas, and darker takes on Marie Antoinette,” says O’Donnell.

But the busy Halloween rush doesn’t just end on November 1st. In New Orleans, a city that prides itself on a good costume, there is always demand for such creations. “After Halloween, we focus on Thanksgiving fascinators and holiday wigs,” says O’Donnell. “New Orleans has so many events, parades, and luncheons, plus a steady stream of bachelorette parties and private parties all year long. That's the life we've found here: In New Orleans, we will always find an excuse to wear a wig.”

Like their cephalopod cousins the octopuses, cuttlefish are masters of disguise.

As they hunt, these marine predators send waves of color across their skin. The rainbow display stops prey in their tracks, granting the cuttlefish just enough time to shoot out two specialized hunting tentacles at lightning speed and snatch them into range of their deadly beaks.

But can cuttlefish actually mesmerize prey with their colorful skin changes? Or is something fishier at play?

Experts told Live Science that cuttlefish are ingenious hunters, but that there's likely an even more mysterious process than hunter to prey hypnosis going down on the seafloor.

Cuttlefish are visual hunters, using their large, complex eyes to locate and track potential meals. In general, their strategy involves a slow, stealthy approach, said Bret Grasse, the manager of cephalopod operations at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Woods Hole Massachusetts.

"The cuttlefish will slowly approach the prey so as to not alert the unsuspecting shrimp, fish or crab," Grasse told Live Science in an email.

During their approach, cuttlefish often put on a showy display, producing moving bands across their body. These displays are produced by a combination of light-reflecting pigment cells known as iridophores, leucophores and chromatophores. These cell types contract and expand to produce specific colors and patterns, all within milliseconds.

Some may wonder whether this trick can mesmerize prey, but scientists don't think that's the case.

Hypnosis, defined as a mental state of increased relaxation and altered awareness, is thought to be a uniquely human psychological state. There is very little research on whether fish can be hypnotized, but a 2005 study found that pushing a steady stream of water through the snapper (Chrysophrys auratus) put them in an immobile state similar to being hypnotized. Another study looked at sedative hypnotic drugs like benzodiazepines and barbiturates and their effects on zebrafish, finding that the fish had molecular receptors for these drugs, which helped render them immobile. However, whether fish can be visually put into a state like hypnosis has yet to be directly studied.

And while some animals, such as chickens, can be induced into trance-like conditions of tonic immobility, an involuntary, temporary state of unresponsiveness that happens when humans or animals experience extreme trauma, it's unlikely that the cuttlefish's color changes "hypnotize" their prey, Connor Gibbons, the cephalopod facility manager at Columbia University told Live Science. Instead, some researchers believe that the color show serves as an ingenious disguise.

As waves of different colors ripple down the cuttlefish's body, the shape of the animal becomes less obvious to its prey, so they may not see the cuttlefish as a threat to flee from, Gibbons said.

"It's only part of something looming at them, which they're like, 'I don't know if you are a predator? Are you just passing waves? What are you?'" Gibbons said. "And then by that time, it's too late."

A cuttlefish uses its tentacles to capture a shrimp

Once they get close enough, the cuttlefish shoots their two long, specialized feeding tentacles from small hidden pouches near the animal's mouth at their prey.

"The closest thing I can compare it to is the tongue of a chameleon extending out to grab an unsuspecting insect," Grasse said. "Cuttlefish feeding tentacles will fire out in the blink of an eye before the prey can react and escape."

Then, the prey is pulled toward the cuttlefish's mouth, pierced by its sharp, chitinous beak and injected with venom. The use of the venom helps the cuttlefish reduce their energy usage when hunting "as the cuttlefish does not want to use unnecessary energy and struggle against the prey while it's trying to eat," Grasse said.

However, the notion that cuttlefish flash colors to hide their body shape is only one idea of why cuttlefish perform these strobing color displays. Other researchers, including Grasse, describe them as a way to distract their prey.

"Essentially, they are trying their best not to alarm the prey to their presence," he said. By breaking up the size of their bodies, cuttlefish can convince their prey they're not as large or alarming as they may seem.

Whether the light shows are for disguise or distraction still needs parsing out, Gibbons said, and scientists also need a better understanding of how the cuttlefish controls its deadly dermal display. "The jury is still out on exactly what is happening," he said.

#5832. Archaeologists Just Found the Lost Church From a Saint’s 1,600-Year-Old Letter.

The fourth-century church and bishop’s palace may explain how Venice rose from a dying city.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

Excavations that revealed a fourth-century C.E. church in the ancient city of Altino have finally substantiated a letter written by Saint Jerome in 396 C.E., and they help illustrate the rise of Venice in the wake of Altino’s demise.

Experts had long suspected that a church stood in the ancient Italian city of Altino, thanks to St. Jerome’s letter, but no physical evidence had ever supported the claim, despite ongoing work at the Altino Archaeological Park. The new discovery not only uncovered the rectangular church building but also exposed a bishop’s palace connected to it. Together, the two structures help tell the story of the population shift from Altino to Torcello, the launching point for the rise of Venice.

“For the first time, we can say with a very high degree of confidence where the church and the bishop’s palace of Altino stood,” Diego Calaon, excavation director with Ca’ Foscari University of Venice, said in a translated statement. “Written sources told us they must have existed. Now we have archaeological evidence to support this.”

Those written sources begin with Letter 60, addressed by St. Jerome to Heliodorus, the first bishop of Altino. In it, Jerome describes the site and the care the priest Nepotian lavished on the church, even documenting its altar, walls, floors, curtained doorways, and sacred vessels.

The newly excavated building matches that picture. Roughly 1,600 years old, the late antique church measured 100 feet long and 65 feet wide. Its hall does not align with the Roman street grid, instead facing east, and its lack of an apse fits traditional northern Adriatic architecture. Immediately to the east, excavations revealed the bishop’s palace: the foundations of what was likely a two-story porticoed structure built with stones reused from Roman roads. The residence featured internal walls and annexes, with the second story probably housing clergy and the ground floor hosting privileged burials.

That reuse of trachyte Roman road paving stones—laid upside down—is one of the site’s most telling details, because it establishes the bishop as a key figure in the city. University experts noted that Roman law protected roads as public infrastructure, so repurposing the stone suggests the bishop held considerable sway.

Elisa Possenti, excavation co-director from the University of Trento, said the finds open fresh perspectives. “Thanks to these discoveries, the city can now be considered alongside other major episcopal centers in late antique northeastern Italy,” she said. “What we could only imagine from descriptions in written sources is finally taking physical form. This will help us reassess the lives and roles of Altino’s earliest bishops, whose histories have largely been shrouded in legend.”

Work at the site continues, and the team hopes to uncover more of the church and the bishop’s palace, including the baptistry.

Pinpointing Altino’s early Christian heritage also helps illustrate the demographic movement to Torcello and the shift of the episcopal seat. Once believed to have been a sudden change, the move now appears to have been both gradual and deliberate. Excavations at Torcello show that the island served as Altino’s port, and an inscription in the baptistry there dates the church to 639 C.E. With Torcello on the rise, Altino became a major source of reusable building material, and that trade eventually paved the way for the move to Rialto and the growth of Venice.

“This analysis allows us to tell the story of the transition from Altino to Torcello and Venice in a new way,” Calaon said, “not as a sudden flight, but as a long, managed transformation in which the ancient city and its inhabitants actively shaped economic and political decisions. At the same time, the buildings and spaces of ancient Altino became a source of materials that helped create a new way of inhabiting the lagoon.”

That recycling may also explain the church’s disappearance: experts believe the Altino church and palace were lost to the stripping of stones and bricks for reuse in construction at Torcello and Venice.

Marianna Bressan, director of the National Archaeological Museums of Venice and the Lagoon, said the excavations add important information for understanding these ancient cities. “Research at Altino provides an opportunity to view the city’s history in a broader context,” Bressan said, “tracing the Venetian area’s transformation from antiquity to the formation of the lagoon as we know it today.”

Rockwell should also show up in best supporting actor, and if McDonagh’s confident direction, Carter Burwell’s swaggering score, the expert casting, majestic cinematography, zippy editing, and glorious costumes and sets are recognized as well, then Banshees’s tally of nine nods would easily be surpassed.

Olivia Wilde’s raucous sex comedy is a total delight, and despite its relatively modest scale—four actors, a single location, a $15 million budget—it’s an awards-season dark horse you shouldn’t count out. Alongside being in the mix for best picture, the helmer and star could herself score a one-two punch with both best-director and best-actress nominations. Meanwhile, Penélope Cruz should get into best supporting actress for her delicious and playful turn as the assured foil to our anxious live wire of a leading lady, and it’s not inconceivable that Edward Norton also squeezes into the best-supporting-actor line-up with his own zany work. (Seth Rogen, on the other hand, is running in the lead category and will almost certainly be shut out of a crowded best-actor race.) The prize this crowdpleasing Sundance breakout is most likely to go home with, though, is best adapted screenplay, for Rashida Jones and Will McCormack’s side-splitting reimagining of The People Upstairs, the 2020 Spanish film on which it’s based. Nods for editing, casting, and production design also feel possible, making this the all-round little film that could—the kind of movie Hollywood wants and sorely needs more of.

Having come out all the way back in March, Phil Lord and Christopher Miller’s sci-fi saga-turned-poignant buddy comedy is at risk of being forgotten. But having grossed almost $700 million and gained a committed fan following, this is a much less self-serious blockbuster that deserves to face off against The Odyssey. We will likely see it in best picture, though Ryan Gosling’s presence in best actor is a little up in the air. Single-handedly holding this movie together with his immense charisma and magnetism, as the school teacher who is dispatched on an impossible extraterrestrial mission, he’s more than earned it, but it’s true that the Academy generally favours more dramatic performances in this category. Still, expect this barnstorming hit to pop up in best adapted screenplay, plus a string of below-the-line races: best sound, production design, cinematography, and visual effects. And if Sandra Hüller comes through with a best supporting actress nomination for her deadpan, karaoke-singing bureaucrat? Well, then it might be an even stronger contender than we previously suspected.

When Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi’s Cannes sensation (the pair took the festival’s best-director prize earlier this year) secured Toronto Film Festival’s Audience Award, a crucial Oscars bellwether, it surged up the proverbial awards season leaderboard. A sumptuously rendered, time-jumping period piece about interconnected queer histories, anchored by a set of memorable performances—perhaps most notably from Spanish musician Guitarricadelafuente, in his acting debut—it currently seems poised to take the best-international-feature prize. But there could be recognition elsewhere, too, including in best picture, director, adapted screenplay, cinematography, casting, production design, and costume design. From Roma to All Quiet on the Western Front, Netflix has a long history of successfully campaigning for non-English language historical fables—but can this be the studio’s first movie to scoop the top prize? Only time will tell.

After Jesse Eisenberg’s quirky character study debuted at Telluride, the news came in that the best-actress race was all but over: Julianne Moore, as the New Jersey housewife who gets a new lease on life when she joins a community theater production, had apparently blown her competition out of the water with her greatest performance to date. Aside from that, the surreal musical comedy has proved a touch more divisive, though it could definitely also show up in best picture, original screenplay, original song (for the delightfully titled “This City May Have Chewed Me Up (But It Hasn’t Spit Me Out),” which will go head-to-head with Taylor Swift’s “I Knew It, I Knew You” from Toy Story 5), and best supporting actor for Paul Giamatti, as the megalomaniacal director who transforms our heroine’s life forever.

Once The Social Reckoning crashed out of the Oscar race, there was room for a tech-world thriller with terrifying contemporary resonance. Enter: Luca Guadagnino’s beleaguered, long-awaited portrait of OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, as embodied by a deeply unsettling Andrew Garfield. This film has had a very troubled road to release—its original distributor, Amazon, dropped it days after signing a $50 billion deal with OpenAI—but, thankfully, it has since been acquired by Neon. Rumors that this frantic hot potato was also to do with the project’s less-than-stellar quality were refuted almost as soon as it premiered at the New York Film Festival: it is, by all accounts, a bracing examination of our unstable, shape-shifting present, which is ready to take awards season by storm. Look out for it in best picture, original screenplay, score (Damon Albarn’s electronic soundscape is hair-raising), and best supporting actor, where previous two-time nominee Garfield may finally get his due. For those who fear for Hollywood’s future in the age of AI, this could be the movie to get behind.

The hottest ticket at Telluride was a secret screening of this head-spinning, jaw-dropping documentary (or is it?) from Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim, which follows Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes in the weeks ahead of her imprisonment. Reviews were rapturous—so much so that many pundits have predicted it to become the first non-fiction film ever to be nominated for best picture. It’s the galvanizing shot in the arm this awards season desperately needed.

Every awards season brings a late-breaking Timothée Chalamet vehicle. Last year it was Marty Supreme, and before that A Complete Unknown. This time, it’s the final installment of Denis Villeneuve’s mind-melting space opera. Dune netted 10 Oscar nods and six wins in 2022, while Dune: Part Two brought five nods and two wins in 2025, though many argued this was because voters wanted to wait for the billion-dollar franchise’s conclusion to honor it as a whole, à la The Lord of the Rings: The Return of the King. Now, they’ll get their chance, though this obviously depends on this last film living up to the hype. If it does, could it take best picture? Can Villeneuve, after four nominations for directing, writing, and producing, get the best-director Oscar that has always eluded him? And what about best adapted screenplay, casting, cinematography, production design, costumes, Make-up, visual effects, et al? Who knows, 2027 could be the year it walks away with (almost) everything.

It’s hard to know what will happen with Cristian Mungiu’s Palme d’Or-winning thriller, an early Oscar contender that has been floundering since its trip around the autumn film festivals, where American audiences have so far received it less warmly than their international peers. The tale of a Romanian family, conservative Christians who relocate to Norway and are looked on with suspicion by their close-knit new community, it’s spiky and confounding, morally murky and emotionally devastating—a brilliant, thought-provoking piece of work which is, nevertheless, a difficult film to really love and root for in an awards-season context. Even if this keeps it out of the best-picture line-up, it should, at the very least, show up in best international feature. Then there are the transformative performances—Sebastian Stan, a previous Oscar nominee for The Apprentice, disappears into the part of the prickly patriarch, but he’s unlikely to show up in best actor simply given how competitive that race is. It’s less clear when it comes to Renate Reinsve—as the clan’s troubled mother, she has the softer, more likeable role and performs it exquisitely. In the running for best actress earlier this year for Sentimental Value, she’s widely beloved and could be back in that category, though it’s far from guaranteed.

The main challenger to Wild Horse Nine’s John Malkovich in best actor is Andrew Scott, who is transcendent in Simon Stone’s Telluride-premiering Shakespearean melodrama, a deeply moving account of actor Ian Charleson’s stint playing Hamlet on the National Theatre stage while slowly succumbing to AIDS. (If Scott wins, he’d—unbelievably—be the first openly gay actor to secure this particular statuette, despite a number of straight men taking it home for playing queer characters.) The film around him never quite reaches the heights of his talents, but it’s possible that his work and the endless goodwill surrounding it propels the tearjerker to a best-picture nod, too.

Hats off to Inde Navarrette, who went from virtual unknown to one of Hollywood’s most sought-after rising stars via her fully committed, nightmare-inducing turn in Curry Barker’s horror mega hit, as the potential love interest who’s turned into an obsessive girlfriend by a magical One Wish Willow. In the best-actress race, she’s currently second only to The Debut’s Oscar-winning, five-time nominated Julianne Moore—no mean feat for a 25-year-old with only a handful of big-screen credits under her belt. But what about the movie more broadly? One of the highest-grossing releases of 2026 so far, it’s made a whopping $500 million from a less than $1 million budget and is a riotously entertaining example of the scrappy, inventive, culturally plugged-in future of cinema. Given the Academy’s continued resistance to this genre, it probably won’t get into best picture, but it’d be very, very exciting if it somehow did.

A heartwarming romp that tracks a 30-something party boy (a winning Jordan Firstman, also making his feature directorial debut here) who is suddenly forced to care for the son he never knew he had, this indie darling rocketed out of Cannes to almost universal acclaim and has continued to soar ever since. It could get lost amongst the noisy blockbusters and big prize winners, but will, I suspect, be a word-of-mouth favorite that many voters will fall head over heels for. It’s deserving of a best-original-screenplay nod, but a spot on the best-picture line-up isn’t entirely out of the question either.

#5830. The 15 Cookbooks We’re Most Looking Forward to This Fall

Fall cookbook season is upon us, and in advance of the holidays and cooler nights, that means big-name releases from restaurant-world heavyweights (ahem, Eric Ripert and Kwame Onwuachi) along with topic-based books delving into underrepresented cuisines (Basque, Taiwanese, and plant-based Ghanaian, to name a few) and even specific courses. There’s plenty to choose from this year, which is why we’re presenting a special supersize fall cookbook preview, with 15 of the titles we’re most looking forward to.

Ghanaian author Afia Amoako has amassed hundreds of thousands of followers on social media, where her account @eatwithafia highlights Ghanaian and West African cultural traditions and recipes through a plant-based lens. In her lush, colorful debut cookbook, Amoako builds a deeply researched archive of a cuisine that’s often passed down only orally, and we all benefit from her efforts: More than half of the recipes are traditional and unmodified, not modern interpretations, creating an essential record of Ghanaian foodways. Recipes include classics such as jollof rice and okra stew, plus “roadside snacks” like ofam (a sweet plantain-based snacking cake) and drinks including iced Moringa milk tea. (Avery, Aug. 18)

Pim Techamuanvivit is a legend. A former food blogger turned chef of several Michelin-starred Thai restaurants in San Francisco and Bangkok, she is uniquely poised to help guide home cooks interested in understanding the nuances of Thai cuisine. Teaming up with fellow food writing superstar Andrea Nguyen, Techamuanvivit created more than 100 new recipes covering an expansive range of Thai flavors and techniques, including chapters dedicated to “Grilled, Roasted, Simmered, and Steamed” dishes, along with “Stir-Fried and Deep Fried” delights, plus soups, curries, noodles, and more. (Ten Speed Press, Aug. 25)

Ali Stoner, aka the delightfully named @rovinghaggis on social media, is a Glasgow-born, Melbourne-based home cook and former Masterchef Australia contestant who tackles the flavors of her heritage in her debut cookbook (the title is Scottish slang for a simple, comforting meal). There are plenty of hearty breakfasts and handheld “savouries” (tattie scones and bacon pieces, “wee tongue sandwiches,” and chicken Balmoral sausage rolls), plus sweet puddings (try the Tipsy Laird trifle), soups, and breads. It’s refreshing to see modern Scottish cuisine given the spotlight, especially in such an approachable, home-cook-friendly fashion—with moody, evocative photos, to boot. (Mitchell Beazley, Sept. 10)

PBS host and Mexico City native Pati Jinich is back with a new cookbook (following 2021’s Treasures of the Mexican Table). This time she focuses on the cuisines of the borderlands between Mexico and the U.S. (or, as she prefers to call it, “la frontera”). What that translates to is thoughtfully researched dishes that go far beyond Tex-Mex, including skirt steak and shrimp hot pot, seafood fried rice with asparagus and mushrooms, and smashburger tacos with poblano peppers. Throughout, she intersperses personal stories of growing up between two cultures and challenges readers to think beyond the confines of a single place. (Harvest, Sept. 15)

Enrique Salmón is a Rarámuri (Tarahumara) scholar at Cal State University, East Bay, and an expert in the connection between Indigenous foodways and land-management practices and climate change. In his second cookbook, he explores the concept of “kincentricity,” an Indigenous worldview emphasizing the interconnectedness of all living beings and our responsibility to be good stewards of the planet we share. After a comprehensive introduction, Salmón dives into the history, harvesting, and cooking methods of more than 50 Indigenous ingredients, ranging from huckleberries to black walnuts to wild onions. The result is as much an ethnography as it is a cookbook, from an author with the lived experience to guide the newly kincentric-curious. (Rizzoli, Sept. 15)

In his second cookbook, Kwame Onwuachi, the Bronx-raised chef behind Tatiana in New York and Dōgon in Washington, D.C., promises personal recipes “about how I really eat” at any and all hours of the day. What that translates to is big, bold dishes that aren’t afraid to mash up cultures and cuisines, like Red Stew Chicken Parm and Drunken Noodles Alla Vodka. (“That’s not a thing! Well, it wasn’t a thing, but now it is,” he explains.) There are chapters dedicated to sandwiches, the “Holy Grail of the Bronx” (aka New York Chinese food), and “Goddamn Veggie Sides,” which should give you some indication of the verve and voice behind this raucous collection. (Knopf, Sept. 22)

Dona Murad is the Bahrain-born owner of New York’s beloved Librae, a self-described “third culture bakery” that draws equally from SWANA flavors and Danish technique. With recipes for pastries like rose and pistachio croissants, za’atar and labneh morning buns, and loomi and lemon curd babka, her debut book is aimed at home bakers who revel in a project, but Murad walks readers of all levels through techniques like lamination with a steady, gentle hand. And with step-by-step photos throughout (plus plenty of beginner-friendly bakes), you’ll be turning out pastries that inspire lines down the block, just like Librae, in no time. (Quadrille, Sept. 22)

In his ninth book, Eric Ripert, the celebrity chef and owner of New York’s fine-dining temple Le Bernadin, draws on his travels to Buddhist monasteries across Korea and Japan to put together 75-plus recipes designed to emphasize the joy and mindfulness that cooking with plants can bring. The recipes here hopscotch across Asian, European, and Latin cultures, in chapters introduced by “guiding principles” like gratitude, intention, and joy. That translates to recipes like Radish & Turnip Carpaccio With Yuzu Kosho Vinaigrette, Tamales Rojos, and Seared Tofu With Chili Crisp, among others. Through it all, the boundlessly enthusiastic Ripert encourages home cooks to consider cooking itself as a meditative act and to enjoy the process of nourishing ourselves in both body and spirit. (Random House, Oct. 6)

The crew behind the acclaimed Montreal wine bar Mon Lapin—namely co-chefs Marc-Olivier Frappier and Jessica Noël, along with sommelier Vanya Filipovic and coauthor Mireille Silcoff—team up for a cookbook that’s every bit as chic and vibey as the restaurant that inspired it. With chapters like “What Is a Wine Bar Anyway?” and “Every Vegetable Is a Summer Vegetable in Quebec,” their collective voice shines through, and recipes like White Asparagus Soldiers with Good Anchovies and Eggplant-and-Cantaloupe Ceviche belie a deep understanding of quality ingredients and elegant presentation. As to be expected, there’s plenty of wine writing throughout, making this a must for future dinner parties. (Phaidon, Oct. 7)

Award-winning New York-based pastry chef Caroline Schiff (formerly of Gage & Tollner) makes her cookbook debut with this bright, cheerful baking book, which encourages readers to consider making and eating some form of sweet treat as a daily ritual. Yes, there are a few advanced-level pastries (such as Hazelnut Roche Profiteroles with Glossy Chocolate Sauce), but most of the recipes are approachable and friendly, as outlined in chapters like “Bake Once, Snack All Week” (snacking cakes) and “Little Bakes Just for You (and Maybe a Friend)”. With a section dedicated entirely to doughs, toppings, and crumbs, Schiff equips bakers of all levels with the confidence they need to make dessert a regular part of their day. (Abrams, Oct. 13)

Ernesto’s chef Ryan Bartlow is American, but he spent years living and cooking at the three Michelin-starred Akelarre in San Sebastián, Spain. Despite his fine-dining bona fides, though, what Bartlow really fell for during his time abroad was the rustic, humble side of Basque cooking, codified, as he writes, “on farms, in fishing ports and cider houses.” In his handsome debut with coauthor Laurie Woolever, Bartlow champions recipes with simple ingredients, like a classic tortilla de patatas, callos de Madrileña (a smoky, spicy tripe stew), and a lush crème caramel, along with pintxos and cocktails. This is a book fully steeped in a sense of place, including helpful guides to the Basque language and Basque-Spanish drinking culture, and a travel guide to the region itself, with gorgeous photographs. (Abrams, Oct. 13)

Eric Sze, the chef behind 886 and Wenwen in New York, teams up with veteran food writer Elyse Inamine for a voicey, intimate, and delightfully neon-soaked exploration of the foods of Taiwan, where he was born and raised. The first half of the book focuses on Taiwanese dishes as they’re presented in Taiwan itself, while the second half is more experimental, covering the fusion-y (a term the chef embraces) recipes Sze has perfected cooking at his New York pop-ups and restaurants, such as Mochi Gnocchi with Chili Oil Pumpkin purée and Beef Noodle Soup Ragu). Shot colorfully on location, this is a book brimming with personality—and a sneakily impressive amount of research—on every page. (Hardie Grant, Oct. 20)

Siberia-born, London-based academic and food writer Alissa Timoshkina is the author of two previous cookbooks on Eastern European foodways, though she’s perhaps best known as the founder of #CookForUkraine, a global fundraising campaign supporting humanitarian relief. It’s fitting, then, that her latest title is focused on humble-yet-mighty soups and stews from across a borderless collection of Ukrainian, Jewish, Tatar, Hungarian, Polish, Slovak, and Roma traditions. Organized into “Soups & Broths,” “Stews & Grains,” “Dumplings & Pasta,” and “Sweet Comfort,” the chapters highlight the myriad ways in which foods eaten with a spoon have the power to soothe, surprise, and connect people across cultures and conflicts. (Quadrille, Oct. 20)

Fuchsia Dunlop, who graduated from culinary school in Sichuan Province, is one of the West’s foremost experts in Chinese cuisine, guiding home cooks with a knowledgeable and compassionate hand. The author of several renowned cookbooks on the topic, she’s back with a strikingly illustrated new volume, focused on the five taste sensations (sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and pungent) that a Chinese cook strives to balance harmoniously within a dish. Instead of a dogmatic approach to traditional dishes, she encourages cooks to apply Chinese seasonings and flavoring techniques to “whatever ingredients you have on hand,” assuring readers that “the ways of the Chinese kitchen travel well.” (W.W. Norton, Oct. 20)

#5829. Pretzel-Crusted Pork Chops

Pretzels and mustard are a match made in heaven, and this recipe brings those classic flavors to a satisfying weeknight dinner. The homemade creamy honey mustard sauce pulls double duty here, acting as both the coating and the sauce. #5826. Someone Hacked Tons of French Supermarket Refrigerators and Set Them to Defrost. It’s Clever—and Alarming.

The fridge heist exposed a hidden plan to turn grocery coolers into giant grid batteries.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

On Monday, October 12, bleary-eyed workers pulling the morning shift at hundreds of supermarkets across France were met with a shocking, foul-smelling surprise. Overnight, hackers compromised internet-connected controllers operating on software provided by the Danish company Danfoss, and set thousands of fridges to “defrost,” sending temperatures inside the units soaring to as high as 50 degrees Celsius (122°F). In some cases, the hack sparked small fires in the refrigerators.

“When I opened the freezers, there was no more cold — just hot air coming out,” Cyril Scheubel, a manager at an Intermarché supermarket outside of Dijon told NPR. “All of our computer data is very well-protected…these hackers hit the only vulnerable point of our stores.”

Ars Technica reports that all three controllers targeted in the attack—Danfoss (Denmark), Dixell (Italy), and Copeland (U.S)—contained critical vulnerabilities that allow hackers full system rights, as if they were administrators. Remarkably, the controllers sat on the public internet with no virtual private network (VPN) shielding remote access. The U.S.-based Copeland company also says the devices impacted were “a discontinued, end-of-life generation of controllers.”

Whatever the cybersecurity lapses, the result was likely millions of dollars’ worth of spoiled food, and the search is on for the culprits behind the attack. However, this strange story raises another question: Why were these refrigerators on the internet in the first place?

Travel to any supermarket in the U.S., and you’ll likely witness rows and rows of freezers and refrigerators continually chilling or freezing, well, pretty much everything—and those systems use a lot of energy in aggregate. In 2015, the Environmental Protection Agency’s National Center for Environmental Economics reported that refrigeration consumed 40 to 60 percent of the energy in a typical U.S. supermarket—enough, collectively, to account for up to three percent of all commercial building energy use in the country. Worse, the hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) inside them are potent greenhouse gases in their own right, more than doubling these systems’ climate impact.

Naturally, with that much electricity on the line, supermarkets want their refrigeration running as efficiently as possible. The key reason, at least in the case of France, is that internet controllers allowed technicians to track cooling levels without having to be physically at the store. However, researchers in recent years have explored even grander ambitions for connecting these refrigerators online.

In 2018, for example, a pilot project in northern Finland tested the concept of demand response, where supermarket fridges could be cut off from power for up to 30 minutes without damaging food or the systems themselves. In a way, these big cold boxes became a kind of battery that would chill food when demand was low and then disconnect from power when demand was high. Part of this project was in preparation for a renewable power system that might provide energy during the day but less energy at night. The researchers found that while regular grocery stores consumed 600 kWh/m2, energy-efficient stores topped out at only 240 kWh/m2, which amounted to roughly $200,000 in savings per year.

The following year, U.K. supermarket chain Tesco partnered with the University of Lincoln to test a similar system, timing the fridges’ defrost cycles to match power availability on the national grid. The appeal is that refrigeration accounts for more than 14 percent of electricity demand in the U.K. at any given moment, and roughly a third of a typical grocer’s energy costs. Tesco alone has some 112,000 refrigeration systems under central software control, so adjusting them in unison has a significant effect on the national grid.

Because the thermal mass of a loaded refrigerated case stores cold like a battery, it can safely coast without power for tens of minutes, allowing supermarkets to chill hard when energy is cheap and pause when it isn’t. However, using refrigeration as grid storage requires precise control to avoid two critical limitations. First, cutting power while a case recovers from its daily defrost cycle severely delays its return to temperature. Second, restarting thousands of compressors simultaneously can cause massive electrical surges on the grid.

Despite these challenges, supermarkets are actively grappling with how to lower their greenhouse gas emissions and electricity costs. A 2025 report by the Environmental Investigation Agency recommended that natural refrigerants can help keep things cool, save money, and avoid HFC pollution. According to the report, Carrefour, one of the chains hit by the hack, spent nearly $90 million on low-GWP refrigeration technology in 2022. The investment now returns roughly $30 million a year and cuts some 67,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide annually.

The connectivity that makes a supermarket’s freezers useful to the grid is also what allowed hackers to switch thousands of them to “defrost.” Advances in supermarket refrigeration could both prop up the grid and drastically cut energy use and greenhouse gas emissions. But the near-term challenge is simpler: keeping those fridges—and the internet-connected gadgets that control them—out of the wrong hands.

#5821. What Are Those Black Dots on Your Microwave For?

They're actually essential for the microwave to work properly.

For most of my adult life, I didn’t own a microwave. It wasn’t a deliberate stand against convenience; I just never got into the habit of using one. Leftovers went into a pan on the stove, frozen foods into the oven, and popcorn into my ancient air popper that looks like it came free with a 1978 station wagon. Even now that there’s a microwave in my kitchen, those are usually still my first moves.

I’ve found uses for it, though. I recently discovered that it’s surprisingly handy for caramelizing onions and making brie chips, and I’m certainly not dirtying a saucepan when I need a little melted butter for dipping artichokes. My microwave is slowly making a case for itself.

Which means I’m finally spending enough time in front of it to notice the door. More specifically, the field of tiny dots between me and whatever I’m heating. Turns out they’re actually doing one of the microwave’s most important jobs.

If you look closely, you’ll see that those dots are actually tiny holes in a metal screen inside the microwave door. That screen is there to keep microwave energy inside the oven while still letting you see your food.

The clever part is the size of those holes. They’re far too small for microwaves to pass through, so the energy stays contained inside. Light, however, can get through—which is why you can stand there watching your soup rotate without the door needing to be one solid piece of metal.

In other words, the dots aren’t really dots at all. They’re the little windows in a safety screen, and without them, keeping an eye on your favorite frozen dinner as it cooks would be considerably more difficult.

The dotted screen isn’t responsible for keeping microwaves contained all by itself. The interior is lined with metal, which keeps the microwave energy bouncing around where it can actually heat your food. The door is designed to seal that enclosure.

There are safeguards built in, too. Microwaves have safety switches that stop them from producing microwave energy when the door is opened—which is why opening the door instantly stops whatever is cooking, even if there’s still time left on the clock.

For a microwave that’s in good working order, there’s no reason to worry that those little holes are letting anything dangerous out along with the light. The FDA regulates how much microwave energy an appliance is allowed to give out, and the permitted amount is well below harmful levels.

A mysterious "guest star" recorded by medieval astronomers nearly 850 years ago has revealed new secrets after scientists obtained the clearest images yet of its explosive aftermath.

New observations of Pa 30, thought to be the leftovers of a historic supernova called SN 1181, show that its spectacular firework-like filaments are not smooth streams of material. Instead, they consist of chains of tiny gas knots.

SN 1181 is so called because astronomers in China and Japan recorded its brief appearance in the year 1181, when they saw it as a naked-eye object in the constellation Cassiopeia. Eight surviving texts date its appearance to between Aug. 4 and 6 of that year. The mysterious object remained visible for 185 days.

"It's rare to get this level of detail on a supernova we can date to a specific week in history," Timothy Cunningham, an assistant professor of astronomy and astrophysics at the University of Warwick who led an international team of researchers to study the object, said in a statement.

SN 1181 is one of only five supernovas in the Milky Way galaxy known to have been witnessed from Earth before the invention of the telescope. (Those optical instruments first appeared in the Netherlands in 1608.) Now, advanced ground-based telescopes can produce wonderfully detailed images of those historic remnants. This latest image is from the Gemini North telescope on Mauna Kea in Hawaii and reveals about 10 times more filamentary structures in Pa 30 than have been seen before. "These images give us the clearest picture yet of Pa 30's structure, and it's not what we expected," said Cunningham, whose team published their findings in The Astrophysical Journal. The knot-like structures may have resulted from variations in temperature or density in material surrounding the star as debris from the explosion expanded.

#5811. We’ve Been Pairing Salt and Pepper Together for Centuries—But Why?

This centuries-old partnership may have started with a French king.

Salt and pepper have been together for so long that the pairing barely registers as a culinary decision. They’re printed side by side in recipes, passed together at the table, and sold in matching sets ranging from handsome little mills to comically flamboyant ceramic chickens. At some point, the two became a package deal, and the arrangement has hardly been questioned since.

But from a culinary standpoint, it’s a curious partnership. Salt is essential, both to our bodies and to the way we experience flavor. Pepper, on the other hand, is a spice with a distinct flavor of its own. I salt nearly everything I cook, but I only use pepper when I actually want something to taste like pepper. The idea that the two should automatically accompany each other has never made much culinary sense to me.

And yet, of all the spices that have passed through European kitchens over the centuries, black pepper is the one that secured a permanent place beside the salt. So how did pepper beat cinnamon, saffron and every other spice to the table? The answer involves changing culinary trends and a king who was very accustomed to getting his way.

Like a surprising number of things involving elaborate meals and strong opinions, this story leads to 17th-century France.

Before then, European cooking—particularly the food of the wealthy—could be extraordinarily heavy on spices. Medieval French recipes regularly called for combinations of ginger, cinnamon, saffron and other imported spices. Pepper was around, but it wasn’t yet salt’s designated companion. Spices generally were added in the kitchen rather than at the table.

By the middle of the 17th century, French cooking was changing. Chefs began backing away from heavily spiced medieval dishes in favor of a lighter style meant to preserve the flavor of the main ingredient. François Pierre de La Varenne helped codify that change with his enormously influential 1651 cookbook, Le Cuisinier François. Imported spices largely gave way to fresh herbs, butter became increasingly important, and seasoning was used with considerably more restraint. Pepper was one of the notable spices that remained.

This is where King Louis XIV enters the story. The oft-repeated explanation is that the famously powerful French king preferred his food lightly seasoned, favoring salt and pepper over stronger combinations of spices. His court was incredibly influential, and French cuisine set the fashion elsewhere in Europe. In that telling, the Sun King’s preferences eventually became everyone else’s.

Of course, food history is rarely quite that tidy. Louis XIV may have helped give salt and pepper their place at the table, but the broader shift happening in French cooking made the pairing particularly well-suited to stay there.

And French culinary influence extended well beyond the royal court. Le Cuisinier François was translated into English within just a few years and went through dozens of editions, helping popularize the newer style of cooking beyond France. French culinary ideas would continue to shape cooking throughout Europe and, eventually, North America. A preference became a convention, and conventions are remarkably difficult to evict from the dinner table.

There was also a practical reason for the pairing to endure. Salt and pepper do two very different jobs. Salt isn’t simply another seasoning; it can suppress bitterness and change the way we perceive other flavors. Black pepper brings its own flavor—warm, aromatic and mildly pungent—without necessarily taking over the dish. Together, they fit neatly into the newer French philosophy of seasoning food without burying its original flavor.

The presentation changed, too. Salt had historically been served from open containers called salt cellars, in part because moisture made it prone to clumping. In the early 20th century, anti-caking agents made free-flowing salt practical, and salt shakers soon became commonplace. By then, salt and pepper had already spent centuries together. The arrival of the salt shaker simply gave an old partnership its now-familiar uniform: two little shakers, side by side. By the time those matching shakers appeared, pepper’s place beside salt was already secure.

Perhaps Louis XIV really did have something to do with it—stranger things have happened at Versailles. Either way, pepper survived a major change in French taste, then rode the enormous influence of French cooking straight onto tables across the Western world.

#5809. What Goes Into Making Real-Deal Prosciutto di Parma PDO

There are precious few food products that have been enjoyed for as much of human history as Prosciutto di Parma. The signature silky texture and buttery, sweet-savory flavor of the “King of Hams” is recognized around the world as the result of carefully controlled production methods, dedication to quality, and traditions continued for centuries.

The history of Prosciutto di Parma reaches back more than 2,000 years, to Roman times, when the art of preserving pork through salting and natural drying was already well established in the Po Valley. As early as the 3rd century BC, Cato the Elder described methods for curing pork legs with salt and allowing them to dry and mature. Today, Prosciutto di Parma is produced exclusively in a strictly defined area of northern Italy, in the province of Parma in the Emilia-Romagna region, where local know-how combines with distinctive climatic conditions. Humid breezes from the Tyrrhenian Sea become drier as they flow over the Apennines and down onto the hills. Prosciutto producers discovered that this created an ideal environment for dry aging, and they built storerooms with tall windows that could be opened and closed as necessary, allowing the air to circulate through the racks of hams for gradual drying.

The specifics of aging, like every step in the process, are governed by a consortium—the Consorzio del Prosciutto di Parma—that was founded in 1963 to protect the authenticity of the product. In 1996, Prosciutto di Parma obtained the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO). These have a red and yellow seal to show that a product is made in a certain geographical area using time-honored, traditional methods. Grana Padano, a nutty, aged hard cheese also from Northern Italy that’s often paired with Prosciutto di Parma on a charcuterie plate, was awarded a PDO the same year.

Grana Padano PDO and Prosciutto di Parma PDO also share the distinction of being fire-branded—a sign for consumers that you are buying the real thing. Only Parma hams that meet the PDO’s specifications can receive the five-pronged Ducal Crown that signifies a true Prosciutto di Parma.

Well before that brand is applied, production starts with the pigs. Which breeds, where they can be bred and raised (in 11 Italian regions), and what they are fed (a list that also includes whey from local cheesemakers) are all precisely detailed. Within the 28th day from birth, the piglets receive a breeder tattoo that will also appear on the final product along with the crown brand. The fresh pork (hind legs only) sent to prosciutto producers must weigh between 26 pounds and 40 pounds, with a set amount of fat coverage. After they arrive at the production facility, the legs are trimmed to form the characteristic rounded shape, chilled to a precise temperature, and salted by hand with sea salt, which joins the pork as the only two ingredients that go into the making of Prosciutto di Parma PDO.

Following a resting period of at least 90 days, the hams are washed then hung in windowed storerooms to dry slowly. The next step is called sugnatura: The parts of the ham not covered by rind are rubbed with sugna—a mixture of pork fat, salt, and pepper—to keep the meat from drying too quickly. For final aging, the hams hang in cool, dimly lit cellars. From start to finish, the process cannot last less than 14 months (many producers continue aging for 24, 30, or even 36 months and beyond). The last step before inspection may be the most fascinating: The hams are tested using a needle made from horse bone, selected because the bone is able to absorb the aroma and quickly shed it. By inserting the needle into different parts of the cured meat and smelling it, experts can determine if a whole ham leg has the qualifications to be named Prosciutto di Parma PDO.

The Consorzio, which started with 23 producers, now numbers 125, and in 2025 they turned out 6.75 million Prosciutto di Parma PDO hams. Beyond its economic significance to the region, the “King of Hams” is a cultural icon and a tourism magnet. The annual Parma Ham Festival is held in September, and there’s even an entire museum dedicated to the region’s signature product—with a tasting room, of course.

However you experience the exceptional flavor and texture of Prosciutto di Parma—on a visit to the museum or from your local specialty store—look for the crown and PDO seal, and take a moment to remember that you are tasting a distinctly delicious slice of history.

When millions of people died in Europe during the Black Death, biodiversity suffered too, new research suggests. The findings suggest that human activities once promoted biodiversity in the landscape — and experts say we could use these findings to boost biodiversity in our modern agricultural landscapes.

The 14th century was a tumultuous time for the people of Europe. Persistent cold, wet weather at the beginning of the century led to widespread agricultural failures and unprecedented levels of starvation in what became known as the Great Famine from 1315 to 1317.

This was followed by the Black Death, an epidemic of bubonic plague caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis that killed more than a third of Europe's population. As a result of the widespread deaths and resultant social upheaval, large swaths of agricultural land were abandoned. Population levels didn't begin to recover until the 16th century.

The Black Death era is the perfect opportunity to look at the effects of humans on biodiversity over a longer period, Jonathan Gordon, a paleoecologist at the Leverhulme Centre for Anthropocene Biodiversity at the University of York and first author of the paper, told Live Science.

In the study, which was published March 4 in the journal Ecology Letters, the researchers compared more than 100 European pollen records that included about 4,600 samples. The records came from sites ranging from the lowlands of France and uplands of the southwest United Kingdom to the Swiss Alps.

The pollen records "give us information on the past diversity of the plant records," Gordon said.

For example, cereal grains ‪—‬ such as wheat, barley and rye ‪—‬ were a fundamental part of medieval agriculture. The representation of pollen from these types of crops in the pollen record points to historic land use.

The researchers divided the pollen data into three categories based on the presence of cereal pollen: places where agricultural production went up, places where it went down, and places where there was no evidence of cereal cultivation.

"We found a decline in biodiversity overall, but the decline overall was driven by those landscapes that declined in agricultural production," Gordon said.

"In those landscapes where agricultural production went down, there was a reduction in biodiversity of around 40%. Then, after about 150 years, as populations started recovering and landscape disturbances started increasing again, biodiversity went up to slightly above the level it was before."

Many people think there was a "stable and pristine world in which the entry of people has only one result: net loss of species and homogenization," Erle Ellis, an agroecologist at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County who was not involved in the research, told Live Science in an email. "The paleoecologists have always known the story is far more complicated."

There is a growing body of evidence showing that people's long-term cultural shaping of their landscapes often creates more diverse habitats, known as "mosaics," that can sustain more species than simple homogenous landscapes can, he said.

Whereas modern agriculture focuses on single crops on plots of land, in previous centuries, "you had all these different types of agriculture operating within the same landscape," Gordon said.

"In that landscape, you had lots of natural features that remained from the Middle or Early Holocene (which began after the last Ice Age about 12,000 years ago)," he added. "You had old bits of woodland, you had heaths, you had ponds, and bits of secondary forest. It was all interlaced with these agricultural mosaics, and that led to raised biodiversity because there were more habitats for different plant species to exploit."

Modern farming often uses monoculture, where one crop species is grown over a large area. This lowers biodiversity in the area.

The findings highlight the differences between medieval and modern land use practices — and their consequences for biodiversity. Research suggests that during the 20th century, global biodiversity declined by up to as much as 11% and human activities‬, such as land-use changes, pollution, and climate change‬, are the major culprits. In agriculture, the practice of monoculture — where a single crop species is grown over a large area — reduces biodiversity.

Functional diversity "is super important for how ecosystems adapt to changes in climate and how resilient they are, which is something we need for future-proofing our agricultural system," Gordon said.

#5803. Can ‘Sleep Banking’ Boost Your Productivity and Performance?

Using the weekend to lie in and catch up on sleep is a way of life for anyone with demanding work or social lives. But could consciously ‘banking’ extra hours of slumber over multiple nights help to counteract the effects of an upcoming period where you predict you might get less-than-stellar shuteye? The concept of ‘sleep banking’—which was first studied in 2009 as a method to help US soldiers improve alertness before missions—has been recommended by wellness leaders like neuroscientist Dr. Matthew Walker, who describes sleep banking as a ‘safety net’ when you know you’re going to be burning the candle at both ends. Longevity specialist Dr. Vassily Eliopoulos says it can aid resilience and recovery, and physical therapist Jason Silvernail recommends it for preserving mental performance when you’re jetlagged. But some researchers argue that sleep functions more like a credit card than a piggy bank—it can’t be saved up for later. We asked health experts to settle the debate once and for all.

Everyone has experienced the misery of slogging through a day at work after a bad night’s sleep (or several in a row), trying desperately to stop your eyelids dropping during a particularly boring meeting or keep your head from lolling onto your laptop. Not only can a sleep deficit leave you feeling exhausted and irritable, it’s also detrimental to your health.

“In addition to performance and alertness, sleep impacts your metabolic functions and cardiovascular health,” says Elizabeth Klerman, professor of neurology at Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School. “It may be that you only need, for example, six hours of sleep to feel rested, but you might need seven and a half hours of sleep for metabolism.” Sleep deprivation is associated with a range of cognitive disorders, says Dr. Valentin Dragoi, neuroscientist at Houston Methodist and Rice University. “In the long run, it could cause Alzheimer’s and dementia, because metabolic waste that accumulates in the brain is eliminated during sleep.”

Unfortunately, the average person doesn’t always register when they’ve racked up a lot of sleep debt, says psychologist and neuroscientist Dr. Lindsay Browning, founder of Trouble Sleeping. “There was a study in 2003 where participants were given two weeks of restricted sleep, but that didn't increase how sleepy they felt, even though they were continually sleep-deprived.”

The term sleep banking was coined in 2009 with a two-week study carried out at the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research. The experiment split 24 adults into two groups. During week one, the first group slept for their usual seven hours a night, while the second extended their time in bed to 10 hours, allowing them to bank extra sleep. During week two, both groups were only allowed to sleep for three hours a night. The results showed that the sleep banking group had better concentration and reaction times on performance tests during the sleep deprivation week, even though they didn’t feel less tired, according to self-report measures.

The theory has since been tested in a variety of other settings. A 2023 experiment found sleep banking for 30 to 60 minutes per night for a week improved performance effectiveness among doctors working night shifts at a Miami hospital, and a 2026 review of 12 studies found that ‘preemptive sleep extension’ improved alertness, reaction times, and mood.

While studies may indicate improvements in physical and cognitive performance following sleep banking, debate exists around whether participants are actually banking sleep, or simply ‘paying off’ existing debt.

“There's no such thing as sleep banking,” says Klerman, who coauthored a paper refuting the claims of the initial 2009 study. “There's no evidence that you can bank sleep when you don't need it. All the studies that claim sleep banking is happening are actually restoring a sleep deficit, like paying off a credit card, not putting money into a savings bank.”

Browning believes there are benefits to intentionally increasing the amount of sleep you get ahead of a time when you need to be at peak performance. “A study of basketball players found that deliberately extending their sleep over a long period—five to seven weeks—was associated with better sprint times and accuracy in their shooting, and improved mood as well.” However, this study didn’t include a period of sleep deprivation, so it can’t technically be placed in the category of sleep banking. Besides, Klerman points out, going to bed earlier doesn’t automatically lead to better sleep. “Going to sleep an hour or so earlier than you usually do is difficult to do because your circadian system decides when you're able to sleep and when you're awake and your circadian system paradoxically promotes wakefulness until a few hours before your usual bedtime to enable you to remain awake for 16 hours.”

Sleep banking is more worthwhile for people who work nights, says sleep medicine and pulmonary physician Dr. Saema Tahir. “I look at sleep banking as a refresher for people who need a boost in alertness and functioning while they’re off of their normal circadian rhythm.” She believes that neither the credit card nor piggy bank analogy is accurate. “You can never really pay back that debt. Can you reset your sleep by starting from scratch and adhering to a schedule and prioritizing it? Yes. But once it's gone, it's gone.”

While experts may disagree about whether sleep functions as a credit or debit system, they’re on the same page about one thing: consistently getting enough sleep is far better for your performance, productivity and mood than hatching any kind of catch-up scheme.

“People should not build up a sleep deficit in the hopes of later paying it back. They should instead obtain sufficient sleep now,” says Klerman, adding that if you have to use an alarm clock instead of waking up naturally, you’re already sleep-deprived.

According to National Sleep Foundation guidelines, adults aged 18 to 64 need seven to nine hours of sleep a night, but requirements can vary greatly between individuals. “If you're sleeping more than nine or 10 hours regularly, your bed partner says you snore or stop breathing or do a lot of kicking in bed, or you still feel tired after nine or 10 hours, you should probably see a sleep specialist,” Klerman says.

Who on earth, you might be wondering, has time to catch nine hours of Z’s? With the pressures of work, social and family commitments, not to mention sleep disruptors like pets, kids, and smartphones, getting to bed and staying asleep is a challenge at the best of times. If you work from home, or are lucky enough to have a place to snooze at work, napping could be a better way to add to your daily sleep total, experts say—with some caveats.

“I often recommend to athletes, or even people who are competing not on a professional level, that short naps earlier in the day are very good for alertness and improving functioning,” says Dr. Tahir. “Never past 3 p.m., and always less than 30 minutes, because the longer they are, the more you risk getting into a deep stage of sleep that's harder to wake up from.” The only exception is if you struggle to fall asleep at night, as napping during the day can reduce the ‘sleep pressure’ needed to drift off later.

“Even a six-minute nap will improve your performance immediately after, and there's evidence that if you close your eyes and try to relax and empty your thoughts, that is also beneficial,” says Dr. Dragoi. So in lieu of sleep, adding a nap into your schedule could be the energy boost you need to feel rested and refreshed.
