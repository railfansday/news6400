#4898. Tesla shares fall more than 3% despite earnings beat and $1.5B in free cash flow

Shares of Tesla Inc. fell more than 3% in after-hours trading today after the electric car maker beat earnings expectations for its fiscal third quarter on revenue that came in a little under forecasts.

Early after-hours gains, which followed a 3.4% rise in the regular session, reversed once the earnings call got underway.

For the quarter ended on Sept. 30, Tesla reported adjusted earnings of 48 cents per share, down from 50 cents a year earlier, on revenue of $28.05 billion, flat year-over-year. Analysts were expecting 45 cents per share on revenue of $28.34 billion, according to the consensus Tesla compiled from 23 sell-side analysts.

Those same analysts had Tesla burning through about $4.5 billion of free cash in the quarter. The company generated $1.53 billion, largely because capital spending of $4.73 billion came in some $3 billion below the consensus estimate. Cash and investments ended September at $45.4 billion.

Operating income fell 56%, to $718 million, as operating expenses rose 39%. Most of the increase came from research and development, where spending reached $2.76 billion against $1.63 billion a year earlier. Gross margin improved to 19.6% from 18%. On an unadjusted basis, net income attributable to common stockholders dropped 43% to $787 million.

Automotive revenue slipped 3%, to $20.56 billion, with regulatory credits contributing $113 million against $417 million a year ago. Deliveries, which Tesla disclosed on Oct. 2, came to 486,532 vehicles, down 2%.

Battery supply was still capping vehicle output, Tesla said in its shareholder update, and the quarter closed with the largest passenger vehicle backlog in four years. Chief Executive Elon Musk told analysts that demand is “exceeding our production by a significant margin.”

Energy storage deployments rose 10%, to 13.7 gigawatt-hours, in a quarter that also saw a new Megafactory in Texas begin production, with planned capacity of 50 gigawatt-hours a year. Revenue from the segment still fell 16%, to $2.88 billion. Musk said Tesla’s Megapack grid-scale battery systems, which it sells to utilities and data center operators, are “almost sold out” for 2027.

Active subscriptions to Full Self-Driving (Supervised) reached 1.74 million, up 67% from a year earlier, and more than three in four new vehicles delivered in North America now come with it. Tesla’s purpose-built Cybercab robotaxis have been carrying passengers in Austin since Sept. 3. Musk said the fleet there has grown tenfold in that time, and Tesla plans to take the robotaxi service to Dallas next month.

Production of Optimus humanoid robots has started on high-volume lines in Fremont, where Tesla said in January it would end Model S and Model X production to make room for the robot. Getting to 20,000 units a week will take “at least a year, maybe a little longer,” according to Musk. He expects to start delivering Optimus to companies outside Tesla around the end of next year.

Tesla cut its capital spending forecast for 2026 to about $20 billion, from more than $25 billion. Chief Financial Officer Vaibhav Taneja said the plan is to fund it “primarily from our cash flows from operations.” Tesla also secured up to $30 billion in borrowing capacity during the quarter, he added.

The shareholder update carried no delivery forecast for the fourth quarter. Musk said on the call that the company is aiming for about 5 million vehicles a year by about 2030.

#4897. USC scientists discover how to create a never-ending supply of cancer-fighting cells

Scientists at the University of Southern California (USC) have discovered a way to produce a potentially renewable supply of cancer-fighting immune cells, opening a promising new direction for cancer treatment. By working with immature cells that can develop into powerful immune defenders, the researchers have created a system capable of generating large numbers of genetically engineered cells that recognize tumors and help activate the body's natural defenses.

The breakthrough, described in a study published in Cell, could help overcome some of the biggest obstacles facing cancer immunotherapy, including the difficulty of manufacturing enough immune cells and keeping them active in the body. In experiments involving mice, the engineered cells established themselves in bone marrow, produced new immune cells, and slowed the progression of both blood cancers and solid tumors.

The USC Stem Cell-led team focused on a type of immature immune cell called granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMPs). These cells normally develop into macrophages and other immune cells that protect the body from infections and participate in immune responses. The researchers found that GMPs could be grown extensively in the laboratory while retaining their ability to develop into functioning immune cells. They could also be genetically modified to recognize specific cancer markers and strengthen broader immune responses.

"The study establishes a scalable and engineerable GMP platform for cellular immunotherapy and introduces concepts that we believe could have broad implications for both cancer immunotherapy and stem cell biology," said the paper's corresponding author Qi-Long Ying, MD, PhD, professor of stem cell biology and regenerative medicine at the Keck School of Medicine of USC.

Scientists Discover a Surprising Ability in Immune Cell Precursors

Beyond its potential applications in cancer treatment, the discovery challenges a long-standing understanding of how blood and immune cells develop.

Stem cells are known for their ability to self-renew, meaning they can repeatedly divide while maintaining their original properties. In the blood-forming system, this ability has traditionally been associated primarily with hematopoietic stem cells, which can produce all the major types of blood and immune cells.

Progenitor cells, by contrast, are further along in development and are already committed to producing a more limited range of cells. They are generally thought to have less capacity for long-term self-renewal.

The USC researchers demonstrated that GMPs can retain this ability under carefully controlled laboratory conditions. Rather than quickly maturing into other cell types, the progenitors continued multiplying while preserving their identity and their capacity to generate functional immune cells.

"The prevailing view has been that long-term self-renewal in the blood system is primarily a property of the hematopoietic stem cells that can generate any type of blood or immune cell," said Ying. "We found that, under the right conditions, GMPs can also self-renew, dividing extensively while keeping their identity and ability to produce functional immune cells. That gives us a scalable starting point for engineering cell therapies for cancer, infectious disease and potentially many other conditions."

This finding could be especially important for developing treatments that require large, reliable supplies of genetically engineered immune cells.

Why Macrophages Could Be Powerful Weapons Against Cancer

Macrophages are among the immune system's most versatile defenders. Their name comes from Greek words meaning "big eaters," reflecting their ability to surround and engulf bacteria, damaged cells, and other unwanted material.

They also help coordinate immune responses by sending signals that influence the behavior of other immune cells. Some macrophages can enter tumors and attack cancer cells, making them attractive candidates for cancer immunotherapy.

One particularly promising application involves solid tumors, which include cancers that form masses in organs and tissues. Existing therapies based on engineered T cells have achieved their greatest successes against certain blood cancers, but treating solid tumors has proven more difficult.

Macrophages offer a potentially different approach because they naturally migrate into tissues and can penetrate tumors. However, turning them into practical treatments presents several challenges.

Once macrophages have fully matured, they are difficult to multiply in large quantities outside the body. They are also challenging to modify genetically and can be damaged during freezing and storage, complicating the production and distribution of cell therapies.

Another problem occurs after administration. Mature macrophages often collect in organs such as the lungs and liver instead of spreading widely throughout the body. Their relatively rapid disappearance can also limit how long a treatment remains effective.

To work around these limitations, the scientists decided to begin at an earlier stage of immune cell development.

Creating a Renewable Supply of Cancer-Fighting Immune Cells

Rather than trying to manufacture large numbers of mature macrophages, first author Shi Yue, MD, of Ying's laboratory and his colleagues concentrated on GMPs, the progenitor cells that can produce macrophages.

The researchers developed a precisely defined mixture of chemicals that allowed GMPs to survive and multiply for extended periods in the laboratory. The mixture prevented the cells from prematurely developing into mature immune cells, preserving a renewable population of progenitors.

Even after extensive laboratory expansion, the GMPs maintained the molecular characteristics that defined them. Importantly, they also retained the ability to develop into functional macrophages and other related immune cells when needed.

This approach could make it possible to manufacture large numbers of engineered immune cells from a starting population that can be continually expanded, instead of repeatedly collecting and preparing mature cells.

To confirm that the method could be reproduced, collaborators in the laboratory of Ravi Majeti, MD, PhD, at Stanford University independently demonstrated that GMPs could be maintained for extended periods and genetically engineered. Their results provided additional support for the reliability of the technique and its potential use in future cell therapies.

Majeti, Director of the Institute for Stem Cell Biology and Regenerative Medicine at Stanford University, noted: "This method for the expansion and engineering of GMPs opens the door to numerous translational applications, much like T cell expansion and engineering. We have already demonstrated engineering of these cells to drive multiple potent functions, and there is a lot more to be explored."

Engineering Immune Cells to Recognize and Attack Tumors

The team next investigated whether these expandable progenitor cells could be programmed to fight cancer.

They genetically engineered GMPs to carry a chimeric antigen receptor, or CAR. This engineered receptor allows immune cells to identify a particular molecular marker on the surface of cancer cells, helping direct their activity toward tumors.

CAR technology is already an important part of cancer immunotherapy, particularly in treatments that modify a patient's T cells to recognize and attack cancer. The USC researchers explored whether a similar strategy could be applied to GMPs, allowing them to generate engineered macrophages and other immune cells.

The scientists also equipped some of the progenitor cells with an additional immune-activating signal. This modification was designed to recruit and stimulate nearby immune cells, including T cells, which can help mount a stronger attack against cancer.

The added signal offered another potentially important advantage. It remained effective even when the immune characteristics of donor cells did not match those of the recipient.

That finding raises the possibility of developing off-the-shelf treatments using cells manufactured in advance from donors. Unlike personalized cell therapies, which must be prepared separately using each patient's own cells, such products could potentially be produced in larger batches and made available to multiple patients.

Engineered Cells Slow Cancer Progression in Mice

After successfully expanding and engineering GMPs from both mice and humans, the researchers investigated their therapeutic potential in mouse experiments.

When administered to mice, the progenitor cells established themselves in the bone marrow and other locations responsible for blood cell production. These environments provided places where the GMPs could generate additional engineered macrophages and related immune cells.

This was an important difference from treatments using mature macrophages. Instead of relying solely on immune cells that may disappear relatively quickly, the progenitors could provide a continuing source of replacement cells from within the body.

The strategy may therefore help overcome the rapid clearance that has limited macrophage therapies, including approaches evaluated in recent clinical trials.

The researchers tested the engineered cells in mice with blood cancers and solid tumors. GMPs carrying CAR receptors slowed disease progression in both types of cancer.

The effects were even stronger when the progenitors carried both CAR receptors and the additional signal designed to activate other immune cells. This combination improved the treatment's ability to stimulate a broader antitumor response.

Although these findings are encouraging, the cancer-treatment results remain preclinical. Further research will be needed to establish whether engineered GMPs can be used safely and effectively in people, particularly if they are intended to persist and produce immune cells over extended periods.

Potential Applications Beyond Cancer Treatment

The scientists also explored whether their approach could help treat conditions involving defective immune defenses.

In a separate mouse experiment, they studied chronic granulomatous disease, an inherited disorder that interferes with the ability of certain immune cells to kill harmful microbes. People with this condition are particularly vulnerable to recurrent bacterial and fungal infections because their immune cells cannot eliminate some pathogens effectively.

When the researchers administered GMPs to mice with the disorder, the cells restored the animals' ability to fight bacterial infection.

This result suggests that renewable, genetically engineered immune cell progenitors might eventually have applications beyond cancer, including treatments for inherited immune deficiencies and potentially infectious diseases.

The broader lesson, according to the researchers, is that the developmental stage of an immune cell may be just as important as the genetic modifications used to turn it into a treatment.

By engineering cells before they fully mature, scientists may be able to create therapies that are easier to manufacture, more adaptable, and capable of maintaining a continuing supply of specialized immune defenders.

"Our study suggests that the future of immunotherapy may depend not only on designing better CAR receptors, but also on choosing the right developmental stage of the cell," said Ying.

The study, published in Cell, is titled "Expansion and CAR engineering of granulocyte-monocyte progenitors for cellular immunotherapy."

#4896. Sentara Health deploys new AI case management tool co-developed with Regard

Not-for-profit health system Sentara Health deployed a new artificial intelligence case management solution Tuesday, which it co-developed with clinical decision support company Regard.

Case managers can use the tool to query charts in plain language, allowing them to quickly surface key insights like patient history, status changes and more. The platform also allows case managers to view changes within the last 24 hours.

“It's incredible to think about how much time we used to spend piecing together a patient's story before they could even start discharge planning,” said Rollin Reeder, M.D., Sentara Health chief health information officer, in a statement. “Now, with Regard, I have information from the chart I could not have found on my own, sometimes critically important information that could change the course of management. This is transformational in many ways, but especially in how effectively and confidently we can move patients through the system.”

The health system is planning to expand the tool across acute care and into ambulatory care. The solution could save nearly 90,000 hours, executives say, which is equivalent to adding more than 40 full-time employees and nearly $5 million in labor costs.

Sentara Health operates 12 hospitals and more than 400 care sites in Virginia and Northeast North Carolina.

The new solution builds on Sentara Health's partnership with Regard—and reflects the clinical decision support company’s expansion into broader health system workflows.

“We’ve built an intelligence platform that physicians trust, surfacing more than 20 million diagnoses that they’ve accepted, with supporting clinical evidence,” said Eli Ben-Joseph, Regard CEO and co-founder, in a statement. “It’s a natural next step for us to extend that complete clinical picture that is reliable and credible to operational teams, giving health systems a consistent layer of support across the organization. That’s the exciting part about getting it right at the bedside. You quickly start to see other high-impact areas where the technology can make a difference.”

Sentara Health has been scaling the adoption of AI solutions across clinical care, operations and technical infrastructure. In mid-September, the system announced it would pilot GW RhythmX’s AI precision care platform to test AI-powered clinical recommendations within clinician workflows at the point of care.

The approval of daraxonrasib, a new life-extending drug for pancreatic cancer, is the biggest advance in decades against a notoriously hard-to-treat disease. But data readouts don’t show what those extra months mean to patients and their loved ones, or the immense resources it can take to access new medicines.

At last week’s STAT Summit in Boston, Dana Senaha told the story of her husband’s experience participating in the clinical trial for daraxonrasib. After Bobby Senaha’s cancer stopped responding to standard chemotherapy, the quest for treatment led their family to California, then Texas, and finally back home to Hawaii, where Bobby died on Aug. 25, 2024.

Below are Dana’s prepared remarks, which begin with the story of enrolling in the clinical trial.

By the time our family arrived in San Antonio, Bobby had already been through FOLFIRINOX, Gem-Abrax, an attempted tumor resection, and another Phase 1 trial. His cancer was progressing. He was sleeping much of the day and was maxed out on oral pain meds.

When Bobby received his first dose of daraxonrasib, the turnaround was the miracle we had been praying for. Within 24 hours, his pain improved dramatically, and he cut his pain medication in half. By day three, we walked more than three miles along the San Antonio River Walk. Within six weeks, his scans showed a 45% reduction in disease, his lung metastases had disappeared, and eventually he came completely off pain medication.

The side effects were hard. Bobby developed a painful rash that could break open and bleed, along with significant inflammation and bleeding around his fingernails. He also dealt with various GI issues intermittently over the course of the trial. But, compared with chemotherapy, he was feeling better.

He had less pain and more energy. He was again able to walk and run, travel, work, and be present with our boys. And, most importantly, his cancer was responding. For Bobby, the side effects were the price of getting his life back and having more time with our boys. For a while, we believed we might actually beat this.

But accessing that treatment required enormous resources. Every 21-day cycle meant flights between Hawaii and San Antonio, clinic visits, scans, labs, and long days of travel. My parents cared for our boys while we were away. We had excellent insurance, flexible jobs, medical knowledge, supportive doctors, family who stepped in, and a community behind us. Even with all of that, we were overwhelmed.

Bobby remained on the trial for 29 weeks before his cancer mutated and began to evade the treatment. We eventually tried another chemotherapy while pursuing another clinical trial. Unfortunately at the beginning of August 2024, Bobby’s condition quickly deteriorated. After a two-week hospitalization, Bobby died peacefully at home in Hawaii. In all, daraxonrasib gave us 10 more good months together.

Bobby always believed he wasn’t a statistic and would beat cancer. He used to tell me, “When I beat this, we have a lot of work to do.”

Bobby and other patients who participated in that early trial helped make it possible for this treatment to continue through development and ultimately reach FDA approval. Another friend of ours, Peter, later got daraxonrasib through expanded access and now picks it up at his local pharmacy.

But FDA approval doesn’t mean the work is over. This medicine may still be out of reach for patients who cannot afford it, don’t have access to cancer care, or who struggle to navigate complicated health care and insurance systems.

Bobby had almost every advantage — and even we struggled. He thought a lot about the patients who don’t have those advantages. People we know who live on the outer islands of Hawaii without access to specialists and clinical trials, people we know who can’t afford to take time off work, who can’t afford their medications. Because access isn’t an abstract concept. For patients and families like ours, access is time, hope, and the opportunity to make a few more memories together.

#4894. Mortgage Rates Drop for First Time in 2 Months as Stock Market Jitters Drive Investors to Bonds

Mortgage rates fell for the first time in two months this week as investors sought safety in government bonds, spurred by cooling stock and tech markets and a sharp pullback in new-housing starts fueling inflation fears.

The average rate on 30-year fixed home loans decreased to 7.32% for the week ending Oct. 22, down from 7.40% the prior week, according to Freddie Mac. The decline follows seven consecutive weeks of gains that sent mortgage rates to three-year highs.

For perspective, rates averaged 6.19% one year ago.

"The average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage declined this week, offering some relief to prospective homebuyers," says Sam Khater, Freddie Mac's chief economist.

The main driver behind this reversal is the 10-year Treasury yield, which averaged roughly 5.13% over the past several days, down from 5.24% the week before—the result of investors adopting a more cautious stance as they follow the ongoing conflict in Iran and await initial unemployment claims data.

It also comes on the heels of worse-than-expected housing data, with housing starts plunging in September 5.2% month over month and 7.4% year over year, according to a report from the U.S. Census Bureau released Tuesday.

During the same period, pending home sales dropped to the lowest level on record, according to the National Association of Realtors®, reflecting priced-out buyers' withdrawal from the market.

"Mortgage rates edging down is welcome news, but we should not get carried away, as today’s modest decline does not put much of a dent in this fall’s surge that saw rates climb 74 basis points in just over a month," says Realtor.com® senior economist Jake Krimmel.

Looking ahead, the Federal Reserve rate picture—like the inflation numbers—has firmed up considerably.

The odds of the central bank holding the federal funds rate steady at the current 3.75%-4% range at next week’s meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) have risen from 42% a month ago to 66% a week ago and around 85% for most of this week, according to CME FedWatch.

Meanwhile, December hike odds have hovered near 90% the whole time, suggesting that the bond market has likely already priced in both a hold next week and a rate increase in two months' time.

"So the question moving forward with the 10-year, and with mortgage rates by extension, is how high the floor is for rates over the next several months and whether we are already there or still have somewhere to go," says Krimmel.

For the housing market, mortgage rates comfortably in the 7% range is reshaping the balance between buyers and sellers. The Realtor.com Market Clock tool shows the national reading at 4 o’clock—a late balanced market trending toward buyers, with about two-thirds of the top 100 metros either favoring buyers outright or moving that way.

However, gaining market leverage and gaining affordability are two different things. Rates climbing from 6.66% in late August to 7.4% on Oct. 8 translated into a 7.2% loss of purchasing power for buyers across the board.

"Even if buyers claw back a bit of that with rates edging down this week, the damage to the fall housing market is already done," says Krimmel, pointing to the bleak pending sales figure and Realtor.com data showing contract signings down 9.8% year over year.

This week, the number of homes for sale rose 7.7% compared to a year ago, the highest weekly growth rate since late January. Inventory has been building momentum throughout the fall, rising from roughly 4% year-over-year growth in late August to nearly 8% now.

At the same time, the median asking price held steady week to week at $415,000.

The comment letter submitted to the U.S. Treasury by the National Cryptocurrency Association (NCA) on the Section 3 rules of the GENIUS Act (Docket TREAS-DO-2026-0496) closes the period of uncertainty surrounding stablecoin regulation.

The document, signed by NCA President and Ripple Chief Legal Officer Stuart Alderoty, states that the industry accepts the requirement for one-hundred-percent reserves but puts forward a practical framework for protecting retail holders, so that Americans do not end up "stranded holding stablecoins if regulations change."

The first requirement eliminates the risk of a sudden change in issuers' legal status. The GENIUS Act established the framework. Now, Treasury’s rules must ensure that Americans are not stranded holding stablecoins if regulations change, have pathways to recover stolen funds, and can self-custody if they choose.

We stay in the cancer space because helping others gives life meaning.

#4872. Opinion: Overdose deaths have plunged. We need to know why

Test your knowledge with our new weekday mini crossword

By J. Travis Donahoe, David T. Zhu, and Michael L. Barnett

Donahoe is assistant professor of health policy and management at the University of Pittsburgh School of Public Health. Zhu is an M.D./Ph.D candidate at Virginia Commonwealth University. Barnett is Sorensen Family Provost’s Professor of Health Services, Policy & Practice at Brown University School of Public Health and a primary care physician at Brigham and Women’s Hospital.

It’s one of the biggest public health reversals in decades: Opioid overdose deaths are falling faster than at any point in the modern history of the epidemic. After persistently rising for more than 30 years, deaths peaked at 81,806 in 2022 and fell by almost half, to 44,564, in 2025. The decline helped push U.S. life expectancy to a record high in 2024.

Many want to take credit for this miraculous recovery. Vice President JD Vance says it happened because the administration sealed the border. Public health experts and agencies point to saturating communities with the overdose-reversal drug naloxone and increased access to treatment.

Yet neither of these stories fits the timing or scale of the decline. Deaths began falling in mid-2023, months before President Trump’s inauguration and subsequent border crackdown. We just published a paper in JAMA, highlighting that expansion of naloxone doesn’t line up with it either. Naloxone is a critical, lifesaving medicine that reverses tens of thousands of overdoses per year; however, emergency medical services administration and EMS-witnessed layperson administration have declined since mid-2023. Improved treatment access isn’t a better explanation. Evidence-based treatment with medications has increased, but steadily and incrementally, nowhere near the order of magnitude needed to explain such a large and sudden drop in deaths.

No one knows why opioid overdose deaths have fallen so dramatically. But the humbling truth is that forces outside medical care and public health policies must be playing the major role.

So far, the most plausible driver of recent declines in overdose deaths is a change in the illicit drug supply. A recent high-profile paper in the journal Science presented data that fentanyl became more diluted and harder to find — online posts discussed “fentanyl droughts” — as deaths fell, a plausible mechanism for these recent overdose declines. But there are large gaps in our understanding.

One big gap is that we don’t know with certainty what the supply shock was or what caused it. The Science paper suggests it was the result of trade negotiations and crackdowns on fentanyl precursor production in China. Yet other plausible explanations exist. The Sinaloa cartel announced a ban on fentanyl production around the same period, and there were news reports of violence and ensuing casualties. Others cite potential sharp shifts in drug use behaviors, such as people stopping opioid use because the supply became less desirable through lacing with sedatives such as xylazine and medetomidine.

All of this matters greatly for informing ongoing policy. Should tens of thousands of American lives have been saved as a result of decisions made about fentanyl precursors in China, the implications are massive. It would suggest that trade policy can play a forefront role in protecting public health. On the other hand, if other hypotheses such as cartel violence or increasing adulteration with xylazine are more correct causes of the decline in opioid deaths, it is unclear that such policies would help much. What is clear is that it would benefit all of us interested in public health to get to the bottom of this.

How is it that we can know so little about the biggest public health turnaround in a generation, even though it has been and continues to be studied so intensely? The answer is best explained by the old analogy of a drunk man looking for his keys under a streetlight, not because that’s where he lost them, but because that’s where the light was brightest. We have been largely limiting our research focus to areas with data.

Researchers like us have spent years studying treatment and harm reduction, a field with a wealth of data, including insurance claims. That research has mattered tremendously, helping lower barriers to care and dramatically reform the delivery of health care and other services to address substance use. It remains essential. But it is a shame that there has not been comparable investment in studying the illicit drug supply and the policies that affect it, despite its clear and profound importance for public health.

The reason is a lack of data. Our main national survey on illicit drugs, the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, misses many of the people at greatest risk, including those who are homeless or incarcerated. It asks next to nothing about illicit drug prices, the main economic indicator of changes in supply, and it did not even include a question on illicit fentanyl use until 2022, when deaths had already peaked. Data on the prevalence of rare behaviors like heroin use are also often not available to researchers below the national level, even though use and supply vary a great deal across areas.

Versions of what a better survey could look like already exist. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention tracks HIV risk behaviors and incidence among at-risk populations throughout the U.S., and Australia has surveyed people about illicit drug use, prices, and purity for decades. A better survey for understanding overdose mortality trends would be designed to reach the people most at risk and who current surveys miss, such as people in shelters and jails. It would ask what drugs people use, how they use them, and what they paid for them. It would test illicit drug samples for what was in them. It would tell us whether and how supply is changing, and how it affects drug use and risk.

Governmental agencies such as the Drug Enforcement Administration have other data that could improve researchers’ understanding of the drug supply, but we only get glimpses of it when they’re forced to release it. As one example, the Automation of Reports and Consolidated Orders System data tracks opioid shipments from distributors to pharmacies and was disclosed under court order following litigation in 2019, about eight years after peak opioid prescribing. It led to studies on interventions that could have saved thousands of lives had they been identified by researchers and implemented or expanded earlier.

The public health gains of the past three years are not guaranteed to last, and there are still far too many overdose deaths. If we cannot explain why deaths are falling now, we won’t know what is driving them up if they rise again. And we won’t know what to do about it.

#4866. A protein that fuels cancer could also be the key to fighting it

A protein that helps cancer grow may also hold a surprising key to fighting it. Scientists at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory (CSHL) have discovered that activating this protein in immune cells can make them more effective at attacking tumors, potentially opening a new path toward treating cancers that resist existing therapies.

The findings, published in Nature Immunology, could be especially important for colorectal cancer, which has become the leading cause of cancer-related death among people under 50. Many patients with difficult-to-treat forms of the disease have limited options, particularly when chemotherapy and newer immunotherapy treatments fail. The research suggests that a protein normally associated with cancer growth could instead become a powerful cancer-fighting "force" when activated in the right cells.

Turning a Cancer-Promoting Protein Into an Immune System Weapon

Scientists have long understood that a patient's physical fitness can influence their ability to withstand illness. But CSHL Assistant Professor Semir Beyaz is exploring a different kind of fitness: the metabolic health and performance of individual cells.

"We hypothesized that the fitness determinants of cancer and our immune cells may not be so different," he said. "They're in the same universe. They're constrained by the same chemical and physical rules. So, it's not surprising they'd end up evolving the same way."

At the center of the research is peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor delta (PPARD), a protein that helps regulate how cells burn fat for energy. PPARD is a transcription factor, meaning it controls the activity of genes involved in important cellular processes.

To understand its effects, Beyaz and his colleagues began by studying mice with colorectal cancer. They found that although cancer cells use PPARD to support their own activity, activating the same protein in immune cells had a dramatically different effect. Instead of helping tumors thrive, it shifted the surrounding environment toward one that favored the immune system's attack on cancer.

"It's all about cell fitness," Beyaz explains. "What cells eat affects who they are. When immune cells burn more fat, they become more fit for fighting cancer."

Supercharging Cancer-Fighting CAR T Cells

The researchers next wanted to determine whether boosting this fat-burning process could improve immune cells' ability to attack human colorectal cancer.

They turned to engineered immune cells known as CAR T cells. These cells are modified to recognize and attack cancer, and CAR T cell therapies are already used to treat certain cancers.

The team activated PPARD in the engineered cells and tested them against several experimental models of human colorectal cancer. These included organoids, which are miniature tumor models grown from patient tissue, as well as human cancer cells cultured in laboratory dishes and implanted into mice.

Across these experiments, the PPARD-activated CAR T cells slowed or stopped tumor growth. The results suggest that changing how immune cells process energy could make existing immunotherapy strategies more effective, although the approach has not yet been proven in patients.

A Potential Strategy Against Multiple Types of Cancer

Colorectal cancer was the researchers' initial focus because of its growing toll on younger adults. However, the team also confirmed promising results in other cancers, including breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and melanoma, a serious form of skin cancer.

These findings raise the possibility that improving immune cell fitness could become a broader strategy for treating tumors that are difficult to control with current therapies.

Beyaz believes the discoveries represent only the "tip of the iceberg" when it comes to finding new ways to strengthen cancer immunotherapy.

The potential applications may also extend beyond cancer.

"Boosting immune cell fitness may also help with other conditions where the immune system fails to clear diseased cells," Beyaz says.

His laboratory is now investigating whether the same approach could help treat endometriosis, a chronic inflammatory condition affecting approximately one in 10 women. Like difficult-to-treat colorectal cancer, endometriosis remains a major medical challenge, with existing treatments often unable to provide lasting relief.

The Surprising Good and Evil Sides of a Cancer Protein

Beyaz compares PPARD's opposing roles to "the force" in Star Wars. Depending on how it is used, the same underlying power can produce very different outcomes.

In cancer cells, PPARD can support disease progression, much like Darth Vader serving the Emperor. But when activated in immune cells, it can take on the opposite role, resembling Anakin Skywalker's return to the light and helping destroy the disease instead.

"It's the classic fight between good and evil," Beyaz says. "How can good ultimately prevail? Activating PPARD in immune cells could be the way."

The final policy excludes therapeutic procedures targeting nerves such as the pudendal nerve and ganglion impar, interventions used for some forms of severe pelvic pain. I have used these interventions to provide relief to patients who previously never had a break from pain, people for whom sitting, putting on a pair of pants, having sex, or simply getting through the day can be excruciating.

As a pain medicine physician, I worry that we are forgetting one of the central lessons of the opioid epidemic: People with chronic pain need alternatives to opioids and more individualized treatment options. Peripheral nerve interventions are not used in isolation. They are part of multimodal and multidisciplinary pain care, helping patients participate in physical therapy, restore function, decrease medications, avoid emergency room visits and hospitalizations, and sometimes delay or avoid more invasive procedures or surgery.

CMS contractors have a legitimate concern. In evaluating these procedures, they point to limitations in the evidence, including small studies, short follow-up periods, and uncertainty about whether benefits last. For several procedures, they concluded that the evidence was insufficient to support coverage.

But chronic pain presents a particular challenge for this style of evidence gathering. Pain is by definition a subjective experience, and every patient experiences pain differently. A treatment that produces limited benefit on average across a study population may provide profound relief to an individual. Population-level evidence matters, but it cannot always capture which patients experience a clinically meaningful benefit. And for the 1 in 4 U.S. adults living with unrelenting pain, even a few weeks or months of decreased suffering can help them feel like themselves again.

My mother taught me that long before I became a pain physician. She is a brilliant cancer research scientist who has lived with chronic migraine and abdominal pain and, like so many patients I now see, often struggles to find physicians who understand her pain or know how to help her. She inspires me to keep looking for answers for patients who have spent years doing the same.

One of them was a remarkably healthy 87-year-old with severe bladder pain. By the time she came to see me, she had been passed from one doctor to another and was in despair. “I see it in their eyes,” she told me. “They don’t know what to do with me, and they look like they are trying to leave the room. I’ve never felt this way; people always want to be close to me.”

In my clinic, I meet patients no one knows how to help. And sometimes, the procedures I perform can be life-changing.

Some may question whether these procedures are used too often, whether the evidence supporting them is strong enough, or whether financial incentives influence their use. Those are fair questions. Not every nerve block works, and Medicare should not pay indefinitely for treatments that do not help.

We need better evidence, including larger studies, longer-term patient follow-up, and federal funding to support this research. The answer to gaps in evidence is to seek that real-world evidence while at the same time preserving appropriate access to effective non-opioid treatments

But absence of perfect evidence should not be confused with absence of meaningful patient benefit.

One of my patients developed chronic pain after gynecologic surgery. After a pudendal nerve block, she told me: “I did not realize how different my life was because of the pain until you gave me the injection that took my pain away. I was able to wear pants, sit in a chair, and spend my day without being distracted by horrible pain.”

That may sound like a modest victory. To someone living with chronic pain, it is anything but. In a few weeks, this pain relief will no longer be accessible to her.

Individualized pain care should not depend on luck, who you know, your insurance coverage, or what is in your checking account. Pain does not always fit neatly into an insurance box of “approve” or “deny.” Effective pain care requires a broad treatment toolkit. That toolkit is meaningless if patients cannot access it.

As these new policies take effect, Medicare contractors must reconsider restrictions as new evidence emerges, while researchers, clinicians, and specialty societies build and identify funding for the larger, longer-term studies Medicare says it needs. We need coverage standards that recognize clinically meaningful benefit while continuing to demand better evidence. We should not put the tools physicians use to relieve suffering out of patients’ reach.

#4855. Stanford’s new microscope reveals living cells like never before

A new microscope developed at Stanford University allows scientists to watch the tiny structures inside living cells move and interact in real time, providing an exceptionally detailed view without the fluorescent labels typically used to make these structures visible.

Researchers at Stanford have created a powerful new imaging system that reveals the inner workings of living cells with remarkable clarity. By combining two advanced microscopy techniques, the instrument achieves a resolution of approximately 120 nanometers, an unprecedented level of detail for an approach that does not require fluorescent labels.

Known as Interferometric Image Scanning Microscopy (iISM), the technology makes it possible to observe multiple cellular structures simultaneously while they move, change, and respond to their surroundings. Scientists can also investigate what happens when cells encounter drugs or disease causing organisms, potentially uncovering biological processes that have been difficult to study.

The breakthrough, described in the Nature portfolio journal Light: Science and Applications, could lead to advances in cancer research, drug development, infectious disease studies, and investigations of how plants interact with microorganisms.

"This new microscope provides a fantastic new view into the cell, where you can see the tiny structures and machines in the cell moving, changing, and interacting without having to add fluorescence to observe them," said senior author W.E. Moerner, the Harry S. Mosher Professor of Chemistry in Stanford's School of Humanities and Sciences. "It's a wonderful look into these complex little cellular boxes that drive our life."

Seeing Inside Living Cells Without Fluorescent Labels

One of the most important advantages of the new microscope is its ability to reveal cellular activity without relying on fluorescent markers.

Many existing microscopy techniques use fluorescent substances to highlight specific structures inside cells. Although these methods have transformed biological research, they come with limitations. Researchers can generally label only a small number of structures at once, making it difficult to observe how different components interact across an entire cell.

Fluorescent signals can also fade during observation through a process known as photobleaching. In addition, introducing the labels can be challenging, and some markers may alter the behavior of the very structures scientists want to study.

The Stanford system avoids these problems by imaging cells without fluorescent labels. This allows researchers to follow numerous cellular components over extended periods while observing their activity in a more natural setting.

Another advantage is that iISM requires substantially less illumination power than comparable label-free methods that produce high-contrast images. Lower light exposure reduces the possibility of photodamage, which occurs when the light used for imaging harms living cells or interferes with their normal functions.

Although other specialized microscopes can achieve finer resolution, the combination of detailed imaging, gentle illumination, and extended observation makes iISM particularly valuable for studying living biological systems.

The researchers emphasize that their technology is intended to complement fluorescence microscopy rather than replace it.

First author Michelle Kueppers, a postdoctoral scholar in Moerner's laboratory, explained that combining the strengths of different imaging approaches could help scientists answer previously inaccessible questions.

"Every method has its advantages and disadvantages, and we believe in a complementary implementation in the future," Kueppers said. "If we use the strengths of fluorescence for molecular specificity and the strength of iISM for label-free context and dynamics, we can really start tackling questions that have been difficult to address before."

How the New Microscope Achieves Unprecedented Detail

The breakthrough comes from bringing together two different microscopy technologies, each with its own advantages.

Moerner received the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his pioneering contributions to super-resolution fluorescence microscopy. He specifically recruited Kueppers to Stanford because of her doctoral research involving a technique called "interferometric scattering microscopy."

This method relies on a familiar property of light: scattering.

Light changes direction when it encounters tiny particles. The same basic phenomenon explains why the sky appears blue. As sunlight passes through Earth's atmosphere, molecules, dust, and water droplets scatter its different wavelengths. Shorter blue wavelengths scatter more strongly than longer red wavelengths, producing the blue color we see overhead.

Interferometric scattering microscopy takes advantage of this behavior on a much smaller scale.

A laser illuminates a cell, causing tiny structures inside it to scatter some of the incoming light. Because the scattered signal is extremely weak, a second laser beam is used to amplify it through interference, allowing the microscope to detect structures that would otherwise be difficult to observe.

The Stanford team improved this approach by combining it with technology adapted from advanced confocal microscopes.

Traditional confocal microscopes use a small opening called a pinhole and a single detector to isolate light from a selected area. More advanced versions replace that arrangement with camera-based detector arrays capable of collecting multiple views of the same region.

The researchers incorporated an array detector into their new microscope, allowing it to collect more light than a conventional system built around a pinhole and single detector.

Hundreds of Views Combined Into One Sharper Image

The advantage of collecting multiple perspectives is similar to the way human vision works.

Our two eyes observe the world from slightly different positions, helping the brain distinguish nearby objects from those farther away. The Stanford microscope uses a related principle, but instead of relying on just two perspectives, its detector array gathers tens to hundreds of views.

The researchers developed a computational method that combines these measurements to generate images with improved sharpness, contrast, depth information, and precision.

By merging the strengths of interferometric scattering microscopy and advanced detector arrays, the team created an instrument capable of resolving structures as small as approximately 120 nanometers.

For perspective, a nanometer is one billionth of a meter. At this scale, researchers can examine extremely small cellular structures and follow their movements and interactions.

Crucially, the microscope achieves this resolution without sacrificing imaging speed or requiring intense laser illumination. Scientists can therefore observe living cells for longer periods while minimizing the risk of disturbing or damaging them.

From Cancer Drugs to Malaria: New Possibilities for Research

Moerner and Kueppers are continuing to refine the instrument, with the goal of eventually making it accessible to scientists across many areas of biology.

Three collaborations with other Stanford research groups are already underway or in development, demonstrating the range of questions the technology could help investigate.

One project uses the microscope to observe interactions among plant cells, fungi, and bacteria as they happen. By watching these organisms interact in real time, researchers hope to gain a clearer understanding of the complex relationships between plants and microbes.

Another collaboration focuses on cancer treatment. Scientists are using iISM to investigate how a cancer drug enters a cell, potentially providing valuable information about how medications interact with their targets.

A third project, still in the planning stages, will examine how red blood cells change shape when exposed to malaria infection. Observing these physical changes could offer new insights into how the disease affects blood cells.

These applications illustrate why the researchers believe their microscope could become a useful tool across numerous scientific disciplines rather than serving only a narrow area of research.

"This is not a niche technique," Kueppers said. "It has broad applications, and we hope the life science community will be well served by it, leading to many new discoveries."

Homes are still selling every single day. Because no matter what’s happening with mortgage rates or the economy, life keeps happening too. People get new jobs. Families grow. Relationships change. People retire, downsize, or need to move closer to the people they love.

And those life changes create buyers and sellers in every market.

Thousands of buyers are still making moves every day. The key is making sure your house is positioned to get their attention.

Today’s buyers have more choices, so they can afford to be more selective about things like price and condition. That makes having the right strategy from the start especially important.

Because you don’t need every buyer to be looking for a home like yours. You just need the right one – and to know how to get in front of that buyer.
