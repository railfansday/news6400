#698. Scientists film deepest ever fish on seabed off Japan

Sign up for CNN’s Wonder Theory science newsletter. Explore the universe with news on fascinating discoveries, scientific advancements and more.

Cruising at a depth of 8,336 meters (over 27,000 feet) just above the seabed, a young snailfish has become the deepest fish ever filmed by scientists during a probe into the abyss of the northern Pacific Ocean.

Scientists from University of Western Australia and Tokyo University of Marine Science and Technology released footage of the snailfish on Sunday filmed last September by sea robots in deep trenches off Japan.

Along with the filming the deepest snailfish, the scientists physically caught two other specimens at 8,022 meters and set another record for the deepest catch.

Previously, the deepest snailfish ever spotted was at 7,703 meters in 2008, while scientists had never been able to collect fish from anywhere below 8,000 meters.

“What is significant is that it shows how far a particular type of fish will descend in the ocean,” said marine biologist Alan Jamieson, founder of the Minderoo-UWA Deep Sea Research Centre, who led the expedition.

Scientists are filming in the trenches off Japan as part of a 10-year study into the deepest fish populations in the world. Snailfish are members of Liparidae family, and while most snailfish live in shallow water, others survive at some of the greatest depths ever recorded, Jamieson said.

During the two-month survey last year, three “landers” – automatic sea robots fitted with high-resolution cameras – were dropped into three trenches – the Japan, Izu-Ogasawara and Ryukyu trenches – at varying depths.

In the Izu-Ogasawara trench, footage showed the deepest snailfish hovering calmly alongside other crustaceans on the seabed.

Jamieson classified the fish as a juvenile and said younger deep sea snailfish often stay as deep as possible to avoid being eaten by bigger predators that swim at shallower depths.

Another clip shot at between 7,500 and 8,200 meters in the same trench showed a colony of fish and crustaceans munching at bait tied to an undersea robot.

Images of the two captured snailfish – identified as Pseudoliparis belyaevi – provide a rare glimpse of the unique features that help the deep sea species survive the extreme environment.

They have tiny eyes, a translucent body, and their lack of swim bladder, which helps other fish float, works to their advantage, Jamieson said.

The professor said the Pacific Ocean is particularly conducive to vibrant activity due to its warm southern current, which encourages sea creatures to go deeper, while its abundant marine life provides a good source of food for bottom feeders.

Scientists would like to know more about creatures living at extreme depths, but cost is the constraint, Jamieson said, adding that each lander alone costs them $200,000 to assemble and operate.

“The challenges are that technology has been expensive and scientists don’t have a lot of money,” he said.

#691. The EU wants an AI revolution. But on whose terms?

As the US and China race ahead, the European Union seeks to boost its economy with AI. Can it compete while protecting workers, safety and the climate?

Step-dancers spun across the circular stage in the middle of a dark hall, entrancing an audience of policymakers and tech bosses. Not simply another bureaucratic conference, the International AI Summit, hosted in Dublin this week, aspired to something more spectacular — a grand announcement that Europe is ready for its artificial intelligence (AI) revolution.

Among the more than 1,500 expected guests were Google DeepMind co-founder and chair Demis Hassabis and Microsoft president Brad Smith, in addition to high-ranking European Union and Irish officials. The event was a key part of Ireland's term as president of the EU Council, but a lack of critical voices to counterbalance the AI enthusiasm on display has drawn accusations of tech boosterism.

The summit was "almost like a party political broadcast on behalf of AI," Barry O'Sullivan, an AI professor at University College Cork, told DW.

The overarching theme was how to use AI to get Europe's sluggish economy moving, boost innovation and catch up with the US and China.

On panels packed with senior executives, the potential benefits were made clear: detecting early signs of cancer, improving warnings of extreme weather, and optimising power distribution to save energy. Turning those promises into reliable services, however, still requires testing and validation.

Ireland's interest in these issues doesn't come purely from goodwill towards its fellow EU member states. The country hosts the European operations of several of the world's largest technology companies, and relies heavily on the taxation of multinational firms. Foreign-owned multinationals paid €28.8 billion in corporation tax in 2025, accounting for 87% of the total, excluding exceptional Apple tax proceeds. That includes pharmaceuticals and other sectors, not just technology.

This means Dublin has a clear interest in shaping European rules while keeping investors on board. But Europe's financial firepower remains far behind America's: Private AI investment reached $286 billion (about €253 billion) in the US in 2025, compared with $21 billion (€18.6 billion) in Europe, according to Stanford University's AI Index.

But does Europe even need to compete on the same terms? O'Sullivan thinks it should focus on its own strengths, including industrial robotics and specialised applications: "We have a unique kind of AI opportunity in Europe, and that's what we should back."

Using foreign models could help European businesses move faster. But it also leaves them dependent on suppliers elsewhere, which can change prices, restrict access and decide which capabilities to release.

EU representatives at the summit were quick to underline that rules were now in place to reap AI's benefits while mitigating its risks.

Michael McGrath, the Irish commissioner responsible for democracy, justice, the rule of law and consumer protection, spoke of a balance between AI's potential and the "appropriate guardrails" required to ensure trust.

The AI Act bans certain practices and imposes stronger requirements on sensitive uses. Its Omnibus amendments, in force since July 2026, aim to simplify compliance, though have als delyed some safeguards. Rules covering areas including employment and education now apply from December 2027; those for AI embedded in regulated products from August 2028.

The Commission has said that companies need time to adjust to new standards and guidance. Critics, however, believe protections being delayed under pressure to compete.

For O'Sullivan, regulation and innovation are not contradictory. "The idea that you can't have regulation and innovation is just a nonsense," he said, pointing to pharmaceuticals and medical devices.

For protesters outside, assurances made by politicians and tech firms ring hollow. Paul Murphy, an Irish lawmaker from the socialist People Before Profit party, argued that even though Europe's regulations are strong than those in the US, the bar remains low. With the latest amendments, he sees a move towards deregulation.

"Big tech is setting the pace and everybody's expected to just get on board," he told DW.

Instead, he wants civil society, trade unions and politicians to discuss how the technology should develop and how limited resources like water and electricity should be used.

Inside, union representative Fionnuala Ní Bhrógáin, one of the few critical voices on stage, questioned who would benefit from higher productivity. Optimization, she said, can put higher pressure on workers.

Murphy, too, fears an increased pressure to work faster, with less autonomy and more supervision. Whether an hour saved on emails becomes time to think, or leads to more assignments, depends on employers' decisions and workers' bargaining power.

O'Sullivan, on the other hand, said that AI removing mundane tasks gives employees "more time to think, and to be more creative in their jobs."

In Ireland, the question of resources is particularly pressing. Data centres used 23% of the country's metered electricity in 2025. That includes cloud storage and other services, not just AI. Murphy argued that their expansion raises household bills and undermines climate goals.

Renewable energy could help meet demand. But additional clean electricity must also replace fossil fuels, while housing and other industries need grid capacity too.

"The sustainability of AI is a huge problem," O'Sullivan agreed, calling for the sector to reduce its overall energy footprint.

Back inside the hall, even industry leaders acknowledged the need for boundaries. Hassabis, the chief scientist of Alphabet, called recent AI hacking incidents "early warning shots" and advocated international oversight. Microsoft president Smith said: "We need law. We need regulation."

Their warnings follow concrete failures. During cybersecurity evaluations in July, OpenAI models bypassed isolation controls, hacked the infrastructure surrounding the test, broke containment, and attacked a real company, Hugging Face, which hosts AI models and datasets. Had similar behavior occurred inside a hospital, power grid, or other critical system, the results could have been much worse. Anthropic separately disclosed unintended actions by its AI on real websites, including a ​false homicide tip to the Philadelphia police, despite instructions not to create accounts or submit anything destructive.

Speakers on and off the stage agreed: The incidents show just how difficult it can be to keep increasingly autonomous systems under control. Developers' assurances alone cannot guarantee safety. Nor can regulation eliminate every risk. But for O'Sullivan and Murphy, it remains essential.

Europe wants to shape AI without compromising on the benefits its promises.This balancing act is a difficult one, involving choices about investment, resources and the distribution of gains. Even a spectacular opening performance cannot dance around those questions.

#689. Scientists find a way to fight Alzheimer’s brain damage without removing toxic tau

Protecting brain cells from destruction remains one of the biggest challenges in treating Alzheimer's disease and related neurodegenerative conditions. These disorders, known as tauopathies, involve the buildup of abnormal tangles of a protein called tau inside the brain. As the tangles accumulate, nearby neurons begin to die. However, growing evidence suggests that tau itself may not be responsible for most of the damage. Instead, the immune system's reaction to the protein appears to play a major role in the loss of brain cells, memory, and independence.

Scientists at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis have now identified a promising strategy for interrupting this damaging immune response. In mice with tau accumulation similar to that seen in Alzheimer's, researchers used an antibody to block a protein called CXCR3, which helps immune cells known as T cells enter the brain. After several months of treatment, the number of T cells inside the brain had fallen by approximately half.

The results were striking. Compared with untreated mice, animals receiving the antibody retained roughly 40% more tissue in brain regions involved in memory and performed better on a memory test. These improvements occurred even though the amount of accumulated tau remained unchanged.

The findings were published September 28 in the journal Neuron.

"In tauopathies, including Alzheimer's disease, there's no treatment right now that actually decreases neurodegeneration," said David M. Holtzman, MD, senior author of the study and the Barbara Burton and Reuben M. Morriss III Distinguished Professor in the Department of Neurology at WashU Medicine. "If we can show that we're really decreasing brain cell death, it's certainly worth trying to pursue that pathway to prevent the most devastating consequences of these diseases."

Why Immune Cells May Be a Key to Alzheimer's Brain Damage

Current Alzheimer's treatments primarily focus on removing amyloid, another protein associated with the disease. Two approved medications, lecanemab and donanemab, target amyloid plaques that develop between brain cells during the early stages of Alzheimer's. These deposits interfere with communication between neurons, contribute to the formation of tau tangles inside cells, and ultimately lead to neuronal death.

Although these medications can slow cognitive decline, they have not been shown to prevent brain cells from dying. They also offer no treatment for primary tauopathies, conditions in which tau accumulates without the presence of amyloid. Alzheimer's is classified as a secondary tauopathy because both abnormal amyloid and tau are present.

Holtzman's laboratory has been investigating whether the immune system offers another way to address these diseases. In a 2023 study published in Nature, the researchers discovered that T cells enter the brains of mice with abnormal tau accumulation, concentrating in regions containing the most tau and contributing to neurodegeneration.

A related study published earlier this month in Nature Neuroscience provided another important clue. It showed that the signals directing these T cells originate in lymph nodes located outside the brain.

"For this therapeutic approach, if it is safe, you wouldn't have to design the drug to get into the brain -- which is a big deal since most molecules don't cross the blood-brain barrier well -- and you don't have to get rid of the tau to get this therapeutic effect."

Scientists Identify the Pathway That Guides Immune Cells Into the Brain

Despite these earlier discoveries, researchers still needed to understand how activated T cells found their way into the brain.

T cells navigate through the body by following chemical signals known as chemokines. Earlier experiments in Holtzman's laboratory had identified unusually high levels of one such signal, CXCL10, in mice with tau accumulation. Other researchers had also reported elevated CXCL10 levels in people with Alzheimer's disease.

Activated T cells carry a surface receptor called CXCR3 that recognizes CXCL10, allowing the cells to follow its chemical signals. This relationship suggested that the CXCL10 and CXCR3 pathway might be responsible for guiding harmful T cells into the brain.

To investigate, the researchers studied mice that developed abnormal tau deposits resembling those associated with Alzheimer's. The team included co-senior author Jason Ulrich, PhD, a professor of neurology at WashU Medicine, and first author Joshua T. Emmerson, PhD, a postdoctoral researcher in Holtzman's laboratory.

The experiments revealed that T cells could not infiltrate the brain when mice lacked either CXCL10 or its matching receptor, CXCR3, on T cells. This protection persisted even when the scientists deliberately triggered inflammation to stimulate an immune response.

Experimental Treatment Preserves Brain Tissue and Memory

With the pathway identified, the team tested whether blocking CXCR3 with an antibody could reduce brain damage.

The researchers selected young mice that already had tau deposits in their brains but had not yet experienced substantial loss of brain cells. They administered the antibody once every five days for three and a half months.

By the end of the experiment, treated mice had approximately half as many T cells in their brains as untreated animals. They also showed less evidence of neuronal injury and retained substantially more brain tissue, including roughly 40% more tissue in memory-related regions. Their performance on a memory test was also better.

Importantly, these protective effects occurred without any detectable reduction in tau tangles. The treated and untreated mice had similar amounts of abnormal tau, suggesting that limiting the immune response could reduce neurodegeneration even when the underlying protein deposits remain.

Another finding could have major implications for future drug development. The antibody reached the boundary of the brain but did not enter the brain tissue itself.

This suggests that it may be possible to reduce neurodegeneration by targeting immune activity from outside the brain. Such an approach could avoid one of the major difficulties in developing neurological treatments: getting drugs across the blood-brain barrier, which prevents many substances from entering brain tissue.

A Potential New Direction for Alzheimer's Treatment

Although the results are encouraging, the treatment has so far been tested only in mice. Additional research will be needed to determine whether the approach is safe and effective enough to evaluate in people.

Holtzman suggested that medications already used to control T cells in other diseases could provide a starting point. Certain treatments for multiple sclerosis and other autoimmune conditions, in which the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy tissue, might be worth investigating for their potential to reduce Alzheimer's-related brain damage.

This would represent a significant shift in treatment strategy. Rather than concentrating exclusively on the abnormal proteins associated with neurodegeneration, researchers could also target the immune responses that contribute to the destruction of neurons.

"Tauopathies aren't thought of as autoimmune disorders, so they haven't been treated the same way, but this study shows for the first time in an animal model that these diseases respond to a specific T-cell therapy," said Holtzman, who also directs WashU's Hope Center for Neurological Disorders and the Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center at WashU Medicine.

"For this therapeutic approach, if it is safe, you wouldn't have to design the drug to get into the brain -- which is a big deal since most molecules don't cross the blood-brain barrier well -- and you don't have to get rid of the tau to get this therapeutic effect."

The findings raise the possibility that protecting the brain from harmful immune activity, rather than removing tau itself, could become an important strategy for treating Alzheimer's and other tauopathies.

#688. Who is close to a yellow card suspension in Champions League?

Arsenal forward Kai Havertz is among the players to have received two yellow cards in this season's Champions League

Players will receive a one-match ban should they receive four yellow cards before the semi-finals of the Champions League.

This rule runs from the start of the league phase until the end of the quarter-finals. After then, all yellow cards are wiped out and players cannot be suspended for multiple yellow cards.

The same rules apply for both the Europa League and the Conference League.

During the opening three rounds of Champions League, 16 players received two yellow cards, meaning they are now two cautions away from a one-match ban.

Shakhtar Donetsk midfielder Marlon Gomes is on three yellow cards, putting him on the brink of a ban.

Players can also be suspended if they pick up six, eight or 10 yellow cards. This would also result in a one-match suspension every time a player hits an even number of yellow cards above four.

Red cards result in a one-match suspension, with Bayern Munich's Michael Olise missing his side's clash with Arsenal after being sent off against Viking in the previous round of fixtures.

Uefa may increase the length of a red card ban for serious offences such as violent conduct.

#673. The Bundestag has debated the genocide Germany carried out in Namibia a century ago. But will it spur progress on addressing colonial crimes?

Opposition parties have forced a debate in the Bundestag on the early-20th-century genocide Germany carried out in Namibia. Will this finally spur progress in addressing colonial crimes?

Gaob Daniel Timotheus Frederick was sitting in the front row of the visitors' gallery in the Bundestag this week. Wearing a suit and hat, he had traveled all the way from Namibia to see this parliamentary debate unfold. For almost 120 years, his family had been waiting for the return of human remains of their ancestor, Cornelius Frederick (sometimes spelled Fredericks), who had led the Nama resistance against German colonial rule and died on Shark Island in Germany's first concentration camp.

In the name of his great-grandfather and as a member of the Nama Traditional Leaders Association (NTLA), Frederick was there to demand an "unconditioned apology" from Germany.

Sitting next to him, in uniform and white gloves, with a carved lion on the handle of his walking stick, was the head of the Ovaherero Traditional Authority, Paramount Chief Mutjinde Katjiua. Speaking before the Bundestag debate, the chief said: "We call upon the Bundestag to recognize unequivocally the genocides committed against the Ovaherero and Nama peoples."

Many people wonder why the German parliament hasn't already done this — and why the government has not yet issued an official apology. There is a draft joint declaration with Namibia in which the German government acknowledges that "from today's perspective, [it] would be called genocide." But there is no German-Namibian reconciliation agreement. The evidence is overwhelming: From 1904 to 1908, the German Empire waged a war of extermination against the Indigenous people of southwestern Africa, including the Ovaherero (also known as the Herero), the Nama, the Damara and the San. At least 100,000 people were killed, and just as many displaced. Many were subjected to forced labor and forced prostitution. The land seizures and expropriation carried out at the time continue to affect their descendants today.

Jürgen Zimmerer, a professor of history at the University of Hamburg whose research focuses on Africa, told DW that the Bundestag and government lack the will to confront this era in German history. "There are Bundestag resolutions on the Armenian genocide, one on the Holodomor [in Ukraine], and one on the genocide against the Yazidis [by the "Islamic State" in Iraq]. But there is no recognition of the genocide against the Herero and Nama by the German Bundestag." If there were any political will to do so, Zimmerer said, it could happen very quickly.

The socialist Left Party initiated the Bundestag debate by drafting a resolution calling for full recognition and compensation in collaboration with representatives of the descendants. The goal: "restorative justice." Paramount Chief Katjiua has rejected the existing agreement between the Namibian and German governments. "Development aid cannot substitute for comprehensive reparations," he said.

Since 2021, the governments have been negotiating a joint declaration that pledges €1.1 billion ($1.24 billion) in reconstruction aid to Namibia over 30 years. Once the agreement is signed, Germany's president is set to issue a formal apology.

But the Foreign Ministry warned: "Legal claims to compensation cannot be derived from the recognition of genocide. With these negotiations — which are unique worldwide — Namibia and Germany have broken new ground and are pioneers in confronting their colonial past."

Political scientist Andreas Mehler, the director of the Anton Bergstraesser Institute at the University of Freiburg, said the government's plan sounded like "checkbook diplomacy."

Zimmerer also said there was a financial calculation: "If we were to recognize an obligation to provide reparations to the families of those killed — given the millions of victims of World War II, the victims of the Germans — it would ruin us."

Particular attention will be paid to establishing a dignified memorial site and the repatriation of human remains."

Compared with the efforts to address colonialism led by the SPD-led government from 2021 to 2025, "the issue has lost visibility," said Alexander Stroh-Steckelberg, a professor of political scientist at the University of Bayreuth who specializes in Africa.

Zimmerer has an even more critical take: "I wouldn't even call it a stalemate anymore. I believe this government is rolling back the progress that has been made in terms of raising awareness of colonial injustices."

Much remains to be done, as far the descendants of the Ovaherero are concerned: The remains of tens of thousands of people are believed to still be in Germany. Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick and other descendants of the victims have been fighting to heal these wounds for generations.

In March, Germany established a coordination council, headed by the Foreign Ministry, which stated that the government did not wish to "dictate to the countries and societies of origin which cultural artifacts and human remains they should reclaim, what priorities they should set or how restitutions should be handled."

Serap Güler, a state minister in the Foreign Ministry and the chair of the coordination council, defends the government's actions so far. Recounting her own official trips — she has already been to Togo, and next week she plans to travel to Rwanda and Burundi — she said: "Everywhere I go, I receive recognition for our sincere and trusting approach to the past — as well as for our determined commitment to a future of freedom, security and prosperity."

Zimmerer said Germany "has earned a reputation as a country that takes a critical view of its own history and learns from its mistakes" — which gives it "enormous cultural capital" on the world stage. But he is concerned that this capital is currently being squandered.

The government's memorial policy does not appear to address colonialism. Zimmerer even detects an attempt to "marginalize German colonial crimes" in the recent proposal by Minister of State for Culture Wolfram Weimer to create a dignified memorial site in Bremen. He notes that victim groups have not been included in Weimer's proposal and said the colonial capitals of the German Empire — Berlin and Hamburg — must also become sites of remembrance.

Ninety-five percent of the colonial troops set sail from Hamburg's Baakenhafen, including Commander Lothar von Trotha, who issued the orders to exterminate the Ovaherero and Nama. According to Zimmerer, the fact that a new opera house — rather than a memorial site — is being built at Baakenhafen constitutes a staggering case of "art-washing of history." Mehler also hopes that a productive debate on memorial sites will still take place and that, overall, "many more places will come to terms with colonial history."

Meanwhile, Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick continues to call for a collective effort to come to terms with the past: "Let us give opportunity to the affected communities that are being represented by us to have a seat on the table to discuss with them the rest of the way."

Editor’s Note:  Eric Tourigny is a lecturer in historical archaeology at Newcastle University, UK. His research interprets osteological and material culture remains alongside historic texts to examine changing human-animal relationships in Europe and North America over the past 500 years. The views expressed in this commentary are his own. Read more opinion on CNN.

Why do we bury our departed loved ones in a cemetery? A primary purpose is to provide survivors with an opportunity to grieve and gain a sense of closure. The cemetery is a place for the living as much as it is a place for the dead.

But what about when the dearly departed are not human – but our pets?

The Spanish city of Barcelona recently announced that it will be investing in the establishment of the country’s first public pet cemetery. Set to open next year, it will offer both burials and cremations – with an estimated 7,000 carried out each year.

For me, as someone who has spent years researching the development of pet cemeteries elsewhere in the world, this news came as a shock. Barcelona is a densely populated city with limited privately owned land – one where 50% of families own a pet.

How did a city that is home to 180,000 dogs not already have a public pet cemetery? Until now, the service was only provided by the private sector, according to Barcelona’s Councilor for Climate Emergency and Ecological Transition, Eloi Badia. He added that the municipal-funded initiative was sparked by “constant public demand.”

After all, public pet cemeteries have been around in Europe and the Americas since the late 19th century. Britain’s first public pet cemetery appeared in London’s Hyde Park in 1881. New York’s Hartsdale pet cemetery was founded in 1896, followed a few years later by Paris’ ornate Cimetière des Chiens in 1899.

I became interested in the history of modern pet burial practices while investigating the archaeological record of a centuries-old house in Toronto. I came across a (very) large dog buried in the back garden which, according to the historical record, was occupied between 1840 and 1870.

This dog survived to an elderly age but, sadly, suffered from degenerative joint disease and severe infections during his last months. His ailments progressed to such a state that suggest he received some level of care in his final weeks. He then was buried in a personal plot behind the family home.

This elderly dog led me to think about the different ways people interact with the bodies of their pets after death. Could this behavior be reflective of the relationships they held with their animals in life? In this instance, why take the time to carefully bury a dog in its own space when other, arguably easier options existed?

This, after all, was an era when people often disposed of their dead pets in the river, or might have sold their bodies for meat and skin.

Good hygiene is an obvious reason to choose a burial – no one wants decomposing animal bodies in the street or their garden – but that wouldn’t immediately warrant a personalized, dedicated burial and gravestone.

The most straight forward option would be to dispose of a deceased animal with the household waste. But such treatment would evidently feel less ceremonious and would not offer appropriate emotional closure to what was likely an important relationship.

Like the burial of people, the burial of pets is an intimate cultural practice, one that changes over time and is reflective of the changing relationships that a society has with its cherished creatures.

My study of historic gravestones and epitaphs in Britain from the Victorian period to today shows this changing human-animal relationship. In the 19th century, gravestones were often dedicated to a “loving friend” or “devoted companion,” suggesting pets were mostly considered important friends.

By the early 20th century, pets had become members of the family – evidenced by the appearance of family surnames on the gravestones, and loving epitaphs written by “Mummy and Daddy.”

Society’s changing attitude towards the role of animals in the afterlife can also be found. Fast-forward a few decades later, and gravestones were more likely to reference a reunion then earlier ones. For example, the owners of Denny, the “brave little cat” buried in an east London cemetery in 1952, wrote on his epitaph “God bless until we meet again.”

I wonder what the epitaphs in Barcelona’s new cemetery will reveal about modern Catalan relationships with animals.

Over time, our ways of treating our animal dead appear to reflect an even closer relationship in life. Once strictly forbidden by law, the last decade saw many jurisdictions, like the state of New York, allowing the co-burial of cremated animals and people, which will undoubtedly lead to changing funerary and commemoration practices for both humans and animals.

For me, the most remarkable similarity between modern and historic pet cemeteries is the striking evidence for the heartbreak and taboos around grieving for animals.

The connection that someone has with their pet can be just as strong and just as significant as their relationships with other humans. Yet today, as in over 100 years ago, individuals continue to struggle to find the appropriate outlet to express their pain, hiding heartache for fear of the social repercussions that might come with public acknowledgment of the existence of such a bond.

The RSPCA reassures the public on its website that they should not feel shame for their grief. In the UK, charities like the Blue Cross and Rainbow Bridge Pet Loss Grief Center offer counseling to bereaved humans.

Close relationships between people and animals have existed for millennia, but in western European cultures, there were few acceptable ways to mourn that relationship. As society becomes more accepting of the importance of human-animal relationships to our collective wellbeing, it is not surprising to see us follow rituals resembling those we use to mourn the loss of our closest human relationships.

At an estimated cost of €200 ($217) per service at the new Barcelona cemetery, it is important to acknowledge that this opportunity to grieve will not be financially available to everyone in the city.

This will not be a space for all of the city’s pets. Pet owners may opt to keep cremated remains within their home or spread out ashes in a meaningful location instead. Online forums and digital pet cemeteries also provide other opportunities to commemorate the relationship and express grief.

Whether one chooses a pet cemetery or not, there are many acceptable ways to express your grief – and to remember your relationship with the important animals in your life.
