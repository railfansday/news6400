#2746. Nearly half the stocks in the S&P 500 are at cross purposes with the rest of the market

Nearly half of the stocks in the S&P 500 are moving against the index with a negative beta, an unusual divergence that is becoming increasingly difficult to ignore.

About 45% of S&P 500 stocks have a negative three-month beta, according to a recent note from Goldman Sachs. The data closely aligns with CNBC's finding that nearly 40% of S&P 500 stocks had a negative three-month beta versus the index, while 17% have a negative one-year beta, based on weekly returns.

Beta measures how a stock moves relative to the rest of the market. A negative beta means an individual stock's returns moved in the opposite direction of the S&P 500 over the measured period.

The surge in stocks with a negative beta dovetails with other unusual market signals. The S&P rallied 1.5% last Monday. The same day 30 stocks touched a 52-week low while just 7 scored a new high. The last time the S&P 500 gained at least 1% while sitting within 1% of a new 52-week high and new lows outnumbered new highs was in December 1999, right before the very top of the dot-com boom, according to Jason Goepfert, founder of SentimenTrader.

The two indicators show that market indexes can remain at or close to records despite wide divergences among individual stocks.

The yawning gap largely reflects how concentrated the S&P 500 has become, according to Adam Turnquist, chief technical strategist at LPL Financial.

Mega-cap technology companies carry an outsized weight in the benchmark, meaning a strong performance from a small number of stocks can drive the index even when many others are moving the other way.

"It only takes a few of those mega caps names to work, and a lot of the smaller weighted stocks don't need to work," Turnquist told CNBC, pointing to unusually low correlations among S&P 500 stocks.

The same dynamic explains why the broader index can look relatively calm even when individual stocks are making large moves, said Bradley Krom, director of investing strategy at WisdomTree.

"Beta is a function of correlation and volatility," Krom said. When stocks experience large moves at different times and for different reasons, those moves can largely offset one another at the index level.

In July this year, Alliance Bernstein, using one-year trailing returns, found an unprecedented share of U.S. stocks displaying negative beta as AI winners powered market gains.

Semiconductor makers, hardware companies and other AI infrastructure beneficiaries have benefited from enormous capital spending, while companies outside the AI trade have struggled to keep up.

"But a narrow market can also distort the signal investors receive from index returns. When a handful of companies dominate performance, many financially sound businesses may lag or even decline, simply because they aren't tied directly to the most powerful market narrative," wrote Kurt Feuerman, chief investment officer of Select U.S. Equity Portfolios at AllianceBernstein.

Energy stocks with negative beta are being driven by different forces.

"Another part of the other story is energy. That's been pronounced this year: higher oil prices, higher energy stocks and then the rest of the market trades lower," said Turnquist, seeing energy as an important part of the negative-beta story, alongside more defensive sectors.

Earlier this month, Evercore ISI used a six-month measure to call out 115 S&P 500 stocks with negative beta, a list skewed toward energy, utilities and consumer staples. The investment bank called the energy sector a "synthetic S&P 500 put option" because of the way it has reacted to geopolitical pressure.

If market leadership broadens out, Turnquist believes the number of negative-beta stocks could decline. But he expects dispersion to remain elevated as investors become remain selective toward beneficiaries of AI spending and seek returns there.

Krom at WisdomTree expects the recent extreme readings to eventually revert to the mean. Similar spikes appeared around the 1999-2000 dot-com bubble, he said, when market concentration and large moves in a narrow group of stocks also triggered unusual divergences.

Turnquist pushed back on comparing today with the dot-com era, leading tech companies now are more mature businesses with established revenue and products. Krom is on the same page. He said the individual pieces driving returns don't have the same historical relationship they've had in the past.

"It is not the same market environment now versus 2000," Krom said. The negative betas seen today boil "down to the amount of market concentration."

#2744. CoreWeave’s market confidence erodes as bond yields spike and stock sinks

CoreWeave is facing a crisis of confidence on Wall Street that's playing out in real time among equity and bond investors.

With interest rates spiking and AI build-out concerns mounting, debt-heavy neoclouds like CoreWeave are being viewed by many market participants as the most vulnerable trade in the artificial intelligence space.

"Sentiment is decidedly negative," Andrew Keches, a credit analyst at Barclays, wrote in a note to clients on Tuesday, referring broadly to corporate debt in tech. Keches highlighted the "speed at which AI vibes are shifting on a day-to-day basis."

CoreWeave, which went public in March of last year and reached a peak market cap of about $90 billion three months later, is now valued at roughly $31 billion, with its stock down 36% this month after plunging 10% on Thursday. Its unsecured debt, meanwhile, is sporting a yield of about 15%.

The spread on CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps, which are insurance against default, is up 179% in the past year and 39% in October. It last stood at 1,145 basis points, or 11.45%.

The story is playing out across the neocloud sector. Shares of Nebius are down 33% this month, while IREN's stock is down 38%. In Australia, shares of data center operator NextDC plunged 40% on Wednesday after the company slashed its annual earnings forecast. Earlier this month, Australian data center company Firmus scrapped its planned initial public offering, citing market volatility and conditions.

U.K. neocloud Nscale is now under the microscope as investors wait to see whether the Nvidia-backed company will go through with its IPO. The company filed to go public on the New York Stock Exchange last month.

A representative from Nscale declined to comment.

CoreWeave is the biggest of the pack, and is intimately tied to the U.S. AI market. Nvidia is its top institutional investor, with a stake of about 9%, according to FactSet. And more than two-thirds of CoreWeave's revenue last year came from Microsoft.

That's put a spotlight on CoreWeave's financials and its ability to remain viable. The company has about $20.7 billion in debt coming due through 2030 and another $14.9 billion thereafter, with the prospects of higher interest rates on future financing.

Sources on credit desks told CNBC that the high-yield debt market is effectively on hold until after the midterm elections in two weeks given ongoing concerns about the political pushback that data centers are facing. One source, who asked not to be named because of the sensitivity of the matter, said fixed income investors "want permitting and regulatory requirements to be substantially resolved" before they commit financing.

"Given recent turbulence, pausing makes sense," Seth Meyer, global head of client portfolio management at Janus Henderson, said in an interview.

CoreWeave CEO Mike Intrator, who co-founded the company in 2017 initially as a crypto-mining venture, sent a memo to employees on Thursday, which CNBC obtained, to try and tamp down concern. Intrator stressed that the company maintains a range of funding options.

"Our financing model combines infrastructure-backed borrowing, corporate debt, equity, and customer prepayments," Intrator wrote. He added that the company has "additional flexibility to raise equity over time as market conditions and our capital needs warrant."

CoreWeave had $5.5 billion of cash and equivalents at the end of the second quarter. The company is expected to report third-quarter results next month. Analysts expect negative free cash flow in the period to swell to $10.1 billion, the highest since the company started reporting quarterly financials, according to FactSet.

One issue being debated heading into the report is how much more capital CoreWeave needs to raise. Rob Oliver, a software analyst at Baird, is estimating roughly $30 billion in 2027.

"Earnings may address financing measures to shore up confidence," Oliver said in an interview. "But they wouldn't need that capital until the first quarter."

Another question is whether Nvidia will step in if necessary?

Nvidia, which supplies the graphics processing units that CoreWeave rents out, invested $2 billion in January for a 10% stake. The purchase price was $87.20 a share. The stock is now trading at $61.82 even after jumping 6% the day of the announcement.

While Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang said last week that CoreWeave doesn't need a backstop, some investors say the chip giant's further participation may be necessary if credit conditions continue to worsen.

Nvidia didn't immediately respond to a request for comment.

Analysts at Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan Chase and Barclays have put out bullish notes on the company this week, highlighting the company's GPU-backed loans and its solid list of investment-grade customers like Microsoft and Meta. Goldman analysts emphasized the company's strength in the market relative to many of its neocloud competitors.

"Not all neoclouds are created equal," they wrote, in upgrading their rating to buy from hold. "CoreWeave is likely in the top quartile of neoclouds, if not the top decile, when measured on performance and ability to execute."

#2738. Devin Booker attempts zero threes in Suns’ season-opening 41-point loss

The official debut of Devin Booker wearing number 15 is a game I'm sure he'd rather forget. His team lost by 41 points in the season opener against the Portland Trail Blazers, trailing by as many as 52. Hopefully I forget it too.

You can understand why Portland came out and blitzed the Suns. It was an emotional night for the fan base, one they'd been waiting more than a season to experience. After being bought out by Milwaukee in Bradley Beal fashion (the 'ole 5-year waive and streeeeeetch), Damian Lillard returned to the Trail Blazers in the summer of 2025. And unleashed it was. The Moda Center was in a frenzy. Add newly acquired Ja Morant making his Portland debut, and the recipe was ripe for an emotional tidal wave that the Suns would have to overcome. Overcome it, they did not.

"Incredibly emotional to start the game before the jump ball was even tipped," head coach Jordan Ott said after the game. "They did the better job of using that to their advantage."

Phoenix managed only 12 points in the opening quarter and posted its lowest point total in a season opener since 2017. Coincidentally, that team also lost to the Portland Trail Blazers, surrendering 124 points while scoring only 76. In that game, a third-year Devin Booker managed 12 points on 6-of-17 shooting, including 0-of-3 from beyond the arc.

And I know it probably doesn't mean much in the grand scheme of things, but at least in that game, Booker attempted three-pointers. In this game? Not one. Zip. Zero. Not a single three-point attempt.

Does anybody else find that strange? Odd? Your team is getting its teeth kicked in, desperate for points, and…nothing? You're supposed to be the tip of the offensive spear. You're the $57 million man. And in a game where your team needs the hemorrhaging to be stopped, you don't attempt a single shot from beyond the arc?

It was only the 17th time in Booker's 738 career games that he failed to attempt a three-pointer, and the first time he'd done so since November 15, 2024. And wouldn't you know it? That was another loss in which the Suns scored only 83 points. Fun Fact: That was the night that, following an abysmal performance and the fact that I watched it whilst waiting for the Mariah Carey Christmas Special to start, we introduced Mariah Carey as a Bright Side Baller option.

Perhaps the opportunities never presented themselves. Perhaps the smothering defense of former Suns draft pick Toumani Camara (palm, meet face) was too overwhelming for Booker to get a shot off. Perhaps he's become trigger-shy after posting the second-worst three-point shooting percentage of his career last season (33.0%), compounded by the fact that he went 2-of-16 from deep during the preseason (12.5%). But when you juxtapose that against what we saw from Damian Lillard, who went 6-of-10 from beyond the arc on his way to 25 points, it only added to the embarrassment and frustration of the night.

I'm not asking Devin Booker to become Damian Lillard. I'm asking him to recognize what the team needs from him, especially when everything around him is falling apart. We know Booker is an elite midrange scorer. We know he's never been a high-volume three-point shooter. But at what point does that become a limitation rather than an explanation? The NBA has evolved, and the best offensive players have evolved with it. Booker is entering his 12th season, and we're still discussing his reluctance to consistently threaten defenses from beyond the arc.

"You can always do everything better in a loss like that," Booker told Duane Rankin of AZ Central in the locker room post-massacre. "Trying to find the flow. We have some work to do and we have some things to learn from."

There's a significant difference between going 0-of-8 and never attempting one. One represents a willingness to take shots the offense needs, even if they aren't falling. The other raises questions about why those shots aren't being taken in the first place. I'm not advocating for contested 30-footers. I'm asking why a player of his caliber can't generate or recognize opportunities from three over the course of 29 minutes.

My concern extends beyond a single game or a single statistical category. It's about whether the version of Devin Booker we're watching today is capable of being the offensive engine this team needs him to be. And after last night, I think that's a perfectly reasonable question to ask.

Ultimately, this may have been the result of a bad matchup against a big, long, physical Portland team fueled by the emotional return of one of the franchise's greatest and most beloved players. But it also brings to the surface some familiar concerns about who Devin Booker is and what he can provide from beyond the arc at this stage of his career.

Perhaps these are merely the ramblings and musings of a frustrated fanalyst who went to open his Christmas present, only to discover that instead of the gift he'd been hoping for, he received the proverbial lump of coal. All that excitement, all that anticipation surrounding the start of a new season, and it was immediately ripped away from the fan base.

Will Devin Booker have great scoring nights this season? Absolutely. Was this a one-game aberration? We certainly hope so. Maybe Wednesday was an aberration. But if the Suns are going to have any chance of making this season meaningful, they need Booker to be more than an elite midrange scorer. They need him to be a complete offensive threat, and that starts with being willing to take the shots the modern NBA demands.

The Federal Reserve Board on Wednesday finalized changes to enhance the transparency and public accountability of its stress test and reduce volatility in its stress test-related capital requirements. The two final rules are largely similar to the proposed rules from 2025. The Board also requested comment on a proposal to better capture differences in banks' business models to generate fee income.

The Board conducts stress tests to ensure that large banks are sufficiently capitalized and able to lend to households and businesses even in a severe recession. In December 2024, the Board announced it would modify the test to improve its resilience.

"The stress test is an essential component of our regulatory capital framework," said Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle W. Bowman. "Today's changes preserve its resilience by ensuring that it is transparent, granular, and risk-sensitive. The public will now have greater assurance that the risks banks take will be reflected appropriately in their stress test losses and their capital requirements."

The first final rule requires the Board to invite public input annually on the stress test scenarios and any material model changes. It also updates the framework that guides the design of the hypothetical scenarios and adopts the models that will be used for the 2027 stress test. In addition, the final rule makes several adjustments to the stress test calendar. The final rule also updates the global market shock component, which is a set of shocks to specific market components applied to banks with large trading books. These banks will now be tested against two global market shock components each year, and the Board will use the shock that produces the largest losses for each firm to calculate the firm's stress test results.

The second final rule requires the Board, when calculating stress capital buffer requirements, to average the results from the two most recent annual supervisory stress tests for firms subject to the stress test in both years. The Board will begin averaging stress capital buffer requirements in 2028 to ensure that only models incorporating public input are used in the calculation.

The proposal would request public comment on a revision to the Board's noninterest income model that would improve the stress test's ability to capture business model diversity across firms. If adopted, these adjustments would replace the current noninterest income model that projects each bank's fee income under stress. Comments are due 60 days after publication in the Federal Register.

Together, the changes are likely to reduce year-over-year volatility in capital requirements by approximately 50 percent and are not expected to materially affect aggregate capital requirements.

Iran has one main source of money: oil. Without oil, there is little money. Without money, there is little ability to buy the weapons, drones, and missiles that cause havoc and spread fear in the Gulf. Iran is going to grow more desperate, which makes Iran even more dangerous. In just the last week or so, multiple ships have been attacked. The point is this is not just a grim recap of where we stand, but an attempt to understand where we're going. As I've said and written before… I have no idea. I'm not sure that anyone really does. The best we can do is gather as much information and intelligence as possible and make educated guesses. Sorry to say it, but right now, guessing is what many are doing. The markets remain one good or bad headline away from a $10 move in oil in either direction.

While we wait, oil markets stay elevated. The paper price of oil is around $100 per barrel. Most companies and countries don't use "paper" oil. As I wrote last week, the price of an actual physical barrel of oil is much, much higher than what you see on your screens. In some cases, it may be double.

Another of the few things we are clear on is that demand destruction is not here yet. All prices are up, and demand is staying relatively consistent. Sky-high diesel prices across Europe are going to hit demand, but they have not landed a knockout blow yet. $100 oil and $10 diesel are not what they used to be. What now is that we keep as calm as we can and carry on. Every week, I write Power Insider knowing tomorrow could be an entirely different headline. #2713. Europe’s winter energy crunch may already be underway. Two U.S. stocks that may benefit

Oil flows through Hormuz are rising, more ships are safely getting through the Strait, and oil prices seem to be coming down — at least for now. Goldman Sachs wrote Wednesday that Persian Gulf exports have "recovered to their 2025 average after doubling in September." The firm expects Brent crude prices to "moderate" to $85 per barrel by year's end. That's good news on oil, and maybe good news for both the United States and China.

My take → I know the term "reopen the Strait of Hormuz" is popular, but I just can't bring myself to say it. Hormuz is an international waterway, and no one country can "close" it. Yes, Iran — or the Houthis in Yemen — can threaten ships in Hormuz or near the Red Sea, making it more dangerous to pass through. But no country has the power to "open" or "close" a public, international waterway. I get that I'm the outlier in saying that — and I wouldn't have it any other way.

The not-so-good news involves natural gas and Europe. Despite the improvement around Iran and oil, it could still be a long, expensive winter for our friends in Germany and much of Europe.

For five years, I've been highlighting a few of the continent's major energy challenges: how more and more U.K. families have had to choose between heating and eating — aka "energy poverty"; how rising electricity costs are hurting industrial companies; and how American liquefied natural gas has become a type of "Marshall Plan for energy," helping make sure the lights can stay on across Europe.

One big worry I highlighted in my reporting was that parts of Europe might face critical natural-gas shortfalls at times during the year. Thankfully, those worries proved wrong. The worst-case scenarios did not happen, largely due to the weather. Much of Europe has enjoyed a multi-year run of fairly benign weather: not too hot, not too cold. That enabled countries to preserve precious natural-gas storage.

This summer, Western Europe broke the 2003 record for its hottest summer on record. And while much of the continent does not use air conditioning at the same levels as the U.S. or some other countries, cooling does exist — and it's getting more and more common. So when temperatures climb, companies and consumers want to crank it. A/C is great, but it's a huge power drain. As power demand rose, natural-gas inventories were drawn down.

The Swiss Federal Office of Energy tracks natural-gas storage levels against the five-year average (for more, click here). The chart below highlights that the European Union's storage level is at its lowest point in those five years.

As you can see HERE, Germany is in slightly worse shape. France, not shown, is as well.

Despite lower storage levels and higher prices, European leaders remain seemingly unbothered. A group representing the coalition writes that, despite "lower storage levels compared to historical levels, the Commission and EU countries reconfirmed that EU gas supply remains stable."

One: Natural-gas storage remains "stable" only because of American exports. U.S. companies are saving Europe from an even more dire energy scenario. This is not hyperbole. The natural gas liquefied and loaded onto ships in Texas, Louisiana, and other ports is the difference between Europe having "stable" supplies and Europe struggling to meet demand. The sabotage of Qatari gas has made Europe even more reliant on LNG from the U.S. and — wait for it — Russia.

It's hard to believe, but Europe is still buying billions of dollars' worth of natural gas from Russia, albeit via ships and not the ill-fated Nord Stream pipeline. My producer Harriet Taylor and I stood incredulously and watched cargoes of Putin's gas steam into the Port of Rotterdam. Many believe because Nord Stream was blown up that Moscow isn't making any money off Europe's energy needs. Think again.

European leaders still argue they will cut out Russian LNG by January 1 and end all contracts to buy Russian gas by late next year. Color me skeptical. Given that people tend to get angry when they don't have electricity or heat, I would venture to say that Russia will be selling plenty of gas into Europe well beyond these feel-good headlines. Time will tell.

Two: It's not just about supply; it's also about price. On the ICE exchange, prices for October futures on European-traded natural gas are more than double those for February. While many power companies have already locked in purchase costs, anyone needing to buy gas on the open market is going to face the double shock of higher gas prices and higher shipping costs to get the LNG. For spot cargoes, Europe has to compete with buyers in Asia, with both sides ready to pay whatever they have to in order to keep the lights and heat on this winter.

If you're looking for a silver lining, it's that prices have come down in recent days, buoyed by hopes of a real, longer-lasting peace deal that allows for risk-free shipping through Hormuz. Peace in the Middle East would be the best outcome on many levels.

Beyond that, it would also be hugely helpful if Europe had another relatively mild winter, reducing demand for heat and natural gas. This would keep storage levels sufficient and allow power companies and countries to get a better head start on refilling natural-gas storage for next year.

Much of that LNG supply is going to come from the United States. We have more natural gas than we can export. If you're looking to invest around this theme, two obvious names are exporters Cheniere Energy (LNG) and Venture Global (VG). Less obvious: the biggest holders of U.S. LNG capacity are based in France and the U.K. They are TotalEnergies (TTE) and Shell (SHEL).

TotalEnergies CEO Patrick Pouyanné — arguably the most important CEO in global oil and gas — spoke with us this week about Europe's energy challenges, prices, and more in a Power Insider interview.

Analyst Kim Fustier also sees "substantial upgrades" across both firms' oil and refining-margin outlooks, as well as huge cash generation and stock buybacks.

BP and TotalEnergies are not the only energy stocks the firm loves. It maintains its buy ratings on Shell (SHEL), Spain's Repsol (REP-DE), and Chevron (CVX). Fustier sees about 20% upside for her Buy-rated stocks.

#2712. A moment of Zen about the Spurs’ opening night loss to the Thunder

The Spurs are tied for last place with a bunch of bad teams. Other winless teams the Spurs are tied with include the Bulls, Bucks, Nets, Pelicans, Suns and Kings. Not a group to be associated with. And because of Tuesday's night's game, the Spurs main rival is undefeated on the season.

And yes, the Spurs' best player had a dreadful offensive game against the Thunder. Near the end of the second quarter, I asked my friend who was watching the game with me, "Does Victor have a basket yet?" The answer was "no". And he didn't actually make a "shot" all night – his one basket came in the second half on a dunk off a lob pass.

And yes, the Spurs second-longest tenured player, Devin Vassell, scored only 7 points in 31 minutes, on 25% shooting. Perhaps being forced to defend OKC bigs like Holmgren on the other end was a bit draining?

And yes, the Thunder's best player had a great offensive game. I was surprised to see that the box score had SGA at only 13 for 27 from the floor. It felt like he didn't miss any. It also felt like a bunch of shots were punctuated with "and one!" Which always sucks.

And yes, the Thunder's bigs looked pretty good. Punching bag Chet Holmgren outscored Victor Wembanyama 14-12 and blocked his shot at least once, looking not at all like the cowering Chet from last season's WC Finals. And the Thunder new kid, Aday Mara, played a very effective 12 minutes, making three times as many shots as Victor, and doing something never before seen: blocking a lob-dunk by our Frenchman. The Spanish dude might be a problem going forward.

And the Thunder defense looked very strong, not missing Lu Dort at all. Alex Caruso was his usual pesky self on D and also nailed both of this three-pointers. SGA was also great on D, getting three steals and three blocks – he is clearly ahead in the MVP race thus far in the season. (Though Anthony Edwards may want a word.)

Based on all of that, the consensus among the media, and presumably everyone who watched the game, is that the Thunder dominated the Spurs, "They just wanted it more". An understandable conclusion, especially because of how the Thunder ended last season, having to watch the young Spurs emotionally celebrate after Game Seven of the Western Conference Finals on their own floor. And then hearing about it all summer.

I agreed with the consensus until I calmed down and looked at the box score, as coaches (and former coaches) like to do.

When I look at the box score, I first look at extra possessions by each team – turnovers and offensive rebounds. The Spurs had two more turnovers (13-11) and the same number of offensive rebounds (10). OKC took two more shots from the field, but the Spurs shot five more free throws. The Spurs also shot a bit better on those free throws – 88% t0 80%. On two-point shots, the Spurs shot better – 26-56 as opposed the Thunder's 27-64. The Spurs also took more threes, 37-31. You can see where this is going. OKC won the game by 12 points because they shot significantly better on threes. They made four more threes than the Spurs on six less attempts. I checked my math with AI, and confirmed that four more three pointers equals 12 more points – the margin of victory in the game.

OKC outshot the Spurs from three largely because Jared McCain came off the bench to go 6-8 from three, with Caruso chipping in his two. If McCain shoots his career average, he only makes three three-pointers, not six. If Caruso misses one of his two, he is still at 50%. And if both of those things happen, OKC has 12 less points, the margin of victory in Game One of Eighty-Two. Not coincidentally, McCain was a game-high plus 20 in his 22 minutes, Caruso plus 16 in his 25 minutes.

All in all, despite the final score, with everything that went wrong for the Spurs and everything that went right for the Thunder, the game was actually quite even. So after carefully analyzing the box score, I felt a bit better about Tuesday night.

And then I looked at the Knicks-76ers box score, and felt worse again. Jalen Brunson shot 6-19 from the floor after seemingly making all his shots against the Spurs in the Finals. OG Anunoby went 1-10 from three, after shooting a bazillion percent from three (actually 50%) in the Finals. Now I am unhappy again. Stupid box scores.

#2711. Jobless claims near the lowest since the 1960s as low-fire trend continues

Employers continue to hold onto workers even as hiring remains weak, sending weekly jobless claims to around their lowest levels in records that stretch back nearly six decades.

Initial filings for unemployment insurance slipped to a seasonally adjusted 194,000 for the week ended Oct. 17, the Labor Department reported Thursday.

That marked another leg lower in the low-fire trend, with the total just off the 189,000 reported for the week of July 18 that was the lowest since September 1969. Claims have averaged 212,000 over the past year, and the four-week moving average is down to 197,750.

Those numbers come against an uncertain backdrop for the labor market.

Nonfarm payrolls rose by just 29,000 in September and have averaged a gain of just 69,000 a month in 2026. The unemployment rate rose to 4.2%, an increase largely due to a sharp spike in labor force participation. Layoffs have remained modest, declining by 61,000 in August, according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data released Sept. 29.

Over the prior 12 months, the layoff rate measured as a share of the total workforce fell to 1%, down 0.2 percentage point during the period. Job openings were relatively flat during the period.

"Despite extremely pessimistic views expressed in consumer confidence surveys regarding the economy overall, countless headlines describing job cuts from a variety of businesses, and the phantom job-stealing power of AI, the claims data suggests that labor market conditions are stable," Thomas Simons, chief U.S. economist at Jefferies, said in a client note.

The data comes during the reference week that the BLS uses to calculate the payrolls growth number. The October jobs report will be released Nov. 6.

Federal Reserve officials gather next week to issue their latest decision on interest rates. Policymakers in recent days have characterized the labor market as a source of general strength and focused more of their attention on the nettlesome inflation problem that has kept the pace of price increases well above the central bank's 2% target.

Markets expect the Fed to hold off on a rate hike in October but then to institute another quarter percentage point increase in December.

(This is the Warren Buffett Watch newsletter, news and analysis on all things Warren Buffett and Berkshire Hathaway. You can sign up here to receive it every Friday evening in your inbox.)

Berkshire Hathaway added to its bet on Lennar this week, raising its stake in the homebuilder to 11.2%, although the pace of its buying appears to be slowing.

In an SEC filing late Wednesday, Berkshire disclosed it bought a total of $53.6 million of Lennar's Class A shares and another $329,000 of the super-voting B shares on Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday.

That puts the position at a total of 26.6 million shares valued at $2.1 billion based on Friday's closing prices.

At the end of the second quarter, Berkshire owned a 5.4% stake of 13.4 million shares valued at $1.2 billion. That was an increase of almost 30% from the 10.3 million shares it owned at the end of the first quarter, which was, in turn, an increase of 43% from 7.2 million shares at the end of last year.

As the stock continued to falter in the third quarter, Berkshire continued to buy in secret until its stake hit 10% in mid-September, triggering an SEC rule requiring it to disclose additional moves within two business days.

That prompted two filings last week revealing Berkshire bought almost $349 million of Lennar shares on six out of the seven trading days between Sept. 17 and Sept. 25.

That's an average of $58 million per calendar day.

This week the average is just under $18 million through Wednesday.

Since Berkshire has not made a new filing as of publication of the newsletter Friday night, it appears Berkshire didn't do any buying on Thursday.

That would put the 4-day average at $13.5 million.

We won't know if there was activity Friday until after Monday's filing deadline.

While Berkshire is making a bet that Lennar will benefit from a long-term recovery in the nation's troubled housing market, Morgan Stanley is pessimistic about the stock's more immediate future.

It started coverage Thursday with an "underweight" rating and a price target of $65 per share. That's a drop of almost 19% from today's close of $79.81, a 2.8% decline for the day.

#2705. The Marcus Sasser opening game experience leaves Mavericks fans wanting more

The Dallas Mavericks got blown out 128-110 against the Houston Rockets on Wednesday night in their season opener. The game was actually quite competitive for three of the four quarters, but an abysmal second quarter saw the Mavs largely get dismantled. Only able to muster a paltry 12 points while yielding 38, Dallas couldn't overcome the second quarter swoon despite outscoring Houston by 14 points in the second half.

Cooper Flagg looked amazing in a 32-point effort and his pairing with Kyrie Irving – who dropped 15 points of his own on 6-of-9 shooting – made for some enjoyable basketball. Aside from Flagg and Irving, Naji Marshall put together a solid game off the bench (16 points, 6 assists and 2 rebounds), but the performances largely fell off from there. Then there was Marcus Sasser.

Sasser was acquired in the large six-team deal that also netted Dallas Santi Aldama and Tarik Biberovic back in July. He was well-regarded when he entered the league during the 2023-2024 season with the Detroit Pistons, but saw his role progressively dwindle as his tenure in Detroit went on. His minutes per game dropped year-over-year, and along with that, his points, assists and rebounds per game decreased year-over-year as well. The emergence of Cade Cunningham certainly didn't help Sasser's cause, but what he would bring to the Mavericks remained a question mark.

A Red Oak, TX native, Sasser played college ball at the University of Houston, so even though the season opener was a road game, Sasser surely felt right at home. It's reasonable to believe the venue contributed to his comfort level, but after missing the first three preseason games, his performance on Wednesday night was more than impressive.

With 6:50 remaining in the first quarter, Sasser checked in for Max Christie. Just over a minute later, he dished his first assist to P.J. Washington. 30 seconds after that, he canned his first shot; a three-pointer. He then checked out at the 3:37 mark.

Sasser got about five minutes of burn and managed to get a steal in what proved to be a nightmare quarter for the Mavs. Even with little statistical output, he looked poised on the floor and showed he could orchestrate a bit.

Sasser checked in, again for Christie, with 6:48 remaining in the frame and played out the quarter. He committed a foul, then drilled a three and showed off his midrange game with a made floater from 14 feet out.

Coach May must have liked what he was seeing as the Mavs chipped away, because Sasser opened the fourth quarter. He missed his first shot from 19 feet, then canned another three. He then hit another floater from 12 feet, got fouled and hit the free throw for a personal six-point run. He had a heat check with a missed three on the next possession, but that was the last time he would miss a shot. He checked out, then returned with four minutes left in the game. From there, he committed a turnover, then grabbed a rebound which he turned into another three-pointer five seconds later.

On the night, Sasser dropped in 17 points on 6-of-8 shooting (4-of-5 from deep), and had a single rebound, assist and steal. All this in just under 24 minutes of play. Is it reasonable to expect this type of performance every night, or was this a byproduct of playing back in his old college town? We have 81 games left to find out, but what we saw Wednesday night was very promising. The Mavericks are somewhat guard-light and we should expect Irving to have his fair share of rest games throughout the season. They are going to need a true guard who can both fill that gap and otherwise come off the bench as a viable sixth man. After the small sample size of Wednesday, it's not difficult to imagine Sasser bringing the heat in the second unit, and it's especially intriguing to think of what he may be able to do alongside Marshall if the latter continues to come off the bench.

The cherry on top? Sasser had this to say about his highly efficient performance: "I feel like I could have played better defensively, especially impacting the ball a little more."

Sasser has an insane 6'7" wingspan relative to his 6'1" height. The physical tools are there for a rangy defender, and the fact that his focus was on what he could have done better, as opposed to what he did well, also bodes well. Dallas may have unearthed a hidden gem from the team's own backyard in Sasser.

#2702. Lawsuit accuses Trump of illegally profiting off foreign crypto deals

A lawsuit filed Thursday accuses President Donald Trump and his family's crypto company of "corruptly profiting" off more than $2 billion in digital currency-related deals, violating constitutional restrictions on accepting foreign money.

"No president in American history has profited from his office so brazenly," alleges the federal civil complaint from a shareholder of Circle Internet Group, a publicly traded U.S. crypto company and competitor of the Trump family business, World Liberty Financial.

The suit filed in U.S. District Court in Washington by shareholder and former Circle employee J. Benjamin Greenberg centers on two allegedly "illegal foreign emoluments" involving World Liberty and Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed Al Nahyan, the national security advisor to the United Arab Emirates.

Days before Trump's inauguration in January 2025, an entity backed by Tahnoon — widely known as the "Spy Sheikh" because of his powerful intelligence role — agreed to buy a 49% stake in World Liberty for $500 million.

The company told The Wall Street Journal, which revealed the transaction in January 2026, that Trump had no involvement in the deal. But Trump would receive millions of dollars from that purchase after becoming president, according to his financial disclosures.

The lawsuit also highlights a "scheme" by an Abu Dhabi-based investment fund chaired by Tahnoon to "inflate the value of President Trump's crypto holdings" through last year's purchase of $2 billion in World Liberty's stablecoin, USD1.

The lawsuit asserts the conduct violates the U.S. Constitution's Foreign Emoluments Clause, which forbids federal officeholders from accepting money or other gifts from foreign officials without the consent of Congress.

"This sounds more like a partisan press release than a lawsuit," Bill Burck of Quinn Emanuel, counsel for World Liberty Financial, said in a statement. "All we know about it is from media reports. Anyone can file a lawsuit in America, even a frivolous one."

"This is yet another frivolous lawsuit that echoes the same, tired narrative that Democrats have pushed against President Trump, his family, and his administration for a decade," White House spokesperson Anna Kelly told CNBC in a statement. "There are no conflicts of interest."

In a statement to CNBC, Matt Pelikan, an attorney at Evergreen Legal representing Greenberg, said, "What President Trump did is brazenly corrupt. He's made millions from these deals by selling out the American people.

"We're suing to stop the corruption and to force President Trump to return the profits to American taxpayers," Pelikan said.

The suit says Greenberg is filing derivatively on behalf of Circle. The company said in a statement to CNBC it was not behind the lawsuit and does not comment on pending litigation."Circle did not initiate, direct, authorize, or participate in the filing," the company said via a spokesperson.

The suit names Trump, World Liberty and U.S. Middle East Special Envoy Steve Witkoff as defendants, as well as four related limited liability companies.

"World Liberty Financial is compliant with all U.S. laws and regulations," David Wachsman, a World Liberty spokesperson, told CNBC. "No one at World Liberty Financial works for the U.S. Government. These claims are without merit and we look forward to resolving them in court."

The White House and a spokesperson for Witkoff did not immediately respond to CNBC's requests for comment on the lawsuit.

In addition to seeking a court declaration that Trump's profits violate the Constitution, Greenberg wants the president to disclose all other foreign crypto emoluments he's received and return any profits found to be illegal.

Trump and his family have rapidly expanded their crypto business since he returned to the White House. World Liberty Financial, launched with members of the Trump and Witkoff families in 2024, operates the USD1 stablecoin, and an entity affiliated with Trump and his family owns about 38% of the company.The business has been highly lucrative for Trump even as investors in the so-called governance token WLFI suffered steep losses. Trump reported more than $1.4 billion in crypto income in his 2025 annual financial disclosure, including nearly $800 million tied to World Liberty. WLFI, meanwhile, fell from about 45 cents in August 2025 to about 5 cents by the end of June 2026.The venture has drawn particular scrutiny over its ties to the UAE.
