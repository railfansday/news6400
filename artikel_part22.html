#5350. United Airlines Opens Flight Attendant Bases In… Dallas & Sacramento?!

Several weeks ago, United Airlines revealed internally that it would open two new flight attendant bases in the near future. We’ve now learned the details of where these bases will be, and I’m kind of caught off guard by them… especially one! I’m curious if anyone has any insights or theories.

Airlines have flight attendant bases at certain airports. While lots of flight attendants choose to commute, the idea is that flight attendants typically start and their trips at their base. These bases are important, since there will also often be reserve flight attendants there on standby, in case they need to fill in for someone else, to keep the operation moving.

As things currently stand, United has roughly a dozen flight attendant bases. There are bases at all the carrier’s hubs, and also in places like Guam (GUM) and London (LHR), which are more focus cities than hubs. There are also bases in places like Boston (BOS) and Honolulu (HNL), reflecting the amount of service the airline has there.

Anyway, JonNYC reports on how United has just revealed its two newest flight attendant bases, and they’re in Dallas (DFW) and Sacramento (SMF).

Flight attendants can bid on where they want to be based, and then they’re selected based on seniority. So I imagine that some flight attendants who live in those cities may be happy about this. For that matter, many new hire flight attendants may end up at these bases, as they really have no say over where they’re based. It would be rather odd to be hired as a United flight attendant, only to end up working out of Dallas, no?

It’s not too hard to figure out why airlines have flight attendant bases at their hubs… that’s obvious. My assumption would be that otherwise airlines select these based on the amount of service they have out of there, and also based on geographic diversity, to some extent, in terms of easily being able to get a flight attendant to a nearby airport, in case a reserve is needed.

So I have to say, the Dallas base sure caught me off guard. Dallas is of course a fortress hub for American, and United has a relatively small presence there. #5347. I made a rookie travel mistake that nearly cost me $4,000 — here's how I fixed it

I've been a travel journalist for over a decade, and I'm here to let you know that even we pros make rookie mistakes.

In fact, at the end of the summer, I made such an elementary-level travel error that it derailed a trip I spent months planning — and nearly cost me thousands of dollars to fix. Luckily, points and miles came in and saved the day, and I'm telling my tale as part of TPG's weekly reader mistake series to help make sure you don't make the same simple error.

My sin? When I booked a flight from Dallas to Fiji, I forgot that I was crossing the International Date Line.

Here's the short of it: For over a year, I was planning a whirlwind trip to Australia with my mom. After months of tracking flights I could book with points and miles across a range of programs, I stumbled on 80,000-mile tickets from Dallas to Sydney via Fiji in Fiji Airways' swanky new business-class product through American Airlines. After mulling it over, we decided that instead of just connecting, we'd extend our layover and spend three days exploring a beautiful country we hadn't visited. Better yet, we booked three nights at the super popular Likuliku Lagoon Resort.

On the Friday before our Thursday night flight, after sitting down to write out an itinerary for my mom (a ride-or-die who gladly goes with me blindly), it hit me: Yes, we leave Thursday ... but our plane lands on Saturday. ON SATURDAY. But therein lies the problem: the hotel reservation, along with our complicated, pre-booked boat transfers, starts on Friday.

My initial thought was just to see if we could extend the stay by one night on the back end of our reservation, but this was no ordinary trip. It was a nearly month-long journey to Fiji, New Zealand, Australia and Hong Kong. There was no way to add a day without causing a domino effect of other changes. The only options were to lose a night in paradise or figure out how to leave the States a day early.

I tried to see if Fiji Airways could move us up a day on their end. No luck. Then I tried American Airlines, which told me I'd have to cancel the award tickets and rebook. (I knew both of us these were long shots, but I had to try.)

But then I ran into the real problem: With less than a week to spare, award availability was practically nonexistent, according to Seats.aero, the award-flight aggregator I turn to first when booking flights.

The day we needed to leave had just one ticket to Fiji available to book with miles, and rather than flying out of Dallas Fort Worth International Airport (DFW), it left from San Francisco International Airport (SFO). That business-class ticket was still just 80,000 miles. "Okay," I thought, "I'll put mom in the front and I'll take an economy ticket." The economy ticket, though, was 100,000 AAdvantage miles. "Hell no," I said. "I'm not paying more to sit in the back of the plane." Either way, I knew we weren't taking the original flight from Dallas, so I canceled the ticket, got my miles refunded instantly and rebooked my mom on the flight from SFO.

That weekend, I tried every trick in the playbook: searching various award partners I could transfer points from my Chase Sapphire Reserve® (see rates and fees) or American Express Platinum Card® to for a different flight, pricing out cash options and, most of all, praying that another award seat would open up. Once again, no luck.

My last-ditch effort was to potentially use my Chase Ultimate Rewards or American Express Membership Rewards to pay myself back for the business-class seat I'd already booked for my mom. Knowing that the cash fare was over $4,000, I went in with little hope. But on the Chase Travel℠ portal, my luck took a turn when I saw that the flight I needed was marked as eligible for the newish Chase Points Boost, which let me redeem my points at 2 cents per point.

I didn't have enough points to cover the entire flight, but the 158,540 Chase Ultimate Rewards I did have, valued at 2 cents each, covered $3,170.80 of the $4,066.50 flight. I paid $895.70 for the rest of the flight with cash. Right now, TPG values Chase Ultimate Rewards points at 2.05 cents each, so I'd say it worked out pretty nicely.

Sure, I probably could have used those points for a better redemption with better planning and more flexibility, but I was in a bind. More importantly, I didn't care about the trips I could take; I wanted to be sipping Champagne with my mom in the front of the plane as we kicked off our wild journey together.

As long as you're a traveler, mistakes will happen, and things will go wrong (even if it's not your fault). When those things do happen — whether it's a delayed or canceled flight, or you simply write down a date wrong — have a plan of action ready, along with resources like The Points Guy that can guide you.

Download tools like the Flighty app, which often lets me know about a flight delay before the airline does, and Seats.aero, to quickly see what award flights are available I could switch to. Study up on the travel insurance benefits that come with your credit cards, and make a mental note of what transfer partners you have access to.

Most importantly, know your limits. I could have easily just accepted the more expensive economy ticket, but I was willing to give it a few days to explore all of my options. But by the end of the second day, I was burnt out and ready to throw in the towel. Luckily, the Chase Travel portal saved the day, but it was truly my last-ditch idea.

For what it's worth, three nights at Likuliku and exploring Fiji's Mamanuca Islands were more than worth all the stress. This trip wouldn't have even been possible without points and miles. That's the beauty of it: points and miles can be a nightmare and a blessing, a gift and a curse, the means to end and the end itself. Or in my case, all of the above.

Now, if I could just learn to use a calendar...

#5344. Trump ties ethanol boon for farmers to a Marshall win

President Donald Trump announced $8 billion in aid to farmers struggling with high diesel prices while also tying his support for increased ethanol content in gasoline — a boon to Midwest corn growers — to the reelection of a Kansas senator.

Trump made the proclamation Monday while stumping for the embattled Sen. Roger Marshall, who’s trying to avoid being the first Republican in his state to lose a Senate race in 94 years, in his race against pastor and first-time political candidate Adam Hamilton, a Democrat.

“With Roger Marshall in the Senate, we will make year-round E15 permanent. But if Adam Hamilton is in, I’m not doing it,” Trump said. “That’s it. I’m only doing it for him.”

Republican lawmakers and candidates from well beyond Kansas have been advocating for changing the law to allow the year-round sale of E15 gasoline, which contains more ethanol blended in than the typical 10 percent ethanol content. E15 sales in the summer can be permitted under current law with an EPA waiver.

It was not immediately clear if Trump would block such waivers in the summer of 2027 if Marshall loses, or if he would oppose enactment of the Senate’s farm bill provision that would amend the Clean Air Act to make E15 blends generally permissible. That’s a big deal to the corn ethanol industry as it would provide certainty that more of their product can get to market without relying on EPA determinations.

The president has also mentioned the ethanol issue in a video message endorsing Rep. Zach Nunn, R-Iowa, for reelection. There are plenty of hotly contested races in the Midwest, including Rep. Ashley Hinson running for Senate in Iowa and Sen. Pete Ricketts, R-Neb., who faces a strong challenge from Dan Osborn, an independent.

Negotiations are still underway to try to find a farm bill compromise that can pass the House, Senate and become law in 2026. Trump has already endorsed year-round E15, signing an executive order on the first day of his second term to require the EPA to grant annual summer waivers.

But oil refiners, who are on the hook to demonstrate compliance with renewable fuel mandates even if consumers don’t want to purchase E15 or have vehicles that can support the higher-octane fuel, oppose codifying a permanent waiver. They’re willing to consider a deal allowing year-round E15, but only in exchange for more generous relief from quotas under the renewable fuel standard, or RFS.

In May, after farm groups and representatives of oil producers, marketers and larger refiners reached a deal with House leadership, the House voted on a bill that would amend the CAA to allow year-round E15. The bill would also provide certainty for smaller oil refiners by getting rid of the annual EPA exemption process, instead creating a system of permanent exemptions starting in 2028 while getting of annual reallocations of exempted blending obligations to larger, non-exempt refineries.

But it would also tighten the definition of small refiner by applying the current 75,000 daily production cap per refinery across all of a parent company’s facilities, which would impact firms with smaller refineries in multiple locations.

The bill passed 218-203, with the split falling along geographic lines as many oil and refinery state members voted against the bill — including Louisiana Republicans Speaker Mike Johnson and Majority Leader Steve Scalise.

The American Farm Bureau Federation, Renewable Fuels Association and American Petroleum Institute all supported the bill. However, some smaller and midsize refiners, including those represented by the Fueling American Jobs Coalition and Small Refineries of America, argued that the bill could force more than 20 refineries out of business because of new exemption caps and changes to the definition of small refinery.

The Senate Agriculture Committee included year-round E15 in its version of the farm bill, which the panel approved 12-11 on Sept. 16, along with some RFS changes intended to ease small refiners’ burden. That legislation also would create a permanent program of small-refiner exemptions, but without the House’s bill’s tighter eligibility definition.

Still, some oil industry players that backed the House’s E15 bill opposed the Senate language, which provides more modest reallocation relief to non-exempt refineries. Key GOP senators whose constituents include oil refineries, such as Majority Whip John Barrasso of Wyoming and Commerce Chairman Ted Cruz of Texas, haven’t publicly commented on the Senate’s E15 compromise.

The political maneuvering became more complicated after the EPA released refinery exemptions that left both sides of the debate unhappy in August, and a draft rule that would reallocate all renewable fuel volumes earlier this month.

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin announced the reallocations during a tour through the Midwest that included a roundtable with Hinson.

Recognizing the political realities on both sides of the ethanol debate, on the same day as Zeldin’s RFS announcement, Vice President JD Vance said the administration would use Defense Production Act authorities to expand a 77,000 barrels-per-day refinery in Tyler, Texas, at a stop with GOP Senate candidate Ken Paxton.

The refinery, owned by Delek US Holdings, also received an exemption from RFS blending quotas.

The farm bill itself has a long way to go, even if oil and farm groups can strike an acceptable balance. Democrats and Republicans remain at odds over changes to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, which threaten to stall a full reauthorization and could mean a more simple temporary extension.

Marshall, the GOP incumbent, is facing a significant enough reelection challenge that Trump was deployed to rally supporters Monday in the Sunflower State. It’s a state Trump carried by 16 percentage points in 2024, and yet the political environment is making it difficult even for the Republicans there.

The president announced a plan to provide $8 billion in assistance to farmers facing high costs for diesel fuel during the harvest season, with the ongoing conflict with Iran continuing to elevate the cost.

Unlike the president’s comments on E15 regarding post-election activity, it appears the farm aid program will get underway immediately, with a notice from the Agriculture Department saying potential farmer beneficiaries should make sure they have up-to-date Login.gov credentials ahead of the rollout.

According to AAA, diesel prices remain above $6 per gallon in Kansas. That’s compared to about $3.39 per gallon just one year ago.

During Trump’s Kansas appearance, he credited Marshall for helping to make the funding a reality.

“Roger Marshall is the leader who fought for that to happen,” Trump said.

#5343. Rivian now #2 US EV brand, up from #10, as Big Auto marginalizes itself

Rivian is now the #2 EV brand in the US, up from 10th last year, taking Chevrolet’s spot as almost every other automaker tries to slow down the inevitable transition to electric cars.

In the last year, the US electric car market has been… volatile, to say the least.

Q3 2025 saw a huge sales jump, in an advance of an effort by US republicans to inflate the price of EVs by $7,500.

So, looking forward to this year, it was inevitable that we would see a large drop in sales in Q3, since records were set in last Q3.

Sure enough, as sales numbers came in, most brands were down. This is to be expected after incentive expiry – though EV sales have been steadily recovering in the intervening months.

Part of the reason for that recovery has been due to a lot of dumb oil conflicts around the world, causing oil prices to rise to record levels (helped by intentional policy decisions to push them even higher). As oil prices rise, the already-obvious case for electric transportation becomes all the more clear.

This has caused EV sales to shoot up in the rest of the world. Unfortunately, American access to affordable EVs has been restricted due to dumb tariffs and other barriers intended to keep Americans spending and polluting more.

EV sales could have been higher in the US, but the reason they’ve stayed depressed from previous levels isn’t due to consumer demand, but because automakers are almost universally refusing to make EVs, according to research from UC Davis.

There are a few exceptions, though. With Rivian as the standout among them.

According to Cox Automotive’s new Q3 sales report, almost every brand saw a decrease in EV sales in Q3, with Mercedes, Lexus, Toyota, Subaru, and Rivian as the exceptions.

Each of these brands have new models which helped to bring up sales. Toyota has its improved bZ and new C-HR, Lexus its new ES, Subaru its Trailseeker and Uncharted, Mercedes its CLA, and most importantly, Rivian has its R2.

Meanwhile, many of the brands with falling sales have cancelled models (even ones that were previously their best-sellers). Unsurprisingly, when Americans are not allowed to buy a car, they won’t buy it.

Rivian’s new R2 is a make-or-break car for the company, a much more affordable, smaller version of its R1S. It just started sales a few months ago, and Rivian hopes to ramp up production, aiming for the same market the Tesla Model Y is in.

The Model Y remains the US’ best-selling EV, but its sales went down year over year as well. Tesla has not so much retreated from EV production, more that its CEO is now spending more money to harm EVs than any other person on the planet. Even with Tesla’s nearly 20% drop in sales year over year, It’s still ahead of the rest of the pack.

But 2nd place used to be taken up by Chevy, which was proud of that fact. It has several capable EV offerings, including the newly-improved, affordable Chevy Bolt (which is, nevertheless, being cancelled next year). (GM is still second place if you combine all of its brands together, though, but if Rivian’s growth continues, that may or may not hold)

Great job Rivian! Persevering through some very tough times. I can't even imagine if their coming of age happened under some of the favorable incentives, government regulations etc of Tesla's timeline.

Last year, Rivian was all the way back in 10th place. But with its growth in sales and everyone else’s retreat, Rivian has now shot up to second place in Q3, selling more EVs in the quarter than anyone except for Tesla.

And that’s just at the start of R2 sales. Rivian only booked 3,456 units of the R2 in Q3, which is fewer than the number of R1S it sold. Given the R2’s much cheaper sticker price, once the company gets up to speed and starts offering lower-end ~$50k models, volume should be much higher than the ~$80k R1S – just as was the case with the Model X and Model Y.

And given the direction society is currently going, towards hopefully greater realization of the need to get off of unreliable, polluting, foreign sources of energy and onto cleaner, sustainable domestic ones, Rivian is positioning itself well while the rest of the auto industry seems ready to throw everything away and hand China the industry on a silver platter.

Flávio Bolsonaro—the eldest son of former President Jair Bolsonaro—and President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva to a runoff. Another key race was decided that day: In congressional elections, right-wing parties triumphed and are expected to hold at least 45 of 81 seats in the Senate.

On Sept. 15, Chief Justice Edson Fachin convened a televised session to decide whether Justice Alexandre de Moraes—who oversaw Jair Bolsonaro’s prosecution and is reviled by the far right—should be investigated by the Federal Police. (Brazilian law requires courts to authorize investigations into their own members.) The justices never reached a decision: They split on a procedural motion, with Moraes voting on his own case, before Justice Flávio Dino asked for up to 90 days to study the file. That clock runs until mid-December—well after the runoff.

The case swirling around Moraes stems from the collapse last year of Banco Master, a midsize Brazilian lender. The scandal—like the Operation Car Wash investigation of the mid-2010s—has ensnared many members of Brazil’s political and business elite. Federal Police hope to examine whether Moraes improperly assisted Master’s controlling shareholder, Daniel Vorcaro, and whether that conduct was connected to a legal services contract between Master and Moraes’s wife’s law firm. Moraes denies wrongdoing. He has not been charged with a crime.

The scandal has become a hot topic on the campaign trail, and one likely to benefit Flávio. By putting off a decision on whether to investigate Moraes, the Supreme Court has handed Flávio a rallying cry, left Lula defending an institution that he has no plan to reform, and made the new right-wing Senate the arbiter of the court’s future.

Prior to its collapse, Banco Master held only 0.57 percent of Brazil’s banking assets. But its unique business model made it significant.

The bank sold certificates of deposit paying up to 140 percent of the interbank benchmark rate, far above what large banks offered. It leaned on the Credit Guarantee Fund, an insurer financed by member banks that covers up to about $49,000 per investor. (Although privately funded, the fund operates under government-set rules, and eligible banks are required to join and contribute.) This allowed Master to raise money without investors scrutinizing what it bought, including illiquid assets and credit portfolios that, according to investigators, lacked adequate backing.

Master also drew on public money. Banco de Brasília (BRB), a lender controlled by the government of the Federal District, bought Master’s credit portfolios. In March 2025, BRB agreed to buy 58 percent of the bank. The Central Bank blocked the deal that September and shut Master down on Nov. 18. The Credit Guarantee Fund repaid roughly $7.5 billion to about 800,000 savers, so taxpayers did not cover that bill. But BRB’s losses fall on the public: Federal Police found that BRB paid about $1.4 billion for Master portfolios before finishing at least one of its required risk assessments. BRB has since asked the district government for fresh capital.

Master’s reach went beyond finance: Vorcaro told police that he had friends in all branches of government, and the case has drawn in politicians across the political spectrum.

Ibaneis Rocha, the former Federal District governor, called the occasion of the proposed acquisition “a day of celebration,” and he left office in March to run for the Senate. He dropped out of the race in July, after the BRB president whom he had appointed was jailed.

Police say Master’s staff drafted an amendment to a proposed constitutional reform to quadruple the Credit Guarantee Fund’s per-depositor coverage that Sen. Ciro Nogueira, Jair Bolsonaro’s former chief of staff, introduced in Congress in 2024. Police have also said that Nogueira received monthly payments of up to roughly $98,000 from Vorcaro and figures associated with Master. Nogueira denies wrongdoing.

The Supreme Court took over the Master investigation because it involves members of Congress, who in Brazil can only be investigated with the Supreme Court’s authorization—and tried by the court itself. Justice Dias Toffoli was initially assigned to the case. He stepped aside in February after police flagged that funds managed by Vorcaro’s brother-in-law had a stake in a resort from a company partly owned by Toffoli and his brothers. Toffoli’s office has said he never had a friendship with Vorcaro.

The case was then reassigned to Justice André Mendonça, a Jair Bolsonaro appointee.

The scandal has reached both presidential campaigns, though not in the same way. Flávio’s exposure is direct: In May, Intercept Brasil published audio of him asking Vorcaro for money to finish Dark Horse, a biopic about his father. Flávio first denied any connection to Vorcaro, then acknowledged seeking the sponsorship, which he described as legitimate private investment.

Records from Brazil’s financial intelligence unit trace about $12.3 million from a Vorcaro-linked company to a Texas fund that managed the film’s money and is run by an ally of Flávio’s brother Eduardo, who successfully lobbied the Trump administration to sanction Moraes. Police documents do not link the money to Flávio’s personal accounts. In July, Mendonça authorized a formal investigation into possible corruption, money laundering, and currency evasion connected to the financing of Dark Horse. Neither brother has been charged in connection with the film’s financing.

Lula’s exposure is indirect. He is not implicated in wrongdoing, according to tax records reviewed by Reuters, though he met with Vorcaro in December 2024. This June, police executed search warrants targeting Sen. Jaques Wagner, who was then the Senate leader, on suspicion that he had advanced Master’s interests in exchange for benefits for relatives. Wagner, a member of Lula’s Workers’ Party, denies wrongdoing. He has since resigned from his leadership post.

For Lula, the larger liability is Moraes. Much of the Brazilian electorate places the justice and the president in the same camp—never mind that Moraes was appointed by right-wing former President Michel Temer.

Flávio has campaigned on the perceived link, saying, “Anyone voting for Lula is voting for Alexandre de Moraes.” Lula has responded by demanding an investigation of everyone implicated in the Master scandal, “no matter who it hurts.” Moraes is also a frequent target of tech entrepreneur Elon Musk, who called him an “evil dictator cosplaying as a judge” during a 2024 clash over court orders that led to X’s temporary suspension in Brazil.

Banco Master signed a three-year legal services agreement, potentially worth roughly $25.7 million, with a law firm where Moraes’s wife is partner. Federal Police investigators have said that metadata from a draft of the agreement shows a username linked to the justice, which they read as a sign that he edited the document. The firm says Moraes reviewed the contract only for potential conflicts of interest and that it never represented Master in court. Editorials in three major Brazilian newspapers have called on him to step down.

Master’s access extended deep into Brazil’s legal and law enforcement establishment. In April 2024, Master sponsored a forum in London and paid for the appearance of former British Prime Minister Tony Blair, who debated Vorcaro. Participants included Moraes, Justices Gilmar Mendes and Toffoli, Prosecutor General Paulo Gonet, and Federal Police Director-General Andrei Rodrigues. Messages on Vorcaro’s phone show him telling an associate that he needed to run the guest list by “Alexandre.”

The Federal Police report records Vorcaro’s claim, but it notes that investigators did not confirm with Moraes whether he had requested or approved the list. Should proceedings against Moraes move forward, every office that would have to investigate, charge, or judge him for potential wrongdoing was represented in that London room.

The court’s paralysis has become a campaign asset for Flávio. Every week without a ruling on Moraes lets Flávio attack the court further.

But it’s unclear whether the scandal has truly impacted the presidential race. In a survey conducted Sept. 8-10 by the polling firm Datafolha, 85 percent of respondents said the Moraes revelations had not changed their vote; 4 percent reported changing their choice, while another 7 percent said the revelations had left them uncertain. The same survey found that 76 percent think that Brazilian Supreme Court justices have too much power.

The Senate is where the court’s future will be settled. Jair Bolsonaro picked allied candidates to build a majority capable of impeaching justices, which requires two-thirds of the Senate, or 54 votes. Counts of incoming senators who back impeachment range from 48 to 54, depending on the survey. Flávio has promised to limit rulings by individual justices, end the court’s original criminal jurisdiction over members of Congress, and pursue amnesty for his father, alongside Moraes’s impeachment. Lula has proposed no structural change to the court.

Either way, the next president will likely appoint four of the court’s 11 justices. (Brazilian Supreme Court justices must retire by the age of 75.) One seat has been empty since April, when the Senate rejected Lula’s nominee, Jorge Messias—the first such rejection since 1894. Three more seats will open through mandatory retirements by December 2030.

The deeper problem will outlast the runoff. In 2024, I argued that Brazil’s democracy, for all its defects, was in no danger of falling apart, and I still believe that. But my view of the court has moved more in the past year than in the previous 10. Allegations against a justice are investigated under the court’s supervision, charged by the prosecutor general, and tried by colleagues. In this case, the prosecutor general appears in the evidence.

That loop has structural roots. In 2025, the court issued 116,170 decisions, and around 80 percent of them came from individual justices. The scholars Diego Werneck Arguelhes and Leandro Molhano Ribeiro call the pattern ministrocracia, or rule by individual justices. The court also writes its own conduct rules. In 2023, it struck down a statute barring judges from cases involving clients of their relatives’ law firms. The National Council of Justice, which disciplines the rest of the judiciary, has no jurisdiction over its members.

Complacency would leave that loop intact, but a court purged by a Senate eager for revenge is no guarantee of accountability. The Senate has never removed a justice, and the first impeachment would set a precedent that any future majority could invoke.

Most of the remedy lies in the court’s own hands. It could require justices to step aside from cases involving themselves or their relatives—which Moraes did not—send major rulings by a single justice to the full court; and assign cases at random, with no exceptions.

“Either we limit ourselves,” warned Fachin, the chief justice, in January, “or there may be limitation by an external power.”

Whoever wins Brazil’s presidency will inherit four Supreme Court seats to fill; a decision on Moraes due in December; and a public that, by roughly 4 to 1, thinks that the justices have too much power. The question for Brazil’s next government is not whether the Supreme Court will be constrained, but who will write those constraints.

#5335. The Real Lesson of the Iran War

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

JUSTIN BRONK is Senior Research Fellow for Airpower and Technology at the Royal United Services Institute.

For months, Iran’s resilience in its war with two of the world’s most capable militaries has perplexed many politicians and confounded public expectations. Israel and the United States have hit thousands of Iranian targets, systematically destroying much of the country’s military infrastructure and killing many of its top leaders. Yet the Islamic Republic has not only survived but also continued to maintain significant offensive capabilities. To account for this, some have pointed to Iran’s effective deployment of low-cost precise mass weapons, such as the Shahed-136 one-way attack drone. Others have looked at the decentralized and self-replacing nature of the regime itself.

Although these and other factors have played a part, there is a more straightforward reason for Iran’s staying power: the American and Israeli decision to rely on a sustained air and stand-off naval campaign to bring the regime to its knees. This approach did not come out of nowhere. Air supremacy has been the defining tenet of U.S. and wider Western military strategy for decades. In the post–Cold War era, the United States has perfected the art of airpower—winning control of the skies so that enemy armies, navies, and territory can be bombed at will—to a degree that no country or even alliance of countries has matched. When pitted against an adversary’s deployed ground forces, this strategy has consistently yielded impressive results. The U.S. ability to achieve rapid air superiority has also long served as a powerful deterrent to would-be aggressors.

Yet there are few examples in history of adversaries conceding to maximalist demands in the face of punitive bombing campaigns alone—even by the world’s greatest air force. Indeed, Operation Epic Fury is only one of several recent or current conflicts—including Russia’s war in Ukraine and international responses to the Houthi threat in Yemen—that have exposed the drawbacks of relying heavily on airstrikes to try to unseat a government or to defeat insurgent forces. For American strategists, then, it will be crucial to distinguish the different uses of airpower. The Iran campaign has been a disastrous misuse of military resources, and it showcases the policy limits of what airpower can achieve. Yet it would be a mistake to conclude that the United States should shift away from its doctrine of prioritizing air superiority, as it prepares alongside NATO and Asian allies for very different threats in the coming few years.

During the major wars of the twentieth century, U.S. and Allied air superiority was critical to successes on the battlefield. But airpower still failed to force political capitulation by adversaries. In World War II, Nazi Germany fought to the very end despite the Allies’ systematic bombing of its cities, its industrial base, and its transport and fuel networks. In the Korean War, North Korea fought the United States and its allies to a standstill, even as fleets of U.S. bombers obliterated most of its cities and infrastructure. And in Vietnam, the North Vietnamese outlasted the United States, despite the U.S. military dropping more than a million tons of ordnance on cities, factories, and supply routes, in successive punitive air campaigns.

In the defining counterinsurgency conflicts of the twenty-first century, there is little evidence to suggest that air superiority proved any more effective. In Afghanistan and Iraq, the United States and its allies quickly established total air superiority over the enemy and were able to call in precision airstrikes at will, giving allied troops a huge advantage in combat. Yet in both cases, these campaigns also ultimately failed to achieve their political objectives.

Despite near-daily barrages of Ukraine’s cities, power infrastructure, and industry, Russia is no closer to vanquishing the country more than four and a half years after its brutal invasion began. In Iran, there has been little sign that U.S. and Israeli airstrikes have fueled popular pressure to end the war. This is despite major antigovernment protests throughout the country shortly before the campaign began, which were stamped out only after mass state violence. Indeed, inflicting hardship on the population seems especially unlikely to change the calculus of the Iranian regime, given its willingness to kill thousands of protestors to stay in power.

If anything, there is reason to think that it may be even harder today to win wars than in the twentieth century. Because of the increasingly multipolar nature of the world order, countries can often rely on international support to mitigate the damage from a sustained enemy strike campaign. In Ukraine, this can be seen in the influx of American and European weapons and economic, military, and technological support into the country since Russia’s 2022 invasion. This assistance has not prevented Ukrainians from experiencing terrible loss and hardship, but it has enabled the Ukrainian economy to continue functioning and its military to consistently replace lost and expended equipment and ammunition over time.

For Iran, China has provided a stable market for Iranian oil and gas exports, which helped keep Iran’s economy afloat until the imposition of a U.S. naval blockade in April. More recently, Beijing has also provided military support in the form of short-range air defense systems, which will sustain and improve Iran’s capability to pose a pop-up threat to U.S. and Israeli aircraft loitering to seek targets at medium or low altitudes. Russia also remains a key Iranian ally, providing Tehran with intelligence to target U.S. forces in the Gulf and helping it develop new military capabilities, such as supersonic antiship missiles. In both wars, external support has made sustained air and missile strike campaigns far less likely to result in political capitulation, or economic or military collapse.

Despite the poor record of punitive bombing campaigns in forcing political capitulation, air superiority has proved decisive in many wars through its impact on the battlefield. The U.S. military’s overwhelming success against the well-equipped and battle-hardened Iraqi army in Operation Desert Storm, in 1991, was, perhaps, the defining example of how an air-centered approach can be effective. The initial U.S. air campaign in January and February 1991 sufficiently degraded Iraqi forces and command and control capabilities to allow coalition ground forces to decisively defeat the Iraqi army in less than 100 hours of ground combat operations.

Other partial successes include NATO’s intervention in Kosovo in 1999, in which a 78-day air campaign led Serbian President Slobodan Milosevic to withdraw his forces. Yet another was the European-led air campaign against Muammar al-Qaddafi’s forces over Libya in 2011, which rapidly destroyed government tanks and artillery attacking the city of Benghazi, and facilitated a subsequent rebel advance to Tripoli with further airstrikes. In each of these cases, air superiority made the battlefield situation untenable for enemy ground forces and forced them to retreat. The fundamental difference that distinguishes this effective use of airpower lies in the objectives that the enemy was seeking to achieve. When an adversary is pursuing goals that require the significant deployment of ground forces—such as an invasion to seize the territory of a neighboring state—establishing air superiority over that adversary almost always confers a decisive military advantage.

In World War II, the Anglo-American combined bomber offensive against German cities and industry failed to force Germany to surrender. But once the Allies established air superiority in any given theater of operations, the operational defeat of Axis armies in the field was only a matter of time. In the Western Desert, and then in Italy, Normandy, and Germany itself, roving Allied fighter-bomber patrolsrapidly rendered movement by armored vehicles and supply columns by day prohibitively dangerous. They also could provide devastating close air support whenever Allied ground units met strong Axis positions or units.

In 1991, Saddam Hussein’s objectives required the Iraqi army to hold territory in Kuwait against U.S.-led ground forces, and U.S. tactical airpower rapidly made that impossible, by destroying Iraqi air defenses and then conducting a massive interdiction campaign against armored vehicles, logistics convoys, bases, and communications infrastructure. In Libya in 2011, Qaddafi’s forces deployed tanks and artillery around Benghazi to bombard rebel forces and civilians, but these were easily found and destroyed by European combat aircraft, as were units that Qaddafi deployed to try to stop later rebel counterattacks toward Tripoli. The ultimate rebel victory and killing of Qaddafi did not deliver long-term policy success in Libya as the Western world would define it. But Qaddafi’s military forces were decisively defeated by Western airpower and his objective of crushing a popular uprising by force was rendered impossible.

In Afghanistan and Iraq, by contrast, none of Washington’s adversaries needed to field regular military forces in the open to achieve their objectives. Simply by maintaining a steady tempo of IED strikes and ambushes against U.S. patrols, and then melting back into the wider civilian population, these groups were able to deny coalition forces the security they needed to foster democratic state building. Of course, whenever militant groups attempted larger operations designed to overrun bases or towns, they were met with ferocious firepower, much of it delivered by airstrikes, thanks to ubiquitous U.S. and coalition air superiority. But just as in Vietnam, victory in most major battlefield engagements was not enough to secure the political objectives that the United States sought. All that the Taliban in Afghanistan or militant groups in Iraq had to do was to maintain a security vacuum for long enough to exhaust U.S. and European patience with discretionary “forever wars.”

Although every war is different, the United States confronts a similar problem in Iran: it is trying to use airpower to achieve maximalist aims against an enemy that can largely afford to keep its conventional military forces dispersed, hidden, or underground. The initial U.S. and Israeli campaign to establish air superiority was very successful, and in general, the advanced aircraft and tactics the two militaries deployed worked as intended. But although subsequent months of airstrikes have destroyed some of Iran’s enormous ballistic missile arsenal, most of the country’s significant remaining stocks and production capacity are in deeply buried “missile cities,” out of reach of almost all conventional bombs and missiles. Because of the earlier 12-day war, in June 2025—when U.S. airstrikes targeted Iran’s nuclear infrastructure with highly specialized 30,000-pound bunker-busting bombs—the bulk of Iran’s remaining highly enriched fissile material, movable equipment, and scientists were likely dispersed and hidden from further airstrikes long before Operation Epic Fury began.

To defeat U.S. war aims, therefore, the regime does not have to deploy regular armed forces in the open. It simply has to maintain control of the Iranian population, which the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps is well placed to ensure through widespread day-to-day repression. And in Hormuz, Iran can easily conceal the small boats it needs to drop mines, its antiship missile launchers, and its drone launch positions along the extensive and rocky coastline. Airpower alone is unlikely to deliver a victory against Iran, despite Israel’s and the United States’ ability to functionally strike almost anywhere in the country, at will, for months on end.

This is in fact the same problem that has frustrated earlier attempts by the United States and its allies to stop antiship drone and missile strikes by the Houthis in Yemen. Airpower can be and has been used repeatedly to destroy any Houthi forces identified in the open, but it cannot prevent the group from posing a sustained threat to commercial shipping with concealed missile and drone launch sites in coastal territory that they control.

The war in Iran has significantly tarnished the global aura of U.S. airpower. It is unlikely that the Trump administration would have launched Operation Epic Fury if it had known that seven months later it would still be facing an inconclusive slugging match—one that has wasted a huge part of Washington’s complex munitions stockpile and come at exorbitant economic and geopolitical cost. Alongside the ongoing Russian invasion of Ukraine, in which neither side has been able to achieve air superiority, there is a real risk that policymakers and the public lose faith in advanced air capabilities as the central pillar of American and wider Western military advantage.

That would be a huge mistake. The first lesson of the war against Iran in 2026—as in numerous other wars, including Vietnam, Iraq after 2003, and Afghanistan after 2006—is that conventional military superiority gained through overwhelming airpower will not be enough to achieve unrealistic political goals. But the second lesson is that this failure is not about weaknesses in U.S. air capabilities themselves. On the contrary, airpower remains the most potent piece of the United States’ conventional forces, as well as its main source of both deterrent capacity and warfighting advantage against conventional aggression by hostile states. If the U.S. goals for Operation Epic Fury had been to defeat an Iranian invasion of a neighboring country, then the air campaign would almost certainly have done that within a few weeks.

That hypothetical scenario is highly relevant because it bears on the most significant nondiscretionary threats that the United States may be forced to deal with in the coming few years. In the case of a Russian invasion of a NATO member state in eastern Europe or a Chinese move to take Taiwan, the enemy would need to make large deployments of regular ground forces to seize and then hold territory. In either case, advanced U.S. and allied airpower capabilities would be absolutely essential to defeating them quickly. As with Saddam Hussein’s invasion of Kuwait, both actions could be rendered militarily untenable if NATO or U.S. forces could rapidly win and exploit air superiority over the territory that had been invaded.

To prevent larger, far more costly wars between Western powers and either China or Russia, then, it will be vital to convince both adversaries that NATO forces in Europe and U.S.-led forces in the Pacific can rapidly establish and leverage air superiority should either power decide to attack. To do so, the United States will need to make the right investments and force decisions. Especially urgent priorities include restocking and improving U.S. air-launched munitions, ramping up production of missile interceptors to defend airbases and naval task forces, and accelerating the development of next generation fighter, bomber, and enabler aircraft.

Although establishing air superiority over an area contested by Chinese or Russian forces will be far more difficult than doing so over Iran, it is an essential task for the United States and its allies if they want to maintain conventional deterrence and avoid a major great-power conflict. Air superiority cannot win all wars. But if it can be credibly achieved, it all but guarantees deterrence by denial against state opponents considering territorial aggression of their own.

It hosts a sprawling U.S.-British military base with key naval, air, space, communications, and logistics facilities. (The Chagossians remain divided over their homeland’s political future and were largely excluded from the negotiations.) Under the treaty, British and American forces would retain access to their base on Diego Garcia under a 99-year lease from Mauritius, with an option to extend the arrangement for another 40 years.

At first, the Trump administration supported the deal. In May 2025, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said that “this agreement secures the long-term, stable, and effective operation of the joint U.S.-UK military facility at Diego Garcia.” He highlighted U.S. President Donald Trump’s personal endorsement, adding that “President Trump expressed his support for this monumental achievement during his meeting with Prime Minister [Keir] Starmer at the White House.”

But on Jan. 20, Trump abruptly reversed course. In a Truth Social post, he berated Britain for committing “an act of GREAT STUPIDITY,” adding, “There is no doubt that China and Russia have noticed this act of total weakness.” It appears there were two developments that led to his change of heart. First, while Trump was intensifying his campaign to acquire Greenland from Denmark, he was angered by Britain’s (and other European countries’) defense of Danish sovereignty over the island. He seems to have seen a through line to Diego Garcia, leading him to argue that Europeans were simply too weak to protect their strategically important territories. The second event involved British Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch, who reportedly raised objections to the Chagos agreement with U.S. House Speaker Mike Johnson on Jan. 19. Johnson subsequently spoke with Trump, whose outburst came the next day.

The saga unfortunately didn’t end there. On Feb. 17, the U.S. State Department reiterated support for the deal—but the very next day, Trump publicly urged Starmer to abandon the agreement, questioning the validity of Mauritius’s claims and warning against relying on a long-term lease. In his post, Trump urged “DO NOT GIVE AWAY DIEGO GARCIA,” invoking the potential need to use the island for military operations against Iran. Indeed, Tehran recognized Diego Garcia’s strategic value and fired two ballistic missiles toward it in March, although neither struck the island. Finally, when asked by a reporter about Diego Garcia during a September meeting with the new British prime minister, Andy Burnham, Trump reiterated his opposition to the sovereignty agreement, saying, “I don’t support it. I think it’s a terrible deal.”

The Trump administration’s back-and-forth over the Chagos agreement has already caused significant problems. In April, London suspended efforts to implement the agreement with Mauritius, and a few weeks ago, Port Louis threatened legal action if Britain does not move forward with the signed treaty. There is no easy off-ramp from the dispute. On the one hand, Britain has committed to ending its colonial administration of the Chagos archipelago and intends to honor the agreement with Mauritius. On the other hand, the joint military base on Diego Garcia and long-standing bilateral arrangements governing its operation mean that London cannot realistically move forward without Washington’s consent. Trump’s intervention has also deepened divisions among Chagossians. Some welcome his decision to hold up the deal because they distrust Mauritius, while others believe Trump is standing in the way of unification.

Washington’s new strategy is little more than a euphemism for unpredictability.

Trump’s intervention also risks complicating America’s relationships in the Indian Ocean, particularly with India, which supports Mauritian sovereignty over the Chagos archipelago. New Delhi welcomed the original agreement as a “milestone achievement” and a “positive development for the region” and “the culmination of the process of decolonization of Mauritius.” At the same time, India has little interest in seeing the American military base on Diego Garcia weakened, particularly given shared concerns about China’s growing regional presence. Trump has put New Delhi in an awkward spot, forcing it to balance its long-standing support for Mauritius with its strategic partnership with Washington.

China, meanwhile, sees an opportunity to criticize U.S. interference and strengthen its own standing with Mauritius, with which it has cultivated close economic and strategic ties. Just days before Trump’s shift of position in January, Chinese Ambassador to Mauritius Huang Shifang denounced the separation of Chagos from Mauritius as an example of “hegemonic mentality” and reaffirmed Beijing’s support for Mauritian sovereignty over the islands.

The Chagos treaty prohibits other military forces in the archipelago without British approval, but concerns persist in Washington and London that Beijing could eventually seek access through civilian or dual-use facilities, as it has done in other island states. Enforcing the treaty’s safeguards in this regard would depend on continued cooperation between Britain and Mauritius.

Mauritius, Britain, and many in the international community believe that the deal eliminates one of the remaining vestiges of colonialism, leaving the United States under Trump on the wrong side of history. Transferring sovereignty over Diego Garcia to Mauritius would not fundamentally alter operation of the joint military base, which would remain under British and U.S. control under a paid lease arrangement. Even if Mauritius wanted to take control of the base, it lacks the military capability to do so.

Trump’s intervention reinforces China’s narrative that Washington disregards the sovereignty of smaller states when its strategic interests are at stake. It also gives smaller and middle powers across the Indian Ocean another reason to value Beijing as an alternative strategic partner in the region. Indeed, in trying to protect Diego Garcia, Washington has scored yet another own goal.

#5326. The Global AI Bust

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

Few people doubt that AI is among the most transformative technologies to emerge in the past century. By many measures, it is spreading faster and more widely than any innovation since the dawn of the Industrial Revolution. Steam engines came into common use in the nineteenth century and electricity in the early twentieth, but their full potential was not realized before the completion of railways, highways, and power grids decades later. Computers for business use emerged in the 1950s, but they gave no visible lift to the economy before the Internet connected them in the 1990s.

Commercial artificial intelligence, by contrast, appeared almost overnight on screens worldwide, at a moment when the overwhelming majority of humans already had access to a computer or smartphone. Less than four years after the release of ChatGPT, roughly 30 percent of Americans are now using AI, five times the share of Americans who were using smartphones, the Internet, or electricity at comparable stages of development. Revenue for AI companies is three times what previous digital technologies, including the Internet, cloud computing, and early mobile apps, generated in their early years.

Policymakers, investors, and business leaders are counting on AI to be a miracle cure for everything from runaway public debt to the costs associated with aging populations. Among investors, even some of the world’s most successful contrarians—who make their fortunes betting against conventional wisdom—are embracing the idea that AI could yield bigger benefits more rapidly than any past technological advance.

The euphoria is not universal, however. An ever-lonelier minority of skeptics argue that AI’s rise may seem unusually fast but is in fact following a centuries-old script. Major technology revolutions have always triggered dramatic boom-bust cycles. People get overexcited about the latest breakthrough and begin investing indiscriminately in the companies driving the innovation. As a team of researchers from Texas Tech University, Texas A&M University, and the Vlerick Business School in Belgium found in 2018, all of the most revolutionary technological innovations of the last two centuries inflated a major market bubble—a rapid increase in stock prices, followed by a precipitous decline.

The word “bubble” tends to conjure an idea as thin as air. But as with all previous tech bubbles, the innovations of the current AI boom are real, the hype born of genuine promise. That promise, however, is exactly what is inflating the bubble. Once the mania begins to crack, as it inevitably will, the shock will ripple back through financial markets into a global economy increasingly dependent on AI for growth. The damage will be felt worldwide, not only in the countries leading AI’s development but in the many now battling to supply them with inputs, as well. The weakest players will be wiped out, and the survivors will consolidate their lead. And from the rubble, a more stable AI ecosystem will emerge.

The AI competition is already reshaping the economic world order. At the top of this order stand the United States, where the foundational AI model, ChatGPT, reached consumers in late 2022, and China, where DeepSeek made its debut around a year later. Since then, the race for supremacy has come to drive both countries’ economies. In the United States, growth has been fueled primarily by the hundreds of billions of dollars of Big Tech investment in AI projects and by consumer spending energized by gains in a stock market dominated by AI. The top ten stocks in the benchmark S&P 500 are all big AI players and together account for nearly 40 percent of the index and around half of its returns this year.

China is spending far less on AI but would be lost without it. After falling in the last decade, net exports—mainly of AI-related goods—now account for a third of China’s growth. The domestic economy is otherwise moribund, hampered by bad debts and a shrinking labor force. Beijing is also heavily supporting the export of its homegrown AI models, which are catching on because they are cheaper than American frontier models but perform nearly as well.

The AI rage now extends beyond Washington and Beijing. Investors are plowing funds into companies that supply American tech giants with the computing power, semiconductors, and other building blocks they need, fueling stock market booms worldwide. In Asia, for example, three major semiconductor companies have reaped the benefits: TSMC in Taiwan and SK Hynix and Samsung in South Korea, which comprise an even larger share of their local stock indexes than big American tech firms account for in the U.S. index. In these relatively small economies, the global frenzy for AI has generated even greater shocks than in the United States. The total value of South Korea’s market exploded from $1.5 trillion at the start of 2025 to over $5 trillion this summer. Individual investors saw bigger gains in stock wealth over 18 months than they had in a lifetime. Chip exports have driven real growth in Taiwan toward double digits in some recent quarters, a pace rarely approached in any economy.

Other countries at this level of the so-called AI stack of computing infrastructure have benefited similarly from the global market tilt toward builders of AI hardware, or “picks and shovels.” High tech now accounts for as much as half of all economic growth in Israel, owing in part to the country’s expertise in the high-speed networks needed to train AI. In Japan, firms that specialize in AI-related equipment, materials, robots, and semiconductors have become market darlings; after Kioxia, a pioneer in memory chips, became a major supplier to U.S. tech companies, its share price spiked to more than 45 times what it had been a year earlier, briefly becoming Japan’s most valuable stock.

All five of these leading tech powers—China, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and the United States—spend, on average, much more than other countries on R & D and technology as a percentage of GDP. But they are far from the only countries racing to secure footholds in the AI stack.

Below them, secondary suppliers are forging ties to the leaders. U.S. and Chinese AI giants are spending billions on data centers in Singapore and Malaysia. Firms are investing heavily in AI server production in Mexico, which is emerging as an important exporter of AI-related hardware, as well. At least half a dozen tech companies have launched AI projects in Vietnam, including a major R & D center and training facilities to develop the country’s talent pool.

In turn, the worldwide AI construction project is expected to provide years of steady demand for the raw materials required to wire and store energy for data centers: nickel from Indonesia, lithium from Australia, copper from Chile, Peru, and the Democratic Republic of Congo. And the large cloud-service providers known as hyperscalers are looking far and wide for that energy. Amazon, Google, Meta, and Microsoft accounted for half of all clean energy deals signed across the globe in 2025.

Not all countries have joined the bonanza. Those outside the stack struggle to attract investment and credit as the overwhelming focus on AI sucks money away from their industries. Much of Europe finds itself excluded. So do countries with emerging economies such as India and the Philippines, doubly shunned because their thriving back-office service sectors are particularly at risk of displacement by artificial intelligence.

It’s hard to think of any period in history when the markets were so obsessed with—and so dependent on the success of—a single sector. The Internet era saw money spill out of the booming sector and into other industries. Today, AI, and little else, is hot. Private spending on data center construction is up by roughly 50 percent from a year ago, while spending on all other building projects in the United States is down by five percent.

So far, the lift from AI has been strong enough to shield the global economy from the geopolitical shocks of the last two years, including U.S. President Donald Trump’s tariffs and the U.S.-Israeli war against Iran. Gains in advanced manufacturing and stock market–driven consumer spending continue to power growth in the United States, and AI-related exports are propping up countries further down the stack. Tech exports from Mexico and Thailand, for example, are rising fast enough to offset slack domestic demand in both economies.

The question is whether AI can sustain the world economy or is a bubble waiting to pop. A bubble can be characterized as a good idea taken too far. And there is mounting evidence that markets have begun to push the tantalizing idea of AI beyond its limits.

Going back centuries, pioneering innovations have spawned remarkably consistent patterns of human behavior. Firms spend heavily on developing a new technology in the hope of dominating a burgeoning industry. Investors follow, with professionals leading the way and amateurs jumping in later. The firms borrow to build; investors borrow to buy more stock in those firms. Trading reaches manic intensity. Prices rise beyond a point that potential profits from the innovation can support. As the peak nears, price increases both accelerate and become more volatile as doubt begins to gnaw at the edges of the frenzy.

This basic dynamic has driven bubbles in everything from tulips to housing since at least the 1600s. In short, the object of desire becomes overvalued, overowned, overinvested, and overleveraged—what I have elsewhere called the “Four O’s” of a bubble.

Historical evidence suggests that the most impressive innovations generate the biggest bubbles. The scale of the bubble also depends on how far and fast word of the hot new thing travels. In the 1870s, a series of bubbles, including one in railroad stocks, moved quickly between Europe and the United States via the new transatlantic telegraph. In the 1920s, electricity and electric gadgets were promoted over the airwaves on one of those gadgets, the radio. Today, hype for AI is spreading on smartphones and social media, digital products that did not yet exist when the Internet bubble popped, in 2000.

Taken together, these phenomena should raise concerns over just how far the mania will go. If AI is indeed the most transformative and most rapidly diffused new technology ever, the bubble it creates could be the most dramatic yet.

In the major bubbles of the past half century, prices have typically risen tenfold, adjusted for inflation, over a period of 15 years. U.S. tech stocks crossed that threshold in 2025. The AI bulls argue that unlike the dot-coms of the Internet boom, today’s marquee tech firms are generating strong earnings and will continue to do so, and their escalating stock prices are therefore justified.

But the excitement about tech bubbles is always stirred by genuine progress, not mere marketing. Although the dot-com era, for instance, is linked in the popular imagination with profitless startups such as Pets.com, these were minor players. The giants of the late 1990s, including Qualcomm, Oracle, and Cisco, logged earnings on par with Amazon, Google, and Microsoft today. The tech sector posted the same annual rate of earnings growth in the late 1990s as it has in the current decade: 22 percent.

The assumption that a boom can’t be a bubble if corporate earnings are strong is belied by history. It is in fact not uncommon in a bubble for earnings to be artificially inflated as well. This twin risk was only partly evident in the dot-com period, but it was clear when the craze for housing and commodities took off in the early 2000s.

What is more, earnings may not be as solid as they appear. They are propped up in part by “circular funding,” in which a hyperscaler invests in a frontier AI lab such as OpenAI or Anthropic. The lab then uses the money in part to buy services from—and thus pump up the earnings of—the hyperscaler. Hyperscalers also revalue their stake in an AI lab and report the increase as “other income,” a category that has accounted for a quarter of their earnings growth this year.

Americans, meanwhile, are investing in stocks as they never have before. The United States is the only country in which families hold more wealth in stocks than in their homes. The typical U.S. household holds more than 30 percent of its wealth in stocks, surpassing the prior peak of 27 percent in 2000. Since much of this money is held in “passive” funds that simply track the S&P 500 index, the bulk of it goes into the AI stocks that dominate the index.

Professional money managers aim to beat the market by being different, but they are finding it difficult to buck the crowd. Their holdings look increasingly like those of the retail investors they once dismissed as “dumb money.” Nearly a third of the stocks held by amateur traders are also held by hedge funds and other quintessentially aggressive pros, a record share.

Over the last five years, the number of shares traded each day in the United States has surged by 50 percent to around 18 billion. Retail investors have surpassed the pros as the most active traders and are aggressively “buying the dips”; in 2026, trading on down days has surged to more than three times the average for all days. Foreigners are betting big on American AI, too; their holdings of U.S. stocks have mushroomed from under $2 trillion to more than $20 trillion since the dot-com bust, with most of that increase coming since the launch of ChatGPT, in November 2022.

AI is thus already overowned by all players in the market. The zeal among retail investors has spread to other AI-driven markets, including South Korea, which in the past has been a bellwether of global stock market movements. Sharp drops in big semiconductor stocks pushed the Korean index down this summer, before locals once again started “buying the dips.” This kind of stop-and-go volatility often signals that a market rally is in its last stages.

Much of the debate about the economics of AI focuses on whether the enormous sums that technology firms are investing in building infrastructure can generate commensurate returns in the foreseeable future. Tech investment has more than quadrupled in the last three years, a boom unmatched in scale or speed by the rise of railways, canals, or the Internet. Tech spending on new plants and equipment hit a record 6.0 percent of GDP at the height of the Internet bubble. Today, it stands at 6.3 percent and is projected to keep increasing.

One way this splurge could pay for itself is by raising productivity, or output per worker, and energizing the entire economy. But at a similar stage of the Internet boom, growth in the key measure of “total factor productivity,” which aims to capture how much output workers are generating from machines, was surging. Now it is barely rising.

Even if productivity does ultimately rise, most AI firms may not reap the rewards. The economist William Nordhaus has shown that only two percent of the value created by innovations of the past, including railroads, airlines, and the Internet, went to the companies that built these networks. The rest went to the consumers and businesses that used the inventions. Most of the 50,000 startups of the dot-com boom were wiped out in the eventual bust, destroying wealth for their investors; around ten survive today as tech giants.

AI faces the prospect of a similar crunch in a wider field. With 70,000 AI-related startups in the United States and abroad, as the tech strategist Jeff McMillan recently put it, “the coming massacre of AI companies and the coming growth of the AI economy are the same event.” But caution fades before a bubble reaches peak size. AI spending is spreading worldwide, with roughly 40 percent of all outlays by U.S. hyperscalers going into projects abroad. Cumulative global AI-related investment is expected to more than double again, to $6 trillion by 2030.

In the biggest bubbles, investment reaches a tipping point after which the cost of the bust in failed businesses, bad debts, and job losses outweighs the benefits of the boom. Even companies at the vanguard of the new technology end up investing too heavily to earn a return. The Bank for International Settlements, a consortium of the world’s major central banks, warned in July that the hyperscalers’ spending race was on track to blow past this “socially optimal level” of investment.

The AI investment boom rests on hidden subsidies and debts, which makes it more fragile and harder to measure. A few years ago, the top five hyperscalers were still generating large piles of surplus cash flow; today, only Microsoft does. Four of them have seen an increase in net debt, issuing bonds as they run low on cash and have to borrow to fund their buildouts.

As of July, U.S. tech firms still had a relatively small reported net debt burden of $250 billion. But these visible figures do not include projects the hyperscalers are financing through complex leases and other off-the-books deals, which could add around $3 trillion to their borrowing over the next decade.

More support is coming from the public sector. During the late 1990s, the U.S. Federal Reserve kept monetary policy tighter than it does now, and the U.S. government ran a surplus. The money that financed the Internet boom came almost exclusively from the private sector.

Everything changed over the next decade, particularly after the 2008 global financial crisis. To revive and sustain growth, the Fed markedly loosened monetary policy—and kept it loose even when good times returned. Today, the Fed is holding its basic lending rate a full point below what standard academic guidelines would advise, given today’s low unemployment and solid growth. Inflation has exceeded the Fed target of two percent for more than five years running.

Meanwhile, the U.S. government deficit has doubled over the last decade to more than six percent of GDP, in effect transferring billions in borrowed money to households and corporations. According to the Kalecki Levy equation, which tracks the flow of government largess into corporate profits, the budget deficit has accounted for more than half of U.S. corporate profits this decade—twice the level of the dot-com era. Without sky-high deficits, AI earnings would not look nearly as strong as they do. And if AI earnings are not what they appear, the industry’s ability to manage a fast-rising debt burden is likely to be shaky, as well.

It’s not unusual for bubbles to hide behind murky accounting, but the scope of these hidden subsidies is new. So are the sources. The torrent of money pouring out of the government, the central bank, and the private sector will make the AI bubble much bigger than any of its predecessors.

Debates over how the current AI mania might end tend to focus on scenarios in which AI firms cut back on investment, report surprisingly weak earnings, or appear to concede one way or another that AI is not going to pay off richly or soon enough to justify current stock prices. Yet bubbles do not burst entirely of their own accord; investment crazes don’t wane because fundamentals suddenly change. Earnings can remain strong as bubbles inflate, and in a downturn, stock prices typically begin to fall several months before earnings do.

The one consistent signal comes from the financiers. Bubbles pop when liquidity tightens, meaning higher rates or stiffer regulation make money harder to borrow, and private creditors grow less willing to lend at any rate. As money dries up, firms and investors are left with less money to invest. This can happen in myriad ways.

Over the past century, central bank rate hikes have preceded the popping of every significant bubble. Now, however, it is governments, not corporations, who shoulder the largest burden of rising debt, and the most important players to watch are no longer central bankers. They are the “bond vigilantes,” investors who periodically punish free-spending governments by selling their bonds, pushing long-term interest rates to painfully high levels. Of late, vigilantes have been rearing their heads in the United States, Europe, and Japan. If they return in force, rising rates and the resulting liquidity squeeze will make it much harder for AI firms to keep funding their expansion plans through heavier borrowing, and could send the stock market plummeting.

This is how the endgame unfolds. Problems surface first in the debt markets, then spill over into other financial markets. Symptoms started to appear this summer in the U.S. and Asian markets with tremors in semiconductor shares, but other AI stocks may be at imminent risk. It’s hardly a surprise, then, that a research note from Bank of America recently warned that “bubble-like instability” was spreading worldwide.

Every boom sows the seeds of its own demise. Big tech stocks had been rising since 2009, but began to falter four years ago, as the Fed aggressively hiked rates. The release of ChatGPT and the end of Fed tightening then gave the tech bull a second wind. As the Bank for International Settlements noted, “The larger the boom, the larger the eventual bust.”

Each bubble since the crash of 1929 has been larger than those of the past. One reason is the financialization of the economy. Financial markets were roughly equal to global GDP in 1980, but nearly three times as large in 2000, when the Internet boom went bust. Today, they are five times larger. As markets have grown to dwarf the economy, the potential fallout of market crashes has grown with them. A 25 percent drop in the S&P 500 index, for example, would be about half the decline of 2001 or 2008 but would generate equally large losses for American households.

When a tech bubble deflates, the fallout tends to follow a standard trajectory. Lenders pull back, firms at the heart of the boom cut costs or close, bankruptcies pick up, building projects are shelved. Laid-off workers compete for jobs elsewhere, depressing wages in other industries. Some economies slow; a few may tip into recession. The economies that gained most at the height of the AI frenzy would be the first to suffer some combination of these effects. The shock would radiate from the United States to Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan, then down the AI stack through smaller suppliers, and eventually to the miners and power producers. Every burst bubble causes economic damage. This one would be felt around the world.

And the fallout could be magnified by distinguishing features of the AI era. To an unusual degree, growth in economies up and down the AI stack is driven by the twin investment and market booms. Rising stock prices are in turn having an inspiring “wealth effect” on consumer spending, as households feel richer and spend more when the value of their investments rises. This narrow dependence on AI could amplify the shock when the bubble deflates.

Another new twist is the debt backdrop. Never has the world entered a crisis of any kind carrying a debt burden this large. Driven mainly by government borrowing, total global debt spiked over the last year at the fastest pace since the COVID-19 pandemic, to more than $350 trillion. That is more than three times the size of the world economy. Once the AI boom ends, few governments will have the money to stimulate battered economies or bail out affected businesses.

Any attempt to rescue AI companies would meet serious resistance. Most Americans say they are more concerned than excited about AI. Seven in ten expect it to leave fewer jobs for humans. Data center projects across the United States now face determined community opposition. Local revolts blocked or delayed projects worth some $130 billion in the first quarter of this year alone. Politicians may read those numbers and think twice before stepping in to rescue faltering AI firms.

The worst bubbles form in industries that offer little in the way of innovation and are financed mainly by debt. The real estate bubble of the 2000s is a classic example. When it burst, the result was the long and painful Great Recession of 2008–9. Overextended lenders and borrowers took years to dig their way out of trouble, and the crash left behind nothing of value—no new inventions, no new industries, just deflated home prices. By contrast, the dot-com boom was a classic “good bubble” in a productive industry, financed by equity rather than debt. After it popped in 2001, the drop in stock prices was painful but fleeting. With debts low, borrowers did not struggle for long, and the resulting recession was short and mild. And this time, the crash left behind something of tremendous value: the Internet.

AI is a hybrid. It is a “good bubble” in an innovative technology financed by huge flows of both equity and debt. The fallout from the most recent big bubbles offers no clear framework for estimating the immediate economic losses of such a crash. This is uncharted territory, complicated by the fact that overextended governments cannot easily step in to stem the losses.

When good bubbles burst, they leave behind a streamlined network of stronger survivors, as was the case with the Internet and the railroad, canal, automobile, and television industries before it. Survivors of the AI crash will push the technology forward to the benefit of users worldwide and, in the rosiest scenario, provide the hoped-for boost to productivity growth.

The question is how big this boost might be—and when it will materialize. Often, new technologies raise productivity growth only decades after they appear in commercial form. Cars replaced horse carriages on the streets of New York in the 1910s but made their maximum impact on productivity only after the construction of the national highway system in the 1950s—decades after the market crash of 1929 wiped out hundreds of automakers, leaving only Chrysler, Ford, and General Motors standing.

A similar shakeout and delay could occur before artificial intelligence makes its mark. The most recent forecasts for AI’s potential boost to annual productivity growth range from 0.1 percentage points, which would barely be noticeable, to nearly 2.0 percentage points, which would be extraordinary. An increase that large would roughly double the sustainable economic growth rate worldwide, ushering in a golden age of prosperity at astonishing speed.

Earlier stages of the digital revolution offer reasons to temper those expectations. The Internet was also expected to spark a productivity boom and did so, briefly, in the late 1990s. But that boom eventually faded, overwhelmed by larger forces including the rise of the regulatory state and increasingly generous government bailouts. An AI-driven productivity boom would face similar headwinds.

That AI is advancing at a dizzying pace does not mean it is advancing outside history or the norms of human behavior. Manias create imbalances too large to last. When the crash comes, the damage will be severe but not permanent. To borrow a line from chemistry, nothing is created, nothing is destroyed, everything is transformed. The technology will keep moving forward even as the speculative frenzy around it fades, leaving behind a more balanced global marketplace, more rational spending by companies, and a reordering of the crazed AI world that exists today.

#5322. Trump-backed Whatley faces balancing act in North Carolina as polls tilt blue

LILLINGTON, N.C. — Making his first run for elective office, Republican Senate candidate Michael Whatley has visited every county in North Carolina as he crisscrosses the state introducing himself to voters.

“Just this morning, I went over 428,000 miles on my truck,” he said Saturday at a campaign stop at a sports bar here in this growing exurb about an hour south of Raleigh.

But in the race for North Carolina’s open Senate seat, Whatley, a onetime lobbyist who previously led the Republican National Committee, has consistently trailed in the polls behind Democrat Roy Cooper, the state’s battle-tested former governor.

Now, with time running out and national party funding drying up, Whatley faces a high-stakes balancing act: winning over unaffiliated voters who have soured on Donald Trump without alienating a Republican base that remains fiercely loyal to the president.

“North Carolina is a purple state,” he said in an interview after addressing supporters in Lillington. “We are 30 percent Republican, 30 percent Democrat, 40 percent unaffiliated, so we know that this is going to be a tight race.”

Roll Call on the Road: Courting the Carolinas

With Trump’s job approval languishing in the 30s amid voter dissatisfaction over soaring prices and the Iran war, the president’s presence looms across the entire midterm map. But the Senate race in North Carolina is perhaps where his influence has been most keenly felt.

Whatley doesn’t just have the president’s seal of approval: He got into the race because Trump publicly urged him to run after Sen. Thom Tillis, one of the president’s most persistent GOP critics, announced his retirement.

Trump, who carried North Carolina in all three of his elections, was in Greenville on Tuesday, rallying Republicans during his second trip to the state since mid-September. Vice President JD Vance campaigned for Whatley and other Republicans in Western North Carolina on Monday.

“I’m the one that got him into this,” Trump said of Whatley during Tuesday’s event. “I hope it works out.”

Whatley’s political career is “inextricably tied to Donald Trump, said Christopher A. Cooper, a political science professor at Western Carolina University (and no relation to the Democratic nominee.) “Even JD Vance was elected to the Senate before he became Trump’s vice president.”

Those ties make it difficult for Whatley, a first-time candidate, to distance himself from the president, according to professor Cooper. Other Republican Senate contenders facing tough races, such as Rep. Ashley Hinson in Iowa and former Rep. Mike Rogers in Michigan, have called for an end to the war in Iran, which has driven up prices, and offered up other critiques of the Trump agenda.

But Whatley is taking a different approach, one based on his belief that Trump “is uniquely capable of turning out Republican votes, and that’s what this election is going to come down to,” the former RNC chairman said in an interview Saturday.

That’s certainly the case for Bill Le Fever, a Republican voter from the Eastern North Carolina retirement destination of New Bern. Le Fever said he didn’t know much about Whatley except that Trump had endorsed him, “but that’s good enough for me.”

“I’m a Trumpy all the way,” added the retired insurance salesman who moved to the state from Ohio about two years ago.

Bill Holmes, a retired land manager who came to the bar in Lillington to hear Whatley, thought Trump was doing an “outstanding job.” But for this Republican voter, the praise comes with caveats: “He’s a little obnoxious, a little ego-ish.”

Then there’s the national debt. “That’s one issue I’ve got with Trump: increased spending,” he said. “For my children and my grandkids, I don’t know what it’s going to be like for them.”

Still, Holmes wouldn’t even consider voting for Cooper, especially after California Gov. Gavin Newsom campaigned for Cooper and other North Carolina Democrats in Charlotte last month.

“He’s a far-left socialist,” Holmes said of Newsom. Cooper’s campaign noted that the pair did not appear together.

The North Carolina race is crucial to determining which party will control the Senate next year: Democrats need a net gain of four seats to take the majority.

Initially viewed as among the nation’s most competitive, the race appears to be slipping from the GOP’s grasp. Cooper led Whatley by 7 points in an East Carolina University poll released earlier this month. After months of attack ads from both candidates — and the super PACs that back them — just 6 percent of the electorate were undecided, the survey found.

While the race was once seen as a Toss-up, Inside Elections with Nathan L. Gonzales now rates it Lean Democratic.

North Carolina Democrats aren’t cracking open the Cheerwine just yet though. History, after all, isn’t on their side in a state that last elected a Democrat to the Senate in 2008.

But Cooper, a genial centrist with an Eastern North Carolina drawl, could be that “once-in-a-generation candidate” to end the Senate drought, according to Anderson Clayton, the chair of the state Democratic Party. He’s run statewide six times — four times as attorney general and twice as governor — and has never lost an election, including winning three races when Trump carried the state, Clayton noted.

That familiarity won him the support of Patrick Robinette, a 50-year-old unaffiliated voter who stopped by a Cooper canvass launch in Rocky Mount last week.

“It’s the devil you know versus devil you don’t know,” said Robinette, who identified himself as a distant relative of former President Joe Biden. (He said his grandfather and Biden’s mother were cousins.) “I mean, he’s been a North Carolina political leader, and we don’t know anything about Michael Whatley.”

From the start of the campaign, Cooper has zeroed in on the cost of living while portraying Whately as a rubber-stamp for the unpopular president. He’s traveled the state blaming rising costs on Trump’s tariffs as well as the war in Iran, a message summed up by his campaign slogan: Make Stuff Cost Less.

“So many people have too much month at the end of the money,” he quipped before a group of supporters in Rocky Mount, where he practiced as a young lawyer fresh out of law school at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. “And we have an administration that promised that cost of living would be the day-one issue. Not only has that not been the case, they have made it so much worse.”

JERUSALEM—For 75 days starting in 2020, Luwam Habteab slept on the floor of a prison cell, given little food and no medical care. She was detained that November by Ethiopian security forces for her reporting on the Axum massacre, in which Eritrean forces killed hundreds of civilians over a weekend—one of the deadliest episodes of the 2020-22 Tigray war.

Upon her release, Habteab found that her apartment had been sealed by federal police. Her family had warned her not to return there, telling her that security forces were waiting. A local archbishop with a connection at the Israeli Embassy interceded, after which Habteab was ushered out of the country. She landed in Tel Aviv in August 2023 and applied for asylum. (Habteab is identified by a pseudonym out of fear of reprisal.)

Habteab now lives in Jerusalem’s Old City, where she spends most of her days praying. As of August, she was one of 1,334 Ethiopians with open asylum claims before Israel’s Interior Ministry, according to figures that the ministry confirmed to Foreign Policy.

Months after Habteab arrived, in January 2024, the Israeli Interior Ministry determined that the situation in Ethiopia was stable enough to send its nationals home, revoking the group protection that had shielded asylum-seekers from deportation. That stability did not last: This July, Tigray’s regional authorities declared that the 2022 peace agreement with the federal government had collapsed, accusing Addis Ababa of preparing for war.

A woman walks past clothes left to dry on the balcony of a school used as shelter at a camp for displaced people in Mekelle, in Ethiopia’s Tigray region, on July 19, 2024. Michele Spatari/AFP via Getty Images

Last month, an alliance of rebel groups launched a full-scale offensive against federal forces, seizing airports across Tigray and pushing into neighboring Afar and Amhara states. Federal forces have since retaken Tigray’s capital, though the rebels have vowed to keep fighting; Eritrea and Ethiopia have cut diplomatic ties. Explosions have been reported in Addis Ababa, which Israeli authorities say is safe for returnees.

Habteab has watched with dread as the fighting returned to her home province. Much of Tigray is under a communications blackout, reviving fears of the total siege during the 2020-22 war that starved the region and killed as many as 600,000 people. Habteab’s family in Tigray is almost impossible to reach, she said.

With Israel’s national elections set for Oct. 27, civil society advocates warn that right-wing politicians are once again casting asylum-seekers as a threat to gain a leg up. Israeli officials refer to some migrants and refugees as “infiltrators,” a term embedded in the country’s own laws, and successive governments have described their presence as a danger to Israel’s Jewish character.

Habteab and other asylum-seekers in Israel have few places left to go as domestic politics turn against them and Western governments dismantle the asylum system that was supposed to protect them.

Ethiopian Orthodox Christian pilgrims light candles during a ceremony on the roof of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem’s Old City on April 19, 2025. John Wessels/AFP via Getty Images

Israel has a long history with the notion of asylum. Founded by refugees in the wake of the Holocaust as a safe haven for the Jewish diaspora, the country was among the first signatories of the 1951 Refugee Convention. Jewish jurists were instrumental in shaping the convention’s core nonrefoulement principle, the rule that no one may be returned to a country where their life is at risk.

Yet, since the Israeli government took over processing asylum claims from the United Nations in 2009, it has granted asylum status to less than 1 percent of those seeking it, according to HIAS Israel, a Jewish humanitarian nonprofit.

Israel has also long held a place in the imagination of Ethiopian asylum-seekers as the land of the ancient Israelites, in whose story of exile and return they saw themselves. In practical terms, the country was also the closest state offering asylum that they could reach without undertaking the perilous Mediterranean crossing.

Of the Ethiopians in Israel who are not Jewish, some—such as Habteab—fled during the Tigray war and its aftermath. But most have been in Israel for a decade or more, arriving on pilgrimage visas or by crossing the Egyptian border before Israel completed its fence there in 2013. They, too, are asylum-seekers: Almost all applied for protection after arriving, having fled earlier rounds of interethnic violence and state persecution, according to the Jerusalem African Community Center (JACC), a support and advocacy organization for the city’s African asylum-seekers.

On arrival, they opened asylum claims with the Interior Ministry and were interviewed about why they had come to Israel. Under Israeli law, the ministry was meant to review each claim and decide whether to grant refugee status—a process that, for most of these asylum-seekers, didn’t happen. Now, according to advocates, many Ethiopian applicants are being summoned back after more than a decade for a second interview to determine whether they can stay in Israel.

Demonstrators wave the former Ethiopian flag, the Eritrean flag, and the Israeli flag as thousands of African asylum-seekers protest against the detention of immigrants and widespread refusal to grant refugee status, seen in Tel Aviv, Israel, on Jan. 5, 2014.Jack Guez/AFP via Getty Images

Asked for comment, Israel’s Population and Immigration Authority said, “there is no policy of deportation regarding this group” and that cases are examined individually. The authority added that it does not determine whether a country is safe for returnees, referring questions to the Israeli Foreign Ministry. (The Foreign Ministry did not respond to a request for comment.)

Anat Ben-Dor, who runs the Refugee Rights Clinic at Tel Aviv University, said the burden of proof placed on applicants for asylum to establish a credible fear of persecution is set very high and that interviewers look for inconsistencies between what an applicant says now and what they said on arrival.

“The whole interview is actually an effort not to find the truth, but to find flaws and problems in the testimony,” Ben-Dor said. “Very few people can successfully pass such an interview, particularly when the system is so slow that people are being interviewed about events that sometimes occurred 10 years earlier.”

Asked whether the system was dysfunctional or intentionally obstructive, Michal Pomeranz, another lawyer who represents asylum-seekers, said it was both. “They clearly want to make people’s lives here miserable, and that’s something even the Supreme Court has said,” she said, referring to a 2020 ruling that struck down a law requiring asylum-seekers to deposit 20 percent of their wages into a fund that they could recover only by leaving the country.

After the follow-up interview, the Interior Ministry decides whether an asylum-seeker may stay in Israel or must leave—but the outcome is almost always the same, according to Yael Paul Strauss, who runs the psychosocial department at JACC.

By her account, 90 percent to 95 percent of those summoned are rejected and given three to four months to leave the country. “It’s presented as giving them another chance, but in reality, they’re just being called in to be told they have to leave,” Paul Strauss said.

The cost of contesting a rejection is steep, running at around $4,000 to petition a denied application and another $4,000 for an appeal, according to Paul Strauss. Few employers will hire someone with a temporary permit, regardless of their profession or level of education in Ethiopia, leaving most asylum-seekers with few ways to raise that kind of money.

Paul Strauss described a message that she had recently received from a mother of three small children who had paid to appeal her asylum rejection, lost, and had no idea where she would find the money to try again. “She turns to me, ‘Please help me, save my life,’ and I have nothing to give her,” Paul Strauss said.

A former refugee from Ethiopia (left) arrives to vote for the first time at a polling station in Clarkston, Georgia, in the United States on Nov. 5, 2024. Yasuyoshi Chiba/AFP via Getty Images

For many Ethiopian asylum-seekers, the answer to Israel’s stonewalling was to seek resettlement in a third country, another cornerstone of asylum law. For years, the main destination was the United States, which accepted a small number of the most vulnerable cases referred by the U.N. However, that route is now all but closed.

When he returned to office in 2025, U.S. President Donald Trump effectively dismantled the U.S. refugee admissions program and suspended the entry of all refugees into the country, including those who had already been screened. Following legal challenges, the Trump administration resumed limited admissions under a historically low cap of 7,500 refugees for fiscal year 2026—admissions that have been allocated almost entirely to white South African Afrikaners.

In December 2025, the Trump administration also terminated the temporary protected status that Ethiopians were granted during the Tigray war. Around 900 asylum-seekers in Israel—mainly Sudanese and Eritrean, along with some Ethiopians—were in the pipeline to be resettled to the United States as of early 2025, according to Sivan Carmel, the country director of HIAS Israel, which processed the cases.

Some had already cleared their medical and security checks, had departure dates, and needed only plane tickets. “They’d left their jobs; they’d already told their landlords they’d be leaving” before their entry to the United States was denied, Carmel said.

The office of the U.N. refugee agency in Israel, which referred those cases and managed resettlement out of the country, has been hollowed out as part of the same pattern of global retrenchment against asylum—its staff reduced to three and its Tel Aviv office cut. Agency staff struggle to keep up with the thousands of open asylum claims.

This has left the work to fall to groups such as JACC, which operates on a shoestring budget. Paul Strauss spends hours filling in resettlement forms with families who cannot afford a lawyer. Each time Paul Strauss submits a file, she said, the U.N. representative reminds her that completing it does not guarantee placement elsewhere.

For those who cannot leave for another country and cannot stay in Israel, the return of war to Ethiopia has taken away their last consolation: the hope that the danger that they fled might be in the past. As armed forces push toward Ethiopia’s capital and much of Tigray remains in the dark, many of the families that Israel intends to send back can’t even get in touch with their relatives.

For those working on behalf of the asylum-seekers, the bigger picture is not lost. “I want to live in a state that I’m not embarrassed by,” Pomeranz said. “I kept thinking things couldn’t get worse. They keep getting worse.”

How Beijing Is Challenging Washington in Outer Space

On the morning of August 19, China’s space industry crossed a major threshold. Shortly after the Chinese aerospace company LandSpace launched a rocket in northwestern China, its booster—the part needed to thrust the rocket into orbit—successfully detached and landed back on earth. It was the first time a Chinese firm recovered a rocket on land, and it came six weeks after the country’s first-ever rocket recovery, carried out in the South China Sea by China’s premier state-owned space contractor. These events mark the dawn of China’s reusable rocket launch—a capability that will unlock enormous potential in the country’s booming space industry.

Washington’s response to these developments has been muted. The United States still conducts more than twice as many launches and has more satellites in orbit than any other country. But China is catching up. Between 2020 and 2025, the number of rockets launched annually by Chinese companies more than doubled, from 39 to 92. Chinese entities have applied for licenses for hundreds of thousands of communications satellites, and Chinese space companies are proliferating. Many of them, such as LandSpace, are relatively new but already innovative.

This progress has both economic and military implications. Chinese companies could soon compete with U.S. firms to provide vast swaths of the globe with space-based services such as satellite Internet. Meanwhile, the People’s Liberation Army (PLA), which sees space superiority as essential to winning future wars, is developing a range of space capabilities as well as a suite of weapons that can attack satellites. This poses a serious risk to the U.S. military, which depends on space for communications, situational awareness, and navigation, as well as for conducting precision strikes and receiving missile warnings.

China’s development as a space power should be a wake-up call for the United States. After the end of the Cold War, Washington began to take its dominance in space for granted. It can no longer afford to do so. As Beijing’s space industry advances, its cost advantages will make it much harder for U.S. companies to compete, and China’s military capabilities will increase the risk that space becomes a battlefield in future conflicts. The era of unassailable U.S. space power is ending, and Washington has no choice but to adapt.

China’s space program dates to the 1950s, when Chinese leader Mao Zedong initiated efforts to modernize the PLA and boost technological innovation. In 1970, China launched its first satellite, and in the 1980s, it made space technology research a national priority. But the watershed moment for China’s space program occurred during the 1990–91 Gulf War, when the U.S. military’s adept use of space-based assets allowed a relatively small but technologically advanced coalition to defeat Iraq’s army in just a few days. Satellites allowed U.S. forces to navigate difficult desert terrain, gave commanders information about the battlefield with greater speed and accuracy, and enabled soldiers to coordinate seamlessly across long distances. Beijing, which had not realized just how far behind it was, leaped into action to catch up.

In the years that followed, PLA doctrine emphasized the growing importance of advanced technology, especially satellites, for warfighting. In 2000, the Chinese military began developing its own satellite-powered navigation service, called BeiDou, so that it would not have to rely on U.S.-operated GPS. In 2015, the PLA created the Strategic Support Force, a now defunct organization that consolidated China’s space, cyber, and electronic warfare missions, with the aim of accelerating the country’s space progress. That same year, a Chinese government white paper identified outer space as a critical national security domain. China has since created spacecraft for communications; navigation; and intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance that would have been unimaginable to PLA officials who observed Desert Storm.

Encouraging commercial space activity has become one of China’s main tools for expanding its space program. In 2014, Beijing opened parts of the space sector to private investment, and more than 400 Chinese commercial space companies have since sprouted. Beijing and provincial governments have provided commercial space firms with financing through state-backed investment funds, subsidized factory upgrades and rocket launches, and given private companies access to government test facilities and technologies. In 2025, commercial space missions accounted for 54 percent of China’s national total, and commercial satellites made up 84 percent of Chinese spacecraft placed in orbit. China’s broad space-industrial ecosystem, composed of commercial companies, state-owned enterprises, research institutes, and universities, has allowed the country to eliminate its dependence on GPS and paved the way for the development of new domestic remote sensing and satellite communications companies.

In reusable rocket technology, the U.S. company SpaceX has enjoyed an enormous advantage, conducting five out of every six launches in the United States. But China’s two controlled recoveries of rocket boosters in July and August suggest that it could be a real competitor to the United States within a decade. Reusable rockets drive down the cost and increase the speed of space launches significantly; should China master the technology, it could develop large satellite constellations much more easily.

Reusable launches will also allow China to develop space-based Internet capabilities to compete with SpaceX’s dominant Starlink network, an extensive satellite Internet system with connectivity that rivals many land-based networks and which has been crucial for Ukraine in its defense against Russia. Although Chinese companies are still in the early stages of developing this technology, they are scaling fast. The entities operating the Guowang and Qianfan satellite constellations, which intend to compete with Starlink, plan to launch nearly 30,000 communications satellites over the next decade or so. These systems are currently much smaller than Starlink, which has more than 11,000 satellites in orbit, but they could eventually become legitimate alternatives. Qianfan already has trial agreements with Brazil, Kazakhstan, Malaysia, and Turkey for the use of its satellite network.

China’s commercial space development has gone hand in hand with a growing ability to use space to support its military modernization. Beijing has spent decades studying the U.S. military’s use of satellites, and it is now developing its own national security–related space capabilities. According to the Pentagon, China operates more than 500 satellites that can conduct intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance missions, in addition to its satellite-enabled positioning system, allowing the PLA to locate and track U.S. and allied forces around the world. PLA strategists have identified Defense Department space assets as valuable targets because the U.S. military depends on them for nearly every step of what it calls the “kill chain”: finding, aiming at, and striking targets, and then assessing the results.

To exploit U.S. dependence on satellites, Beijing is investing in antisatellite weapons. Since 2007, when it conducted its first successful test of an antisatellite missile, China has developed other tools, including electronic warfare and directed-energy weapons, which can interfere with satellite signals or damage sensors without physically destroying satellites. Chinese spacecraft can also execute increasingly sophisticated maneuvers: in 2022, a Chinese satellite called Shijian-21 attached itself to a defunct Chinese satellite and moved it into a different orbit. Such an action could be benign—it could be used to repair and refuel satellites or remove debris—but it could also enable Chinese platforms to manipulate other spacecraft, including U.S. military satellites. Beijing’s ability to take down U.S. satellites could pose an especially serious challenge for the U.S. military in East Asia, where, in the case of conflict, China would be much less dependent on space-based assets than the United States.

China’s space rise is already influencing broader international security dynamics. During Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, in 2022, Chinese firms provided satellite imagery of the battlefield that helped Russian forces conduct military operations. Throughout the war in Iran, Chinese entities have also supplied Tehran with satellite imagery, including images of a base in Jordan that Iran struck in July, resulting in the deaths of three American service members. As China’s commercial space sector expands, so could these relationships, giving more countries and nonstate actors without advanced space programs access to their benefits. Such expanded access could be used during natural disasters or other emergencies to save lives. But it could also provide adversaries with the ability to more effectively target U.S. and allied forces across the globe.

As China’s space power grows, Washington has moved to protect some of its satellite networks. The U.S. Space Force has diversified the placement of some of its most sensitive assets and increased procurement of new satellites. Such investments are useful but insufficient to address the United States’ vulnerabilities. Given the capabilities that China already has or will develop soon, there are now many possible scenarios in which the U.S. military would have to operate when satellite communications are denied or degraded, GPS signals are jammed, and intelligence from orbit is restricted.

The U.S. military should invest in nascent technologies, such as inertial navigation systems and quantum sensors, which could one day help its forces move with precision without GPS. It should also invest in emerging alternatives to satellites, such as high-altitude relays, which include drones or balloons that operate closer to Earth and can link communications networks; laser-enabled communications, which use light to transmit information and are difficult to disrupt; and mesh networks, which automatically reroute signals around nodes that have been destroyed, making communications more resilient. Mechanisms for delegating decision-making will be even more essential than in the past, as the danger of disconnection increases.

Washington should also increase its efforts to foster a more competitive domestic launch industry. The United States leads the world in space launches largely because of SpaceX. Although the company is an enormous asset, its near monopoly is a risk. If technical failures or damage to launch infrastructure were to interrupt SpaceX’s operations, the United States’ ability to launch satellites would be severely curtailed. This year, SpaceX announced plans to retire the Falcon 9, its main reusable rocket. The Falcon 9 will eventually be replaced by Starship, a new high-capacity rocket that will carry more satellites into orbit with each launch. But the beginning of the phase-out has already created a launch shortage that threatens the continued development of the U.S. space economy. To mitigate this risk, Washington should continue awarding government contracts to more companies.

In addition to investing in launch capacity, the United States should provide support to companies building emerging space technologies that have important national security applications but which struggle to attract sufficient commercial demand in their early years—an approach it took with SpaceX starting in the first decade of this century. Federal agencies could be anchor customers for technologies that Washington identifies as crucial for national security purposes, providing companies that make them with stable revenue and legitimacy that will allow them to grow.

Identifying important emerging technologies and consistently funding them is essential to maintaining a lead in space. Unfortunately, U.S. priorities in the sector have been susceptible to changes across presidential administrations and in Congress. When the Soviet Union collapsed, so did Washington’s willingness to invest heavily in space. The United States did not, of course, completely abandon space, as evidenced by projects such as the space shuttle program, the Artemis moon missions, and the founding of the U.S. Space Force. But the government’s interest in the domain has waxed and waned. Beijing, on the other hand, has pursued its space objectives consistently since the early 1990s, revising programs as technology evolves without losing sight of its end goal. If Washington is to continue competing with China, it should sustain its commitments, too.

Finally, the United States would benefit from working with its allies. NATO is already experimenting with ways to share members’ space expertise and integrate it into allied operations. In July, eight U.S. allies launched the HALO initiative, which aims to connect sovereign military satellites into a network that streamlines communications, intelligence, and missile tracking. NATO’s STARLIFT project, established in 2024, is exploring how launch sites across allied territory could speed up access to orbit during a crisis. The United States, which has deprioritized international security cooperation in recent years, should recommit to allied space security. Having access to allies’ space resources will be essential in future conflicts that stretch across vast distances, especially if the United States’ own space-based assets are taken down.

Even the most effective modernization and resilience measures will not be enough to protect U.S. assets in space from determined adversaries. For decades, the United States was able to depend on space dominance in fighting wars. Now, overdependence on satellites and on SpaceX are major liabilities. Washington must protect itself in a world in which it is no longer the sole space power. Staying ahead in the new space race will require both investing in space architecture and establishing backup plans to offset the dangers of the emerging multipolar space age.

#5310. Mercedes’ new GLC 400 EV crushes range estimates, driving nearly 400 miles

The new 2027 Mercedes-Benz GLC 400 4Matic EV has an EPA-estimated range of up to 331 miles, but in the real world, it will likely go even farther.

Electric vehicles are becoming more efficient thanks to advances in batteries, drive units, and more, enabling longer driving ranges and faster charging.

The new 2027 Mercedes-Benz GLC 400 4Matic EV has an EPA-estimated range of up to 331 miles, which is more than enough for at least a few days of commuting on average.

In everyday driving conditions, it may drive much farther than its official numbers suggest. During Edmunds’ real-world range test, the GLC 400 EV drove 392 miles, or 18% more than its EPA estimate.

An extra 61 miles of range is huge. It could add another two, three, or even more days of commuting between charges.

The electric GLC was also more efficient, consuming just 28.4 kWh per 100 miles, about 14% less than the EPA’s official 33 kWh per 100 miles.

Mercedes’ new GLC outperformed most of the competition in the real-world range test. The 2027 Volvo EX60 P10 AWD traveled 352 miles, the 2025 Cadillac Optiq covered 339 miles, and the 2027 Audi Q6 E-Tron Prestige managed 337 miles.

The Edmunds real-world range test is designed to provide a more accurate estimate of an EV’s range in everyday driving conditions.

It consists of 60% city and 40% highway driving with an average speed of 40 mph. The vehicle stays within 5 mph of the posted speed limit, and the climate control is set on auto at 72 degrees.

While charging speeds weren’t tested, the GLC 400’s 94 kWh battery supports DC fast charging up to 330 kW. Mercedes says it takes about 22 minutes to charge from 10% to 80%.

One thing is for certain: electric cars are quickly becoming more efficient with faster charging and longer driving range.

Several articles have implied that a tested EV has "blown past its EPA estimate." Not really. Edmunds uses a slower average speed for their tests than EPA. Other testers evaluate highway mileage runs, and guess what, they fall well short of their EPA test.

These results are exactly what one should expect.

The number of electric cars offering 300 miles or more of EPA-estimated range has grown from just 1 model in 2016 (the Tesla Model S 90D) to around 60 by the end of 2026.

Several new 2027 models are now pushing closer to 400 miles of EPA range on a single charge. A recent BMW Group study found that Americans want more range than what they actually need.

The study found that 73% say 75 miles or less would cover their daily driving routine, while 42% want at least 300 miles before considering an EV. Only 18% of respondents said they wanted 400+ miles.

#5309. The Middle East’s Quiet Liberal Revolution

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

Mansoor Moaddel is Professor of Sociology at the University of Maryland, College Park, and the author of Cracks in Tradition: Liberal Trends in the Middle East.

The idea that the Middle East is becoming more liberal may sound counterintuitive. Nearly 50 years ago, a revolution in Iran dismantled the country’s secular order and ushered in an absolutist clerical regime grounded in Shiite fundamentalism whose battle cry was “Death to America.” The region has since been an incubator for numerous terrorist attacks in Europe and North America, insurgent movements committed to authoritarian theocracy, and repressive regimes that subjugate women, crush free expression, and marginalize minorities. By now, it might seem easy to conclude that Middle Eastern societies are simply less interested in liberalism—that they are governed by a fundamentally different, more religiously oriented “regime of truth,” as the philosopher Michel Foucault declared in 1978 about the uprising in Iran. In this view, a system of rule in which fundamentalists claim authority to define the faith as they see fit—subordinating individual liberty to religious diktats, promoting male supremacy, and imposing restrictive dress codes on women—will forever distinguish the region from the West.

But this assessment mistakes the consequences of domination for its causes. Beneath the authoritarian entrenchment, another history has been unfolding. Between 2000 and 2020, I supervised a series of surveys in the Middle East, conducting face-to-face interviews with representative samplings of people in five key countries to understand why citizens hold the values they do and to track shifts in those values. To varying degrees, respondents in these countries—Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Saudia Arabia, and Turkey—expressed an increasing preference for liberal values, even as their rulers sought to suppress popular demands for democratic change. (We focused on these countries because their size and political importance made them valuable cases for testing sociological theories.)

The survey queried respondents about a broad range of values, but we sought to capture their attitudes toward liberal values with 15 questions covering three dimensions: expressive individualism, gender equality, and secular politics. Expressive individualism was measured by a preference for marrying for love rather than parental approval, independence and imagination over obedience and religious faith as desirable qualities in children, and the right of women to dress as they wish. Views on gender equality were elicited through questions on polygamy, wifely obedience, and giving priority to men in politics, the job market, and education. Perspectives on secular politics were assessed through queries about separating religion from politics; Western-style governance; Islamic government; and sharia law. We then combined the responses to generate a “liberalism index” score of 1 to 4 for each population in each survey, with higher scores indicating greater support for liberalism.

These indices showed significant increases in liberalism, although the magnitude of the change differed by domain and country. Support for Islamic government and sharia generally declined across the region. Among Iranians, there has been a growing demand for the separation of religion and politics, for gender equality, and for freedom of dress. These changing values have translated into political action, most notably in the Woman, Life, Freedom movement of 2022 and the nationwide protests of 2025–26, when tens of thousands of protesters were reported to have been killed by the Islamic Republic’s security forces. In Egypt, the proportion of respondents who considered Islamic government to be “very good” fell from 25 percent in 2011 to 16 percent in 2020; the share who felt sharia law was “very important” declined from 28 percent to 18 percent. Among Iraqi respondents, the proportion that strongly supported Islamic government and sharia law fell from 30 and 34 percent, respectively, in 2004 to 15 and 14 percent in 2011.

Although many survey questions on forms of government were excluded in Saudi Arabia because of political sensitivities, surveys in that country captured significant changes: the proportion of respondents who considered it very important to implement sharia law fell from 73 percent in 2003 to 35 percent in 2011. On gender equality, meanwhile, the share who strongly agreed that university education is more important for boys than for girls declined significantly, especially among Egyptians (from 15 percent in 2001 to eight percent in 2020) and Iraqis (from 29 percent in 2004 to eight percent in 2011—a level that remained unchanged, on average, in 2021–22, according to the research group Arab Barometer).

A more fine-grained look at the results in individual countries suggests that the experience of religious domination itself may have fed, rather than foreclosed, a desire for a responsive political order. It can take time for popular ideas to reshape a society’s governing institutions. Yet Middle Eastern history shows that shifts in values can be crucial in bringing about changes in social and political organization. The rise of liberal nationalist movements in Egypt, Syria, and Iran in the early twentieth century, for example, was preceded by the emergence of liberal and nationalist ideologies decades earlier. The same pattern is evident in the rise of Islamic fundamentalism in the 1960s and 1970s, which culminated in the establishment of Islamic regimes and the spread of Islamist extremism.

Between 2000 and 2020, we conducted five surveys in Egypt, three in Iran, seven in Iraq, two in Saudi Arabia, and three in Turkey. We also considered specific findings in surveys conducted after 2011 in Iraq by Arab Barometer and in Iran by the World Values Survey in 2020, in which the questions mirrored our own. These data reveal a striking contrast between political developments and public opinion: even as the Arab Spring was smothered in its cradle, jihadist movements terrorized the region, Turkish leader Recep Tayyip Erdogan pursued dreams of Ottoman revivalism, Iran’s supreme leader sought to cast the country as the center of a Shiite-led axis of resistance, and Saudi leaders defended Sunni sectarianism, publics in each of these key Middle Eastern countries increasingly favored liberal values.

For instance, Egypt’s liberalism index rating rose from 2.09 to 2.28 among men and from 2.08 to 2.39 among women. The most striking change was a surge in support for separating religion and politics and for adopting a Western style of government. Respondents were asked whether they strongly agreed, agreed, disagreed, or strongly disagreed that Egypt would be a better place if religion and politics were separated and if its government resembled those of Western countries. The percentage that strongly agreed or agreed with separating religion and politics rose from 44 percent in 2007, when we first asked the question, to 56 percent in 2011, 82 percent in 2016, and 81 percent in 2020. Support for Western-style government increased from 30 percent in 2007 to 37 percent in 2011, 55 percent in 2016, and 82 percent in 2020.

To understand these shifts, we interviewed a sampling of panel respondents whose views had changed between 2011 and 2020. Among those who remembered their earlier responses, a large majority attributed their newfound support for separating religion from politics to disillusionment with the Muslim Brotherhood, the Islamist movement whose candidate won Egypt’s 2012 presidential election. Those who had come to favor Western models of governance cited the economic prosperity of Western countries and hoped that the adoption of similar institutions would bring comparable prosperity to Egypt. Egyptians’ support for gender equality and expressive individualism also increased, albeit more modestly.

Turkish popular opinion also shifted significantly toward liberal values. Between 2013 and 2020, Turkey’s liberalism index rose from 2.64 to 2.88 among men and from 2.70 to 2.91 among women. In both Egypt and Turkey, liberal values expanded more broadly among women than men. But whereas the liberal shift in Egypt was especially pronounced in attitudes toward secular politics, in Turkey the largest shifts toward liberal views related to views of gender equality and expressive individualism.

These liberalizing trends unfolded even as Erdogan pursued an increasingly authoritarian, Islamist political project. Erdogan’s appointment as prime minister in 2003 after a ballot-box triumph by his Justice and Development Party was initially hailed as a sign that a form of Islamism could coexist with democracy. But he would eventually abandon any real commitment to democratic governance, dismantling institutional checks and balances and forcing a 2017 constitutional referendum that entrenched his power. Erdogan’s attempt to foster an Ottoman-style pan-Islamism appears to have accelerated liberalization rather than strengthened religious conformity.

An important indication of this process is the shift in Turkish respondents’ self-identification toward nation rather than religion under Erdogan, alongside a decline in mosque attendance. In 2001, 64 percent of respondents identified primarily as Muslim, and only 34 percent identified foremost as Turkish citizens. By 2013, these figures had shifted to 39 and 44 percent, respectively; in 2016, they stood at 36 and 53 percent, and by 2020, at 24 percent for Muslim identity and 63 percent for Turkish citizenship. Mosque attendance also declined over this period: the proportion of respondents who reported attending a mosque more than once a week fell from 19 percent in 2001 to 16 percent in 2013 and then to just 11 percent in 2020. Given that mosque attendance was already declining before Erdogan came to power, his initial appeal may have owed less to religious conservatism than to his record as mayor of Istanbul and widespread frustration with Turkey’s secular political establishment. But our survey respondents’ subsequent shift toward national identity, coupled with a further decline in mosque attendance, suggest that Erdogan’s turn toward authoritarianism may have intensified an ongoing trend toward liberalization.

It may seem an irony of history that the very forces seeking to discredit liberalism have helped generate support for it. But conventional explanations emphasizing liberalism’s association with economic development and the rise of a middle class cannot fully account for ideological shifts. A given class may embrace liberalism under one set of historical conditions and illiberalism under another. (For instance, liberal values in early-twentieth-century Egypt were supported by the new middle class, the effendiyya, but later, the same class came to favor the fundamentalist Muslim Brotherhood.) Rather, ideas acquire meaning in opposition to other ideas that dominate society. This contestation takes place through philosophical debates, religious disputations, and public discussions, whether direct or indirect, open or clandestine. When a constellation of ideas comes to occupy a dominant position in a society, it simultaneously becomes a target of contestation and a catalyst for the production of ideas. A yearning for political equality tends to arise in opposition to authoritarian power, just as demands for gender equality emerge in reaction to patriarchal institutions, the desire to express one’s individuality in response to familial or communal constraints, and the demand for freedom of dress in defiance of restrictive sartorial regulations.

The Middle East’s interest in liberal values—that is, the value of separating religion from politics, the celebration of individual judgment, and the recognition of the individual rather than a collective as the fundamental unit of a political community—appears to have developed in recent decades in opposition to Islamic fundamentalism and its coercive regulation of conduct. Restrictions on women’s mobility in Saudi Arabia and dress regulations imposed on Iranian women, for example, made the exercise of religious control over individual conduct especially visible. Resistance to specific limits such as these can help foster a wider conviction that political power should not arbitrarily determine matters properly belonging to individual judgment.

This process is evident in Egypt and Turkey, where declining mosque attendance has accompanied the growing appeal of liberal values, particularly secularism. In Egypt, average mosque attendance (scored on a range of 1 to 4) fell from 2.65 in 2011 to 2.30 in 2020, while in Turkey, it declined from 1.96 in 2013 to 1.82 in 2020. It is striking that Iran and Saudi Arabia, both governed by Islamic regimes, recorded the lowest levels of mosque attendance among the countries we surveyed in the early 2000s, with an average of 1.86 in each country. In Iran, this figure declined over time, reaching 1.79 in 2020.

Religious coercion has contributed to the rise of militant liberalism among Iranians. The Islamic Republic has faced sustained opposition for more than three decades: first, the reformist movement of the 1990s; then, the Green Movement of 2009; and, more recently, the nationwide protests after the 2022 death of Mahsa Amini, a young woman who died in police custody after being arrested for allegedly violating compulsory veiling rules. Over time, the objectives of this opposition have shifted from reforming the regime to challenging its authority. Although the regime has retreated from enforcing compulsory veiling, the opposition movement has continued to radicalize. The revolutionary movement of early 2026 expressed a growing determination to liberate the country from the authoritarian rule of the Islamic Republic itself.

Authoritarian conditions may lead people to conceal their true preferences in surveys. Nonetheless, the data reveal a marked decline in Iranians’ religious attachment over the past two decades. The proportion of survey respondents who considered religious faith an important quality for children to possess fell from 71 percent in 2000 to 55 percent in 2020. Similarly, the share of Iranians who expressed “a great deal of” confidence in religious institutions dropped from 59 percent to 36 percent. Religious intolerance also declined sharply: the proportion of respondents who said they would not want members of other faiths as neighbors fell from 80 percent in 2000 to 34 percent in 2020.

A 2022 survey of approximately 20,000 Iranians conducted by GAMAAN, a research foundation based in the Netherlands that focuses on Iran, further illustrates this shift. Fully 88 percent of respondents expressed favorable attitudes toward democracy, while 67 percent held negative views of a government based on sharia. A secular republic or constitutional monarchy was preferred by 53 percent, compared with just 22 percent favoring an Islamic government.

In Iraq, the 2003 U.S. invasion appears to have contributed to a rise in Iraqi nationalism and a stronger sense of national identity. Respondents were asked, “Which of the following best describes you above all: (1) I am an Iraqi; (2) I am a Muslim; (3) I am an Arab; (4) I am a Kurd; or (5) other?” The share identifying primarily as Iraqi rose from 23 percent in 2004 to 68 percent in 2020. Attachment to a shared national community, rather than to religious or ethnic identity alone, is an important component of liberal citizenship. The sectarian violence that followed the invasion appears to have convinced Iraqis of the importance of both national solidarity and separating religion from politics. The share of Iraqi respondents who agreed that religion and politics should be separated rose from 54 percent in 2004 to 76 percent in 2008, before falling to 69 percent in 2013–14 and rising again to 80 percent in 2018.

The parallel increases in support for secular politics and a sense of national identity are unlikely to be accidental. Sectarian violence made religious mobilization increasingly unattractive, while foreign occupation provoked an awareness of Iraqi nationhood. After 2014, the expansion of Iran’s meddling in Iraq may have additionally driven national solidarity.

Even if governance in these Middle Eastern regimes does not yet clearly reflect a shift toward liberal values, it may in the near future. After all, ideas drive history—and they have clearly done so in the Middle East. The formation of modern secular states in Egypt, Iran, and Turkey in the early twentieth century was preceded, in the late nineteenth century, by a rise in the popularity of liberalism, constitutionalism, nationalism, and secularism. The subsequent emergence of Arab nationalist governments in Egypt, Iraq, Libya, and Syria from 1952 to 1969 was likewise heavily influenced by the pan-Arab nationalist ideology formulated several decades earlier by Sati al-Husri, a Syrian thinker, and Darwish al-Miqdadi, a Palestinian educator. The late-twentieth-century rise of Islamic states and movements followed an earlier ideological transformation in the opposite direction, as secular projects lost legitimacy and political Islam gained influence.

Egypt and Iraq illustrate how changing social values can reshape politics in different ways. In Egypt, disillusionment with the Muslim Brotherhood appears to have weakened the appeal of political Islam as an alternative to authoritarian rule, while growing support for secular politics and Western-style government points toward a broader rejection of the country’s established authoritarian ideologies. Such attitudes do not by themselves produce democracy, but they may create the foundations of a future democratic movement. And because liberal values have spread more extensively among women than men, they may encourage greater female participation in political life. In Iraq, by contrast, growing support for secular politics could strengthen movements that appeal to territorial nationalism as an alternative to sectarian mobilization.

The spread of liberal values among ordinary people does not guarantee political liberalization. Yet the political alternatives that once dominated the region have lost their capacity to command popular allegiance. This has important implications for how outsiders view the Middle East. For decades, Western democracies have often treated Middle Eastern politics as a choice between a stable authoritarian rule and political Islam. This binary no longer represents the preferences of the region’s citizens. And the rise of liberal values in the Middle East should not be understood simply as a result of Western cultural influence. The liberal awareness documented here has emerged from distinctly regional experiences: religious authoritarianism has exposed the dangers of mixing sacred and political authority in the same institutions, sectarian violence has made national citizenship more attractive, patriarchal regulation has accelerated demands for gender equality, and restrictions on speech, belief, and the body have boosted the appeal of individual autonomy. Liberalism has gained ground in response to the failure of the political projects that preceded it, most significantly the rise of religious regimes, the persistence of religious monopolies, and the maltreatment of the human body, particularly of women. Even where efforts to build democratic institutions have faltered, as after the Arab Spring, liberal ideas have nonetheless persisted and expanded.

Although support for liberal democracy appears to be in retreat in parts of the West, the history of democracy is far from over. The Middle East has already experimented with Arab nationalism, state socialism, secular authoritarianism, monarchy, military rule, and political Islam. None of these experiments have offered political accountability, legitimate governance, or economic prosperity. Liberalism’s next chapter may arise in societies that have experienced the failure of alternatives firsthand. The important question now is not whether the Middle East is ready for democracy but whether its political institutions can keep pace with growing public support for individual rights and freedoms—and whether democracies outside the region are willing to acknowledge and back its people’s democratic aspirations.

This is the crux of what we in Europe call a social market economy. And this is what people expect when they do their part, which is to study, work, and pay taxes.

This social contract has been eroding in the developed West. The last several decades of growth in industrial productivity are reversing and the influence of trade unions declining. To understand this, we need to look back to the 1960s and ’70s, as post-World War II societies saw the proportion of total economic output paid to workers in the form of salaries and benefits grow. That share of labor to GDP, as it is known, peaked in countries that are part of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) in the 1970s at around 70 percent. For most of these countries, the share of labor to GDP dropped in the 2000s to between 50 and 60 percent. Put simply, ordinary citizens in rich societies are getting a smaller portion of the total economic pie. And young people who grew up watching their parents flourish under the old social contract are now developing a sense of intergenerational hopelessness.

A key feature of this problem is the fact that rising wealth in society tends to accumulate mostly in residential real estate. Housing remains the only asset class that provides daily value while accumulating wealth as prices rise. But as younger generations find the housing ladder increasingly out of reach, their salaries minus rent provide them with what can only be described as mediocre prospects. They become more dependent on social security systems as asset prices continue to gobble up most of the rise in real wealth.

This challenge has found two widespread solutions in the rich world. Middle-class members of society tend to gravitate toward arbitrage opportunities in countries where average incomes are lower than their own, boosting their spending power. Common examples include how British publics buy real estate on the Iberian Peninsula or take cheap flights to vacation in Asia. The other solution has been for governments to provide widespread social support to prop up living standards for people who have started to fall out of the middle class.

Initially, this state support was necessary only for the underskilled. This allowed minimum wages to remain below the actual cost of labor. Businesses started to rely on this competitive advantage. The more they convinced governments to keep minimum salaries low—or not fixed at all—the more they could push the cost of labor onto social budgets. Gradually, state support would become necessary for more skilled laborers as well.

This system was never sustainable. Since tax bases are relatively wide in rich Western democracies, the revenues of governments depend on the middle class—salaried professionals. But more than that, gathering taxes and spreading them through burgeoning welfare states is costly, unfair, and erodes the self-esteem of people who work full time and still cannot make ends meet. They cannot buy homes and are forced to live in government-subsidized housing. They cannot put their children through the schools and universities they want. All of this makes social support schemes more necessary, and they in turn allow enterprises to pay workers less. People living on social support become the cattle of a workforce, with fewer and fewer choices available to them. In addition, they realize that not being educated and not being able to demand the full cost of their labor means their children go to subpar schools and don’t get the necessary education levels to create their own destinies.

All of this results in a downward spiral. Wage growth is suppressed, and as tax revenues ultimately depend on the wages of the middle class, lower government revenues lead to lower salaries for many educated professionals on the public payroll. Teachers, nurses, and police officers are increasingly finding themselves qualifying for social support, particularly in wealthier areas where they can no longer afford to live and work without government assistance.

The results are plain to see. In the 1970s, a single-salary household in Western societies could afford a decent life. Today, two average salaries rarely mean the same quality of life enjoyed by the previous generation. In macroeconomic terms, if labor compensation accounted for about 70 percent of GDP in OECD countries in the 1980s, with social spending at around 16 percent and a deficit of around 2 percent, today the pie has been rearranged to accommodate less money for workers, much higher social spending, and almost double the deficit. Governments feel powerless to break the cycle. This results in a form of intergenerational hopelessness for more and more people in developed societies. People work full time yet cannot hope to make ends meet. They feel the system doesn’t work. And they don’t see a way out. This breeds the resentment and anger that we now see bursting out in various protests.

Is there a solution? I think there is. To start with, governments and businesses need to recognize that the status quo is unsustainable. Radical changes are necessary. Reforms need to ensure that people earn salaries that cover the full cost of labor in their particular region. Salary rises mean businesses must be allowed to pay less taxes. Governments can then gradually reduce the amount of social support they dole out. Such a system would at least mean ordinary citizens feel more in control of their finances and fates. It would allow for an honest conversation about costs and trade-offs rather than the convenient fiction that the old ways can continue without a course correction.

Is any of this easy to achieve? No, especially considering the permanent competition among countries for investment and business development based on low wages. But this traditional hold is weakening. More and more people work independently and not necessarily in the country they live, raising their bargaining power and lowering that of businesses. Smarter policies around setting localized values to labor are necessary, although countries will need bodies such as the European Union and the OECD to take the lead in publishing methodology and science to help determine adjustments. A big question here is the role of artificial intelligence, which could hurt job growth but also boost productivity and raise salaries if societies pay attention to how it is rolled out.

Part of the puzzle will be figuring out a wholesale rethinking of taxation. Independent workers enjoy corporate tax models that salaried individuals do not. Governments must recognize these inequities and address them. All income—salaries, dividends, and rents—should be taxed similarly to create a fairer system for the modern economy.

The risk of inaction is clear from the headlines in the news. Intergenerational hopelessness brings revolutions. Even if people see that the proposals of populists or extremists are not going to work, they want to break the old system because it has failed them and their children. They have little to lose. But destroying the system will make it impossible to create widespread progress or face communal challenges such as climate change. That is the real downward spiral we must all avoid.

#5304. Defense Production Act funds steered to GOP-backed energy projects

A Korean War-era law enacted to ensure domestic firms could adequately supply the U.S. military has become a piggy bank of sorts for a range of industrial needs, including energy projects favored by the Trump administration and GOP lawmakers.

The money is coming from a $1 billion pot Republicans set aside for purchases using authorities under the Defense Production Act of 1950 as part of their massive budget reconciliation package enacted in July 2025.

The latest $42 million cash infusion, announced Monday, is intended to help complete work on the long-stalled Constitution Pipeline to run natural gas from Pennsylvania to New York, where state officials have opposed it.

But building the 125-mile pipeline is a key aspect of the Trump administration’s energy plans and is backed by local Rep. Mike Lawler, R-N.Y., a Trump ally in a swing district facing a tough midterm race. Lawler joined Energy Secretary Chris Wright and Interior Secretary Doug Burgum at a natural gas power plant in Dover, N.Y., for the announcement.

“Utility rates are up 58 percent under New York’s disastrous energy policies,” Lawler said. “We need to approve the Constitution Pipeline to get gas here to this critical power plant and across New England and the region.”

Cait Conley, the Democrat running to replace Lawler, has advocated for expansion of renewable energy projects to bring costs down, as well as “greater accountability” from utilities. Inside Elections with Nathan L. Gonzales rates the Lawler-Conley race a Toss-up.

A few days prior, the Energy Department said it would use the 1950 law’s authorities to provide up to $50 million toward a two-year project expected to add some 290 megawatts of transmission capacity for Virginia and West Virginia customers of Appalachian Power, a subsidiary of Ohio-based American Electric Power.

Rep. John McGuire, R-Va., was on hand along with Wright for the Lynchburg, Va., announcement, along with local officials and power company executives. Appalachian Power said it expects to receive $40 million to upgrade 14 miles of transmission lines in the Lynchburg area.

Democratic former Rep. Tom Perriello is challenging McGuire for the 5th District seat; Inside Elections pegs McGuire as potentially vulnerable, though still rates the seat as likely to stay Republican next month.

Also last week, Vice President JD Vance announced the use of Defense Production Act authorities to expand the capacity of a Delek US Holdings oil refinery in Tyler, Texas. The administration hasn’t yet detailed a specific funding amount or detailed plan, but Vance said delivering more fuel would help lower consumer transportation costs and help supply the military as well.

Vance was campaigning on behalf of GOP Senate candidate Ken Paxton, who’s trying to fend off a tough challenge from Democrat James Talarico to keep the open seat in the red column. Inside Elections calls the race to succeed outgoing Sen. John Cornyn, R-Texas — whom Paxton defeated in the primary — a Toss-up.

The recent announcements follow earlier DOE moves to set aside up to $425 million to upgrade 13 coal-fired power plants in various states, as well as up to $75 million for a coal export terminal project in Oakland, Calif.

“By investing in both coal generation and critical export infrastructure … the Energy Department is strengthening U.S. energy security, reinforcing strategic supply chains, and advancing American energy dominance,” Wright said in June.

Republicans on the Senate Banking and House Financial Services panels, which have jurisdiction over Defense Production Act funds, wrote the $1 billion appropriation into the 2025 budget package, to remain available until Sept. 30, 2027.

No further direction was written into the statute to avoid running afoul of budget rules, but the White House and GOP lawmakers backed the funding with fossil fuel-related energy projects in mind. DOE announcements about tapping the fund have said the money was appropriated in last year’s law “for energy-related projects deemed critical to national security.”

An executive order signed by President Donald Trump in March extended authority to Wright to provide financial incentives to industry to boost energy production on a fast-tracked basis. In September, the president signed an executive order extending similar authorities to Burgum.

Following the DOE executive order, Trump issued a series of “presidential determinations” outlining specific needs related to the Defense Production Act. One such finding in April justified tapping the funds because “ensuring sufficient natural gas and liquefied natural gas … capacity is critical to sustaining United States defense operations and ensuring allied energy security.”

That determination, which DOE cited in the Constitution Pipeline announcement, marked natural gas and LNG capacity, as well as transmission, processing, storage and other related elements as “critical infrastructure” needed for national defense, making them eligible for DPA funds.

A separate presidential determination cited for the Appalachian Power project names “grid infrastructure” and components including “transformers, transmission lines and conductors” and more as “essential to the national defense.”

The project selections have frequently included references to both “energy security” and a desire to reduce energy costs — both issues that have plagued the White House amid rapidly expanding energy demands and rising prices. Inflation and utility bills, in particular, have posed a major challenge to Republicans on the campaign trail ahead of the midterms.

Wright said the latest funding announcement for the Constitution Pipeline would help expand the reach of natural gas — an abundant fuel in the U.S. that he said was hampered by a “lack of pipeline infrastructure.”

The project is expected to fuel about 3 million homes in the region, according to its developer, Tulsa-Okla.-based The Williams Cos. The natural gas giant says in a fact sheet that delivering Marcellus Shale supplies from Pennsylvania to the Northeast will lower electric bills that can run as much as 55 percent higher than those of Keystone State consumers.

New York state regulators and environmental activists oppose the Constitution Pipeline, arguing it would result in destructive forest clearing and damage to sensitive ecosystems, health risks from increased fracking, and displacement of renewable power sources that could lower electric bills.

Funding the pipeline and other fossil fuel projects isn’t the only novel use of a law originally intended for wartime purposes.

During the COVID-19 pandemic under the first Trump as well as Biden administrations, Congress provided $11 billion in Defense Production Act funding for purchases of protective equipment, vaccine materials, testing kits, ventilators and more.

In 2023, the Biden administration Defense Department tapped DPA funds from a variety of sources, including Democrats’ 2022 budget reconciliation law known as the Inflation Reduction Act, to expand mining and production of materials necessary for national security needs, like lithium, nickel, graphite and aluminum.
