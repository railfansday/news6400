#4998. Another megawatt-level charger enters China: 300 miles in 5 minutes

Chery has announced a 1.44 megawatt charging system, able to deliver 500km (311 miles) in 5 minutes. And it’s not even the fastest system available in China.

It’s become common knowledge at this point that Chinese EVs are the world’s state of the art, but the country isn’t sitting on its laurels, it’s still racing to compete with newer and better technology.

A case in point has been the country’s DC fast chargers, where we’ve seen a slew of announcements of new systems in just the last year.

BYD has a 1.5MW charger which it has already installed 10,000 of, Geely just announced a 2.2MW charger, and CATL has a hyper-fast charging battery.

The competition is so fierce that firms are starting to compete over seconds, not minutes of charging time.

And now, a new challenger appears: Chery.

Chery announced a 1.44MW charger that can add 500km (311miles) of range to a battery in 5 minutes, and can charge from 10-97% in 6 minutes and 50 seconds, as reported by CarNewsChina.

The numbers put it in league with the best of the best in the country. Per CarNewsChina’s accounting, the new Chery “Volt-Dragon” battery is the second-fastest at reaching 97% state of charge, 23 seconds behind CATL’s 6 minute, 27 second record time. FAW, Geely and BYD are all between 8 and 9 minutes for the same full charge metric.

Some other firms released times for charging in very cold or very warm weather, which Chery declined to release during its announcement. Though it did say that it could still deliver 500km of range in 8 minutes in “conservative scenarios.”

In Chery’s announcement, it dropped the same buzzword as Geely did: “AI-Powered.” Chery calls its system CheryMind, and supposedly it adjusts charging power based on temperature changes within the battery, to help maintain optimal charging speeds and battery health.

Meanwhile in the US, the fastest public chargers are around 400kW, with 750kW potentially coming next year. That’s all plenty quick enough for refueling, especially on a roadtrip, but for high-throughput DC charging locations, especially those in urban areas where some people might not have a consistent parking spot to charge in (and yes, we need to fix that), higher speed chargers can relieve a lot of pressure.

Chery has only just started installing chargers, and says it will have 100 chargers open in 10 Chinese cities soon. It plans to install 20,000 stations worldwide by 2029, which is quite a bit slower than BYD’s target to have 20,000 chargers installed by the end of this year.

#4997. Toledo Evacuations: How To Find Out If High-Risk Chemical Tanks Are Threatening Your Neighborhood

A chemical leak in Toledo, OH, is shedding light on industrial hazards that could affect nearby homes.

Following the leak on Oct. 21, Toledo Fire & Rescue ordered residents within a 1-mile radius of MC Tank Terminal on Progress Avenue to evacuate.

By early Wednesday morning, those within a 3-mile radius were directed to shelter in place with windows closed and ventilation systems off.  A total of 983 residents live within the 1-mile evacuation zone, according to Toledo Mayor Wade Kapszukiewicz.

“Given how industrial the area is and how many different businesses are located in that zone, there could be ... as many as 5,000 people working, during the day, in that area,” the mayor added in an official statement.

The chemical release was due to an “error that occurred when the material was being transferred,” a spokesperson for the Ohio Environmental Protection Agency said on CNN.

Glycidyl methacrylate, the chemical involved, is used to manufacture dental resins. It can irritate the eyes, nose, mouth, and skin. Exposure may also lead to headaches, dizziness, fatigue, nausea, and vomiting.

Unfortunately, this situation could happen anywhere, making it important to understand the hazards by your home before a hazard occurs.

There are a number of resources to help you find potential hazards in your local area.

Start with state resources. Many states have a database that lets you search for potential problems in your proximity.

“Pennsylvania, for example, has the DEP's Environmental Site Assessment Search Tool. States like California, New Hampshire, and New Mexico have similar interactive tools. Other states have spreadsheets you can download,” explains Jason Iuculano, founder at ZeroToxins in San Diego.

You can also call your local fire department.

“Facilities that store hazardous chemicals file inventory reports with fire departments, so they know which sites store what, what incidents have occurred, and what emergency plans are in place,” says Jason C. Pancoast, CEO of Envicon Group, an environmental consulting and engineering firm in Jersey City, NJ.

Lo Choe, licensed life safety professional and owner of Aura Fire Safety in San Francisco, says it is the same on the West Coast.

“All California facilities that store more than 55 gallons of liquid, 500 pounds of solid or 200 cubic feet of gas will file a Hazardous Materials Business Plan. This plan is with the fire department or county hazmat office (CUPA). It’s public record and will list chemicals, amounts, and a map of the facility,” Choe says.

Depending on where you live, you may also access a Tier II report. To view it, contact your State Emergency Response Commission or your county’s Local Emergency Planning Committee.

If you don’t find the information you need through local sources, you could use federal tools.

EPA ECHO, for example, lets you plug in your city or ZIP code and search nearby industrial facilities and review their compliance history, violations, and enforcement records.

You could also go to the EPA Toxics Release Inventory, which will show you facilities that report releases of certain toxic chemicals.

OSHA is an option as well. Use it to look up industrial operators and any workplace safety violations involving hazardous materials.

If you’re interested in a property and the listing says no reported issues but you see large storage tanks and chemical tanker trucks nearby, you should be concerned.

Railroad loading areas, industrial zoning, and persistent unusual odors can be issues as well.

A history of chemical releases or repeated regulatory violations, however, is a bigger concern.

Keep in mind that living near an industrial facility doesn’t by itself mean a significant health risk.

“The risk depends on what the facility handles, how it manages those materials, and what an accidental release could do. And you can’t count on your sense of smell to tell you whether you’re breathing something hazardous,” says Pancoast.

By being proactive and preparing for a hazard-related emergency, you can protect your household and navigate the situation with less stress and more confidence.

First, Iuculano suggests you invest in a HEPA filter that has a pre-filter, a substantial (high-quality) activated carbon filter, and HEPA.

“Carbon is what removes volatile organic compounds, not HEPA. That’s why it’s critical to have all three stages. It's also important to match the size of the air purifier with the size of the room you want to filter,” explains Iuculano.

Central air filtration with a MERV 7 or 8 can also be worthwhile to remove the larger particulates such as dust, pollen, and pet dander.

Also, remove your shoes and encourage family and guests to do the same.

“A substantial amount of toxins, such as pesticides, fecal matter, lead, and bacteria, can be tracked into the house this way and can cause all sorts of skin irritations and illnesses, especially for kids playing on the floor,” Iuculano explains.

Once you’ve taken these measures, sign up for your county’s emergency alerts and learn your local evacuation routes.

If officials tell you to shelter in place during a release, get inside right away.

“Then, close your windows and doors, shut down any HVAC that pulls in outside air, and seal vents and openings with plastic and tape if directed,” says Pancoast.

Make sure you have access to your phone or radio so you can monitor it for updates. And keep a basic emergency kit on hand with water, medications, phone chargers, and important documents.

The kit, which should be refreshed and restocked every three years, can allow you to leave quickly if you’re told to evacuate.

If you do need to evacuate, follow the designated routes and don’t come back until you’re told it’s safe to do so. Otherwise, you may be walking toward the release, instead of away from it.

#4994. Eating late at night linked to up to 30% higher risk of liver disease

That late-night snack might have consequences beyond extra calories. Researchers at Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU) have found that eating late in the evening is associated with a significantly higher risk of liver disease, suggesting that when you eat could be an important factor in protecting your liver.

The study, published in Clinical Gastroenterology and Hepatology, found that people who regularly ate after dinner or after bedtime had a 20% to 30% higher risk of liver disease compared with those who avoided eating late. The conditions included metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, commonly known as fatty liver disease, and cirrhosis, a serious condition involving extensive scarring of the liver.

Previous studies have connected late-night eating with obesity and cardiovascular problems. However, researchers wanted to determine whether the habit might also be associated with liver disease.

"A few studies have hinted at this idea, but this is among the first long-term analyses to explicitly draw the connection to fatty liver disease," said lead author Robert Wilechansky, M.D., a hepatologist at OHSU who specializes in liver transplantation.

Nearly 200,000 People Reveal a Troubling Pattern

To investigate the connection, researchers examined information from three major nationwide studies: the Nurses' Health Study, the Nurses' Health Study II, and the Health Professionals Follow-up Study.

Together, these datasets included nearly 200,000 participants who had provided information about their eating habits, daily routines, and other lifestyle factors. The researchers divided participants into groups according to when they typically ate, then compared those patterns with reports of fatty liver disease and cirrhosis.

The results revealed a consistent association. Participants who reported eating late at night had a 20% to 30% higher risk of liver disease than those who did not, raising questions about how the timing of meals might influence long-term liver health.

Fatty liver disease affects as much as 30% of the U.S. population. It develops when excess fat accumulates in the liver and can sometimes progress to inflammation, liver damage, and eventually cirrhosis. However, most people with fatty liver disease do not develop advanced liver scarring.

"It's important to remember, of the people who developed fatty liver disease, only a small fraction develop serious forms of the condition like liver cirrhosis," Wilechansky said. "Even so, the big take-away here is that confining your calorie intake to several hours before bedtime might be beneficial."

One possible explanation involves the body's circadian rhythms, the internal biological processes that help regulate sleep, metabolism, and other functions throughout the approximately 24-hour daily cycle.

The liver is particularly sensitive to these rhythms. It helps process nutrients, regulate blood sugar, and manage how the body stores and uses energy. Eating at times when the body is biologically preparing for sleep may interfere with some of these processes.

Wilechansky became interested in the connection partly because of evidence involving night shift workers. People whose work schedules conflict with the body's natural sleep and wake cycle experience higher rates of obesity and heart disease. This raised the possibility that irregular eating times could contribute to metabolic problems, including liver disease.

"We know the liver is very much in tune with the body's circadian rhythms," he said. "So we're interested in changing the timing of eating in a way that could make a difference in health without changing the composition of what people eat."

The idea is appealing because changing when meals are consumed could be easier for some people than substantially changing their diets.

Andrew McHill, Ph.D., a study co-author and associate professor specializing in sleep and circadian physiology at OHSU, said the findings strengthen existing evidence that the timing of everyday activities can have important health consequences.

"We are seeing over and over again that the timing of behaviors, whether it's sleep, eating, or exercise, really matters," said McHill. "For example, we have shown that eating during the night, when the internal biological clock is promoting sleep, reduces the ability of your body to metabolize foods as efficiently and can eventually lead to obesity, diabetes, and now poor liver health."

Previous experiments involving animals have also identified the liver as one of the organs especially vulnerable to disruptions in the normal relationship between eating and sleeping.

Could Eating Earlier Help Protect Your Liver?

Although the findings raise concerns about late-night eating, the researchers emphasize that the study cannot establish cause and effect.

The analysis was observational, meaning it identified relationships between eating habits and health outcomes without directly testing whether changing meal times prevents liver disease. It also relied on participants' self-reported information, including relatively broad descriptions of when they ate.

These limitations mean that other factors could help explain the association. More carefully controlled research is needed to determine whether eating earlier actually improves liver health.

Wilechansky is now involved in an OHSU clinical study that is recruiting participants to investigate that question. Rather than relying solely on past reports of eating habits, the team plans to monitor meal timing directly and evaluate whether shifting food consumption earlier leads to measurable improvements in people with underlying fatty liver disease.

The results could help determine whether meal timing deserves a place alongside established approaches to managing metabolic and liver health.

"Our findings add to the growing body of literature linking late-evening eating patterns to adverse metabolic and liver outcomes," the authors write.

For now, the research offers an intriguing possibility: Improving liver health may not always require changing what is on your plate. Simply giving your body more time between its last meal and bedtime could potentially make a difference.

In addition to Wilechansky and McHill, the research team included Kevin Casey, M.P.H., Andrew Chan, M.D., Ph.D., and Tracey Simon, M.D., of Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston.

#4991. HHS national kidney care plan takes aim at prevention, dialysis and transplant access

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) announced Tuesday a coordinated plan to improve the nation’s kidney care.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects more than one in 10 adults—or 37 million people—in the U.S., data from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) show.

The Kidney Care National Plan has three priorities: preventing kidney failure, improving dialysis care while expanding patient choice and reforming the transplant system.

Department-wide agencies will use the plan to advance policy, payment, research, regulatory and oversight actions, according to the announcement.

Moreover, HHS will use the plan to advance a National Transplant Expansion Strategy, which it says expands on a 2019 executive order from President Donald Trump. The order—entitled “Advancing American Kidney Health”—established a “federal vision” for such care, officials say.

“President Trump changed the course of kidney care in 2019 by challenging a system that waited for patients to reach kidney failure,” said Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., HHS Secretary, in a statement. “Now HHS is building on that work by detecting kidney disease earlier, expanding treatment and home-care options, removing barriers to innovation and transplantation, and strengthening safety across the transplant system. Our goal is simple: preserve kidney health, give patients more choices, and help more Americans avoid kidney failure and live longer, healthier lives.”

The National Kidney Foundation said in a statement it “welcomes” the department’s focus on improving such care.

“This plan provides an important roadmap,” the organization said. “As HHS moves from recommendations to policy, NKF looks forward to working with the Administration, Congress and the kidney community to ensure patients remain at the center—and to build a kidney care system focused not simply on managing kidney failure, but on preventing it whenever possible.”

Kidney dialysis services provider DaVita said in a statement it shares the Trump administration’s “urgency to accelerate progress for Americans living with kidney disease,” adding that the plan “reflects many of DaVita's priorities.”

“Where we differ is the suggestion that kidney care has lacked innovation or that scale stands in its way,” the company said. “Kidney care is increasingly a proving ground for technology, clinical science and new models of chronic care.”

In a statement, Fresenius Medical Care said it supported HHS’ kidney care national plan, particularly its focus on “incentivizing innovation, increasing access to home dialysis, simplifying the path to transplant, and advancing earlier intervention to slow the progression of kidney disease.”

“Many of the goals and priorities outlined in the plan reflect areas where we are already investing and driving progress, including introducing innovations such as hemodiafiltration (HDF) to the U.S., a Referral Ready program that helps streamline the transplant referral process, and continued efforts to expand access to home dialysis and other patient-centered care options. Kidney disease requires solutions across the full continuum of care, from prevention and education to advanced treatment options at home and in-center dialysis, and transplantation,” Fresenius Medical Care said.

The agency has taken steps to reform the organ transplant system in recent months.

In August, it announced it would decertify organ procurement organization (OPO) Network for Hope.

Kennedy said the decision was made after “extensive reviews” from the U.S. Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) and the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) “found persistent patient safety failures” after repeated warnings and corrective action plans.

The federally funded OPO serves Kentucky and parts of Indiana, Ohio and Virginia. Last September, HHS also announced it would decertify Life Alliance Organ Recovery Agency, a division of the University of Miami Health System.

The agency first announced a “major initiative” to overhaul the U.S. organ transplant system last July, building on Congressional and Biden administration-era work on reforming the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN).

#4986. Is the ‘Kidulting’ Boom Really Nostalgia for an Affordable Adulthood?

Given the choice between saving money and indulging in the pleasures of childhood, a majority of millennials say they would choose the latter.

Some 51% reported they would rather do “kidulting” activities than saving the money, according to the Harris Poll’s Rise of Kidulting Report.

The term describes adults buying products or participating in activities traditionally associated with childhood. Think building a model set or collecting toys—but this time, with your own hard-earned money.

That appetite is fueling a boom in childhood novelties. U.S. toy sales to adults grew 25% in the first half of 2026 compared with a year earlier, according to Circana—helping propel the industry even as consumers remained anxious about the economy.

Nowhere is that anxiety more obvious than in the housing market, with many young adults struggling to afford a place of their own. Nearly one-third of adults aged 18 to 34 lived with their parents in 2025, according to Realtor.com® research.

On the surface, it's easy to cast those trends as evidence of a generation reluctant to grow up, with kidulting starring as its latest shortsighted indulgence: the new avocado toast supposedly standing between young adults and a home of their own.

But economic research suggests that explanation may get the relationship backward.

Among Harris respondents who had participated in play during the previous year, 46% said a product or experience being affordable or easy to justify would make them more likely to buy it. Meanwhile, 41% selected a reminder of something they loved growing up.

Those two motivations—affordability and nostalgia—help explain why kidulting fits this particular economic moment. One offers a pleasure people can still afford. The other offers the promise of something familiar.

That stands in stark contrast to the uncertainty surrounding one of adulthood's most important milestones. Some 69% of millennials and 70% of Gen Z identify homeownership as a life goal—while only 57% and 54%, respectively, believe it is achievable for them, according to a Realtor.com survey from 2025.

Importantly, the gap between wanting a home and believing you can get one did not emerge from a collective failure of discipline—the economics changed.

In 1990, a typical home cost roughly three times a typical household’s annual income. By 2025, that ratio had climbed to roughly five. That means the price of admission to homeownership has risen sharply relative to what households earn—even before mortgage rates enter the equation.

And when the future becomes both more expensive and less certain, looking backward can become more appealing.

Across eight studies, Sheng Bi and colleagues found that consumers who felt powerless showed a stronger preference for nostalgic products than those who felt powerful. Their study, “When Feeling Powerless, We Crave Nostalgia,” identified uncertainty about the future as an underlying mechanism.

Their findings offer a way to understand why someone might look backward while struggling to move forward. A home can remain out of reach despite years of planning. A childhood pleasure offers something familiar to enjoy today.

That uncertainty can also change what feels worth saving for.

Economists Seung Hyeong Lee and Younggeun Yoo propose a model in which households respond differently depending on whether homeownership still seems attainable: Those who see a path toward buying may cut spending to improve their chances, while those who see the goal as beyond reach may spend more in the present.

The logic turns the avocado toast argument on its head. Giving up a $50 or $100 pleasure makes sense when each sacrifice appears to move someone measurably closer to a down payment. Once the gap becomes enormous, the same sacrifice buys far less progress.

Again, it's easy enough to measure how this timeline has broken down for young adults today. While it took just three years to save for a down payment in 1990, it now takes closer to a decade—nearly three times as long, according to research from Realtor.com.

And whether hobby spending substantially changes that timeline is a separate question. Harris measured a hypothetical preference, not how much respondents actually diverted from savings.

YipitData’s analysis found toy and game spending concentrated among middle- and higher-income households. Shoppers aged 35 to 44 with household incomes of at least $150,000 were disproportionately represented in building-and-construction toy spending.

It's further evidence that kidulting is not literally draining millennials' down-payment accounts en masse. Instead, it's flourishing in a culture where delayed milestones have weakened the old assumption that today's restraint will reliably purchase tomorrow's adulthood.

A September analysis from Boston College’s Center for Retirement Research estimates that economic conditions account for roughly half the increase in adults aged 25 to 34 living as guests in their parents’ homes since 2007.

Likewise, a 2024 study found that renters in less affordable housing markets began having children later than those in more affordable markets.

The findings help put kidulting in a different context. The small indulgence of an affordable collectible can't be blamed for delaying the launch of entire generations into adulthood—but the rising cost of reaching its traditional milestones can.

And when adulthood itself stretches out, consumer behavior is likely to stretch with it.

Someone can be 32, building a career, paying bills, and still living at home. They can want a home of their own while knowing it could take years longer to reach. And while those milestones move further out, the pleasures of childhood remain relatively easy to buy.

#4976. Homes Are ‘Seriously Unaffordable’ in Nearly a Third of U.S. Counties, New Report Finds

Housing affordability remains heavily strained across the United States, and housing is "seriously unaffordable" in about a third of all U.S. counties, according to a new report. Real estate analytics firm ATTOM's Q3 2026 U.S. Home Affordability Report found that major homeownership expenses exceeded historical affordability norms in 98% of the 583 U.S. counties analyzed in the third quarter, up 1% since the first quarter, when ATTOM last evaluated affordability at the beginning of this year.

“Affordability remained a challenge in the third quarter, even as some of the underlying factors moved in a favorable direction,” said Rob Barber, CEO of ATTOM. “Home prices declined nationally, and wage growth outpaced price growth in many counties, but those gains were not enough to meaningfully improve affordability across much of the country.”

ATTOM determined affordability for average wage earners by calculating the income needed to cover major monthly homeownership expenses—including mortgage payments, mortgage insurance, property taxes, and homeowners insurance—on a median-priced single-family home and condo.

ATTOM then used Bureau of Labor Statistics data to determine whether a homeowner could afford the property while keeping a front-end debt-to-income ratio of 28%, which is considered a healthy percentage of income to spend on housing expenses.

According to ATTOM's calculations, 1 in 3 counties is now considered “seriously unaffordable,” meaning typical ownership costs consume more than 43% of an average local worker’s wages.

That's despite the median sales price for single-family homes and condos falling to $368,000 in the third quarter of 2026, down 1% from the second quarter ($370,000) and 2% from the third quarter of 2025 ($375,000).

Nationally, the average annual wage is $79,534. But to afford a home at the median price, you'd need to make $90,191, based on a 20% down payment and 28% front-end debt-to-income ratio.

#4975. Yale scientists discover how stress changes the brain to make memories stronger

Stress does more than influence how we feel. It can also change what we remember, particularly when an experience is emotionally intense or personally meaningful. Scientists have long known that cortisol, a hormone released during stressful situations, can strengthen emotional memories. What has been less clear is exactly how this happens inside the brain.

Researchers at Yale University have now uncovered important clues. Their study reveals that cortisol strengthens emotional memories by changing how brain networks involved in emotion and memory function and communicate with one another. The findings offer a deeper understanding of why certain experiences leave such powerful impressions while others quickly fade.

"We all experience stress, and my lab is interested in understanding how stress can be helpful," said corresponding author Elizabeth Goldfarb, an assistant professor of psychiatry at Yale School of Medicine and of psychology in the Faculty of Arts and Sciences.

The findings were published in the journal Science Advances.

Previous research involving both humans and rodents has shown that stress and cortisol can improve memory for emotional events. Scientists also know that certain brain regions, including the amygdala, play an essential role in this process. The amygdala is particularly important for processing emotions and identifying experiences that may be significant.

However, understanding precisely how cortisol strengthens memories has been challenging. Many brain regions participate in several mental functions, making it difficult to determine whether the hormone is influencing emotional responses, memory formation, or both.

"Forming memories for emotional experiences involves different processes in the brain: first, perceiving an experience as emotional or intense, and second, encoding that experience into long-term memory," said Goldfarb, who is also a member of Yale's Wu Tsai Institute, an interdisciplinary research initiative that explores cognition.

The researchers wanted to examine these processes separately to determine how cortisol affects the brain's ability to recognize emotional significance and preserve an experience as a lasting memory.

Watching the Brain Form Emotional Memories

To investigate, the Yale team conducted an experiment in which participants took a pill containing either hydrocortisone, a form of cortisol, or an inactive placebo. They then viewed a series of pictures while undergoing functional MRI (fMRI) scanning.

This imaging technique measures changes in blood flow and oxygen levels associated with brain activity, allowing researchers to identify which areas of the brain are more active during different tasks.

As participants examined the pictures, they reported their emotional reactions to each image. The following day, researchers tested how well they remembered what they had seen.

Each person completed the experiment twice, once after receiving hydrocortisone and once after receiving the placebo. The study used a double blind design, meaning neither the participants nor the experimenter knew which substance had been administered during a particular session.

This approach allowed the scientists to compare how the same person's brain responded with and without the additional cortisol.

A Five-Second Look at Brain Network Activity

One of the study's most important advances came from the way researchers analyzed the brain scans.

Scientists often study a measurement called functional connectivity, which describes how closely activity in different parts of the brain fluctuates together. When two regions show synchronized patterns of activity, it can indicate that they are participating in related processes.

Traditionally, examining these relationships requires several minutes of brain imaging data. The Yale team instead analyzed functional connectivity during individual trials lasting only around five seconds.

Using these brief snapshots, the researchers tested whether patterns of brain activity could predict two different outcomes: how emotionally intense participants found a picture and whether they would remember that picture the following day.

The approach worked for both. By identifying patterns associated with emotional intensity and memory formation separately, the scientists were able to examine how cortisol influenced each network and the interactions between them.

This gave the researchers a more detailed picture of the changing brain activity associated with emotional memory formation.

Cortisol Changes How Brain Networks Work Together

First, cortisol improved participants' ability to remember emotionally significant experiences, confirming what earlier research had suggested.

Second, and more importantly, the hormone strengthened emotional memory through distinct changes in the brain networks responsible for processing emotion and forming memories.

In networks associated with emotion, cortisol produced more consistent patterns of activity and increased their engagement. This suggests that the hormone helped the brain respond more strongly and reliably to the emotional significance of an experience.

In memory networks, cortisol had a different effect. It made their activity patterns more specialized for emotional information, suggesting that the brain was adjusting how it encoded emotionally meaningful experiences.

The hormone also increased coordination between the networks involved in emotion and memory. In other words, cortisol appeared to help these different brain systems work together more effectively when processing experiences that carried emotional weight.

Together, these findings suggest that stress strengthens emotional memories through several interacting brain mechanisms rather than through a single region or process.

Why Emotional Experiences Can Be So Memorable

The results help explain why stress does not necessarily improve memory for everything. Instead, its benefits appear to be selective, making experiences that feel particularly intense or meaningful more likely to be remembered.

The researchers believe this reflects the brain's ability to adapt to stressful situations by prioritizing information that may be important.

"Stress responses are fundamentally adaptive and can help you form strong memories -- but this is specific to experiences that you find to be emotionally intense or meaningful," Goldfarb said. "This is in part related to your brain increasing engagement of networks that track emotional intensity. So, if you are stressed out and trying to learn something new, it might help to think about exciting elements or strong feelings connected to what you are learning."

The findings also point to a potentially useful insight about learning. Connecting information to something exciting, personally important, or emotionally engaging could make it easier to remember, particularly during stressful situations.

Rather than simply disrupting memory, stress can sometimes help the brain identify which experiences deserve special attention and strengthen the networks involved in preserving them.

Other Yale researchers involved in the study include Rajita Sinha, the Foundations Fund Endowed Professor in Psychiatry and professor in the Child Study Center and of neuroscience at Yale School of Medicine (YSM), and Todd Constable, the Elizabeth Mears and House Jameson Professor of Radiology and Biomedical Imaging and professor of neurosurgery at YSM.

The research was supported by grants from the National Institutes of Health.

#4964. Critical PHP vulnerabilities send organizations scrambling to secure their systems

The maintainers of the PHP programming language today released patches for two critical vulnerabilities that affect up to 70% of the world’s websites.

Several cybersecurity agencies including the National Security Agency are urging organizations to shut down affected systems while they implement the updates. Many municipalities, schools and private companies have taken their advice. Content delivery network operators Cloudflare Inc. and Fastly Inc., meanwhile, reported that hackers are already trying to exploit the bugs.

“This is not a theoretical vulnerability,” said Fastly Chief Product Officer Kelly Shortridge. “We observe many billions of PHP requests a day across Fastly’s global network, and we are seeing evidence of active attacks.”

The two exploits are together known as PHP2Shell. They enable hackers to deploy malicious code on vulnerable servers. The installation process takes only a few seconds in some cases. Once the malware compromises a website, it can read its database and launch cyberattacks against visitors.

The first PHP2Shell exploit emerges when a piece of PHP code receives more data than it can process. PHP responds to such situations by deleting some of the ingested data. According to the language’s maintainers, the data left over after that operation can be used by hackers to launch cyberattacks.

The second PHP2Shell exploit relates to global state, information that is shared by multiple components of a program. Each piece of global state is associated with a specific request to a PHP application. When the application processes a request, it deletes the associated global state. PHP2Shell enables malicious code to access some of that data before it’s deleted, which opens the door to cyberattacks.

“This is about as broad as a critical vulnerability gets,” said Moshe Siman Tov Bustan, a cybersecurity research team lead at application security startup OX Security Inc. “If you have a publicly accessible PHP instance and haven’t patched it, start from the assumption that you’re exposed. If you find evidence of exploitation, assume compromise, not just exposure.”

The underlying technical issue behind PHP2Shell has been present in PHP for decades. However, researchers believed that it can’t be exploited in practice. That changed in August, when an employee of cybersecurity company Calif.io Inc. used GPT-5.6 Sol to scan PHP’s code base for bugs. The audit, which was financed by OpenAI Group PBC, revealed that PHP2Shell can be targeted after all.

Google LLC’s Wiz cybersecurity unit made a number of additional discoveries about the issue shortly thereafter. Over the following weeks, tech firms and cybersecurity researchers formed an industry group to tackle PHP2Shell. The effort reportedly produced more than 10,000 lines of code and fixes spanning over 20 PHP files.

The consortium originally planned to release the patch on Thursday, but the vulnerability was leaked yesterday on a popular developer forum. That led PHP’s maintainers to rush out the fixes today.

“A leaked embargo is a leadership test as much as a technical one, because the decisions that matter today get made before anyone has full details,” said Andrew Obadiaru, chief information security officer of cybersecurity company Cobalt Inc. “Security leaders need to be able to answer a few questions quickly: where PHP is running, who owns each of those systems, and who has the authority to take something offline if it can’t be patched.”

#4963. Ozempic and Wegovy linked to rare risk of sudden vision loss

A large study led by Rutgers researchers has identified a possible vision risk associated with widely used diabetes and weight loss medications, including Ozempic and Wegovy. The findings suggest that people taking these drugs may face a slightly higher risk of developing a rare eye condition that can cause sudden and potentially permanent vision loss.

The condition, known as ischemic optic neuropathy, occurs when blood flow to the optic nerve is reduced. Because the optic nerve carries visual information from the eye to the brain, damage to it can lead to significant vision problems.

Study Links GLP-1 Drugs to Rare Vision Loss

The study, published in Annals of Internal Medicine, analyzed information from a large database of US adults ages 18 to 65 with Type 2 diabetes. Researchers focused on patients who had recently started taking medications to manage their condition, including a class of drugs known as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, or GLP-1s.

These medications include semaglutide (sold as Ozempic and Wegovy) and tirzepatide (sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound). In addition to helping control blood sugar and manage weight, GLP-1 medications can reduce the risks of certain cardiovascular and kidney problems. However, scientists are still investigating their potential side effects as their use becomes increasingly widespread.

The researchers found that GLP-1 use was associated with a modest increase in the likelihood of ischemic optic neuropathy. Although the condition was uncommon, its potential consequences make the finding important.

"Although ischemic optic neuropathy was rare, it is clinically important because it can involve sudden vision loss," said Chintan Dave, a core faculty member of the Center for Pharmacoepidemiology and Treatment Science at Rutgers Institute for Health, Health Care Policy and Aging Research. "The increase in risk was small -- approximately three to four additional cases per 10,000 patients treated with GLP-1s over 18 months, but clinicians and patients should be aware of this potential association."

While the absolute risk remains low, researchers say the growing popularity of GLP-1 medications makes continued safety monitoring especially important. These drugs are now widely prescribed to people both with and without Type 2 diabetes.

The study also identified patterns among patients who developed the eye condition. Cases were more common among men and adults in the older portion of the study population.

"Most patients in our study who had ischemic optic neuropathy were either men or men and women aged 50 to 65," said Dave, an associate professor at the Ernest Mario School of Pharmacy and the lead author of the study. "Because ischemic optic neuropathy can present with sudden vision loss, which may be permanent, early recognition of symptoms and prompt ophthalmologic evaluation may be especially important for patients at higher baseline risk."

More than 80% of ischemic optic neuropathy cases are estimated to involve the non-arteritic anterior form of the condition. This occurs when blood supply to the front portion of the optic nerve becomes insufficient, potentially damaging the nerve and interfering with vision.

The resulting vision loss is often permanent. According to Dave, approximately one-third of affected patients experience some recovery, usually during the first six months. Even when improvement occurs, some degree of visual impairment typically remains.

Rather than causing complete blindness, the condition usually affects part of the vision in one eye. Patients may notice sudden blurriness, dimming, or missing areas in their field of view.

Do Weight Loss Drugs Actually Cause Vision Loss?

Despite the association, the researchers caution that their findings do not establish that GLP-1 medications directly cause optic nerve damage.

Other factors, including differences in patients' underlying health conditions, could help explain the increased risk observed in the study. Additional research will be necessary to determine whether the medications themselves contribute to the condition.

For now, the findings highlight the importance of recognizing sudden vision changes and seeking prompt medical evaluation, particularly among people who may already be at greater risk.

The research team also included Kamika Reynolds and Kimberly O'Malley of the Institute for Health, along with Jason Roy of the Rutgers School of Public Health.

#4953. Nvidia turns the AI factory into a product, and agents into the workload

ANALYSIS by Zeus Kerravala

For most of the past half-decade, the narrative around Nvidia Corp. has largely centered on the graphics processing unit. The questions have been who has allocation, how much faster the next generation is and whether anyone can close the gap.

At GTC Berlin early today, Chief Executive Jensen Huang spent surprisingly little of his keynote on silicon. Instead, he argued that artificial intelligence constraints have shifted to deployment speed, power, security, and the ability to put enterprise data to work, and that Nvidia intends to have an answer for each. “We were early to observe that, in fact, AI is a manufacturing industry,” Huang told the audience. “Rather than a tool that’s used by us, it is now part of machinery, an infrastructure, factories that are producing intelligence.”

Those comments from Huang tie together most of the massive news payload from Berlin. A summary of the news includes Nvidia introducing DSX POD, a reference design for complete Vera Rubin AI factories built by partners in containers; launching the OpenShell Reference Architecture to run agents safely; expanding its Agent Toolkit with an Agent Knowledge Foundation and a new family of structured-data models called Kumo; and deepening its push into telecom with Nokia and European operators.

Individually, these are product launches. Together, they show Nvidia moving from selling the most important component in AI to selling the operating model for AI.

One of the more interesting moments in the keynote came when Huang did back-of-the-envelope math on stage. A 1-gigawatt AI factory costs about $50 billion and should generate roughly $40 billion in annual revenue. Delay it by a quarter, and about $10 billion in revenue is lost. “$10 billion worth of revenue will make anybody anxious,” he said.

That’s an interesting and correct way to think about AI infrastructure economics. The cost that keeps AI builders up at night is no longer the price of hardware; it’s the cost of hardware sitting idle while crews pull cable. A modern AI build is a construction project, with kilometers of copper and fiber assembled on site by scarce, specialized labor, especially in Europe.

DSX POD embeds that work into the factory and hides the complexity from the customer. Each pod holds seven Vera Rubin NVL72 racks, with roughly 20 kilometers of NVLink copper and 17 kilometers of scale-out networking, all cabled, plumbed, and tested before shipment. Nvidia says that deployment at a prepared site drops from six months to as little as 14 days.

The more interesting question is where this fits. DSX POD won’t replace gigawatt campuses, which still benefit from economies of scale. The sweet spot is the hundreds-of-megawatts tier, where an operator has a site and power but lacks the time or permitting runway for a full land, power, and shell build. That’s exactly where Europe’s neoclouds, sovereign AI projects and telcos operate. The 14-day figure assumes the site is ready, with delivery set for early 2027. Containers compress integration, but they don’t fix grid interconnect queues. DSX POD shifts the long pole in the tent; it doesn’t remove it.

The DSX platform beneath the pod is where Nvidia does some of its most important engineering work, which tends to fly under the radar. Warm-water, closed-loop cooling lets operators in suitable climates skip evaporative towers, which Nvidia estimates saves 54 million liters of water per day at 2 gigawatts. DSX MaxLPS directs power to where workloads need it, and Nvidia says this delivers the same output with up to 30% less power.

The piece I’d watch most closely is DSX Flex. During the Euro 2020 match between England and Germany, millions of Britons put the kettle on at halftime, and the grid saw roughly a one-gigawatt spike. Today, when a grid operator needs a data center to back off, the process is mostly manual, often a phone call. DSX Flex automates that interaction, allowing AI factories to defer or throttle work and return capacity.

That matters because data centers that behave like flexible loads are easier to connect and more readily accepted by communities. The industry must stop treating data centers purely as consumers on the grid and start treating them as partners to grid operators. Hyperscalers are already moving in this direction; the difference is that Nvidia is trying to make it a standard.

The second half of the keynote shifted from the factory to what runs inside it, increasingly agents rather than models. Huang described an agent as consisting of five parts: the model, the harness, the sandbox, knowledge and tools. He was clear about the priority. “The agent is an autonomous system that has access to files, resources and networks, as well as tools, and we need to make sure that the privileges we provide this autonomous system are minimal and that we constantly monitor it,” he said.

Much of the AI safety debate has centered on model alignment and guardrails, assuming a well-behaved model is enough. It isn’t. No model can be trained to understand the policy requirements of every business and regulated industry. Agents are probabilistic systems that will find creative ways to solve problems, and some of those approaches will touch data or take actions no one intended. They need deterministic controls enforced outside their reach.

That’s the thesis behind the OpenShell Reference Architecture. Harnesses run on Vera CPUs, models run on Vera Rubin, and BlueField-4 with Nvidia Sentry runs as an isolated monitor between them, capable of cutting off an agent in milliseconds. Network professionals will recognize the pattern. That’s why the industry moved away from host-based security alone and shifted enforcement into the network. It also lets security teams author policies for their own country and industry, which is especially important in Europe.

As expected, the ecosystem response to align with Nvidia has been strong, with CrowdStrike Holdings Inc., Palo Alto Networks Inc., Zscaler Inc., Cisco Systems Inc. and others adding support. The question is whether OpenShell, which is open source and runs on x86 or Arm, can provide hardware-isolated enforcement without BlueField. Enterprises should distinguish the protections available through OpenShell software from the additional hardware enforcement provided by BlueField-4 and Sentry.

“What turns these agents into super agents is giving them access to your company’s knowledge,” Huang said. Enterprises hold an estimated 70% of the world’s data, and most of it hasn’t been prepared for agents. The industry’s attention has focused mostly on documents and PDFs, yet much of the business value lies in the rows and columns of enterprise resource planning, supply chain and financial systems, and language models aren’t built to reason over that data.

Historically, getting a prediction from a table meant building a custom model for a single question. Kumo is a family of predictive models that can answer many “what-if” questions against the same data. That’s the kind of capability that turns agents from chat interfaces into operational tools.

It also debunks the ridiculous “AI will kill software” narrative. “Some people said that AI is going to be the end of software, the end of tools companies,” Huang said. “It’s exactly the opposite.” I agree. Agents need simulators, solvers and databases, and the companies that own those tools become more valuable when they package them for agents.

Huang also made a bold telecom prediction. “Every single telco in the future, with 100,000 sites representing some 100 gigawatts of distributed telco base stations and data centers, will evolve into a distributed AI network,” he said.

The European numbers support the thesis. About 20% of Europe’s data centers are owned by telcos, and there are more than 1,500 telco facilities that could host GPUs. I’ve watched operators chase edge compute before, and multi-access edge computing never delivered. The difference now is a real inference workload, plus the ability to run the radio network itself on the same platform, as T-Mobile and Nokia demonstrated with the first AI-RAN call on a commercial network.

Physical AI strengthens the case. Robots will handle perception on board but will sometimes need to “phone a friend” to an on-premises server, a telco edge site, or the cloud. That hybrid model fits operators well, provided they can build the go-to-market muscle to sell it.

Physical AI is no longer a science project for Nvidia, either. The company said physical AI revenue reached nearly $11 billion in the first half of 2026, about 30% higher than all of last year. Europe, with its precision manufacturing base and Mittelstand of midsize manufacturers, has the most to gain now that the technology is good enough for companies without in-house robotics teams.

As mentioned, telcos have had opportunities to increase their value to customers before. Cloud, mobile and the internet put telcos in a position to be something other than a commodity, but they failed. However, this time Nvidia is helping them make this shift, and the upside for Nvidia is high if the telcos succeed.

Nvidia describes its approach as vertically optimized but horizontally open, meaning customers can use as much or as little of the stack as they want. Berlin showed how far the vertical part now extends, from container logistics and grid integration to agent security and structured data models. The more of the stack Nvidia supplies, the more that openness promise will be tested, and buyers should hold the company to that promise.

But the strategic point has been made. Nvidia’s rivals are still largely competing on chips, while Nvidia is increasingly competing on time to revenue, backed by an installed base where Volta GPUs from 2019 are still serving customers. For chief information officers and operators weighing multibillion-dollar AI investments, that durability, combined with faster time to first token, is a harder argument to beat than any benchmark.

Honda’s very first four-wheeled vehicle was a pickup truck, the T360 kei truck built for its home market in 1963. Now, however, Honda is coming out with a proper body-on-frame pickup.

Just not in North America. A new memorandum of understanding has been signed between Mitsubishi and Honda, with the stated intent of developing a Honda-badged pickup by 2028. Said pickup will be based on the current Mitsubishi Triton and sold in the Southeast Asia market: Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, and so on.

For the proposed draft to work, the crypto industry demands five fundamental changes.

In August, the SEC first presented this compromise plan: startups will be allowed to raise up to $75 million a year under a simplified scheme and will also be granted “Safe Harbor” status, which protects matured tokens from prosecution by regulators.

Before that, the agency assessed the crypto market using the 1946 Howey test, created for trading shares in Florida citrus plantations. Because of the legal impasse, American Web3 startups have spent years moving offshore or blocking U.S. users.

Hyperliquid’s lawyers point out that the SEC’s new compromise is still detached from reality. The policy center’s main objections boil down to five key categories:

The regulation must clearly state that a free distribution of tokens for on-chain activity is not an investment contract, since users do not invest money and bear no financial risks. Moreover, completing verification (KYC) or connecting a wallet to receive an airdrop cannot be legally interpreted as “payment” for the asset.

The other three changes concern the long-term survival of projects. Currently, to enter the Safe Harbor, a project must acknowledge that, at the development stage, the token depended on management, which automatically exposes startups to lawsuits for past years.

HPC demands that companies be given the right to declare that their tokens have never been securities. In addition, the proposed rules do not describe the status of decentralized protocols (DEX) at all, although without the right to legally trade tokens on on-chain platforms from day one, the entire regulation loses its economic meaning.

Finally, the regulation currently exempts approved projects from oversight by the authorities of individual states, but this immunity expires upon exit from the Safe Harbor. The industry demands that this protection be made indefinite, to relieve businesses of oversight from more than 50 jurisdictions at once.

By defending the market, Hyperliquid is acting preemptively. The platform is the leader in the on-chain futures sector, but because of strict U.S. laws, it is forced to fully block American residents. At present, there are no official lawsuits or fines against the platform from the SEC or the CFTC.
