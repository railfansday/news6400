Tesla says it is preparing to launch its shiny gold Cybercab in new markets outside its home of Austin, Texas, in a bid to “literally take over every part of terrestrial transportation.”

“We’re starting with getting people from one place to the other, but we want to basically own transportation of any kind on Earth,” Chief Financial Officer Vaibhav Taneja said on the company’s third-quarter earnings call Wednesday.

Tesla has previously shown a concept version of a multi-passenger vehicle it calls the “Robovan,” but it’s unclear if Taneja’s bombastic comments mean the company is working on other types of transportation.

What was clearer on the call is that Tesla’s Cybercab expansion will begin in Dallas next month and spread to other cities by the end of this year. Tesla already operates robotaxi versions of its Model Y SUV in small fleets in Dallas, Houston, and the Florida cities of Miami, Orlando, and Tampa. The company also said on its earnings call that it will start selling Cybercabs to fleet operators by the end of this year.

Broader expansion of the Cybercab could set up a real fight with Waymo, which is currently the market leader for robotaxis in the United States, with more than 4,000 of them deployed across more than a dozen cities. Tesla has argued that its Cybercab is cheaper and easier to build, which should allow it to undercut Waymo and other robotaxi operators on price. But the company has to reach real scale outside Austin in order to prove that point.

Tesla said it’s ready thanks to a dramatic increase in the number of miles its self-driving cars have traveled in recent weeks. While that may be true, Tesla was mum on Wednesday about how much money the overall robotaxi effort is bringing in. Tesla CEO Elon Musk said the new ride-hailing business won’t be noticeable in the company’s overall financial figures until the second quarter of 2027, “and probably a big deal by Q3 or Q4 of next year.”

Tesla also made a change to its letter to shareholders that further obscures what little visibility there was into its robotaxi business.

In Tesla’s second-quarter letter, the company included a chart for “Cumulative Paid Robotaxi Miles.” A close read of that chart showed a 36% decline from the first quarter of the year. In the new letter released on Wednesday, Tesla changed that chart to show “Cumulative Unsupervised Robotaxi Miles” — an indicator of how far the fleet cars are driving, whether or not they have paying riders on board.

There wasn’t much more clarity on the call, either. When asked about paid robotaxi miles traveled, Taneja said the company is “seeing utilization improving,” but didn’t offer specifics.

Instead, Taneja kept most of his comments generally focused on Tesla’s plans to scale the Cybercab. That included praise for Tesla’s engineering team for creating a vehicle that, according to the manufacturing team, is very easy to build — and build quickly.

“They think this is one of the easiest products for them to scale in terms of manufacturing,” he said. “So scaling would not be that much of a challenge.”

What might be a challenge, Taneja said, is that Tesla needs to make sure it has “all the regulatory approvals” so it can “start deploying the Cybercabs as fast as we can produce them.” That echoes many previous comments from Musk, who has long claimed that “regulatory approval” was the biggest barrier to Tesla’s robotaxi plans, not technical progress.

Musk has always been a bit hazy about what approvals, specifically, he felt were needed. The company has run into a regulatory hurdle this year, though. In September, just hours after Tesla formally launched its Cybercab in Austin, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) opened an investigation into whether the vehicle complies with current federal motor vehicle safety standards.

All automakers self-certify that their vehicles conform to these standards, and Tesla has claimed that the Cybercab is compliant. But it is missing features that the current standards require, like a brake pedal and side mirrors. The NHTSA has proposed removing some of these requirements, but that rule change is likely still at least a few months away.

Not every Tesla executive agreed on this during Wednesday’s call.

Asked about the remaining bottlenecks for a wider robotaxi rollout, Ashok Elluswamy, Tesla’s vice president overseeing autonomy, said they are “not purely regulatory.”

“It’s technology-driven,” he said. “We had initially had the robotaxi service from 6 a.m. to 9 p.m., and then expanded to 10 p.m. to 11 p.m, now it’s going to be pretty soon, [to] 2 a.m. So we’re slowly expanding the availability of robotaxi, and that is driven by the capability of the AI software.”

That could be why Tesla’s service still doesn’t operate on highways, and in certain conditions, although it has been spotted operating in heavy rain many times in Austin — something Waymo’s robotaxis have largely avoided. Elluswamy said Wednesday that Tesla plans to tackle more of these challenging driving scenarios by the end of this year, before scaling further.

“We just want to make sure that there’s not a single safety incident. That is our operating principle. And even if there’s the slightest possible risk at all, we don’t like to take on that risk,” he said.

#148. Airbnb scraps guest checkout chores such as cleaning

Under new rules, guests will no longer have to do tasks that cleaners would normally handle. Hosts can now only make "reasonable" requests, such as leaving dirty dishes in the sink and sorting trash from recycling.

Chores such as washing dishes, stripping beds and taking out the trash at the end of your Airbnb stay will soon be a thing of the past.

Under new rules unveiled this week, the short-term rental platform says hosts should only make "reasonable" requests of guests.

Airbnb said hosts are generally not allowed to "ask guests to complete tasks that require significant effort or time, or would typically be performed by a cleaner, including stripping the bed, taking trash offsite, or washing and putting away dishes."

Checkout chores have been a contentious issue for guests, especially in cases where they are also charged cleaning fees.

Airbnb has previously admitted that such fees can create confusion between guests and hosts.

"Guests expect checkout to be relatively simple, and most want to avoid unexpected chores," Airbnb said in a message to hosts on the platform.

"Asking them to do things a cleaner should handle can negatively affect their stay and your reviews."

The new rules come amid growing protests against housing shortages in many European cities, with protesters arguing that short-term rentals such as Airbnb have pushed them out of their own cities.

In September, the European Commission proposed more restrictions on Airbnb and other short-term rental companies in tourist hubs.

Guests can still be asked to leave dirty dishes in the sink, to gather used towels and separate trash and recyclables, for example.

Hosts can also require guests to put trash in animal-proof containers areas where bears are active or ask them to "limit shower time due to local drought-related water restrictions."

But hosts will no longer be allowed to ban guests from using amenities listed in the property description, such as air conditioners, nor will they be allowed to fine guests.

Instead, hosts must seek compensation for any damage from insurance that the platform provides.

The new policy applies to bookings starting on November 20.

#144. Moderna shares plunge after cancer vaccine data leaks — despite positive results

Moderna shares dropped as much as 25% on Thursday following the leak of partial data on its closely watched cancer vaccine developed with Merck.

Shares of both drugmakers fell even though the data, released in full later Thursday following the leak, appeared meaningful for patients and positive relative to Wall Street expectations.

The personalized vaccine, known as intismeran autogene, paired with Merck's immunotherapy Keytruda reduced the risk of recurrence or death in certain melanoma patients by 42% compared with Keytruda alone.

The data also showed that the shot along with Keytruda reduced the risk of cancer spreading to distant parts of the body or death by 38%, compared with just the immunotherapy.

The companies said about 82% of patients who received the personalized cancer vaccine alongside Keytruda were alive without their melanoma returning at two years after receiving the shot, compared with 70% of those who received Keytruda alone.

"This is a huge development for our patients," said Dr. Hussein Tawbi, a melanoma expert and trial investigator at the University of Texas MD ​Anderson Cancer Center, about the results.

He said that the added benefit of the vaccine also comes with much milder toxicity, or side effects like injection-site pain, compared to other cancer treatments.

The detailed phase 3 trial results were set to be presented at the European Society for Medical Oncology conference this weekend.

"Whenever you put a therapy on top of something that works really well, like Keytruda, sometimes it's harder to show additional benefit. We have no problems of showing additional benefit here," Dr. Dean Li, Merck Research Laboratories president, said in an interview ahead of the results.

The benefit appeared even larger after the six-month vaccine regimen, with a separate analysis showing a 58% reduction in risk of recurrence or death. The companies say it is still too early to determine whether the treatment helps patients live longer overall.

Several analysts acknowledged investors' elevated expectations ahead of the release, but noted that the data is overall positive.

"We assert this relative reduction in recurrence/death is clinically meaningful," BMO Capital Markets analyst Evan Seigerman said in a research note on Thursday following the leak. "Such a [stock] reaction likely reflects inflated expectations into the full data release, rather than a true indictment on the efficacy of the combo therapy."

Oncologists and investors see the targeted vaccine as a possible breakthrough treatment, with the potential to be used to treat other forms of cancer in the future. Merck and Moderna are studying the vaccine in several trials across other tumors, such as non-small cell lung cancer, bladder cancer and renal cell carcinoma.

But Li acknowledged the companies have more work to show the product can be effective across tumor types.

"I can dream of creating a log cabin, but we got some wood chops, and we've just knocked down one tree," Li said. "Knocking one tree does not give you a cabin or a village."

The shot is designed to target the specific mutations in each patient's tumor rather than using a one-size-fits-all approach. The idea is to train the immune system to recognize and kill those unique cancer markers and then pair that response with Keytruda, which helps the body attack the cancer more effectively.

It could also offer Moderna a critical additional commercial product in its pipeline, following vaccines for Covid-19, the flu and respiratory syncytial virus.

In a research note Thursday, UBS analysts said "this is overall a strong data set" and gives more confidence in upcoming studies of the vaccine in kidney and bladder cancers. They added the stock reaction may be due more to massive expectations than disappointing data.

Against the backdrop of those high expectations, Moderna shares have spiked more than 530% year-to-date as of Thursday afternoon.

Shares of Merck, a much larger drugmaker, fell by about 1% on Thursday. The company's stock has climbed nearly 41% this year.

Since the vaccine is personalized, the complicated manufacturing process can take six weeks. But Merck CEO Robert Davis told CNBC last week that the company believes it can shorten that timeline in the future, potentially allowing the shot to reach more patients more quickly.

#142. Meta’s Ad Sales Are Suffering Now That Europeans Can Pick Less Personalization

Meta’s advertising growth in Europe is slowing now that residents have the option for less of their personal data to influence what advertisements they see.

Advertising revenue in Europe continued to decelerate in the three months through September, the company said on Wednesday, underperforming its global figures. In the region, advertising revenue grew 18 percent year-on-year, slowing from the previous two quarters and lagging the 33 percent growth seen in the US and Canada.

Though Meta did not explicitly blame the introduction of the Less-Personalized Ads model on Wednesday, chief financial officer Susan Li had previously warned “we’re seeing the impact of less personalized ads offering in Europe now that it’s fully rolled out, and that may be an additional headwind,” in July’s second quarter results follow-up call. The preceding two quarters had seen advertising growth fall from around 40 percent to 24 percent; in the US and Canada, it had accelerated from 30 percent to 31 percent.

“We believe the EU’s free, personalized advertising option is contributing to the European slowdown,” Dominic Ball, equity research director at market analysis firm Rothschild & Co Redburn, told WIRED.

Meta began to offer Instagram and Facebook users the LPA option at the beginning of this year, after EU regulators ruled that it was breaching its Digital Markets Act obligation to give consumers the choice of an equivalent service that uses less of their personal data and hit the firm with a €200 million fine. Sales growth has slowed in the region since the start of the year, when Meta rolled out the option. The European Commission said that charging EU users who did not want their personal data used for personalized advertising for an ad-free service—a measure Meta introduced in the EU in November 2023—did not satisfy this. Meta does not disclose how many people have chosen each option.

LPA uses 90 percent less data than personalized ads, Meta says, with ads shown based on what a person sees in a particular session on the site, plus some data points like their age, location, and gender. It leads to poorer returns for EU advertisers, it warned in a response to the EU’s ruling. Less-personalized ads are less efficient, leading to fewer transactions, and more ads being closed due to being irrelevant or repetitive, it said. Indeed, the price advertisers were willing to pay per ad in Europe is not growing as fast as in the US and Canada, Wednesday’s results showed.

Li told investors on Wednesday the company has “a lot of runway” to increase ad conversions and efficiency, as it is “continuing to find ways to either enhance the actual performance of the recommendation engine.” Rothschild & Co Redburn’s Ball believes the company will be able to use AI to improve its ad targeting in Europe despite working with fewer personal data signals.

Investors were keen to understand how this fits into Meta’s personal AI agent, Muse, launched last month, questions about which dominated the call. Li said the company is seeing high commercial intent from people using Muse—asking for product recommendations, researching alternatives, and comparing options—which will allow them to eventually tap into the advertising market with that product.

#141. Exercise in a pill

In unraveling the pathways by which movement helps the body, we could develop medicines that offer some of the same benefits, without the workout.

We all know exercise is good for us. It builds muscle, strengthens bones, lowers blood pressure, and boosts mood. By deciphering how movement benefits the body, researchers hope to develop medicines that can offer some of the same perks—without the workout. The idea isn’t to replace exercise but to bring its upsides to people who struggle with frailty, disability, or chronic illness.

It will take a lot of experimentation to untangle the myriad pathways by which exercise promotes health. But IDing the cascade of hormones, enzymes, and other molecules related to physical activity is just the first step. Scientists need to figure out how to turn that knowledge into safe and effective drugs. That’s a tall order: More than 90% of treatments that enter human studies fail.

There are regulatory hurdles too. The FDA approves medications to treat diseases, not to enhance wellness, says Thomas Burris, a pharmacologist at the University of Florida whose work focuses on diseases that respond to exercise, like obesity, type 2 diabetes, and dementia.

There may never be a pill that delivers all the benefits of exercise, but research could lead to new medicines that mimic some aspects of working out. Scientists have already identified many important exercise-related signals and have even developed compounds to regulate them. (Some preliminary results have been so promising that doping authorities have proactively banned the compounds.)

Burris and his colleagues are focused on a receptor that’s always switched on, even without exercise. Working out appears to boost that receptor in muscle cells, which in turn helps bolster endurance over time. Burris’s lab has spent years designing a drug that cranks up the receptor’s activity directly, and mice treated with the drug ran longer and farther than mice that weren’t. They “become marathon mice without having to do any type of exercise,” Burris says. There are even indications—also in mice—that the compound mimics some of the cognitive benefits of exercise and could help treat heart failure. Burris founded a company called Pelagos to carry the work forward into a clinical trial.

Other workout-mimicking drugs have already entered human studies. In June, the biotech firm Cambrian Bio reported that its experimental drug ATX-304 boosted metabolism and reduced liver and belly fat in people with obesity and prediabetes. It activates a metabolic switch that helps cells sense when energy supplies dip and coordinates the production of more fuel—the same switch that helps us sustain long sessions jumping rope or swimming laps. Cambrian hopes it will help people lose weight without losing muscle, a common concern with GLP-1 medicines.

Our understanding of how exercise benefits the body is still coming into focus, and drug development is prone to failure.

#134. Nuclear-powered spaceships

A new era of spaceflight could save astronauts time and cut exposure to cosmic radiation as they rocket to Mars—and beyond.

Just before the Artemis II crew began its historic slingshot around the moon this past April, NASA announced plans to build Space Reactor-1 Freedom. SR-1, the first-ever nuclear-reactor-­powered interplanetary spacecraft, is meant to fly an uncrewed mission to Mars by the end of 2028, arriving in 2029. A successful mission would herald a new era of more efficient, potentially faster spaceflight—saving astronauts time and cutting their exposure to harmful cosmic radiation as they travel to Mars and beyond. “You wake up to that announcement, and it puts a big smile on your face,” says Simon Middleburgh, co-director of the Nuclear Futures Institute at Bangor University in Wales.

Running a nuclear reactor in space may sound like lunacy, but it’s not. The Soviet Union sent dozens of reactors into orbit, often to power spy satellites. And experts say there aren’t any major technical obstacles to making nuclear space propulsion happen. “I think it’s a very doable technology,” says Philip Metzger, a spaceflight engineering researcher at the Florida Space Institute.“I’m happy to see them finally doing this.”

Still, developing space propulsion using reactors has been slow going. The US has started—and terminated—several programs looking into it over the past 75 years. The latest casualty was DRACO, a collaboration between NASA and the Department of Defense, which ended in 2025. The project was canceled because of high experimentation costs, an inability to compete with conventional rocket propulsion on price, and difficulty ensuring that ground tests could be performed safely and effectively. (You are creating an incredibly powerful nuclear reaction, after all.)

After much wavering, it now appears that nuclear-powered interplanetary travel is a $2 billion bet NASA is willing to make. The agency is pursuing an approach called nuclear electric propulsion, which uses the neutrons produced in a self-­sustaining, uranium-­atom-splitting reaction to create heat and ultimately electricity. The electricity is used to ionize a gas, which is blasted out of the spacecraft with the help of magnetic fields, generating thrust. The approach “is very low thrust, but very efficient,” says Sebastian Corbisiero, the US Department of Energy’s national technical director of space reactor programs. “So you can use it for a long period of time.”

What might such a rocket look like? A presentation by Steve Sinacore, program director of NASA’s SR-1 program, offers some clues. So far, the concept art makes the craft look like a colossal fletched arrow. The back will house the power-and-propulsion system, while the tip will hold a uranium-filled nuclear reactor with a capacity of at least 20 kilowatts. (For context, a typical nuclear plant on Earth is about 50,000 times more powerful.) The “fletches” on SR-1 are large fins that help cool the reactor by radiating heat into space.

Like many other pieces of space hardware, the reactor will need to be put through its paces on the ground before it’s cleared for the rough rocket trip to space. “Going through the launch safely is going to be a challenge,” says Middleburgh. “You are being shaken, rattled, and rolled.” For safety reasons, the reactor will be switched on around two days post-launch, when it’s comfortably far from Earth. Uranium isn’t tremendously dangerous by itself, but that can’t be said of the waste products that emerge when the reactor is running. If SR-1 works as planned, its journey to Mars will take about a year.

Will this novel form of space propulsion actually reach the Red Planet by 2029? It’s possible. NASA says the Idaho National Laboratory, which is building SR-1’s reactor, is making progress and is on target for a late-2028 launch. “Procurement of major components is underway, and integration work on the power and propulsion element is advancing steadily,” Sinacore told MIT Technology Review. The outlook will get even sunnier if the Artemis program, which aims to start laying the foundations for a base on the lunar south pole by the decade’s end, keeps to its own bold deadline.

“It’s an aggressive timeline,” says Lindsey Holmes, who is vice president of advanced projects at Analytical Mechanics Associates, an aerospace company in Virginia. She suspects the push is being driven partly by China’s and Russia’s own deep-space nuclear ambitions. The two countries have spoken about plans to place a reactor on the moon’s surface in the mid-2030s to power a jointly operated lunar base. Whether it flies or fails in space, SR-1’s development should help NASA put its own reactor on the moon; the technology involved in the two projects is broadly similar, given that the lunar surface, like space itself, is a vacuum. If an interplanetary nuclear-powered spacecraft works, a nuclear reactor on the moon should too.

Most of the technology for SR-1 already exists; it’s going to come down to piecing it all together and then seeing how it performs in space.

#124. Meet Mori, a Cute Robot Mushroom That Doesn’t Really Say (or Do) Anything

You've had a long day. You finally get home, fling the door open, plonk on the couch, and let out a big sigh. In the corner of your eye, you notice your mushroom lamp perk up. Its microphone must have heard you come in. Its head raises to reveal round, animated eyes, and a little diamond-shaped nose—which actually houses a camera.

It does a little jiggle because it sees you're back. Its name is Mori.

As you move around the space—maybe it's time to start cooking dinner—the little robot's face follows you, observing your movements and making soft sounds that are a cross between a bird and R2-D2. The day's stress catches up, and you need a moment to relax, so you head over to Mori, touch its head, and its eyes and camera-nose dip back inside its body. It goes back to being a lamp, except now it plays nature soundscapes so you can feel at ease.

That's the pitch from OKA, or One Kind Artifact, a new startup based in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and Shenzhen, China, focusing on experimental technology. CEO Hua Wang tells WIRED he wants his company to create five experimental “Artifacts” over the course of OKA's lifetime. Mori is the first.

“There are certain relationships in this era we want to fix between humans and our surroundings—human with nature, human with one another—every product is an experience trying to reconnect some of those relationships,” Wang says. Before cofounding OKA, he was a general manager at Meipai, a video-sharing app from the Chinese phone brand Meitu. He also cofounded Bondee, a now-defunct social media app that briefly went viral in Asia.

According to the company, the point of Mori, the nearly 12-inch-tall home robot lamp, is to reconnect its owners with nature. It can play nature soundscapes. It will glow different colors—inspired by rain, forests, fire, and wind—while reacting to your movements. It can guide you through a calming breathing session.

It also uses its built-in camera and microphones to serve as a fun little interactive companion in your home. Ask it how your outfit looks, and Mori will jiggle excitedly. If you're playing music or watching TV, Mori can dance to the tunes and react to the action on your TV screen.

It intentionally does not do anything else. Unlike an Amazon Echo, it isn't an assistant: You can't use voice commands to control smart lights or cue up Spotify tracks, and it won't answer if you ask about the weather.

The Founder's Edition is a staggering $649 and is available starting today, though stock is limited to a few hundred units. That price also includes a two-year warranty and two years of Mori's subscription service ($17 per month or $80 a year). If you miss out on the Founder’s Edition, you can still buy a standard edition Mori, but that costs $849 (there's a preorder price of $799), and that higher price shockingly does not include any of the subscription features.

Wang says today's home robots have mostly taken on “machine-like” forms that don't mesh well with personal environments, so OKA designed a lamp that blends in, but is also anthropomorphized to make being at home “feel better.”

OKA's AI algorithms (powered by multiple local large language models) let Mori understand the room's context through the camera and microphone, so it knows when to interact and when to stay quiet. The company calls this “Attentive Intelligence.” It has memory, so it remembers you and interacts with you a certain way over time, whereas it might act a little more shy if you bring home a guest. If it doesn't see someone for a month, it will forget them.

It might notice, for example, that you've been on your phone doomscrolling for two hours straight on the couch. Mori might then try to get your attention so you come over, listen to some wind rustling through the leaves, and give your eyes a break. When you're on a phone call, or maybe having an argument with a significant other, it understands what’s happening and goes silent so as not to distract you.

“All gadgets try to capture more of your attention,” Wang says. “Mori gives your attention back to you. It invites you to do meditative experiences. Nature has this restorative power to humans.”

The robot can connect to Wi-Fi, which should give you pause from a security standpoint, but Wang says the internet connection is only used for firmware updates.

Mori processes raw images and audio locally, and then discards the files. Facial feature data—which is collected so it knows to react to you versus a guest—is encrypted on the device, and never uploaded. The camera turns off when the robot retracts into a lamp, and the microphone turns off when you double-tap the top of Mori's head. It is battery-powered, so you can take it from room to room, or just leave it plugged in.

Wang says Mori's skill set is limited for now, though it could expand in the future. He suggests it could connect to your Oura Ring data in the future so it knows exactly when you need some relaxing sounds, or to your Spotify account to play different content. Voice commands could even come later—like asking Mori to play nature sounds instead of having to touch it.

“People feel isolated and want this kind of presence in their home. A lot of people leave their TV on to fill the emptiness of the room,” Wang says, pointing to a time when he was feeling isolated while studying abroad in the US. “It's not particularly for the elderly or children, but for younger generations.”

Cute robot pets that interact with you have been welcomed into our homes before. Chris Harrison, director of the Future Interfaces Group at Carnegie Mellon University, points to Sony's Aibo dog from 1999 as an early digital companion. However, he says the crucial ingredient they have been missing is what artificial intelligence can now provide. These previous robots were too simplistic, but now that the modern versions can understand and react to human actions and emotions, they could become more impactful.

“Humans have always wanted to anthropomorphize everything—they love making things cute,” Harrison says. “We want to make robots cute; we want to put cute little cases on our iPhones.” He points to Meta's new Muse agent, which has a cuddly avatar. “It is almost like a pet, but a hyper-capable pet.”

When I point out that Mori doesn't seem very capable with its limited feature set, Harrison likens it to people owning a goldfish or a hamster.

“Are we holding [Mori] to an unrealistic bar? People do extract a lot of pleasure out of taking care of their pets and snuggling with their pets and interacting with their pets that I wouldn't say are necessarily more sophisticated.”

Whether people will shell out hundreds of dollars for a robotic pet that can’t fetch—or even tell you the time—is up in the air. But if this mushroom-shaped, camera-wielding lamp can fool overstimulated brains into feeling a little less alone at the end of the day, maybe that’s enough.

#109. AI’s hidden storage bill: What inference leaves behind

Enterprises need economically scalable capacity to keep pace with the data created by AI inference.

Ask most executives to picture the cost of AI and they’ll likely see GPUs—racks of accelerators with gigawatts of power—and a waitlist for silicon. But that picture leaves something out: Data storage costs.

What happens to data after a model responds? Much of the back-end data that’s generated—from billions of daily prompts, images, and videos—needs to be stored. But data storage is one of the harder infrastructure costs to plan for, and it’s often overlooked in initial AI infrastructure planning.

Traditionally, AI infrastructure conversations have centered on the compute-intensive process of training a model. Training is finite: a run starts, burns through compute, and ends. But inference runs continuously, every time a model answers a prompt. For infrastructure planning, that difference matters: A training run is a project you can budget for and retire. Inference doesn’t retire; every inference request leaves something behind.

Take AI-generated video. In this workload, the back-end spend can be significant. From internal measurements, WD found that an 8-second clip (returned to a user as a 3.97MB 720p file) left more than 500MB of associated data behind on the back end, including logs, cached context, immediate renders, backups, and safety records. That’s a 130x increase over the output file size the user saw. Scale this to an enterprise generating longer, higher resolution video and the back-end footprint grows into the hundreds of gigabytes per clip.

And that back-end footprint is growing fast. McKinsey projects AI inference demand will grow at a 35% CAGR through 2030, versus 22% for training, making up more than 40% of total data center demand by the end of the decade.

A recent WD-sponsored IDC global white paper that surveyed 763 IT and business decision makers found that nearly 95% of respondents reported an increase in data volume due to generative AI over the past 12 months, with roughly 61% reporting growth of 25% or more.

“You can think of having more storage as an optimization for keeping the GPUs busy,” said a senior product manager of a hyperscale cloud service provider quoted in report. “The biggest challenge is to ensure that the GPUs are not idle, waiting for data to show up—hours of GPU idle time becomes very expensive, very fast.” Put in dollar terms: if your storage spend is growing 25% year over year while your compute budget grows in the more predictable cyclical way, storage ends up outpacing the budget line that receives the most attention.

Compute demand rises and falls with training cycles, product launches, and hardware refreshes. But the volume of retained data doesn't fall back down with it when compute demand slows. Model logs, synthetic outputs, inference results, and more keep accumulating, and organizations are holding onto more of it for longer.

This changes the role of retained data. Information that once moved into an archive and rarely surfaced can now become an input to future AI workloads. As more data is retained, retrieved, and reused, storage must support not only growing capacity, but increasingly dynamic access to it. The same WD-IDC survey found 96% of respondents anticipate needing faster archive retrieval to support inference or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).

AI workloads require both performance and capacity, with different technologies serving different needs across the data lifecycle. And for the vast volumes of data that must be retained and revisited, HDDs provide a cost-effective, high-capacity foundation, with a significant cost-per-terabyte advantage

For a CFO or CIO, this means “AI infrastructure spend” may be scoped too narrowly on compute. A model that idles while data loads costs about as much as one idling for lack of a GPU. But the storage bottleneck stays hidden until it’s already a problem, surfacing in operating costs and retrieval delays rather than a CapEx line next to the accelerators.

Consider a retailer generating AI product videos for its catalog, or an insurer running claims documents through a RAG pipeline for faster underwriting. In both cases, the compute cost is a known, budgeted quantity: a per-generation or per-query rate that finance can forecast. The storage cost is different: It compounds with every video generated and every claim processed, because none of that back-end data disappears once the task is finished.

A team that budgets AI as “compute plus a rounding error for storage” may find, in 12 to 18 months, that the rounding error has become a significant fraction of the program’s total cost—right around the time leadership is deciding whether to expand.

Addressing this challenge starts with architecture. Storage strategy tends to be designed for expensive, hot-tier performance (for data accessed instantly and constantly), while most AI data by volume accumulates in the warm, cool, and cold tiers behind it.

What enterprises need is economically scalable capacity: Infrastructure designed for the full AI data life cycle, balancing performance with capacity, power, reliability, and total cost of ownership as data persists, compounds, and is increasingly reused. That means treating archives that once sat untouched as active assets for RAG and inference, and setting retention policies for data’s growing strategic value rather than its original storage cost. It also means recognizing that decisions about storage play out across fleets, not individual drives.

Such infrastructure requires storage tiers, where individualized power consumption, reliability, density, and operational efficiency all determine whether the system remains economically sustainable as capacity scales.

#104. How AI is changing Halloween

Growing up in Pittsburgh, my childhood Halloween hauls were 70 percent Clark Bars. Emptying my bag onto the living room floor, I’d channel my inner Ralphie from A Christmas Story, muttering a disappointed "Ohhhhh fuuudge!" at all that local chocolate.

Times have changed. Those stores are gone, Clark Bars are rare, and I’m old enough that I don't have to say "fudge" when I'm angry. After I reluctantly grew up, I went into retail, leading media relations for Kmart and Sears. Our Kmart stores would load up on cheap candy before Halloween, and sales always topped costumes or decorations. Corporate buyers spent months planning exactly what to sell, how much to order, and where to ship it across 1,000 stores. Then, like clockwork on Nov. 1, the leftovers went on clearance. Apparently, peanut butter cups don’t age well on store shelves.

That human-driven process seems so stale now. Nowadays, people snicker at the butterfingers of humans, proverbial pez heads who can let things slip be-twix their fingers. But don’t be too quick to kiss humans goodbye.

Today, childhood nostalgia is being systematically optimized away by advanced predictive analytics and logistical AI. The invisible hand of the candy aisle knows your neighborhood’s demographic profile better than you do, and it has already decided if your zip code deserves the "good stuff." Where I grew up, we wouldn’t be getting Toblerone, that’s for sure.

Global candy manufacturers and big-box retailers openly deploy AI-driven "demand sensing" models. Retailers now feed past transactions, social media trends, and hyper-local product inventory into machine learning networks. By analyzing this data, algorithms can forecast exactly how a specific neighborhood will shop.

The system cross-references local census records and homeownership rates to shift delivery pallets before the first plastic pumpkin hits shelves. Quite frankly, that is a helluva lot of data.

The result is a hyper-focused predictive allocation engine. If a well-to-do suburb experiences a millennial baby boom, something like Walmart’s demand-forecasting algorithms catch the trend early. By analyzing local sales history and foot traffic, the system uses machine learning to automatically trigger massive shipments of premium, multi-brand chocolate variety packs to those specific high-demand stores weeks in advance.

Conversely, if you live in a high-density urban apartment block of young professionals or an aging retirement community, the AI predicts low trick-or-treater turnout. Premium inventory is rerouted elsewhere, leaving your local store shelves bare of anything but low-tier hard candies.

In other words, your neighbors couldn't buy the good candy even if they wanted to; the algorithm decided they weren't worth the shipping cost. What a snobby algorithm.

Then I got to thinking, what happens when these algorithms collide with regional history, creating an inevitable feedback loop? Take my childhood Pittsburgh data. Today's predictive AI won't see a city of disappointed children. Nope, it will only see a massive, recurring seasonal spike for Clark Bars.

Blind to our melt-away misery, the algorithm would flag this as a "regional preference," boost bulk buying, slash the price, and flood local shelves. By automating past behavior, the machine ensures future generations face the exact same crisis I did. A lot of kids like me venting an "Ohhhhh fuuudge!"

The algorithm isn't just looking backward, either. It also parses unstructured data like live weather forecasts. If the machine catches even a whiff of rain on Oct. 31, it can instantly reduce shipments of trick-or-treating candy to your entire region, prioritizing indoor alternatives like party kits or ghoulish plastic backdrops.

The system is also secretly scrolling social media. The second a viral TikTok video of an influencer with a trending marshmallow ghost Peep with bug eyes catches fire, the algorithm intercepts the digital signal. It immediately diverts entire shipping containers of Peeps to the hip, urban zip codes driving the clicks, leaving the surrounding suburbs out of the loop.

We spend a lot of time fretting about generative AI replacing human artists or stealing digital identities. Yet, the most immediate way AI alters our culture is deceptively invisible; quietly sucking the random, chaotic joy out of a community holiday and dressing it in the costume of an optimization spreadsheet.

So, when the kids dump their plastic pumpkins onto the living room rug this year, look closely at the haul. Don’t curse the house down the street for handing out generic chocolate or cheap hard candy. They weren't being stingy.

They were just the unfortunate casualties of a cold, unfeeling algorithm. The machine unwrapped their zip code, crunched the numbers, and chewed on the fact that their neighborhood simply didn't possess the premium metrics required for a Reese's Cup.

By trying to optimize our choices, the algorithm inadvertently condemns them to a bleak, automated future of pure, inescapable Clark Bars. “Ohhhhh fuuudge!”

#101. Embarrassing name? How we live with names we don't like

Names are central to our identity, so what happens when that name is a source of ridicule? Research points to negative life outcomes, but some people learn to embrace unfortunate names.

As a child growing up in Australia, Louise Pain hated her surname.

"People used to tease me; I was called things like 'Pain in the a*s,'" she told DW. "I remember actively hating my surname when I was a child and wanting to change it."

The jokes have followed her throughout her life, even after she moved to Germany. But one thing did eventually change: Pain's feelings about her own name.

Names are not only a fundamental part of how we identify ourselves — they can, beyond teasing, also influence how other people treat us. So, what happens if we dislike our own name — or if it is perceived negatively by those around us?

"A person's name is vital to their sense of identity," psychoanalyst Mikita Brottman told DW. "When someone has an embarrassing name, that embarrassment can be incorporated into their identity."

Research on name bias has linked negatively perceived names with romantic rejection,as well as lower educational outcomes, lower self-esteem and more frequent smoking.

This suggests that names could influence our life trajectory, namely through self-esteem and opportunity.

But a person's relationship with their name is not necessarily fixed. Pain's lived experience, and that of another woman with the first name Tiny, shows how people can find ways to reclaim, and even come to value, names that once triggered ridicule and rejection.

Names can act as "signals" when you first meet someone, personality psychologist Jochen Gebauer told DW. Those signals may lose importance as people get to know each other, but Gebauer said their effects could also accumulate over a lifetime, potentially contributing to "real measurable advantages and disadvantages."

Research led by sociologist Anthony Lee found that a name can evoke inaccurate assumptions about intelligence, competence, trustworthiness, sociability and attractiveness. For Pain, other people's reactions have extended beyond jokes. In high school, a teacher refused to believe that 'Pain' was her real surname and reprimanded her in front of the class.

"Your name couldn't possibly be Pain. Write your real name!" she recalled the teacher saying.

In Lee's UK-based research, less familiar names tended to fall along a dimension partly characterized as "weirdness," which may help explain why difficult-to-pronounce or culturally unfamiliar names receive more negative evaluations.

Tiny, now 54, encountered the same questions about her unfamiliar name growing up in Greece.

Her parents had adapted "Tiny" to "Tinie" to make it sound more Greek, but people would repeatedly ask what her real or baptismal name was.

Even relatives have refused to accept it. Recently, two elderly aunts introduced her to someone as "Haritini," a conventional Greek name.

"I didn't even bother correcting them at this point," Tiny told DW.

Tiny's relationship with her name has also evolved.

"The jokes [from others] got repetitive and boring pretty fast, so at least I was left with the sense that I have something unique with my name going on."

Pain, now 41, discovered how much cultural context could change the reaction to her surname when she began learning her father's native French as a teenager. In French, 'pain' means bread.

"People started calling me 'Bread! Louise Bread.'"

Later, after moving to Germany, the jokes became mostly bread-related rather than the more scathing ones she had experienced in Australia. "That helped me to feel better about my name," she said.

As they got older, both Pain and Tiny developed coping strategies to pre-empt people's reactions to their names.

Tiny sometimes introduces herself with: "Hi, I'm Tiny. It's my name and not a statement," or, "I'm Tiny. Not a difficult name to live up to."

"This usually stops people from asking awkward questions."

Pain also began to embrace the humor surrounding her name.

"I was convinced that I should become either a doctor or a dominatrix, or perhaps both, to become either 'Doctor Pain,' or 'Mistress Pain.'"

"Names can conjure that whole spectrum of shame and pride," psychoanalyst Melissa Daum told DW.

Pain now finds that people tend to see her surname as "funny and endearing… it's become something that is more of a funny talking point," she said.

But the childhood discomfort has not entirely disappeared. When Pain thinks about having children, her feelings about the name become more complicated.

"I don't really want to pass on my surname ... so there is a deeper wound there."

But Pain also believes her father would be deeply hurt if future children had a different name. The surname she once hated connects her to him and to her French heritage.

"Something that once feels like an inconvenience or a source of self-consciousness can become a marker of distinctiveness, history, or belonging," Daum said.

"My parents seemingly made an unfortunate choice, but by now I have embraced both the name and the sense of uniqueness that comes with it."

That does not mean she uses Tiny everywhere. She avoids it on dating apps because "Tiny might give off the wrong message" and does not use it in her email address when she wants to appear professional.

Pain, meanwhile, uses a Germanized version of the French word for bread as her social media surname.

Online, people have more control over what name they present to others. As Daum put it: "Choosing one's own name can feel like an act of mastery, even a refusal of that original passivity: I will decide what I am called and how I am encountered."

But accepting a name does not necessarily mean embracing it in every situation.

Pain once wanted to change her surname. Tiny considered using her middle name in her thirties. Neither ultimately abandoned the names that had caused them difficulties.

For Tiny, at least, a name that once made her feel awkward has become something she can imagine carrying for the rest of her life.
