#5750. Sweet Potato Casserole

Some say the best sweet potato casserole is covered in marshmallows; others swear by a buttery streusel or toasty maple-slicked pecans. While I firmly believe crunchy contrast is essential to this classic Thanksgiving side dish, there’s one small problem: I’m allergic to nuts.

My sweet potato casserole recipe swaps the nuts for crispy, sweet-and-salty cornflakes—the marshmallows can stay. Tossed with melted butter, brown sugar, cinnamon, and salt, the cornflakes turn golden and crunchy in the oven. Add a handful of chopped pecans if you must, but trust me, you won’t miss them.

Instead of boiling the spuds or reaching for canned sweet potatoes, I roast fresh ones until tender, concentrating their flavor instead of washing it down the drain. For the silkiest purée, skip the potato masher and give the cooked sweet potatoes a whiz in the food processor.

Can you make sweet potato casserole ahead of time?

Yes. Get a head start by roasting the sweet potatoes up to 3 days in advance. Let them cool, then cover and refrigerate until you’re ready to make the casserole. Add the cornflakes and marshmallows just before baking, so the topping stays crisp.

For more ways to get ahead of the holiday rush, browse our make-ahead Thanksgiving recipes.

Can you make sweet potato casserole without marshmallows?

Sure. The brown-sugar-coated cornflakes bring plenty of sweetness and crunch on their own. Simply skip the marshmallows and scatter the cornflakes evenly over the purée. Watch the topping as it bakes so it doesn’t get too dark.

Or try our savory sweet potato casserole, with brown butter, rosemary, and crisp breadcrumbs in place of the sweet topping.

Is this sweet potato casserole gluten-free?

It can be. Some cornflakes contain malt syrup or malt extract, made from barley, so they aren’t gluten-free. Check the box and choose a certified gluten-free cereal if needed.

Are yams the same thing as sweet potatoes?

Preheat oven to 350°. Place 3 lb. sweet potatoes (about 3 large), peeled, cut into 1" pieces, in a 13x9" baking dish or on a foil-lined baking sheet. Cover with foil and bake until tender, 50–60 minutes.

Do Ahead: Sweet potatoes can be baked 3 days ahead. Let cool; cover and chill.

Whisk 2 large eggs, room temperature, ¼ cup half-and-half, ¼ cup (packed; 50 g) dark brown sugar, ¼ cup (½ stick) unsalted butter, melted, slightly cooled, and 2 tsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¼ tsp. Morton kosher salt in a small bowl to combine.

Transfer half of sweet potatoes to a food processor, add half of egg mixture, and process until smooth, about 2 minutes. Transfer purée to a large mixing bowl. Repeat with remaining sweet potatoes and egg mixture. Scrape all of purée into baking dish (no need to clean).

Combine 2 cups cornflakes, 1 tsp. ground cinnamon, remaining ¼ cup (packed; 50 g) dark brown sugar, remaining ¼ cup (½ stick) unsalted butter, melted, slightly cooled, and remaining 1½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or 1 tsp. Morton kosher salt in a large resealable plastic bag, seal, and shake to coat cereal.

Arrange cornflake mixture and marshmallows over top of purée in alternating diagonal stripes. Bake casserole until marshmallows are golden brown, 25–30 minutes.

A gene variant called APOE4 dramatically increases the risk of late-onset Alzheimer's disease, the most common form of dementia. Now, emerging research is revealing APOE4's true role in the condition, and a new study pinpoints a possible way to treat it.

Historically, scientists studying Alzheimer's disease have focused on two proteins that accumulate in patients' brains: amyloid-beta and tau. However, there's a "third thing that people didn't pay attention to, until recently," said study co-author Li-Huei Tsai, a neuroscientist at MIT. That third thing is the accumulation of droplets of fat, or lipids, in the brain, she told Live Science.

The APOE gene makes proteins responsible for transporting fat around cells throughout the body and brain. A version of the gene called APOE4, which increases the risk of late-onset Alzheimer's three- to 12-fold, makes proteins that are less efficient at this function, Tsai said. But how APOE4 affects different brain cells was not clearly understood.

Tsai's team has been working to close that gap for about a decade.

"APOE4 can influence many different cell types," she emphasized. "It's not just one cell type."

In past laboratory research, Tsai's team found that APOE4 causes a buildup of cholesterol in astrocytes, star-shaped brain cells that support the function of neurons. Genetic analyses suggested that the gene variant may mess with how these cells process fat.

Another study zoomed in on oligodendrocytes, cells that help insulate neurons' outgoing wires with a fatty material called myelin. This material helps neurons send messages more efficiently. APOE4 caused the oligodendrocytes to accumulate cholesterol. So, in lab mice, the team used a drug to help the cells get rid of that extra cholesterol, and it improved the cells' ability to make myelin and the mice's performance on cognitive tests.

In their latest research, published Thursday (Oct. 15) in the journal Cell Stem Cell, Tsai's team dug into the question of why APOE4 causes fat to accumulate in the first place.

They used stem cells to grow APOE4-carrying oligodendrocytes and then turned specific genes on or off, to see if they increased or decreased the formation of fat droplets. This screening pointed to a major group of genes involved in cell-to-cell communication, called the Wnt signaling pathway. This included a key gene called DKK1, which acts as a brake on this signaling system.

Disabling DKK1 — essentially lifting the brake — reduced the concentration of fat droplets in the cells, the team found.

In brain tissue samples from organ donors with APOE4, the team also found signs of low Wnt signaling. This finding "establishes a very strong link" between the signaling pathway and formation of fat droplets, said Rosie Jackson, a cell biologist at the University of Dundee who was not involved in this study.

Myelin (orange) is a fatty material that coats the outgoing wires of many neurons, helping the cells send signals more efficiently.

The researchers then investigated exactly how Wnt signaling was disrupted in oligodendrocytes.

APOE4 seemed to decrease the cells' supply of a protein that helps relay Wnt signals. That helpful protein was being broken down by an enzyme whose levels had been boosted by the APOE4. In mice, the team used a drug to block that extra enzyme, and the treatment ultimately restored normal Wnt signaling, reduced fat buildup, and helped the cells make myelin properly.

This could change how scientists think about treating Alzheimer's disease, Jackson said. It "opens up a new wave of potential therapeutics" that could target the accumulated fats, rather than going after only amyloid-beta and tau, she said.

"I think that this is becoming a really common idea in the field — that we're going to need multiple medications that target slightly different elements," she told Live Science. "And targeting lipids will be a really important part of that."

That said, the findings have not been confirmed in humans yet.

"It's very encouraging that facilitating lipid clearance and lipid movement is a potential therapeutic avenue," said study co-author Joel Blanchard, a neuroscientist at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. The question is how we can do that in a safe and effective manner in humans, he said.

Blanchard has led other research that underscores APOE4's effects beyond fats and myelin. The gene variant may affect cells that support blood vessels in the brain by driving scarring and contributing to amyloid-beta buildup. It may also mess with the "trash collectors" inside astrocytes, again leading to a buildup of harmful proteins.

While cells with other versions of the APOE gene can put a lot of energy toward breaking down the proteins, APOE4-carrying cells put all their energy toward fat storage, Blanchard said.

But more research is needed to understand how to fix the problem, he said. Hopefully, targeting fat storage "could rescue not just one thing but multiple things that go wrong in the course of Alzheimer's disease," he told Live Science.

#5747. Hasan Minhaj Thinks America’s Greatest Accomplishment Is Cereal

This is Amused Bouche, SAVEUR’s food questionnaire that explores the culinary curiosities of some of our favorite people. This interview series dives deep into their food routines, including dinner party strategies, cherished cookbooks, and the memorable bites they’d hop on a flight for.

Comedian and actor Hasan Minhaj is known for quippy questions, biting comebacks, and colorful commentary, but his most delightful soundbites are about food. During a recent  hour-long chat, he named a wide range of favorites, including bananas and oranges (“they come prepackaged and are great for traveling”); macaroni and cheese (“I love every category and version of it, from the box of Kraft to anyone’s Thanksgiving mac ’n’ cheese”); and dal chawal (specifically his mom’s version of the lentils and rice dish, “pure comfort food and my love language”).

These are the best-of-the-best bites for Minhaj, which is coincidentally the name of his new movie—Best of the Best, streaming now on Netflix—that he cowrote and produced with Prashanth Venkataramanujam and stars in as a professor and former dancer. The film pays homage to real-life Bollywood competitions, following collegiate dancers Maya and Anjali (played by Maitreyi Ramakrishnan and Priyanka Kedia, respectively), whose characters were inspired by “deeply passionate college kids in the biggest world that a lot of people have never heard of.”

Fiery passion is at the center of Minhaj’s life, especially when it comes to actual spice. “Some people want to drive at 85, some like a nice even 65, and some take it five miles below the limit,” he explains in a speedy analogy. “My spice level? I want to feel things in all caps. Life is to be lived. So I’m like, ‘Give it to me!’ Give me the full robustness of flavor that’s possible. I am a maximalist and love it all!”

His other spice of life is chai, “a form of commiseration, community, and love in Indian households—and an entry point to gossiping.” He loves that everyone does chai differently (he and his wife, Beena, prefer a more ginger-forward profile, while his parents like it sweeter). That’s why he became an investor and partner in the New York City-based Kolkata Chai in 2024, working with brothers Ani and Ayan Sanyal to expand the reach of a drink so engrained in their lives and culture. “It’s a welcome change to a lot of people’s only chai experience being at Starbucks in Middle America. It’s really amazing how you can change the complete perspective of an entire culture because of a drink that people can explore and make their own.”

Read on for Minhaj’s answers to our Amused Bouche questionnaire—it’s no surprise they’re chock-full of equally heartwarming and appetizing soundbites.

If you could only eat one thing 24/7/365, what would it be?

America is celebrating its 250th-year anniversary, and I think as a country, America’s greatest accomplishment is what we brought to cereal. I have been caught many a time red-handed with my full paw inside of some Golden Grahams, going to town. I will kill half a box, straight up, no milk. It is a snacking cereal. I’ll be watching a prestige drama and stress-eating Golden Grahams. Sometimes I’ll even drizzle honey over a bowl. I’ll also do the same with Honey Bunches of Oats. There’s a Montagues-and-Capulets debate here of blue box or orange box. I’m a blue box guy, because I love the almonds, extra crunch, and sweetness. It was my granola mix before I started eating granola, and it’s like trail mix without dastardly raisins coming in and f—ing shit up!

What’s the first thing you learned how to cook?

A strawberry Toaster Strudel, one of my all-time favorites. I popped it in the toaster as a kid, and it was an eye-opening experience. When I came home from elementary school, it felt like I was a master chef making myself this crisp little strudel cake and frosting it. I felt like a confectionery master. It was incredible. I toasted it on a level three and would sometimes pop it up and flip it midway through.

I actually didn’t hit my growth spurt until much later in high school, so I was quite undersized and malnourished-looking when I was younger. I was bullied a lot. My mother would make me eat three hard-boiled eggs on the way to school. And I hated hard-boiled eggs. Now that I’m a parent of small children, I understand the efficacy of hard-boiled eggs. But now I will pan-fry hard-boiled eggs, because really my hatred of them came from texture and temperature issues. But if you cut them in half and fry them yolk side down, there’s a crispness that makes it turn into a crunchy, almost taco-like topping that you can put on top of bread and butter.

It’s a really delicious, warm-and-gooey snack that I started making when I tried to give my daughter a hard-boiled egg and she immediately spit it out. I’m like, “Yeah, the cycle of torture continues! There needs to be a better way! Let me figure out a way to make this delicious.” The secret was making a quesadilla as the on-ramp she needs to get eggs in her. It was an experiment I worked backward from, kind of like writing a joke, telling a story, or working on a screenplay. My daughter loves quesadillas but hates eggs, so by making it into crunchiness that would blend right in, it wouldn’t feel like a squishy thing that I’m force-feeding her.

Growing up, we were never allowed to order dessert from restaurants. It is my greatest joy to order dessert from restaurants. I’ll tell the missus to order whatever she wants. Sometimes she’ll be indecisive. She always wants the chocolate lava cake, but she’ll be eyeing a berry tart. It’s a distant second, but I’ll take the bullet and order both for us to share. Sometimes when I do it, I’ll look over my shoulder to be like, “Are mom and dad going to find out?” But now I’m dad. We’re mom and dad!

I do not cook from cookbooks, but I will cook from YouTube Shorts. There’s just something about the looping nature of it breaking down the recipe. It’s tickling something in my mind and letting me know, “Hey, it’s not that hard. You can totally do it! You’ll get to completion, and it’s going to look pretty good.” And then as soon as doubt kicks in, the video starts playing again and snaps me out of it. It’s like the feeling of playing a song that you love over and over again, like “This Is How We Do It” by Montell Jordan. Play it again! “Men in Black”—play it again! I want to listen to it again, and that’s the feeling I get when I search “chicken tinga recipe” on YouTube Shorts.

Is there a cooking disaster that made you swear off a dish forever?

My wife does most of the cooking, but I want to put on the record that I try. I do cook, but a lot of the reaction is like, “Oh, you tried. I appreciate the effort. And that’s great for you. Why don’t you enjoy the omelet that you’ve made?” But I think I make a great soft-scramble, and what I bring to the breakfast table is pretty solid. I used to burn all of my food because I got impatient and cranked up the heat too fast. I learned you can’t bring microwave energy to a stove. Once I understood that foundational element in not burning your eggs, it was a huge unlock. Then I got to experience the full variety of eggs that aren’t charred.

Which nostalgic foods from childhood bring you the most comfort?

Dal chalwal: just lentils and rice. It has always been the first thing my mom makes for me when I get home to Sacramento, from college until now. It’s this beautiful gesture of love that I’m eating dal chawal with my mother, and her grandchildren are eating it with her at the same time. It’s pure comfort food and my love language.

When you’re playing dinner-party DJ, what’s spinning?

It’s a seasonal thing. Beena is really the entertainer and has been an incredible host since we met. For a holiday party, she’ll queue up a Pentatonix holiday playlist. Thematic, but a little left of center and unexpected.

What is your biggest entertaining flex to impress guests?

Beena loves a theme. We host Halloween on our block every year and call it Boo Bash. We go all out with a Halloween-themed live band, fog machines on the dance floor—aka the driveway—children’s face painting, bounce house, fake blood as drinks in IV bags. Her level of joy and imagination is unparalleled. She’s incredible. I add pizzazz, but I’m like, “Go off, queen!” She is the show. She’s the show creator, and I’m the showrunner.

Tell me about a meal so good you would hop on a flight to relive it.

My grandparents on both sides of my family have passed away, but I’m lucky enough that I was raised by my grandmother on my mother’s side from third to fifth grade. There’s a specific memory of when my grandmother made biryani. When the lid came off the pot and the steam and aroma of all the flavors escaped for the first time, she would put a ladle in and scoop it out for me, with meat that is so tender because it’s been cooking for so long. That’s the feeling that I would chase and book a flight immediately for, but it’s just a feeling. A feeling of love from it taking all day to make biriyani and that she loved me enough to put in that time and energy. Goat biriyani especially was a special-occasion dish, like when a cousin was getting married, and I associate it with being with family and something happy happening. In Hindi you call it ghar kha khana, the food from home. Biryani is the crème de la crème and the chef’s kiss of Indian cuisine for me. Especially my grandmother’s.

#5746. Nearly 5,000 acres of North America’s grasslands are lost to farming each day

North America's grasslands are disappearing by thousands of acres a day. A new report uncovers an overlooked path toward recovery.

A cornfield looks permanent, and after enough summers it’s hard to picture it as anything else. But across North America, the plow is still moving. By the World Wildlife Fund’s (WWF) count, an estimated 1.8 million acres (730,000 hectares) of grassland and other open land became row crops such as corn, soybeans, and wheat in 2023.

That grassland loss works out to nearly 5,000 acres (2,000 hectares) a day. WWF says this is the first time it has tallied the loss across the United States, Canada, and Mexico together.

The figure comes from WWF’s 2026 Plowprint Report, which the group has published since 2014. It is the organization’s own analysis, not a peer-reviewed study, and every number here is WWF’s estimate.

Alexis Bonogofsky, vice president of WWF’s Great Plains program, said the report helps people see what is disappearing. “Grasslands don’t always get the attention that forests or oceans do, but they’re every bit as extraordinary,” she said.

Of the 1.8 million acres lost in 2023, about 1.15 million were in the Canadian Prairies. The contiguous United States lost about 446,000 acres and Mexico about 204,000.

Corn and soybeans drive most of the new plowing in the United States. In the Canadian Prairies, it’s wheat.

Between 2020 and 2024, farmers expanded the total U.S. cropland footprint, the area they have cropped at some point, by about 2.1 million acres, or 0.4%. Canada’s grew 5% between 2019 and 2023, and Mexico’s grew 7% between 2020 and 2024.

In an interview with Earth.com, Bonogofsky said the U.S. pace is slower than in earlier periods of farm expansion. Even so, the country still loses hundreds of thousands of acres a year. “Conversion is accelerating most rapidly in Canada and Mexico, while the U.S. footprint remains the largest,” she said.

The Plowprint compares government land cover maps, built from satellite images, from one year to the next. In the United States, the yearly updates come from the National Land Cover Database.

WWF counts an acre as converted only after farmers plant row crops on it for two consecutive years. That rule prevents the analysis from mistaking a field left unplanted for a season, a drought year, or a misread satellite pixel for permanent loss.

The two-year rule is also part of why the latest year in the report is 2023. Canada’s land cover data runs a year behind, so 2023 is the last year all three countries can be reported together.

WWF paused the report last year after the underlying government data changed, then rebuilt its methods. The biggest change was extending the U.S. historical record back to 1986. Earlier editions could only see plowing after 2008, so a field broken in 1995 and later returned to grass looked, to the model, like it had never been farmed.

So WWF says the 2023 figure shouldn’t be set beside earlier editions, including the 1.9 million acres it reported for 2022. “Because we changed our methods, we can’t compare this year’s numbers directly with earlier reports,” Bonogofsky told Earth.com.

The longer record showed WWF something the old approach couldn’t. Only about 65% of the land inside the U.S. footprint is actively planted in a given year. Nearly a quarter, roughly 111 million acres (45 million hectares), has gone back to grassland or rangeland, including about 63 million acres in the Great Plains.

Bonogofsky said that returned ground is where restoration is achievable. In Canada and Mexico, by contrast, more than 80% of the footprint is still in production.

“We spend a lot of time talking about what we’re losing, but this is a reminder of the tremendous opportunity to restore grasslands alongside protecting the native prairie that remains,” she said.

Grass that returns isn’t the same as grass that was never broken, though. Studies of abandoned farmland have found old fields stay altered for centuries.

Grasslands cover nearly a third of the lower 48 states, according to WWF, and the group says nearly half of Americans know little or nothing about them. They are home to bison, pronghorn, black-footed ferrets, swift foxes, and migratory grassland birds, along with wild pollinators that crops depend on.

Native Nations manage more than 6% of the Great Plains, and the report calls for strengthening Tribal leadership in grassland stewardship, bison restoration included.

WWF and America’s Grasslands Coalition have launched a campaign under the line “We Are Grasslanders” to change how little people know.

Asked what an ordinary person can do about grassland loss, Bonogofsky’s first answer was policy. “Much of the answer is policy, so one of the most important things people can do is make their voices heard,” she said.

She named three measures WWF is pushing for. One is expanding Sodsaver, which trims crop insurance subsidies on newly plowed grassland and now applies in only six Great Plains states. The others are reforming crop insurance incentives and strengthening the Conservation Reserve Program, which pays farmers to put environmentally sensitive cropland into grass or other cover.

For landowners, she had a more direct answer, naming the Conservation Reserve Program and a second one, the Environmental Quality Incentives Program, or EQIP. “And if you own or manage land, programs like CRP, Grassland CRP, and EQIP can help you keep grass in grass or restore marginal cropland,” she said.

The report counts acres, not condition. An acre inside the footprint can be a cornfield or a restored pasture, and the analysis doesn’t yet grade how intact any of it is. WWF said it is working with the Conservation Biology Institute on a measure of grassland intactness, due in an interim update.

The three study areas aren’t alike, either. The U.S. Great Plains run from wet country to dry, while the Canadian area is more like the productive grass of the Northern Great Plains, so WWF cautions against a direct comparison.

And because the method changed, there is no trend line yet. Whether 2023’s loss was higher or lower than the years before it is a question this edition can’t answer.

For 2024, WWF estimates the United States converted another 425,000 acres and Mexico 178,000. Canada’s 2024 figure will arrive when its data catches up, and only then will the continental count have a second year.

#5745. Scientists Say a Simple Blood Test Could Reveal Depression

Scientists have discovered that aging immune cells may hold a hidden blood-based clue to detecting depression.

Scientists have discovered that the biological aging of certain white blood cells may provide an important clue to depression, potentially opening the door to a blood test that could help identify the condition.

The study found that measuring how quickly these immune cells age can help predict emotional and cognitive symptoms of depression, including hopelessness and loss of pleasure. Interestingly, the same biological markers were not associated with physical symptoms such as fatigue or changes in appetite.

Published in The Journals of Gerontology, Series A: Biological Sciences and Medical Sciences, the findings could bring scientists closer to developing an objective biological marker for depression, a mental health condition that affects nearly one in five US adults.

Doctors currently diagnose depression primarily by evaluating symptoms reported by patients. Although blood tests may help eliminate other possible medical explanations, there is still no established biological test that can reliably identify depression or detect its earliest signs.

Part of the challenge is that depression does not affect everyone in the same way. Some people develop physical (or somatic) symptoms, including exhaustion, reduced appetite, or agitation. Others experience changes in thinking and mood, such as persistent hopelessness or anhedonia, a condition characterized by an inability to experience pleasure or maintain interest in activities that were once enjoyable.

“Depression is not a one-size-fits-all disorder—it can look really different from person to person, which is why it’s so important to consider varied presentations and not just a clinical label,” said study author Nicole Beaulieu Perez, assistant professor at NYU Rory Meyers College of Nursing. “Our study reveals unique biological underpinnings of mental health that are often obscured by broad diagnostic categories.”

Depression occurs more frequently among people living with certain conditions that affect the immune system, including HIV. Several overlapping factors may contribute to this increased risk, such as ongoing inflammation, social stigma, and socioeconomic challenges.

Women living with HIV face particularly high rates of depression. When depression goes untreated or unrecognized, it can interfere with their ability to participate in medical care and consistently take antiretroviral medications, which are essential for managing HIV.

“For women with HIV who may be experiencing depression, we want to better understand what’s going on and catch it earlier so that it doesn’t harm their whole overall health,” said Perez.

To investigate possible biological connections between depression and HIV, the research team focused on accelerated biological aging. Previous studies have linked this process to both conditions.

Unlike chronological age, which simply counts the years since a person was born, biological age reflects changes occurring within the body over time. Researchers can estimate biological age using tools known as “epigenetic clocks,” which analyze chemical changes to DNA. These modifications can influence how genes function without altering the underlying genetic code.

The researchers examined information from 440 participants in the Women’s Interagency HIV Study, including 261 women living with HIV and 179 women without the infection.

To evaluate depression, they used the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), a standardized 20-item questionnaire designed to assess both physical and nonphysical symptoms.

Blood samples were then analyzed using two different epigenetic clocks. One estimated biological aging across multiple types of cells and tissues, while the other focused specifically on monocytes, a type of white blood cell involved in the body’s immune defenses.

Monocytes help the immune system respond to infections and other threats. They are especially important in HIV infection, and previous research has found elevated monocyte levels in people experiencing depression.

The results revealed a notable difference between the two aging measures.

Biological aging in monocytes was associated with nonphysical depression symptoms, particularly anhedonia, hopelessness, and feelings of failure. This relationship appeared in women both with and without HIV.

However, the same aging measure was not associated with physical symptoms of depression.

“This is particularly interesting because people with HIV often have physical symptoms like fatigue that are attributed to their chronic illness rather than a depression diagnosis. But this flips that on its head because we found that these measures are associated with mood and cognitive symptoms, not somatic symptoms,” said Perez.

The broader epigenetic clock, which measured aging across multiple cell and tissue types, showed no association with depression symptoms.

These differences suggest that examining biological aging in specific immune cells may reveal connections to mental health that are missed when researchers measure aging more generally.

Although the findings are promising, Perez emphasized that additional research is necessary before measurements of immune cell aging can become part of routine depression diagnosis or treatment.

The study identifies a potential biomarker, but it does not establish a clinically validated blood test for depression. Researchers will need to investigate the relationship further and determine how reliably these biological signals can identify depression across different populations.

Eventually, such a test could complement traditional mental health assessments, potentially helping clinicians recognize depression sooner and tailor treatment to individual patients.

One possible application would be using a person’s biological profile to help predict which antidepressant medication is most likely to be effective. This could reduce some of the uncertainty involved in finding appropriate treatment.

“I think about the adage, ‘What gets measured gets managed.’ An aspirational goal in mental health would be to combine subjective experience with objective biological testing,” said Perez. “Our findings bring us a step closer to this goal of precision mental health care, especially for high-risk populations, by providing a biological framework that could guide future diagnosis and treatment.”

We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday.

This tension had been playing out in miniature on a website Bloom runs that tracks progress on more than 1,000 problems originally posed and collected by Paul Erdős, one of the 20th century’s most prolific mathematicians. Here, people used to collaborate to discuss open problems, or ones for which the best solution either hasn’t been found or isn’t known to the greater math community. But increasingly, visitors were competing to close open problems with AI proofs that they often didn’t understand. Bloom, a mathematician at the University of Manchester in England, doesn’t want to spend his days refereeing AI-generated work. So, soon after we spoke, and before he knew that OpenAI would be announcing its latest results, Bloom posted that he’d no longer tag problems as open or solved, and he also closed comments.

It’s definitely exciting that AI can tackle so many open problems so quickly. But can it help mathematicians understand the answers and build on them?

Just two days after OpenAI’s deluge of results, the peer-reviewed edition of a Google DeepMind study became public. First reported in a preprint earlier this year, it offers an inside look at how one AI system tackles open math problems. The system called AlphaProof Nexus solved 9 Erdős problems and made a number of other interesting mathematical advances, researchers report October 8 in Science.

The report includes a detailed description of how the results were obtained. Plus, human mathematicians vetted many of the proofs. That’s “quite different” from how OpenAI and Anthropic are handling their math results, Bloom says.

“The ultimate objective of any AI for math research,” says research scientist and study co-author Swarat Chaudhuri of Google DeepMind in London, should be “to advance mathematics as done by human mathematicians.”

In the recently published study Chaudhuri and his colleagues tested different configurations of their system. To tackle a collection of 353 open Erdős problems, they used multiple agents powered by the Gemini language model. These could call on AlphaProof, an AI model specially trained to do math, and the inspiration for the name of the overall system, for difficult steps. The agents also relied on Lean, math software that identifies steps that remain unproven, to check their logic. The agents shared their work, circulating promising proof attempts and discarding ones that weren’t going well.

The team also tested a simpler setup on the nine problems the full system solved. Independent Gemini agents worked through proofs using Lean’s feedback, without sharing attempts or consulting AlphaProof. That setup solved all nine, too. It was cheaper—using fewer computing resources—than the full system on easy problems and more expensive on the two toughest ones. The team did not establish whether the simpler setup would solve any of the problems the full system had missed.

The fact that the simpler setup could solve the same nine problems as the full system surprised Chaudhuri. It suggests that increasingly capable language models can tackle research mathematics with relatively simple checking tools.

But computer-checking comes with a caveat. Lean checks only the statement it’s given, as it is written in its code. And a mathematical statement may be incorrectly translated to or from Lean code. An October 6 preprint shows why this translation matters. In the paper, the researchers pinpoint mismatches between parts of OpenAI’s written Navier–Stokes proof and its Lean counterpart. The mismatches do not mean the written proof is wrong, the team writes, but they note that a valid Lean proof does not automatically validate an AI-generated math paper.

That’s why the vetting human mathematicians did for the DeepMind publication is so important. “The human mathematician has to really think through the problem and set goals for the system,” Chaudhuri says. And even if the system failed to finish a proof, its checked steps helped mathematicians focus on the remaining gaps. “Math is ultimately a human art,” Chaudhuri says. “The system enhances the human user’s insights, and the human user is able to guide the system with new ideas.”

The mathematical results that the AlphaProof Nexus system achieved are “not really at the top level of importance,” says Bloom, who wasn’t involved with the work. But that underscores the team’s more measured approach. Google DeepMind, he says, is “trying to be more responsible with the science,” while companies like OpenAI are using math to showcase their software. OpenAI’s papers and accompanying Lean proofs were entirely AI-generated, leaving it up to the math community to work out what’s useful or useless.

OpenAI says they worked with an independent advisory group to develop best practices on how to share their AI math results. But that group has said their advice was to not test unreleased AI models on advanced math, guidance the company clearly ignored.

The company’s fast-moving, competitive approach continues to alienate mathematicians such as Bryna Kra of Northwestern University in Evanston, Ill. She says the company made some improvements in their efforts to cite past literature. And she’s excited about AI’s potential to solve important problems in math. But there is widespread concern, she says, that AI models have accessed people’s unpublished work. “We had a healthy, collaborative, sharing culture that is being destroyed,” she says. “Now, people are wary of sharing their ideas.”

Not all mathematicians feel this way about AI. The technology is “making math more accessible,” says Andrew Turner, who wonders why a small group of mathematicians should get to decide how we solve problems. Turner, who studied math and physics and is Now CTO of the company Plasma AI, based in New York City thinks “math is becoming engineering.” And doing math with machines will allow people to “answer questions previously unfathomable.”

A few days after Bloom shut down the competition on his website, Plasma AI announced a new website to track progress on Erdős problems. They will welcome AI results.

Bloom doesn’t see math as a Silicon Valley–esque move-fast-and-break-things competition. I agree, and hope that curious people will continue to explore problems they care about, with AI helping rather than taking over. At the end of his post announcing the changes to his website, Bloom wrote something that makes me tear up every time I read it. #5741. A Magnetar Can Be Born When Two Neutron Stars Touch

All neutron stars have intense magnetic fields. The typical magnetic field of a neutron star is about 100,000 times stronger than the strongest magnetic fields we can create on Earth. But about 1% of neutron stars have even stronger magnetic fields. Known as magnetars, their magnetic fields can be 1,000 times stronger than a regular magnetic field. They are a likely cause for powerful transient events such as soft gamma-ray repeaters and fast radio bursts. A new study looks at how magnetars might form.

The work focuses on the mergers of binary neutron stars. Often when two neutron stars collide they form a black hole. This is because the combined mass of the two neutron stars is greater than the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff (TOV) limit, which is around 2.6 solar masses. Most neutron stars are more massive than white dwarfs, which have a Chandrasekhar limit of about 1.4 solar masses. But some neutron stars have smaller masses. If they happen to have a total mass less than the TOV limit, then they can merge and become a single neutron star.

Using high-resolution simulations run on the Japanese supercomputer FUGAKU, the team looked at the merger of low-mass neutron star binaries. To simplify things a bit, they assumed stars with identical masses, but otherwise just typical neutron stars. They assumed the magnetic field strengths of these stars were around 3 trillion Gauss, which is in the upper range of typical neutron stars. Their simulations ran from just moments before the stars collide until just after.

What they found was that within milliseconds after the two stars make contact, the neutron fluid within the region enters a KelvinâHelmholtz instability. This is a physics way of saying the fluid becomes turbulent. You've likely seen such an instability before. If you gently blow out a candle, there is a smooth stream of smoke that rises off the wick. But as it rises, the stream becomes swirly and random. That transition is where it reaches the instability.

The thing about KelvinâHelmholtz instability is that it can mix things up tremendously. In the simulations, this mixes the neutron material so fully and quickly that the magnetic field is amplified significantly. This mixing trickles through the rest of the material, dragging the magnetic field with it. As a result, the resulting magnetic field of the merged neutron star can be more than 300 times stronger than the initial magnetic fields. It's also possible that further turbulence would strengthen the field even more, though this is beyond the limits of the simulation.

What's surprising about the simulation is just how quickly the magnetic field is amplified. It ripples through the stars within about a millisecond, meaning that it could play a role in how the merger progresses and could impact the structure of the resulting neutron star. More study will be needed to determine just how it plays out. But it is clear that magnetars can form through mergers like this.

#5740. 0 to 15 MPH in a Single Swipe: New Research Reveals How Ancient Giant Sea Monsters Ambushed Their Prey

Giant marine reptiles that dominated prehistoric oceans may have relied on surprisingly different hunting strategies.

A giant marine reptile that lived during the age of dinosaurs may have reached speeds of roughly 15 miles per hour with a single powerful sweep of its tail. That estimate, calculated for an exceptionally large Tylosaurus, describes a brief burst of acceleration rather than sustained swimming. New research suggests that differences in tail anatomy gave some mosasaurs an advantage in sudden attacks, while others were better equipped to pursue prey across open water.

These formidable ocean predators were mosasaurs, a group of marine lizards that thrived during the final part of the dinosaur era. “At the same time dinosaurs like T. rex were ruling the land, mosasaurs were ruling the seas,” said Kiersten Formoso, an assistant professor at Rutgers University who led the study, published in Current Biology.

To investigate how these ancient predators moved, researchers reconstructed four mosasaur species from their fossil skeletons and used physics to calculate their potential acceleration. Rather than examining steady swimming, the team focused on a sudden movement called a “slam-start.” During this maneuver, an animal bends its tail to one side before forcefully sweeping it through the water, propelling its body forward.

“It’s the tail itself pushing off the water,” Formoso explained, comparing the motion to a swimmer launching from the wall of a pool. Because muscles rarely survive fossilization, the researchers estimated the missing tail musculature using anatomical evidence from living lizards, including Komodo dragons. They then tested different assumptions about muscle strength, tail flexibility, and water resistance to evaluate how these factors affected acceleration.

The simulations consistently showed that Platecarpus and Tylosaurus could accelerate more rapidly for their body size than Mosasaurus and Plotosaurus. Of the four species studied, Platecarpus achieved the fastest modeled lunges. Its advantage came primarily from a relatively long, flexible portion of the tail that could bend farther before delivering a powerful stroke. The extent of this bending influenced acceleration more strongly than any other variable the researchers examined.

These differences suggest that Platecarpus and Tylosaurus were particularly well suited to ambushing prey in shallow seas. Mosasaurus and especially Plotosaurus, by contrast, appear to have been better adapted for sustained pursuit in open water. “That doesn’t mean Plotosaurus was slow,” Formoso said. “Its tail was built for sustained, tuna-like swimming rather than sudden bursts.”

The proposed hunting strategies are consistent with other fossil evidence, including tooth wear, estimates of bite force, and chemical analyses that provide clues about mosasaur ecology. The researchers also investigated exceptionally large individuals, including a Mosasaurus reconstructed from a New Jersey fossil preserved at the New Jersey State Museum in Trenton. Although the precise swimming speeds remain uncertain, the results provide what the researchers describe as the first numerical estimates of burst swimming performance for marine reptiles from the dinosaur era.

Formoso, who also advised the documentary series Prehistoric Planet on mosasaur movement, studies how animals descended from land-dwelling ancestors adapted to aquatic environments. Her team is making its modeling tools publicly available so other scientists can investigate the swimming abilities of extinct animals, including species without close living counterparts. The approach relies on physical principles that have remained unchanged for millions of years. “Physics is physics,” she said.

#5739. How Long to Cook a Turkey by Weight

Several factors contribute to a perfect Thanksgiving, but the holiday hinges on one question in particular: How long will it take to cook that turkey? Notoriously prone to overcooking, turkey meat has been ridiculed for its perceived dryness time and again. On top of the meat’s naturally lean characteristics, these very large birds have sections that vary widely in shape and thickness, making it tough (pun intended) to achieve a juicy interior and crispy, golden-brown skin. Sadly, not even the richest turkey gravy can save a bird that isn’t cooked properly.

So, how long does it take to cook a turkey? For a whole, unstuffed turkey, plan on roughly 13 minutes per pound at 350°F. That works out to about 3 hours for a 12- to 14-pound bird, though the precise roasting time depends on its size, your oven temperature, and how you prepare it.

But that doesn’t mean it has to take up all of your prep time. Armed with the turkey-cooking time chart below and a few essential roasting tips, you’ll have ample time to focus on arguably everyone’s favorite part of the Thanksgiving dinner spread (the side dishes) without stressing too much about the main event.

The simplest approach is to roast a whole, unstuffed turkey, breast-side up, at 350°F. Ideal for first-time turkey cooks, this method requires relatively little attention and results in an excellent bird. As a general rule, allow about 13 minutes per pound, but keep in mind that cooking times are only estimates.

These times assume your turkey is fully thawed and unstuffed. Start checking for doneness about three-quarters of the way through the estimated roasting time, then check more frequently as the turkey approaches its target temperature. Remember, the thermometer—not the clock—gets the final say.

One more thing: Stuffed turkeys take much longer to cook through, making the breast meat prone to drying out while you wait for the center of the stuffing to reach a safe temperature. We prefer stuffing recipes (a.k.a. dressing recipes) that bake separately.

A perfectly browned bird isn’t necessarily a fully cooked one. The most reliable way to check for doneness is with an instant-read thermometer, inserted into the thickest part of the breast and the innermost portions of the thigh, without touching bone.

The USDA recommends cooking turkey to an internal temperature of 165°F in all three spots. Food director Chris Morocco recommends cooking dark meat to an even higher final temperature—around 175°–190°F—so its connective tissue has more time to break down. That’s part of what makes cooking a whole turkey tricky: The breast and legs don’t reach their ideal temperatures at the same time.

You’ll also want to account for carryover cooking. A turkey’s internal temperature keeps rising after it comes out of the oven because of residual heat. Some of our favorite roast turkey recipes use that residual heat to finish cooking the meat, but the temperature increase varies with the size of the bird and the roasting method; don’t assume it will rise by a specific number of degrees.

The Bon Appétit Test Kitchen recommends removing your turkey from the oven when the breast has reached an internal temperature of 150°F. Check the temperature periodically while the bird rests to ensure it reaches 165°F.

Before you start carving, give your turkey a rest. For a whole bird, 30–60 minutes allows the juices to redistribute, helping keep the meat moist when you cut into it.

Set the turkey on a cutting board with a trough or a platter to collect any runoff juices (save them for gravy). You don’t need to tent it with aluminum foil: the bird’s insulation will keep it warm surprisingly long. Plus, trapping steam beneath the foil can cause crispy skin to go limp.

Whether you roast your turkey whole, spatchcocked, or cut into parts, take these preliminary steps. They won’t necessarily shorten the cooking time, but they’ll help you get that succulent-on-the-inside, crackly-on-the-outside result.

Unless you look forward to eating leftover turkey for days, shop for a bird that’s just big enough to feed your Thanksgiving crew. A smaller turkey will cut roasting time, leaving the oven free for sides and pies.

A frozen turkey needs time to thaw—and not just a few hours on the counter. Plan on about 24 hours in the refrigerator for every 4–5 pounds of turkey. That means a 12- to 14-pound bird will need roughly three to four days to thaw completely.

A partially frozen turkey can seriously throw off your roasting time, and without thawing, you may not be able to remove the giblets. If Thanksgiving is approaching faster than your bird is defrosting, there are other safe ways to speed things along.

Dry brining is key to moist, succulent meat. Whether you choose plain kosher salt, a salt-and-pepper mix, or a custom spice blend, salt draws moisture to the turkey’s surface. The salty juices are then reabsorbed into the meat, carrying the seasoning with them. This helps the turkey retain moisture as it cooks, tenderizes the meat, and encourages skin browning—all hallmarks of a properly cooked bird.

What about a wet brine, you ask? We’re firmly in the dry-brine camp. It’s easier, less messy, and doesn’t require finding space in an already crowded refrigerator for a giant container of salt water. (Fridge space is at a premium leading up to Thanksgiving.)

Give your dry-brined turkey at least 12 hours and up to three days in the refrigerator, uncovered, before roasting. That might sound lengthy, but the salt needs time to work its way through the turkey’s big muscles. If you’re running behind, you can start dry-brining before the bird has completely thawed. Once the exterior is pliable enough to season, apply the brine and return the turkey to the refrigerator to finish thawing.

Invest in an oven thermometer before the big day arrives. Oven temperatures can vary from what the dial shows, and even a small discrepancy can make a difference when you’re roasting something for several hours.

Once the turkey is in the oven, resist the urge to keep checking on it. Opening the door lets heat escape, which can lengthen cooking time. A thermometer and a little patience will serve you better than peeking at the bird repeatedly.

This method comes from Andy Baraghani’s dry-brined roast turkey recipe (a.k.a. the no-fail turkey method we come back to every Thanksgiving). Andy had two main goals for his perfect turkey: brown the skin so it’s crackly and delicious, and roast the meat so it’s tender and moist.

The secret to accomplishing both? Two oven temperatures. Andy starts his turkey at relatively high heat—425°–450°F for 30–45 minutes—to encourage browning, then reduces the temperature to 300°–350°F for another 65–85 minutes, following the recipe’s internal-temperature guidance. As a bonus, he brushes the outside of the bird with a tangy honey glaze to build a glossy, bronzed shellac.

This two-temperature method can get a 12- to 16-pound turkey out of the oven in around two hours, considerably faster than the standard approach. You’ll have to pay closer attention, though: Keep an eye on the skin, and follow the recipe’s instructions for adjusting the heat if it starts to get too dark.

For the full technique, including the dry brine and glaze, follow Andy’s recipe.

Roasting a turkey in parts is another way to address uneven cooking. With the pieces arranged separately, each one gets optimal exposure to the oven’s heat, giving you more control over when the breast and dark meat are done. And yes, even turkey pieces benefit from a dry brine, so the preparation tips above still apply.

You can break down the raw turkey yourself if you feel confident (and have a sharp enough knife), but there’s no shame in asking your butcher to handle the job. Request five pieces: two legs, two wings, and one whole turkey breast, with the backbone removed. Save that backbone for turkey stock.

Instead of a typical roasting pan, this method uses a wire rack set inside a rimmed baking sheet. The setup lets hot air circulate around the meat, giving the skin its best chance to crisp while catching the umami-packed drippings.

In another favorite Thanksgiving turkey recipe, the dry-brined pieces roast at 425°F for 20–25 minutes, then 300°F for another 50–70 minutes. Cooking in parts lets you can monitor each piece individually, so you can pull the breast when it reaches the right temperature and give the legs more time if needed. Follow the recipe’s instructions for assessing doneness, keeping in mind the USDA’s 165°F minimum temperature recommendation.

The only downside to roasting a turkey in pieces? You’ll lose some of the drama that comes with ushering a whole roasted bird to the table—but who needs “oohs and ahhs” when a chorus of “mmms” awaits?

#5736. Mysterious changes in barn owls reveal evolution in action

Genomes from over 3,000 Swiss barn owls show natural selection pushed the birds toward redder, more heavily spotted plumage within 30 years.

Thirty years is enough time for a child to grow up and raise a family. On farms in western Switzerland, it has also been enough time for generations of barn owls to start looking different.

The birds have gradually become redder and more heavily spotted.

Someone watching an owl cross a field would probably never notice such a slow change, but decades of records have made the pattern clear.

Their DNA now suggests that natural selection helped drive part of it.

The discovery brings evolution into a familiar setting, among birds hunting and raising chicks on farmland, and leaves scientists wondering what gives one inherited trait an edge.

Barn owls come in more shades than their familiar pale appearance might suggest.

In central Europe, their chests and bellies range from white to deep reddish brown, with black spots of different sizes.

Those colors can go along with differences in how the birds make a living. Earlier research has linked feather color to hunting success and the number of young an owl produces.

In one study, the reddest owls hunted less successfully on moonlit nights and brought less food back to their chicks. The whitest owls showed no such drop.

That makes the new finding all the more curious.

A gene version associated with red feathers has been spreading even though reddish coloring does not seem to help an owl in every situation.

Researchers have caught and measured owls in this part of Switzerland since 1986.

Each time they handled a bird, they compared its breast and belly feathers with a chart of eight shades, then counted and measured its spots.

A color chart and a spot count might seem modest tools for studying evolution.

Repeated across decades, though, those measurements revealed a pattern that would be difficult to recognize in any one bird.

Owls born in later years tended to look redder and spottier than earlier generations.

The trend appeared in both sexes and remained when researchers excluded the earliest years, when they had measured fewer birds.

Tristan Cumer, a research fellow at the University of Lausanne, led the new study in Professor Jérôme Goudet’s group.

The team analyzed DNA from 3,102 wild owls to investigate what lay behind their changing appearance.

Much of the difference between a pale owl and a reddish one came down to inherited DNA.

Genetic differences accounted for about 80% of the variation in feather color, and a similar share of the variation in spotting.

A gene called MC1R showed the strongest connection to reddish feathers. It helps control the kind of melanin pigment a cell produces.

The researchers then followed one version of this gene through the breeding population.

During the first four years of records, this red-associated version made up about 5% of MC1R copies, and its share rose over the following decades.

That suggested the birds carrying it might have an advantage.

When an inherited trait helps birds survive and leave more descendants, those descendants can gradually spread it through the population.

A useful trait is not the only thing that can spread.

Some owls leave more descendants simply through luck, which can make their gene versions more common without those versions providing any special benefit.

Scientists call that process genetic drift. The team tested it with 1,000 computer simulations, and very few produced an increase in the red version as large as the one the researchers recorded.

“A genetic variant associated with reddish-brown plumage became far more common than chance alone could explain. Only simulations that included natural selection reproduced the changes we observed,” Cumer said.

Owls arriving from elsewhere seemed unlikely to explain the increase, either. Locally born birds and birds first caught as adults carried the red version at similar rates over the years.

For an owl, a change in the food supply could make a familiar hunting strategy more or less rewarding.

Study co-author Alexandre Roulin, whose group has followed these birds for decades, thinks their prey could help explain the red gene version’s apparent advantage.

“A change in prey availability may have favored reddish-brown barn owls, which eat more voles, whereas white owls tend to feed more on wood mice,” Roulin said.

“The increase in spotting could be linked to greater environmental stress.”

In other words, a change in the menu might favor birds that already eat more of a particular prey animal.

The researchers still need evidence that this happened in the Swiss population, and they have yet to establish whether stress explains the extra spots.

The spots added a twist of their own. Although the owls became spottier, one DNA version associated with heavier spotting actually became less common.

Its decline stayed within the range that chance could produce in the simulations.

The researchers found little evidence that natural selection had driven the change in that particular version.

The known spotting genes also left some of the inherited differences unexplained.

Other genes, changes in the environment, or both may help account for the birds’ increasingly spotted appearance.

The evidence for the two changes is therefore uneven.

Evidence that selection favored a gene linked to red feathers does not automatically explain why the same birds grew more spots.

Even the red feathers might be only an outward sign of something else.

A gene can belong to a biological system with several jobs, so an advantage may come from a trait that nobody can see by examining an owl’s chest.

“We also do not know whether plumage color is itself the trait directly favored by natural selection,” Goudet said.

MC1R belongs to a system that researchers have also linked to stress responses and aggression.

Selection could favor a related trait, with redder feathers becoming more common as a consequence.

The researchers now want to use photographs to measure feather colors and spots more precisely.

They also plan to use DNA to estimate the plumage of owls that died very young.

Comparing those birds with survivors could help reveal whether certain feather types come with better chances of survival.

That would bring the team closer to understanding what actually favors the gene version linked to red feathers.

A person could have watched these fields for years without noticing that their owls were changing.

Decades of checking feathers have allowed researchers to follow an evolutionary change that an occasional glimpse of a bird would never reveal.

The study is published in the journal Nature Communications.

#5732. Brain implants are helping people who’ve lost their voice

Every print subscription comes with full digital access

The technology could get those with ALS, Parkinson’s disease and brain injuries “talking” again

This is a human-written story voiced by AI. Got feedback? Take our survey . (See our AI policy here .)

On paper, my interview with Casey Harrell looks like any other.

I ask the questions, he answers them. He tells me about his background as a climate activist, his Southern roots and his daughter, who as a preschooler loved to dress up like a cheetah and roar around the house. “My daughter was incredibly cute in her cheetah outfit,” Harrell says.

The transcript tells the story of our conversation. But between the lines is an entirely different story — the story of where Harrell’s words come from.

Harrell started losing his ability to speak in his early 40s, nearly six years ago. He talked to me using a brain implant that can decode his words directly from his thoughts. The device, called a brain-computer interface, is experimental. But it has transformed Harrell’s life. And it could soon transform others. Advances in computing power and AI, lessons gleaned from patient experience and investments from neurotechnology companies are starting to yield real progress on brain-computer interfaces for people who can no longer talk. In other words, Harrell’s new voice might not be so singular in the future.

“There’s a lot of excitement and hope in the field right now,” says Matthew Willsey, a neuro­surgeon and biomedical engineer at the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor, who recently implanted a new type of speech interface in a woman’s brain. For years, Willsey says, scientists didn’t know whether attempts to decode speech would be successful; now, a body of research results points clearly to yes. “They’re thinking, ‘Wow, this is actually a usable technology that addresses a real problem that people suffer from.’”

Harrell has amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or ALS. The progressive neurodegenerative disease, which affects an estimated 35,000 people in the United States, kills nerve cells in the brain and spinal cord that control muscles. As the neural connections between Harrell’s brain and body began to falter, he gradually lost his ability to move his muscles, leaving him confined to his bed or a wheelchair. Some of those dropped signals directed the muscles in his throat and mouth, the ones that allow him to speak.

We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday.

“I began to lose my ability to talk shortly after my diagnosis in 2020,” Harrell says. After that, it was “a slow-motion car crash” as fewer and fewer people could understand him. “[My voice] is the most important thing I have lost in this whole disease,” he says.

In a stroke of luck, Harrell learned through a family member about a clinical trial happening at the University of California, Davis — one that attempted to restore speech with a brain-computer interface. In 2023, Harrell had four small devices implanted on the left side of his brain. These grids of electrodes reside on Harrell’s speech motor cortex, a stretch of brain along the precentral gyrus that controls the movements required for speech.

Today, Harrell uses his system for about 10 to 14 hours daily, making him, as he puts it, “the world’s biggest power user of the brain-to-computer interface known to man.” Hard data suggest he’s right. A recent count shows that Harrell has used his system to communicate nearly 2 million words, the scientists working with him reported in June in Nature Medicine.

“I began to lose my ability to talk shortly after my diagnosis in 2020 … [My voice] is the most important thing I have lost in this whole disease.”

The very existence of Harrell’s words shows it may be possible to restore speech for people with ALS, as well as for those whose speech is impaired by stroke, paralysis, cerebral palsy and Parkinson’s disease. “This kind of technology has the potential to dramatically change the conversation,” says David Brandman, a UC Davis neurosurgeon and neuroscientist who works with Harrell.

Harrell’s interface still has room for improvement. It’s bulky, relatively slow and limited in what sorts of nonword sounds it can decode.

But it is a “beginning,” says Leigh Hochberg, a neuroscientist at Brown University who works with Harrell. “It’s still early days. There’s a lot of research to happen, and it’s going to be very exciting.” The end game, Hochberg says, is a world in which people could use a device to communicate seamlessly, quickly, clearly — and in their former voices.

Success would mean restoring more than just people’s words, Brandman says. It would mean restoring their ability to connect in all the ineffable ways that make us human. “Speech is so fundamental to who we are,” he says. “Imagine living in a world where you couldn’t tell someone you’re in pain, or you couldn’t say that you love them … It’s not hard to imagine how devastating that would be for your quality of life.”

On the path to a technology that enables fast, natural speech, scientists first had to answer a more basic question: Could the brain’s electrical chatter be decoded and translated into action outside of the head?

Nerve cells speak to each other with tiny pulses of electricity, signals that can be picked up with electrodes. The existence of this electrical language raised the tantalizing idea that electrical messages could be decoded straight from where they originate in the brain, bypassing damaged links to the rest of the body.

“This has really started as early as the 1950s, the first time scientists put electrodes into brains to record neural activity,” says Sergey Stavisky, a neuroscientist and neural engineer at UC Davis. “You can record the electrical activity of brain cells or parts of the brain. And that’s really when modern neuroscience started trying to study, how does the brain compute? How does it allow us to move our muscles?”

Figuring out how to decode simple neural commands was the first step. Animal studies paved the way, including experiments in which rhesus monkeys consciously controlled the movements of a robotic arm using only brain signals detected by implanted electrodes.

What if you lost your voice? Today, AI can re-create it, and brain implants can turn thoughts into speech. This season of The Deep End follows people living through these vocal transformations — and asks what these remarkable technologies can teach us about why our voices matter in the first place.

By 2004, scientists had enough confidence in the approach to launch a clinical trial in humans called BrainGate. Researchers implanted a grid of electrodes in the brain of a paralyzed man named Matthew Nagle. A former star athlete, Nagle was able to control a computer cursor with his thoughts alone. “That was really the starting gun of the modern version of the brain-computer interface field,” Stavisky says.

From there, attempts to link brain activity with some physical outcome — such as moving a computer mouse, a wheelchair or a robotic arm — blossomed. Some of these efforts focused on the brain signals and regions that controlled movements of the hands and arms. Others focused on a very tricky version of the same basic problem: talking.

A brain-computer interface that could detect the intention to say a word and bypass the muscles needed to say it would be a powerful workaround for people with diseases like ALS or brain injuries. And it is no small thing to people who can no longer talk. “Losing their speech is way more devastating than losing their mobility,” says Jessica Pietrowski, a speech and language pathologist at UC Davis who has worked with many such patients. “Speech is how they maintain their identity in their relationships.”

Harrell emphasizes this point. “What does my voice mean to me? It means so much,” he says. “It is the ability to parent my daughter. It is the ability to connect with my wife. It is the ability to make a wry joke with my friends.”

Talking feels effortless to most of us, but just saying hello involves a sweeping array of actions — and upwards of 100 muscles. “It’s incredibly complex and intricate, and really, an incredible human feat,” Pietrowski says.

Consider the “h” sound in “hello.” Known as a glottal fricative, it involves an orchestra of tiny movements. First, the diaphragm contracts to fill the lungs with air. Chest and abdominal muscles push air up through relaxed vocal cords and surrounding tissues. This puff of air then travels to the mouth, where lips, jaw and tongue execute complex sequences of movements that create the rest of the sound. Finally, air waves, perfectly shaped, ripple from the mouth. Then, and only then, “you have speech,” Pietrowski says.

But the beginnings of this nearly miraculous transformation start in the brain, where tiny electrical nerve pulses command the body to speak. In Harrell’s trial, he would attempt to speak words, prompting his motor cortex to send signals to his body. The implanted electrodes would detect the patterns and send them to a computer to interpret. With this work-around, it didn’t matter that Harrell’s muscles didn’t get the message; the computer would.

After Harrell recovered from surgery, researchers began testing the system in his home. They collected his neural data and streamed it to computers on a nearby cart, where machine learning algorithms attempted to match Harrell’s brain activity patterns to those that predictably occur with certain sounds. Called phonemes, these sound units are the building blocks of words. The algorithm assembled the phonemes into words, as it made its best guess about what Harrell was trying to say. To limit the computational power required, the algorithm used a vocabulary of just 50 words.

When the system was plugged in and ready to go, scientists asked Harrell to try and say a specific, random sentence built from that library: “What good is that?”

Harrell got to work. His eyebrows shot up as he saw the words appear on a screen in front of him in response to his thoughts. The decoder worked.

Everyone paused. Some of the researchers started to cry. Harrell and his family became emotional. Being in the room when the switch was flipped on was “incredible,” Brandman says. “The majority of my adult life was working up to that moment, and to see it come to fruition was an incredible experience. But more than that was seeing what impact it immediately had for Casey and his family.”

“Imagine living in a world where you couldn’t tell someone you’re in pain, or you couldn’t say that you love them … It’s not hard to imagine how devastating that would be for your quality of life.”

Harrell remembers his feelings that day, too: “Pure joy.” Even though he wasn’t choosing his words for that first test, “it was a profound moment,” he says. “The possibilities for the near future for me and the rest of humanity were overwhelming to realize.”

The second day of testing was even more profound, but in a personal way. This time, the system’s algorithm was using a vocabulary of over 125,000 words. This would be more challenging for the decoder, but it would give Harrell more autonomy to say what he wanted.

Harrell started the session with a joke, Hochberg recalls, along the lines of, “Is this thing on?” But his next line was a real puzzler: “I’m looking for a cheetah.”

Hochberg, who had joined remotely on a video call, was sure it was a mistake. “There’s no way that the second sentence on the second day of using a brain-to-computer interface is going to say anything about a cheetah,” Hochberg says. “That’s obviously a decoding error.”

But there was something the scientist couldn’t see. Harrell’s 4-year-old daughter had bounded into the room, dressed as a cheetah.

The discovery was big scientifically, but it was bigger for what it represented to Harrell. Brain technology could restore not just his words, but also the spontaneous exchanges that had once made up his everyday life.

“It was so amazing to be able to be understood right from the get-go and to be able to talk and respond to my daughter,” Harrell says, “especially because that was lacking completely from my life.”

Hochberg says moments like that represent the promise that brain-computer interfaces hold. “To me, seeing it work that quickly and that powerfully really, really emphasized that these are technologies that are going to be able to restore not only activities of daily living,” he says, “but they’re going to restore communication between parent and child.”

Casey Harrell’s brain-computer interface extracts neural signals from his brain and, through a few key steps, transforms them intowords. Electrodes implanted in Harrell’s speech motor cortex detect neurons’ electrical signals (1), generating raw neural data (2). Advanced machine learning efforts (3) map chunks of the data to phonemes, the building blocks of words (4). Those phonemes are then assembled into sentences by a predictive language model, or LM (5), and put on a screen where they can be read or spoken.

Harrell uses his interface to send email and Slack messages and play Wordle with his wife and child. He uses it to work; in fact, he’s just getting started on a new climate consultancy project. Harrell’s interface has connected him to the world in a way that would have been impossible even a few years ago.

But the system is still a research device. And like any early design, it comes with limitations. First, it isn’t portable; Harrell has wires coming from a port that sits on the top of his head, and he can use it only at home. And it is slow by the standards of typical conversation. You can’t tell from the written transcript of our interview, but our conversations always came with built-in delays.

For example, as we chatted, we discovered that we had both worked in the same part of Washington, D.C. I asked him if he remembered his old address. The answer came some two minutes later.

“I make a very simple analogy where I talk about having the brain-to-computer interface being akin to trying to catch a train, running to get the last car before it leaves the station,” Harrell says. With the current interface, “if people are patient and wait for my sentence correction, I can [catch the train and] be a part of the conversation.”

Harrell’s system translates his neural activity into words on a screen. If he chooses to do so, he can then use a computerized voice to say the words out loud. “It is brain-to-text, and then it gets translated to the voice,” Harrell says. A brain-to-voice system, in which a person’s thoughts are translated directly into spoken words, he adds, “is the holy grail of what this will become.”

Harrell and the scientists working with him are getting closer to this very type of real-time communication. Last year in Nature, they debuted a new algorithm that attempted to turn Harrell’s brain signals straight into sounds. The results: He could speak his mind with a delay of just 10 milliseconds. That’s very close to a real-time conversation. But the sounds Harrell made were hard to understand, with just over half of them being intelligible to listeners.

The next generation of devices aims to do better. Willsey, the University of Michigan neuro­surgeon, is working on one such device, a fully implantable array. In June, he implanted what’s called the “cortical module,” about the size of a dime, in the brain of a 68-year-old woman. Like Harrell, the woman could no longer talk in a way that most people could understand.

Unlike the BrainGate system, this new device — made by Austin, Texas–based neurotechnology company Paradromics — is portable. Surgeons implanted it in a stretch of brain tissue that made up the woman’s speech motor cortex, along the precentral gyrus. The cortical module collects neural data and sends it to another device implanted in the chest called a transceiver. From there, signals shoot to a receiver on her chest, where the data gets transformed into sounds — spoken words that play out of speakers on a nearby computer.

Paradromics’ priority is to test whether the implant is safe. If so, scientists can turn to the goal of restoring speech in real time. “The moment that you’re thinking the words, you don’t have to wait for the sentence to complete,” says Willsey, who, like Brandman, is a site investigator for the Paradromics clinical trial. “You can let the words kind of flow out as they would in a normal conversation.”

Scientists also want to decode more than words. Qualities of voice, such as pitch, are important for marking questions, says Vikash Gilja, a Paradromics neuroscientist. Speakers can also alter or pace their conversation to show sarcasm or concern. These sounds are all made by moving muscles, Gilja says, and can therefore be decoded from the brain.

“Our near-term goal right now is just to restore speech,” says Paradromics neurosurgeon Stephen Ryu. “But eventually, we’re hoping that we can actually restore someone’s ability to be fully involved in a conversation, to basically decode things like their timing, their emotions, their personality.”

Other researchers are trying to incorporate the gestures that many of us use to communicate.

“Some gestures can substitute for speech entirely, like nodding yes, shaking your head no, waving, shrugging, giving a thumbs up,” says bioengineer Samantha Brosler of the University of California, San Fransico. Gestures can also change the meaning of words; a “maybe” with a head nod is close to a yes, for instance. “I think of gestures and body movements as an additional communication channel that can work together with speech,” Brosler says.

In a new study, Brosler and colleagues enabled two people with paralysis who had lost their speaking ability to instead use their brain signals to control both the words and gestures of digital avatars. The advance, reported in September in Nature Neuroscience, adds to the repertoire of signals that could be decoded and turned into action.

And as all this is happening, scientists are creating smaller, more stable types of electrodes to embed in the brain; more powerful computational algorithms to handle larger amounts of data; and better models of how those signals relate to sounds, words and gestures, Gilja says. “We can see all the pieces coming together.”

“It was so amazing to be able to be understood right from the get-go and to be able to talk and respond to my daughter, especially because that was lacking completely from my life.”

There are many reasons to be optimistic about the future, says Brandman, who is an investigator for the clinical trial led by Paradromics. “I think I’m about five years away from being able to meet a patient with ALS and having the following conversation,” he says. “‘Ms. Smith, I’m sorry you’ve been diagnosed with ALS. I don’t have a cure for you, but I do have a device that I can implant that can help you communicate.’”

That conversation is tantalizing. But there are no guarantees that these systems will work as hoped, or that they will be widely available. Such implants would probably come with hefty price tags, invasive brain surgeries and perhaps more demand than the few capable clinicians and technologists could meet.

And there are underlying questions about the brain itself. One concern is that changes to the brain over time might render neural signals unreliable, especially as diseases progress. If that happens, a person’s interface might produce a scrambled message — or be left without enough neural data to decode.

“These are real challenges, and the field is working on them,” Stavisky says. “But they’re not solved yet.”

Despite these hurdles, the push for voice-restoring technology can’t come soon enough for people who have lost theirs. In September, researchers reported that the woman with the Paradromics implant had a message for them. “OK,” she used her brain-computer interface to tell researchers. “I have a lot to say.” With her system, she was able to call her grandchildren on the telephone.

And Harrell is now living a life that looks very different from the one he might have imagined six years ago. Although his disease keeps him close to home, he can now share his thoughts with the world — and his family — instead of locking them away inside.

“The ability to scream when you are running and want to get out some stress, the ability to say sweet nothings into your partner’s ear,” Harrell says. “These are the things that life is all about, and when you are robbed of those times, you want them back at all costs. And this is why this is good enough for now.”

But he too has a vision, a day when these devices are commonplace, “when I am one of many, instead of being one of one.”

#5730. Stanford Scientists Discover a Remarkable Way To Rescue Dying Corals in Just Seven Weeks

Bleached corals fully recovered within seven weeks after brief contact with healthy clones in laboratory tests, suggesting a possible way to speed reef restoration.

Warm water can leave corals alive but starving. When the animals expel the colorful algae living in their tissues, they lose the energy those partners supply for growth and turn ghostly white. Bleached corals become vulnerable to invasion by harmful algae, and even those that survive can take months or years to recover.

In a laboratory, researchers have now restored bleached corals on the brink of death through brief contact with healthy clones, the first time full recovery has been observed this way. The corals returned to health within seven weeks, the fastest documented recovery to date, according to the study published October 14 in Science Advances.

The approach takes advantage of how corals grow. Corals are animals, and in most species, individuals multiply by producing genetically identical copies. These clones grow together into vast colonies that build reefs.

“Genetically identical corals recognize one another when they come into contact,” said study coauthor Steve Palumbi, the Jane and Marshall Steel, Jr. Professor in Marine Sciences. “Their tissues fuse if they are from the same clone. We see something similar in humans. Organ transplants are often successful if the donor is your identical twin.”

To test whether contact could help a bleached coral recover, lead author Danny Collins, an oceans PhD student at the Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability, assembled 10 pairs of fragments. Each pair contained one healthy fragment and one bleached fragment from the same animal. He placed five pairs close enough to touch and left gaps of just a few millimeters between the fragments in the other five. The bleached fragments that touched their healthy partners fully recovered, while those separated by the small gaps died.

The researchers then tested whether the fragments needed to stay together throughout recovery. By separating pairs at different intervals, they found that one week of contact, and sometimes just five days, supplied enough beneficial algae for the bleached fragments to finish recovering on their own. Recovery after separation took seven weeks, matching the recovery time of pairs left in continuous contact.

The idea of bringing the fragments together originated with two corals that had tipped over in a tank. In 2025, Palumbi noticed that a healthy fragment and a bleached fragment from the same colony were touching in his lab. Color was returning to the bleached fragment where they met, signaling that beneficial algae were returning.

“While bleached corals may be dying, they are not dead yet,” said Collins, who is also a Knight-Hennessy Scholar. “The question we wanted to answer is, once a coral has bleached, what can we do?”

Around the time of Palumbi’s observation, Collins was studying the origins of coral disease, particularly the transmission of bacteria carried on plastic debris that settles onto corals.

“Harmful bacteria enter corals through physical contact,” said Collins. “We were curious about what would happen if we used contact to reintroduce something good – the beneficial algae from healthy corals.”

To investigate how that transfer worked, Collins and his colleagues examined the contacting tissues with a powerful microscope. Shortly after the fragments touched, microscopic channels in their tissues aligned, opening pathways through which beneficial algae and the nutrients they produce could flow. Within three days of contact, algae had moved from healthy fragments into bleached ones.

“Once beneficial algae enter bleached corals, they don’t just spread out,” said Palumbi, who is a professor of oceans in the Doerr School of Sustainability and of biology in the School of Humanities and Sciences. “They flood in and start dividing at the fastest rates seen in corals.”

During the second week of contact, the algae inside bleached fragments doubled every four days, roughly ten times faster than the rate observed in wild corals. The ability of fragments to keep recovering after separation led the team to conclude that algae multiplying inside the bleached coral, rather than a continuing supply from its healthy partner, primarily drove the rest of its recovery.

Translating the laboratory results into reef restoration will require a different way to deliver those algae. The tested approach depends on having a healthy, genetically identical copy of every bleached coral, a requirement the researchers caution is not feasible at the scale needed to restore reefs.

Climate change is driving more frequent and intense marine heat waves, and some estimates suggest corals could disappear within a few decades. Researchers have spent decades developing ways to protect them from bleaching, disease outbreaks, increasingly acidic waters, and other threats. Those efforts include selectively breeding heat-tolerant corals, growing corals in nurseries to restore degraded reefs, and transplanting coral microbiomes, the communities of microorganisms associated with corals.

“Bleached corals are often considered a lost cause because recovery after a heatwave is very slow, if it happens at all,” said Fiorenza Micheli, the David and Lucile Packard Professor in Marine Science and a faculty advisor of Collins. “We hope this new research will inspire solutions that keep corals healthy long enough to enable full recovery after heat waves through slower processes such as growth, reproduction, and recruitment of new colonies.”

Many questions remain about how to turn the findings into a practical treatment. In the coming months, the team plans to explore alternative ways of delivering beneficial algae that do not require continuous contact with healthy corals. One possibility is to collect algae from healthy corals long before heat waves arrive, then deliver them in large batches to bleached corals to accelerate recovery.

“These new insights into how corals can naturally recover give us hope for the future,” said Micheli, who also co-directs the Stanford Center for Ocean Solutions. “They open the door to what’s possible and may lead to the breakthrough we need to accelerate reef restoration.”

One of the best fall meteor showers peaks this week, when pieces of the famous Halley's Comet streak across Earth's atmosphere as fast-moving "shooting stars."

The Orionid meteor shower will peak overnight Wednesday (Oct. 21) through Thursday (Oct. 22), potentially producing around 20 meteors per hour under a dark sky, according to Time and Date. The Orionids are caused when Earth passes through the debris left by Halley's Comet, which last passed through the inner solar system in 1986 and will return in 2061.

Halley's Comet is presently beyond the orbit of Neptune, according to The Planets Today, but it doesn't need to be close to Earth to cause "shooting stars." Every time the comet travels through the inner solar system, heat from the sun causes it to shed dust and rocky particles along its orbital path. Earth's own orbit takes the comet through streams of that ancient debris several times a year, with the particles vaporizing as they slam into our planet's atmosphere.

The Orionids are particularly fast meteors, striking Earth's atmosphere at around 41 miles per second (66 kilometers per second). They can produce bright streaks, occasional fireballs (extremely bright, sometimes colorful meteors) and "persistent trains" — glowing trails that remain visible briefly after a meteor has disappeared. The best way to view them is with the naked eye, although a nice pair of stargazing binoculars or a small backyard telescope will help you enjoy more sights during peak skywatching season this fall.

Halley's Comet produces not one but two annual meteor showers. Its orbit comes close to Earth's at two different points, so our planet encounters the comet's debris twice each year — once during the Orionids in late October, and again during the Eta Aquariid meteor shower in early May.

There's a slight complication with the Orionids this year. A 71%-illuminated waxing gibbous moon will brighten much of the night, making it harder to spot meteors. However, the moon will set early Thursday morning, creating a valuable window of darkness before dawn. That's also when the constellation Orion will be high in the sky, making the predawn hours the best time to watch.

You can trace the paths of Orionid meteors back to their radiant: the famous red supergiant star system Betelgeuse in the constellation Orion. (That's why the shower is called the Orionids.) However, there's no need to look directly at Orion. Meteors seen farther from the radiant can produce longer streaks across the sky; each one comes from a tiny piece of Halley's Comet completing a journey that began many centuries ago.

#5726. Global warming could help deadly fungi invade the human body

Rising temperatures may have helped the deadly, drug-resistant yeast Candida auris adapt to human body heat and emerge worldwide.

A thermometer usually comes out when someone feels unwell. We look for a fever, then give the number little thought once it returns to normal.

That ordinary warmth serves an important purpose: it makes the human body too hot for most fungi to grow.

We breathe in fungal spores every day, but most cannot survive and establish themselves inside us.

A warmer world could help some fungi get past that barrier. For someone already very sick in a hospital, that could mean facing an infection doctors have rarely encountered before.

In a new review, Carla Schuh and Chaitanya Gokhale at Germany’s University of Würzburg examine whether rising temperatures helped a drug-resistant yeast called Candida auris become a threat.

They bring together earlier research, and their paper does not prove that climate change caused its emergence.

Fungi have no shortage of ways to cause damage, from destroying crops to devastating amphibian populations. Humans, however, have largely avoided that level of harm.

Scientists estimate that Earth holds 3 million to 5 million fungal species, yet only a few hundred are known to infect people.

Most of those pose the greatest danger when someone’s immune defenses are weak.

Our usual body temperature, around 98.6°F (37°C), helps explain this protection. A fungus that grows well outdoors may struggle to grow inside a person.

As conditions outside get hotter, fungi that tolerate heat may gain an advantage.

Over generations, that could leave some better prepared to survive our body temperature, although they would still need to overcome other defenses to cause illness.

Candida auris has already cleared the temperature hurdle. It grows readily at body temperature, and some samples survive temperatures as high as about 117°F (47°C).

Scientists first identified it in 2009 after finding it in a patient’s ear in Japan. Later tests of older samples showed that it had been present in South Korea since at least 1996.

For years, laboratories often confused it with other yeasts. That meant scientists were trying to understand its spread with an incomplete record of where it had been.

Within a decade of its discovery, C. auris had appeared on five continents. Its ability to cope with heat was only one reason hospitals struggled with it.

Once C. auris gets into a hospital, it’s hard to get rid of. The yeast can survive on surfaces for months, and standard cleaning doesn’t always remove it.

People can also carry the yeast without feeling sick, which makes infection control harder. Someone with no symptoms can still pass it on to others.

Treating an infection brings its own problems, because some strains resist all three main types of antifungal medicine.

New treatments are hard to develop, too. Fungal cells are similar to human cells in many ways, so there are fewer targets a drug can attack safely.

The danger is greatest for people with weakened immune systems, and long stays in intensive care and catheters raise the risk further.

Among patients with Candida auris bloodstream infections, studies cited in the review reported death rates ranging from 20% to 67%, although not all deaths were necessarily caused by the infection.

The yeast’s rise left researchers with a puzzle. How had it become a problem in several far-apart places within such a short time?

Between 2009 and 2016, four groups of the yeast, each with a different genetic makeup, appeared in separate regions. These differences suggested that the groups had followed separate evolutionary paths.

If one recent outbreak had spread to all the other locations, researchers would expect much more similar samples.

Instead, the findings suggested that something might have pushed different populations toward becoming a threat at roughly the same time.

Schuh and Gokhale see rising temperatures as the simplest explanation for that pattern. Warming could favor heat-tolerant fungi in many places at once, but the pattern alone cannot show that this is what happened.

The concern reaches beyond this one yeast because many fungi possess similar ways of handling stress.

Some of the biological systems that help C. auris tolerate heat already exist across the fungal world.

Heat is not the only hardship this yeast can handle. It also tolerates water nearly three times as salty as seawater, suggesting that its defenses can cope with some demanding conditions.

Surviving heat and surviving a medicine may seem like different challenges, but some fungal defenses can help with both.

An adaptation that helps a fungus survive outdoors could therefore leave it better prepared to withstand treatment.

The review describes another yeast that caused bloodstream infections in two unrelated patients in China.

When researchers grew it at body temperature in the laboratory, it rapidly developed genetic changes, with some resulting forms becoming better at causing disease and resisting two antifungal drugs.

A major piece of the story is still missing: scientists do not know where C. auris originally lived.

Without that starting point, they cannot easily trace the conditions that shaped it before it reached hospitals.

Wetlands and coastal waters are possible candidates because salt and sun-warmed shallows could favor tolerance to both salt and heat.

Scientists have found the yeast in India’s Andaman Islands, but people may have carried it there.

Farm fungicides offer another possible explanation for some drug resistance. However, they do not explain resistance to every drug family, and farmers used them for decades before scientists identified C. auris.

“This is another reason why there are several possible explanations for why the fungus appeared so suddenly and spread so rapidly,” Gokhale said.

For patients and hospitals, earlier warning could make a difference. At present, scientists often recognize an emerging fungal threat only after it turns up in someone who needs care.

Schuh and Gokhale want researchers to sample fungi regularly in wetlands, coastal areas, farmland and hospital air.

Computer models could use those findings to flag species becoming more tolerant of heat, giving health services time to improve testing and prepare.

“The research community agrees that fungal infections are becoming increasingly significant worldwide. However, the exact causes and the role of individual factors remain the subject of heated debate,” Schuh said.

The authors call for particular support in tropical and low-income regions, where the risks are high and laboratories, treatments and hospital resources can be scarce.

The study is published in the journal mSphere.

There are two types of snow blowers for homeowners, based on the number of “stages” in the mechanical process they use to collect and distribute snow.

Dual-stage snow blowers, as the name suggests, have a two-stage process for collecting and expelling snow. Like single-stage models, they have an auger that pulls snow into the intake. The dual-stage snow blower follows up by pushing the snow into an impeller that ejects it out through the chute.

The width and depth of a snow blower’s intake determine how much snow it can move with each pass, and thus, how long it takes you to get the job done. Most single-stage electric snow blowers have a clearing width of around 20 inches and a clearing depth of around 8 inches. That’s good for carving out walks and medium-sized areas. Some heavy-duty two-stage electric snow blowers can remove a 24-inch-wide and 20-inch-deep swath of snow, which will make quick work of a large driveway.

Throwing distance refers to how far a snow blower can launch snow out of the chute. This gives you a sense of how to plan when and how you’ll dispose of the snow in your path. The average single-stage snow blower typically has a short throwing distance—around 25 feet. Unsurprisingly, a two-stage snow blower can send its snow as far as 50 feet.

Snow blowers typically have a throttle that allows you to change their mechanical speed, which will adjust how far it throws your snow. There are also controls that allow you to rotate the chute to change the direction of the flow of snow. If you can map out your snow-blowing route, check and make sure your snow blower throws the snow far enough to keep it out of your way until it melts.

Most electric snow blowers receive power from multiple large rechargeable batteries, which allow them to produce nearly as much power as a gas snow blower. No battery supplies uninterrupted power for as long as a tank of gas, though: On average, you should expect a battery-powered snow blower to run for about 30 to 45 minutes on a single charge. You can extend that time if you have multiple batteries, but the low run time still caps how much ground they can cover.

Though we largely consider them to be obsolete, some manufacturers also still produce corded electric snow blowers, which plug into a standard electrical outlet. They’re often cheaper than battery-powered models and run as long as you need them to. However, having a power cord substantially reduces their range: You will likely need to purchase an extension cord to extend its reach slightly beyond the length of the area you need to clear. No matter how much slack you have, though, the cable makes the whole process a little more awkward.

“Cordless snow blowers are easier, better, and safer to use,” says Popular Mechanics Test Editor Brad Ford. “Corded is old, out-of-date tech in most cases.”

They’re also limited to smaller, 15-amp motors that run on the power an outlet can provide. That usually translates to narrower clearing widths, shallower clearing depths, and shorter throw distances. Unless you have a very tight budget, we highly recommend going with a battery-powered electric snow blower rather than a corded one.

The most important thing you can do to keep your electric snow blower running season after season is to simply make sure that you store your charger and batteries indoors when you aren’t charging or using them. Lithium cells aren't able to hold the same amount of charge when the temperature drops below freezing. By storing and charging those batteries indoors, you’ll get the most run time out of them.

If you use a corded snow blower, you shouldn’t plug it into any old extension cord. These machines draw a lot of power, and not every cord can handle it. Using the wrong one can create a fire hazard or damage your snow blower. Make sure to check your snow blower’s specs before buying an extension cord to pair with it, so you can be sure it can handle its amperage.

Most corded electric snow blowers have a 12-amp rating, in which case you’ll want a 12-gauge extension cord that can provide at least 12 amps of power. You’ll also want to make sure it’s long enough for you to reach the far end of whatever walkway or road you plan to clear, preferably with some slack to spare for maneuverability. We recommend starting with 100 feet and scaling up based on your property.

Regardless, it would be wise to buy an extension cord to match the specs you need for your snow blower, rather than using one that you happen to have lying around.

That said, the most powerful electric snow blowers still don’t match the snow-moving capabilities of the largest gas-powered snow blowers on the market. #5721. JAXA's Martian Moons eXploration Mission is on its Way to Mars and Phobos

JAXA, the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, has finally launched its Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission. The launch occurred at 04:41:03 JST on October 20, 2026 from the Tanegashima Space Center, Japan's primary spaceport. 17 minutes after lift-off, MMX separated from its launch vehicle and began its journey to Mars and Phobos, the largest of the planet's two moons.

MMX will reach Mars in August 2027 and enter an orbit around the planet. After about one month, it will transfer to an orbit around Phobos. MMX consists of the main orbiter and a small lander named Idefix, developed by Germany and France. The mission will place Idefix on the surface of Phobos, where it will perform science operations and help find a suitable landing for the main part of the MMX mission: sample collection.

Idefix will simply be dropped onto the surface of Phobos from between 40 and 100 meters above. The small moon's gravity is so weak that the rover won't be damaged. Regardless of how it lands, it will right itself and get to work.

In 2030, MMX will then land on Phobos, either once or twice, and collect regolith samples. The goal is to collect at least 10 grams of material. Then MMX will perform flybys of Mars' other moon, Deimos, before sending the sample back to Earth in a capsule. The sample, if collected successfully, should be back on Earth in 2031.

This figure highlights the different parts of MMX's mission to Mars and Phobos. Image Credit: JAXA.

The sample return capsule will be inside MMX's Return Module, which will separate from the Exploration Module. The Exploration Module, which contains the lander's legs, rover deployment system, and science instruments, will be left to its fate. The capsule containing the samples will separate from the Return Module and be sent to the Woomera Prohibited Area in South Australia to be retrieved, while the Return Module will burn up in Earth's atmosphere.

JAXA has completed two successful sample return missions already. Hayabusa returned a sample from Asteroid 25143 Itokawa in 2010, and Hayabusa 2 returned a sample from Asteroid 162173 Ryugu in 2020. Their track record suggests that MMX will be successful, too.

If MMX is successful, it will return our first ever sample from the Mars system. The sample will hopefully provide answers to some stubborn questions regarding the Martian moons.

The origins of Mars' two moons is a subject of vigorous debate in planetary science. There's tangible hope the MMX and its samples can resolve it.

One theory is that a giant impact on Mars' surface early in the Solar System's history created the moons. The powerful impact created a debris ring around Mars, and both moons coalesced out of that debris. Because the moons follow orbits that are near-circular and near-elliptical, that theory is well-supported. But there's also a problem with it. Debris rings tend to form close to the bodies that were impacted, and Deimos is much further away than Phobos. Some research shows that the chance of an impact creating both moons is less than 3%.

This artist's illustration shows a body about one-third the size of Mars slamming into the planet. The collision could've created a debris ring, out of which Phobos and Deimos formed. Image Credit: Paris Diderot / Labex UnivEarthS

Another theory is that both moons are captured asteroids. Phobos and Deimos appear to be made of different material than Mars, which not only goes against the impact theory, but supports the asteroid capture theory. But both moons orbit within 2 degrees of the Martian equator, which makes two separate asteroid captures unlikely. But some research shows that this is still more likely than the impact scenario.

A third possibility is that Mars captured a single asteroid. That asteroid was shredded by the planet's gravity, forming a debris ring. Both moons formed from that debris, which could explain why they seem to have a different compositon than Mars, but also follow very similar near-equatorial orbits.

Another possibility is even more complex. It starts with a body one-third the size of Mars slamming into the planet and creating a very wide debris disk. In the inner part of this disk, a large moon formed, 1000 times larger than Phobos. Gravitational interactions with this large moon created more moons in the disk, including Phobos and Deimos. In a few thousand years Mars had a retinue of ten small moons, and the one massive moon. The debris disk dissipated after millions of years, and Mars' tidal effects brought most of the moons crashing down onto the planet, including the large moon. Only Phobos and Deimos remain.

This illustration explains the scenario described above. A massive object slammed into Mars creating a wide debris disk. A massive moon formed that helped drive the creation of 10 smaller moons. All of those moons slammed into Mars and are gone, while only Phobos and Deimos remain. Image Credit: Antony Trinh / Royal Observatory of Belgium

It's possible that a mere 10 grams of regolith from Phobos could settle the debate, with help from MMX's remote-sensing data.

If the material clearly matches Mars' mineralogy and isotopic ratio, that supports the giant impact scenario. If the material more closely matches asteroidal material, with different isotopic ratios and mineralogy than Mars, that supports the captured asteroid scenario. In the partial capture or hybrid scenario, the sample material should be asteroid-like, but have some differences in bulk composition and volatile content. The fourth and final scenario is much more difficult to resolve.

In any case, nearly every sample returned to Earth, and every other mission or space telescope, generates some surprises. There may yet be a surprise waiting in the sample, when it returns. Perhaps a surprising answer, or more surprising questions.

#5720. A 58-Million-Year-Old Mammal Is Challenging Long-Held Assumptions About Evolution

A surprising discovery about ancient horned mammals is challenging long-held ideas about sexual dimorphism.

For decades, scientists have pointed to a group of enormous, horned prehistoric mammals as a classic example of how dramatically males and females can differ in appearance. But a new analysis of fossil skulls suggests that one of paleontology’s most familiar examples of sexual dimorphism may rest on surprisingly weak evidence.

Researchers at the University of Kansas examined approximately 30 skulls belonging to the extinct species Uintatherium anceps. Their findings, published in PLOS One, challenge the longstanding assumption that males were substantially larger and possessed more prominent horns and canine teeth than females.

“Males have been interpreted as being much larger and possessing far more dramatic cranial features than females,” said study author Kevin Mulcahy, a doctoral student at the KU Biodiversity Institute & Natural History Museum.

Uintatheres first appeared around 58 million years ago, less than 10 million years after the asteroid impact that wiped out the nonavian dinosaurs. These large mammals, distinguished by their imposing skulls, horns, and canine teeth, persisted in the fossil record for roughly 20 million years.

The investigation began when Mulcahy and his doctoral adviser, K. Christopher Beard, senior curator at the institute, attempted to identify a broken upper molar recovered during fieldwork in Montana. Suspecting that it belonged to a uintathere, they visited the American Museum of Natural History in New York to compare it with other fossils.

“We had a research visit at the American Museum of Natural History in New York, where our main goal was just to compare that tooth to teeth from other uintatheres and figure out what type of animal we had,” Mulcahy said. “They have this big collection — entire shelves occupied with these skulls, and each one of them is almost a meter long. These are famous specimens that are in the literature, and I was originally just looking at them to compare their teeth, and I noticed a ton of variation in a way that didn’t appear binary.”

The museum’s skulls, some approaching 1 meter (3.3 feet) in length, displayed substantial anatomical variation. However, the differences did not appear to separate neatly into the two groups expected if males and females had dramatically different physical characteristics.

Mulcahy began measuring the specimens to determine whether their anatomical differences followed the pattern expected from strong sexual dimorphism. In species where males and females differ substantially in size or anatomy, measurements may form two distinguishable statistical groups, known as a bimodal distribution. A unimodal distribution, by contrast, has a single dominant peak.

“I just, really hastily, with a ruler, gathered a bunch of measurements of the skulls while we were there,” Mulcahy said. “I started playing around with statistics, and there are ways you can look at different measurements to see if they’re best fit by a classic unimodal distribution, or if you were to think you’re dealing with something that exhibits strong sexual dimorphism, you’d see something with a bimodal distribution — that there are two means, one for males and one for females.”

The preliminary results showed no clear separation. Mulcahy subsequently expanded the dataset with measurements from other institutions and applied additional statistical techniques, eventually examining approximately 30 Uintatherium anceps skulls.

“It wasn’t like there’s one group of specimens that look bigger than another, and then there’s another group that are smaller with smaller horns and canines,” he said. “So I was kind of interested — are these really good examples of sexual dimorphism after all? Is there actual evidence in the morphology of these skulls, in the distribution in particular of the ranges of morphology we’re seeing, that that’s what’s going on?”

Although the expanded analysis detected some possible signs of sexual dimorphism, the evidence remained inconclusive. Mulcahy considered whether the statistical methods themselves might be unable to detect genuine differences between males and females in fossil populations.

“Maybe there were some little signals of dimorphism here and there, but nothing really concrete,” he said. “I had an idea: Maybe it’s just that the techniques I’m using aren’t robust enough to actually measure sexual dimorphism. With fossil animals, you don’t actually know which specimens are males and which are females. You can guess and then test whether or not the ones you guess are males differ from the ones you guess are females, but that’s super circular, because you could just be picking the most extreme ones to be one sex and the least extreme to be the other. Of course they’re going to differ.”

To evaluate the reliability of his approach, he applied the same statistical techniques to modern bison, large horned herbivores with ecological similarities to those proposed for uintatheres. Unlike the fossil specimens, the bison could be classified by their known sex, allowing the analysis to be checked against actual male and female groups.

“When you’re dealing with living animals, you can just take the males, take the females and compare them,” he said. “Bison are another really big herbivore, with a similar sort of ecology that’s been proposed for uintatheres, and they also have big horns. What I noticed is that the signals of dimorphism I was picking up in bison were a lot clearer. When I was looking at the bison, it was almost perfect that all of the females were most similar to the females, and all the males were most similar to the males.”

The contrast suggested that the methods could detect pronounced sexual differences when they were present, strengthening the case that Uintatherium anceps did not exhibit the degree of dimorphism traditionally attributed to it. The results do not establish that males and females looked identical, but they provide little support for the previously assumed extreme differences.

Mulcahy also suggested that the conventional interpretation of uintathere fossils may partly reflect the scientific and cultural assumptions prevalent when these animals were first studied in the late 19th century. Early paleontologists frequently worked within a framework that expected males to be larger and more imposing than females.

“Back then, this is right when evolutionary theory, sexual selection, natural selection, all of that is kind of coming into play,” he said. “I think it’s really important to remember that these things were first discovered in the late 1800s by a bunch of male paleontologists working in a very Victorian-era mindset. The idea is basically assumed at that point that males are going to be bigger and scarier than females. It’s really interesting: When you step back and actually test that hypothesis, it doesn’t really hold up.”

The findings also raise questions about other extinct mammals whose presumed sexual differences were inferred primarily from variations in fossil size and anatomy. Without independent evidence of a fossil animal’s sex, distinguishing sexual dimorphism from ordinary variation within a species remains difficult.

Mulcahy argues that pronounced male-biased sexual dimorphism may have evolved independently in different mammalian lineages rather than being an inherited characteristic of early mammals. He points to recent research on living mammals indicating that larger males are far from universal.

“Strong, big male-style sexual dimorphism was probably not ancestral at the root of the mammalian tree, and among the mammals where we do see this pattern today, it’s almost certainly shown up independently several times,” Mulcahy said. “It had long been assumed that sexual dimorphism is the norm, that most species show that males are bigger than females. But just a couple years ago, a paper came out showing that among living mammals, most species either don’t show any dimorphism or actually have larger females. The idea that males are always bigger than females is really just a choice of what animals were people looking at, what are the things that biologists in the past were interested in.”

Do birthdays matter? Not really. But they are, at the very least, an excuse to do something you might not usually do as a middle-aged father of two. On this most recent occasion, my birthday was a reason to drag an assortment of friendship flotsam across the Los Angeles coastline, working our way down through a selection of Southern California beach dive bars. (It also marks the second time I’ve used my assignments for Eater and Punch to rationalize, or maybe subsidize, a birthday bar crawl.) I truly believe that these old, salt-crusted pubs are an essential, last-remaining stronghold of our regional culture, and that they demand both our respect and our money.

This crawl took place on a Saturday in September, filled with drinks and snacks (but mostly bites of bar burgers), working our way from Santa Monica to Manhattan Beach before wandering inland for dinner in Torrance, home to some of the best Japanese food in the United States.

The plan was to start relatively early, enjoying as much sunlit ocean breeze as we could, while also allowing me to get a normal amount of sleep at the end of the night before waking up at traditional dad hours. The great thing about a bar crawl is that it allows people to jump on and off as they see fit. I’m also a sucker for a brisk itinerary, moving quickly from place to place: the key is to not care who shows up when and where, simply telling someone to add the necessary ETAs to the group chat as applicable.

Opened in 1959, and a roughly 8-minute walk from the beach, Chez Jay has remained a staple and a refuge in a city that has been rapidly pricing out old neighborhood bars and restaurants. It is a place to drink a martini or a beer, where the only rules are that you “take no pictures, and throw your peanut shells on the floor.” (I broke one of the rules.) The kitchen was not open yet, but the peanuts were plentiful and the pours were heavy. The red booths and red checkerboard tablecloths evoke an old Italian restaurant, though it is certainly more of a steak-and-seafood kind of place to have dinner. It started strong, with six people showing up, including my dad and stepmom.

Hinano Cafe has been operating on a busy stretch of Venice Beach since 1962. This perpetually packed place has become an iconic surfer hang serving beer and burgers (no full liquor license here) — and like a true surfer bar, it serves breakfast from 8 a.m. until noon. Our group swelled with more friends at our second location, where pitchers of beer were poured in a standing-room-only setting, alongside board-short-wearing day drinkers who were far too young and attractive for us to stay any longer than it took for this gaggle of graying dads to order and split a hamburger. At $12.50, this perfectly acceptable bar burger on a sesame seed bun was a pleasant enough edible intermezzo. I’d prefer to come back again at another time, like maybe 1975.

We had now crossed to the other side of the marina — something of a traffic dividing line in Los Angeles County — and settled into a perfect little three-bar-crawl that exists on a 0.2-mile stretch of this hidden, working-class LA beach community. The sun was starting to drop and the ocean breeze was relaxing our shoulders (although that also may have just been the drinks). We sauntered into Prince O’ Whales, which has been serving drinks under this name for over five decades, with another 18 years before under a different one (“The Bowsprit”). There are two indoor bars, though one was closed for the afternoon. Our bartender was the kind of grumpy you look for in a sailor’s saloon, and the room was crowded with some rather loud folks watching college football. (It turns out I’m getting old.) We took our drinks to the back patio, a serene little patch of cement, brick, faded paint and sun-withered wood. There was also a ping-pong table, and this round was highlighted by exactly the kind of late-summer weather that makes people live by the beach in Southern California.

After walking for about 90 seconds, we made it to a place known as “the smallest dive bar in Los Angeles.” It would feel like a California-themed yokocho in Tokyo if it weren’t so perfectly lived in, lined with old wood that feels like you’re drinking in the bowels of a turn-of-the-century vessel crossing the Atlantic. A drunk, nonthreatening stranger joined our group and asked a lot of questions. I ordered a vodka-soda and they gave me a gin and tonic, so I ordered a gin martini. It came with a “sidecar” that was, incredibly, just a rocks glass with a splash of rapidly warming gin in it. I curiously wandered into the adjoining Italian restaurant and was convinced by some very friendly women to order some food. I told them to pick their favorite dish and near the end of my martini, a foil container of steaming hot ravioli was dropped at our table. If my wife divorces me, I could see myself paying rent on one of these barstools, living out my twilight years and wondering where it all went wrong.

Looking back at my iPhone photo timestamps, I am incredibly impressed that we were able to have a round of martinis and a plate of ravioli in 26 minutes. We soldiered on, with an even shorter walk to our next location: the classic old surf bar and restaurant, The Shack. It feels almost set-decorated, with the perfect amount of wear and tear. This place was significantly more spacious and we found room at the bar, where we ordered the Shack Burger for all to try, as well as some wings. The signature burger comes, miraculously, with a butterflied Louisiana hot link on it, operating like juicier, snappier, spicier bacon. I, meanwhile, remained impressed with my ability to have five drinks (or was it six?) — a skill that rarely gets tested anymore.

Nearing its 100th year of operation (though not always as a bar — it opened during Prohibition), this could easily have been the last stop of the tour, the flagship bar you build a crawl around. Known among my friends as “the best dive bar burger in LA County,” this proud, defiant establishment has stood the test of time, surviving rapidly escalating real estate prices to remain the great neighborhood dive it has always been. It feels like a sacred escape from the rest of this wealthy beach community. The whole place seems to be made of wood and beer signs; there is one line for ordering and another for the bathroom; and every single patron — whether gray-haired in Hawaiian shirts or tatted-up bikers — seems to know how lucky they are to be there. There is a small grill behind the bar in the corner, under a yellow sign proudly proclaiming “no fries.” The cheeseburger is an exercise in simple chargrilled perfection, its meat sourced from the butcher shop next door, all served in a basket with a bag of potato chips. The menu also claims to have a teriyaki chicken sandwich, though I’ve never seen one there. I wished we could have stayed longer, or to have eaten a whole burger instead of just two bites, but such is the nature of a crawl. We quickly made our way out, calling our next restaurant to let them know we were running about 20 minutes late for our reservation.

Stop 7: Torihei — Torrance – sometime not long after 8 p.m.

We did a decent job of snacking rather than eating on our beach dive crawl, so after we settled into a long table adorned with sake and beer, there was perhaps no more comforting bite of food in the world than Torihei’s simmered slab of daikon radish, served in dashi and blanketed in katsuobushi (smoked, dried skipjack tuna flakes). It warmed my chest. This classic standby izakaya specializes in yakitori (grilled chicken skewers) and oden (simmered ingredients in broth). We sipped beers and crunched tender chicken cartilage alongside grilled chicken meatballs before finishing the night with some soborodon (a ground chicken and rice bowl).

I was home, showered, and in bed before 11:15. I think we nailed it.

FRASCATI, Italy — Observing Earth from space provides a much-needed insight into the evolution of our planet's health. This is particularly important in the face of climate change, as environmental degradation, food insecurity and extreme weather events can all reveal themselves in stark images taken from high above our planet's surface.

Simonetta Cheli is the director of Earth observation programs at the European Space Agency (ESA) and the head of the European Space Research Institute (ESRIN) in Frascati, Italy. With nearly 40 years of experience in the field, she is one of the world's leading experts on Earth observation.

Live Science spoke with Cheli on a recent visit to ESRIN to talk about why Earth observation is so important, how NASA budget cuts and political changes have altered the picture, and why private space satellites risk our ability to view the planet clearly.

She also discussed how new supercomputers, like ESRIN's newly operational Space HPC, are helping to process vast amounts of data like never before — and shared her favorite recent satellite snap of our planet

Simonetta Cheli became ESA's director of Earth observation programs and head of ESRIN on Jan. 1, 2022, more than three decades after joining the agency. She was born in Siena, Italy, and went on to study law and economics at Yale University before gaining a degree in political sciences at the University 'Cesare Alfieri' in Florence, Italy. She also holds a diploma of advanced studies from the Centre d’Etudes Diplomatiques et Stratégiques in Paris.

Harry Baker: Why is observing Earth from space so important?

Simonetta Cheli: It's important because we need to know today what is the state of our planet, if it's healthy or if it's in danger, if it's stressed or not.

It's important to have objective information on many different variables, such as deforestation, the quality of the air that we breathe, the evolution of the oceans or the melting of ice masses. Basically, we need to be aware of what the situation is on Earth today, so we can preserve it tomorrow.

HB: How has ESA's Earth observation program changed throughout your career?

SC: When I started at ESA [in 1988], we were relying on missions from partners, including the Americans and Japanese. We didn't have our own missions.

We launched the first mission [ERS-1] in 1991. Today, just here at ESRIN, we manage over 20 missions and are planning more than 40 more. We have also launched three different missions in the last three weeks — one to look at how oceanography impacts extreme weather events [International Report for an Innovative Defence of Earth]; one to monitor the fluorescence and health status of vegetation [Fluorescence Explorer]; and one to measure temperature on land and sea [Sentinel-3C], which was a super useful thing this summer.

Cheli is one of the world's leading experts on Earth observation and currently oversees more than 20 different missions that are monitoring our planet from above.

Europe is now a leading power in this domain worldwide. We have evolved a lot.

HB: How important is it to cooperate with other countries when observing our planet from above?

SC: Well, I must say, in ESA, it's in the DNA of our work. ESA includes 23 countries today, so we work together daily. We also work very closely with other commercial partners and the European Union, as well as international partners like NASA, Brazil, India and Africa. So this is part of our job, and very much also in the domain I'm managing.

We used to go to the climate conferences together. We are not anymore.

To tackle climate and environmental stability, you cannot just do things alone at the national level. You have to share data and work with everybody in the world, where you have to have common objectives. For example, what happens to Greenland affects everybody on Earth, not just the people who live there. So the collaboration, I think, is very much inherent in our daily job at ESA and between our partners.

HB: Given the recent cuts to NASA's funding, including its Earth observation budget, does ESA feel a bigger responsibility to step up and fill the gap?

SC: I think we certainly have a bigger opportunity in the sense that today, with the systems we have and the satellites we have and the missions we have, even before the political context. For example, we were already leading the U.S. with data on emissions.

Live Science recently visited Frascati to see ESRIN's newly-operational Space HPC supercomputer.

We have a very good working-level collaboration with our American partners. This is somehow preserved, although politically it is, of course, more delicate. We used to go to the climate conferences together. We are not anymore.

HB: ESA just launched the latest batch of satellites in the IRIDE constellation (an Italian funded network of Earth-observing spacecraft designed and operated by ESA). What makes these satellites so special? And how are they helping your research?

SC: We launched the latest eight [satellites] last night! (The IRIDE satellites launched on Oct. 1, the day before this interview took place.)

Sixty-eight satellites are planned overall in this constellation, and we have launched quite a few [39] already, and plan to launch some more before the end of the year.

On Sept. 14, ESA also launched the Sentinel-3C satellite on board at Vega rocket that lifted off from the agency's launch site in French Guiana.

The network will collect a lot of data from the optical radar spectrum that will support different user needs: for example, in the civil protection area, in coastal erosion monitoring, in coast guard operations and in environmental stress analysis. And so this system will certainly be a big contribution to the wider European Earth observation setup.

HB: The number of private satellites, like in SpaceX's Starlink constellation, is increasing fast. Are you worried about the impacts that this might have on your scientific spacecraft?

SC: We are worried because we see that the anti-collision maneuvers are increasing and increasing. When my mission manager tells me we have to move the satellite because there is a risk of collision with another satellite, we have to go check with ESO [European Southern Observatory] in Germany, and then the operations center moves the satellite. And the number [of times we've done this] has certainly increased in the last four or five years due to the increased number of satellites up there.

For the moment, the technical collaboration with partners is going quite well. So when we see someone else's satellite, we actually try to organize things as best we can, and technically it's working well. But what we need for the future is a clear framework at the legal level, which is engaging everybody.

"Everybody" in space is no longer only the institutional partners; it's not just the space agencies of the world that should engage. Today, you have private actors, like Starlink, and if they don't engage in the same rules, you risk having a delicate situation. But this is something that we are discussing daily, and we're tackling.

HB: The whole point of Earth observation is to protect our planet. How do you feel about some of the negative impacts of launching satellites, such as exhaust plumes, metal pollution and space junk?

SC: There is a very strong objective linked to sustainability at ESA. It is not just about using the data to better preserve our planet; it's also about being sustainable in the daily work we do.

Chelli has been working at ESA for nearly 40 years, and believes that global cooperation is essential in Earth observation.

This is linked to, for example, trying to have a space debris policy; trying to have enough propellant on board of the satellites that we build to make sure that once they finish their lifetime, they come back with a re-entry assist and they don't stay there in orbit. And the same is true in the launcher domain, where there's a very strong effort from our partners in French Guiana [where ESA's only active launch site is located] to lessen our impact as much as possible on the environment with the launch setup.

There's a really dedicated team doing all this.

HB: How will AI systems and advanced computers, like Space HPC, help researchers process data? And what sort of things could that help you do?

SC: We have today a wealth of data that is continually increasing. Just in the Copernicus program [Europe's biggest Earth observation initiative], we receive 500 terabytes of data per day.

We also have big volumes of data in the archives here at ESRIN from science missions of international partners, including Japan, India, the U.S. and others. This "heritage data" guarantees that archival data of missions that have ended are preserved, which is super important.

Chelli (center right) was one of the people chosen to cut the ribbon at the opening of ESRIN's Space HPC supercomputer on March 12, 2025.

So instruments like SpaceHPC, which have high computing capability, help us a lot to process those datasets faster.

HB: What is your favorite satellite photo of Earth?

SC: I have too many favorite satellite photos, I must say. But this summer, the one I really found striking was a thermal image of Europe's heatwaves because, in that image, you see that the temperature on land and on sea was increasing so much.

This photo, captured by ESA's Sentinel-3B satellite in June 2026, shows land and sea surface temperatures spiking across Southern Europe. While it is concerning, Cheli says that the image gives her hope about our ability to monitor such extreme weather events in the future.

It was captured by Sentinel-3B [a predecessor of the recently launched Sentinel-3C], and you see the red in the sea, and you see the red on the land, and I think it gives the feeling of the heat wave that we've all been living through this summer. We had, I would say, the hottest summer ever.

But it makes me feel that we can do a lot about it. It makes me feel that we have tools and certainly information capabilities to support, help, and also raise awareness about this topic.

So right now that is a favorite, but there are so many [other photos].

#5717. Could Our Universe Be a Simulation? ‘Glitches’ at the Limits of Reality Could Offer Clues, Physicists Suggest

A simulated universe implies that the cosmos has an intentional design—or does it?

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

Reality appears to be continuous in every direction. Spacetime looks smooth and measurable—and for the most part, it is. But when you get down to the fundamental universal constants underpinning everything, such as Planck length and the speed of light, science can’t yet explain their origin. Some scientists have broached the controversial idea that our universe has certain properties because it was designed to.

It starts with examining these constants, the unchanging physical properties that can’t be parsed any further. Planck lengths, which represent the smallest scale we know in which matter can exist, could represent “pixels” that form the framework of space. Each “pixel” is only 1.6×10−35 meters, which is infinitesimally small, even compared to a proton, a building block of atoms. A proton is still 100 billion billion times the Planck length.

This “pixel” length is important—physicists derived it as the spatial scale at which quantum mechanics and gravity intersect. And it represents the smallest discrete distance that light travels. In a sense, the speed of light—a constant 186,000 miles per second in a vacuum—may be the “frame rate” at which reality updates each pixel. At this tiniest of scales, quantum physics principles begin to take over. We see it in the phenomenon between atoms or electrons, sometimes called “spooky action at a distance”—that’s when an action performed upon one tiny particle produces an effect in a very far-removed one.

The quantum world seems to defy the everyday world around us. What makes its mechanics difficult to understand is that quantum particles only seem to have definite properties when we measure them; it’s as if they are rendering on demand, like in a game. Entangled particles maintain correlations across vast distances, behaving less like separate objects and more like data.

So let’s add all of these elements up: the pixels, the speed of light, and the quantum phenomena. Why is our reality apparently built around these fundamentals? Paul Davies, PhD, a theoretical physicist at Arizona State University, has proposed that these properties of the universe combined seem to indicate that reality acts like a simulation. The idea of “simulation theory” has been around for at least 20 years, popularized by philosopher Nick Bostrom, but appearing in science fiction long before that. However, Davies notes that this idea is a thought experiment only. He also cautions that the hypothesis may never be testable, and so may never hope to gain the status of a true scientific theory.

“In a sense, one can make up anything,” Davies says. “The best comment I know about it is an article by John Barrow many years ago, in New Scientist, entitled “Glitch”—in which he says that ‘the flaws of physics may tell us more about reality than the laws of physics.’ Basically, if the universe is a simulation, it need not be perfect, so one might look for ‘glitches’ in the natural order of things.”

How do you falsify the simulation hypothesis? Davies says that while it’s an intriguing idea, the end point might be closer to mythology than science. “So the argument is fun to think about—and I keep a little corner of my mind open to it—but it doesn’t provide a sensible career path for a practicing scientist. It’s more of interest to philosophers, and theologians.”

It’s also of interest to physicist Melvin Vopson, PhD, at the University of Portsmouth. He has examined whether the universe could be “tuned” to existence as a simulation and has published a book about it, called Reality Reloaded: The Scientific Case for a Simulated Universe. Testing the nature of our reality has limitations, he says, but there may be forms of evidence that we can pursue—those glitches Davies mentioned.

“Assuming the universe is indeed some kind of computational construct, essentially to prove it one would need to have access to similar capabilities and tools utilized to run the computation that renders our reality. Since these capabilities would be beyond our constructed realm, what we are left with is a range of limited scientific and metaphysical clues that we could identify and stress test empirically,” he says. “Strangely, those very limitations I mentioned could also be a valuable pathway to extracting these clues, by intentionally pushing physical systems to the limits of our constructed reality and observing their behavior.”

Vopson named a couple of ways we could do that. One kind of experiment would be conducted close to the speed of light, and so show how space and time are intertwined at the universe’s essential speed limit. Another would be through quantum mechanics tests, which would show the limits of space, matter, and energy at a very small scale. By looking at these scales, he says, we are already able to shatter illusions, such as the world as a continuum, “when in fact the universe is discrete, much like a pixelation or discrete digits in a computer system.” The entanglement of quantum particles is a good example of this discreteness, because it shows that matter itself “has in fact a dual wave-particle nature, and matter is only rendered on demand and on observation,” he says.

Vopson does add that “nobody will ever accept the sim theory as a fact, without empirical evidence, and that includes me, too.” However, he suggests that empirical evidence could arise someday from a novel experiment, though he can’t foresee how it will come about. Or perhaps new evidence will emerge through advancements in artificial intelligence (AI), he says—that, again, is difficult to forecast.

While we can’t quite prove that the universe is a simulation, what we can show is that we have a lot of tools for considering the cosmos. Perhaps with advancements in AI or experiments, we can learn more, but for now we will need to be content with leaving many of these questions about our reality to philosophy, rather than science.

It’s an approach that Davies agrees with. He says his biggest objection to a scientific investigation is that calling the universe a simulation is a “cop-out.” That’s because scientists, by their nature and profession, are looking for scientific explanations of the universe around us. On the other hand, he says, it’s easy to imagine “a world in which pretty much anything can happen, like the dream world, and explain it by saying that this reality is simply what the simulator has chosen.”

But a shattered tooth suggests a more nuanced tale of life

A hole in this 75,000-year-old shoulder blade suggests that this Neandertal was stabbed by a spear around the time they died.

A new set of remains found in modern-day Iraq suggests that one Neandertal probably suffered a devastating blow to the face before dying at the point of a spear many years later.

The size and structure of a puncture wound in a shoulder blade suggest that it was made by a wooden spear shortly before or after death, researchers write October 15 in Scientific Reports. But violence isn’t all this Neandertal may have known. A shattered tooth found with the remains hints that the deceased suffered a facial injury at some point in their life. Surviving this injury indicates that Neandertals may have helped each other in times of need, the study authors speculate.

These remains with visible trauma are “super cool,” because “there’s a lot of ways to die that leave absolutely no mark on your skeleton,” says Libby Cowgill, a biological anthropologist at the University of Missouri in Columbia who wasn’t involved with the study.

Neandertals were often injured throughout their lives, probably from hunting. But archaeologists have also uncovered occasional signs that Neandertals and other hominids — modern humans and their extinct cousins — were stabbing and pummeling each other, maybe even intentionally, as far back as 430,000 years ago. Signs of injuries caused by other hominids have included another Neandertal at the same Iraq site whose rib was probably stabbed.

During an excavation that began in 2019 at Shanidar, the famous Neandertal burial site in Iraqi Kurdistan, researchers uncovered remains from at least three previously unknown individuals. The set included ribs, hand bones, teeth and a perforated shoulder bone that probably belonged to a single individual who lived around 75,000 years ago.

At first, the team had no idea what caused the hole, says Emma Pomeroy, a biological anthropologist at the University of Cambridge. “It’s so round, it’s like, what is it?” she recalls thinking at the time.

The team removed the shoulder blade with the surrounding sediment and tested for evidence that roots or insects had damaged the fossil. But that didn’t fit the nature of the damage.

Instead, they found fragments of bone from the shoulder blade in the dirt that suggested it had been pierced when the individual, which the team named Shanidar A, was alive. The team also didn’t see any signs of healing around the puncture wound when they analyzed the bone under the microscope and using X-rays — suggesting that the injury had occurred around the time of death.

The next task was to find out what instrument might have caused the injury. The team compared the puncture with the fossilized remains of butchered animals found alongside human tools, as well as wounds created by archaeologists stabbing animal corpses with re-created Paleolithic weapons. Stone-tipped spears usually create a diamond-shaped hole. Wooden spears, on the other hand, tend to leave round puncture wounds.

The wound was “absolutely, without any question,” made by a wooden spear, says Bruno Maureille, an anthropologist at the University of Bordeaux in France who wasn’t on the study team.

The team also found a broken incisor alongside the rest of the remains. The tooth was so shattered that Shanidar A probably experienced a serious injury to their face. Tartar buildup suggests that the tooth splintered in half years before Shanidar A died, meaning they lived with their disability for a long time.

“Given it would have affected their ability to eat, to drink — they’d have been in massive amount of pain at first — it’s likely [to] have needed some care,” Pomeroy says. That one set of remains suggest both violence and care show that Neandertal “behavior is complex,” Pomeroy says. “Like us.”

#5712. Powerful solar storm exposed a hidden radiation risk for aircraft

A powerful solar storm sent radiation levels soaring above Earth, revealing unexpected risks for aircraft and gaps in aviation warning systems.

On November 11, 2025, a rare X5-class solar flare sent a burst of high-energy particles hurtling toward Earth. By 10:15 UTC, those particles were penetrating the atmosphere and reaching ground level.

This event was the 77th Ground Level Enhancement ever recorded, and the largest in nearly two decades.

For most people, the day passed unremarkably. For a small team of space weather researchers, it was the moment years of preparation had been pointing toward.

The study was led by researchers at the University of Surrey’s Surrey Space Center.

What made GLE 77 scientifically extraordinary was not just its size. It was also the fact that an unprecedented array of instruments was watching when it happened.

Four newly commissioned ground-level neutron monitors across the UK – in Lerwick, Guildford, Lancaster, and Camborne – were already running.

Weather balloons carrying compact radiation sensors were launched within minutes of the alerts.

Two Boeing 777s on transatlantic routes out of London Heathrow were fitted with the same type of sensor.

“For me, the most exciting moment was watching the event unfold in real time,” said co-lead author Ben Clewer from the Space Center.

“We started getting alerts from neutron monitors around the world suggesting something unusual might be happening and, within minutes, the balloon launches were triggered.”

“We were then watching measurements come back live from the balloons, aircraft, and ground sensors as the storm developed.”

“Having all those systems operating together during the largest observed Ground Level Enhancement in almost 20 years gave us an incredibly rare dataset and the most complete picture we’ve ever had of this type of event.”

The data revealed something that uniform models of radiation risk have difficulty capturing. It showed that geography matters enormously, and it matters fast.

At Lerwick the neutron monitor recorded a 150% increase above background levels at the event’s peak. That happened because the site is the closest to the North Pole, where radiation hits hardest.

Lancaster, 700 kilometers to the south, saw a 24% increase. Guildford and Camborne, further south still, saw increases of 14% and 12% respectively.

The event was not washing evenly across the atmosphere. It was landing with far greater intensity in the north, and fading as latitude decreased.

The balloon data made the altitude dimension equally vivid. Business jets typically fly higher than commercial aircraft.

At these altitudes, radiation levels above Lerwick exceeded the International Civil Aviation Organization’s (ICAO) “moderate” advisory threshold several hours after the storm peaked.

At altitudes associated with supersonic flight, readings exceeded the ICAO’s ‘severe’ threshold, by a factor of two.

“The first balloon launch from Lerwick recorded an extraordinary radiation environment high in the atmosphere,” said co-lead author Fraser Baird from the Space Center.

“It shows just how geographically variable these events can be, and why having coordinated monitoring systems in place is so important.”

The commercial flights told a more muted story – but not a reassuring one.

The Heathrow-to-JFK service that departed at 11:45 UTC, during the event, recorded a 42.6% increase in total ambient dose equivalent compared to typical background conditions on the same route.

The later Heathrow-to-Newark flight, departing at 17:30 UTC, showed no significant increase. This suggests that timing relative to the storm’s peak matters as much as geography.

Neither flight crossed the ICAO threshold for issuing a radiation advisory.  Yet, the earlier flight came closer than current models would necessarily predict.

That gap is the paper’s most important finding for aviation safety.

Current ICAO advisory systems are built on model outputs, not direct atmospheric measurements.

They were not designed to capture the kind of sharp geographic and temporal variation that GLE 77 revealed.

Under the conditions observed above Lerwick, no international radiation advisories were issued for aircraft.

“The data received during this event, both from the monitors on the ground and from the balloon launches, are vital for our understanding of how space weather impacts radiation levels throughout Earth’s atmosphere,” said Krista Hammond, the Met Office Space Weather Manager.

“This is the first time radiation data has been collected through the atmosphere during a space weather event in this way and this represents a big leap forward in scientific understanding and will make a real difference to our space weather forecasting capability.”

The significance of that last point is growing. Airlines are flying more routes at high latitudes, where solar radiation exposure is highest.

Aircraft electronics are becoming more sensitive, not less. This increases vulnerability to the class of radiation-induced malfunctions that intense solar events can trigger.

An Airbus precautionary fleet action issued just over two weeks after GLE 77, related to radiation effects on A320-family aircraft, underscored that this is more than a theoretical concern.

GLE 77 was, by historical standards, a moderate event. The largest ever recorded, GLE 5 in 1956, produced neutron monitor increases of around 5,000%.

What November’s storm demonstrated is that even a relatively modest enhancement can produce radiation environments well above advisory thresholds in specific locations and at specific altitudes.

Unfortunately, the monitoring infrastructure to detect and respond to that in real time has, until now, simply not existed.

The dataset gathered on November 11, 2025, is the most comprehensive ever assembled during a ground level enhancement.

The researchers hope it will be used to validate and improve the forecasting models that aviation currently relies on.

The study is published in the journal Space Weather.

#5711. New Research Reveals a Hidden Weakness Inside Electric Vehicle Batteries

Researchers analyzing years of EV driving data discovered how differences between individual battery cells can intensify over time.

An electric vehicle (EV) battery can reach the end of its useful life even when many of its individual cells still have substantial capacity remaining. New research shows that a small number of rapidly aging cells can shorten the lifespan of an entire battery pack, leaving a significant share of its potential energy resources unused.

Published in Nature Energy, the study analyzed batteries operating in real vehicles over more than three years. Researchers found that differences between individual cells can reduce battery lifespan, restrict power output, and become more pronounced as vehicles accumulate mileage.

EV battery packs contain numerous cells that must work together to store and deliver energy. In a series-connected battery pack, however, the weakest cells can determine how much of the pack’s total capacity is actually available. During charging, a cell with reduced capacity may reach its maximum allowable voltage before the others. During discharge or high-power operation, a cell with greater internal resistance can reach its operating limits first.

These differences develop for several reasons, including variations in raw materials, manufacturing, cell grouping, temperature, and driving conditions. As cells age at different rates, the pack can eventually reach its retirement threshold even though many of its cells remain functional.

Led by Zhongwei Chen of the Dalian Institute of Chemical Physics (DICP) at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) and Changfu Zou of Chalmers University of Technology, the research also involved scientists from Beijing Institute of Technology and Zeekr Technology Europe AB.

The team examined electric passenger cars using nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) batteries and electric buses equipped with lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) batteries. Some vehicles had traveled as far as 300,000 kilometers (about 186,400 miles). Using routinely recorded voltage, current, temperature, and state-of-charge data, the researchers combined battery modeling with neural networks to estimate changes in individual cell capacity and internal resistance. Their approach accounted for differences in operating conditions and seasonal temperatures, allowing more consistent comparisons of battery aging.

In passenger cars, differences in cell health generally remained small at lower mileage. Once vehicles exceeded approximately 170,000 kilometers (about 105,600 miles), however, some cells in many battery packs began aging more rapidly, increasing the gap between stronger and weaker cells. The timing and severity of this deterioration varied among vehicles.

Using a common retirement threshold, the researchers calculated that uneven cell aging reduced usable pack-level state of health by 6.2% in passenger cars and 7.5% in buses. Compared with the average potential lifespan of their individual cells, battery packs had lifetimes that were 17.7% shorter in passenger cars and 22.8% shorter in buses.

Differences in internal resistance also reduced available power capability by 12.9% and 15.1%, respectively. By comparison, differences in state of charge typically reduced usable charged capacity by less than 2% under the charging and balancing strategies used in the studied vehicles.

Across their calculated lifetimes, passenger-car battery packs utilized just 80.7% of their potential energy resources, while bus batteries utilized 72.9%. Under the study’s retirement criteria, this meant that approximately 19.3% and 27.1% of potential energy resources, respectively, remained unused when the weakest cells reached their limits.

The findings suggest that battery lifespan cannot be accurately assessed using only the average condition of its cells. Comparisons between the vehicle fleets also indicate that battery balancing strategies may influence how differences between cells develop over time.

The researchers identified several potential improvements, including more consistent cell manufacturing, better cell grouping, advanced balancing controls, and improved thermal management. Reconfigurable battery systems, which could adjust how cells are connected and used, may offer another way to reduce the constraints imposed by weaker cells.

“Our findings show that the performance and lifetime of an EV battery pack are not determined solely by the average aging level of their cells, but can be strongly constrained by a small number of faster-aging cells,” said Prof. Chen. “By quantifying this weakest-cell effect under real-world EV operation, our study provides a basis for improving battery utilization, lifetime management, and system-level optimization.”

They then examined how temperatures, water vapor, atmospheric pressure, and UV light affected the formation of formaldehyde. Their simulation showed that the chemical dissociation of water by UV light in Mars' atmosphere would release the reactive hydrogen essential to form HâCO, which would fall to the surface in rain.

Because water influenced both the formation and distribution of HâCO, the researchers hypothesized that Mars' ancient water cycle may have determined where it would accumulate. The last step in the map's production was to plot these potential hotspots were in relation to landing sites for past Mars missions. Some areas stood out from than others, including mountainous regions like Tharsis and Elysium, which recieved about ten times the global average. Said Dr. Koyama:

By comparing our map with findings from rovers, we can begin to test whether places that received more HâCO were also more favorable for early life-related chemistry. If future observations confirm this relationship, our map could help identify promising targets for future Mars missions.

While this study does not show how much formaldehyde remains on the Martian surface today, the map could help in the selection of landing sites for future missions. In the meantime, it also provides hints as to how organic chemistry evolved on Mars billions of years ago, which could also help future missions refine their strategies for finding evidence of it today.

#5709. A Woman Jumped Off a Cruise Ship and Survived at Sea for 20 Hours. Here’s What Saved Her.

Rescues make headlines, but staying alive at sea comes down to a well-ordered checklist.

Although most of us spend our lives as landlubbers, we live on an ocean world. Just 29 percent of Earth’s crust rises above the water’s surface today. During the Archean eon—the planet’s tumultuous toddlerhood—almost nothing did. With some 8.3 billion of a certain kind of poorly-adapted mammal on board, it’s only a matter of time before one of us has an unfortunate run-in with those vast stretches of watery nothingness.

And in those moments, survival rests on circumstance, preparation, and a lot of luck.

This was certainly the case earlier this month when the Hellenic Coast Guard successfully rescued a 44-year-old El Salvadoran woman who jumped off a Royal Caribbean cruise ship because she “wanted to disembark” (you have to admire the confidence). The woman creatively “disembarked” from the Odyssey of the Seas around 10:30 a.m. local time while sailing through the Ionian Sea along Greece’s western coast. Twenty hours later, she was spotted bobbing along in a bright orange lifebuoy by a cargo ship assisting the search-and-rescue operation.

Despite what Hollywood would have you believe, a person’s immediate threat on the open water is not other animals. The ocean is so massive that the odds of a run-in with a predator is vanishingly thin, and even if such an encounter occurred, humans are not natural prey for these animals. As long as you limit thrashing to a minimum (a good idea in order to conserve energy while also not triggering a prey response), it’s unlikely you’ll wind up as dinner for some sharp-toothed monster of the deep.

Instead, survivalists often say that a person’s top four priorities when adrift at sea are securing freshwater, shelter, food, and a signaling device. In this particular instance, the woman’s saving grace was her bright orange lifebuoy, which helped her stand out, even at night, among the Mediterranean’s endlessly undulating waves. Without it, exposure and dehydration would have killed her.

Some have drifted for months, even years. The difference is that they usually go into the water with a vessel, supplies, or tools—unlike someone who simply goes overboard. In August, the Mexican Navy pulled two fishermen from the Pacific roughly 150 miles off Chiapas, where they’d spent five days floating in a cooler box after a wave sank their boat. They survived on nothing at all—no food, no water—which is precisely why five days was close to their limit. Longer ordeals demand more than endurance, and past instances of other daring feats of open ocean survival can provide some clues about what that entails.

For example, a little over a decade ago, Jose Alvarenga drifted in the open ocean for more than a year. He secured freshwater by devising ways of trapping rainwater, and when storms proved scarce, he relied on drinking the blood of turtles captured by hand. Duke University medical specialist Claude Piantadosi, speaking with National Geographic after Alvarenga’s shocking rescue in 2014, said turtles likely helped the adrift survivor stave off another well-known illness that’s plagued seafarers for millennia.

“The availability of fresh turtle or bird meat—for example, the liver—would have prevented him from getting scurvy,” he said. “Blood from birds and turtles is a good source of water—70 percent water—and has no more salt than human plasma.”

Piantadosi also mentioned that drinking urine can offer a method of last resort, since you’re re-ingesting salt already eliminated by your kidneys. This fact also points to why you can’t just drink the endless seawater around you to stay hydrated. According to NOAA, human kidneys can only make urine less salty than salt water, so it’d take even more freshwater to get rid of the excess salt, making you more thirsty and dehydrated in the process.

So if you find yourself adrift in the open ocean, not all hope is lost. Secure a source of freshwater, provide shelter from the sun, limit activity to store energy, and signal to passing ships and aircraft if possible. With some forethought—and a little bit of luck—you’ll come out the other side a survivor.

#5706. Many American diets may not include enough of this essential nutrient

Salt iodization was a public health victory that calls for renewed attention today

Iodized salt hit store shelves in 1924 in the United States, and companies heralded the health benefits, like in this 1925 Morton’s Salt Company ad. The body needs iodine to make thyroid hormones. Iodine deficiency can cause an enlarged thyroid gland, called a goiter.

Grocery store shelves are bursting with fancy salts: Himalayan, kosher, sea salt and more. Considering these choices, it can be easy to forget about the public health victory found in basic, iodized table salt.

The nutrient iodine is a crucial component of thyroid hormones, necessary for fetal and early childhood brain development and health throughout life. At the start of the last century, iodine deficiency was a rampant and visible public health problem in the United States, with the telltale sign of enlarged thyroid glands, called goiters, especially prevalent in much of the north. A series of steps taken by a medical society in Michigan and U.S. salt producers brought iodized salt to tables across the country, and by the 1950s, occurrences of goiters had dropped precipitously.

But more recently, the United States appears to be backsliding on iodine nutrition. In 2017 and 2018, 24 percent of women and 30 percent of men, aged 14 and older, weren’t getting an adequate amount of the nutrient. That’s about twice as many as in 2001 and 2002, researchers reported in the Journal of Nutrition in 2025. Breaking the data down further, nearly half of pregnant women haven’t been getting enough iodine. The study reflects an estimated, short-term look at population iodine levels.

Long-term, a consistent dearth of iodine robs the body of the ability to produce sufficient amounts of thyroid hormones. If iodine levels in the population continue to trend down, that could eventually result in more goiters and harm brain development in children.

“The group that has the most severe potential consequences of iodine deficiency is pregnant women,” says Elizabeth Pearce, an endocrinologist at Boston University Chobanian & Avedisian School of Medicine. “Even mild degrees of iodine deficiency in pregnancy may be associated with measurably lower IQ in children.”

Yet women of reproductive age, at the U.S. population level, are on the cusp of mild iodine deficiency, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention stated in its 2026 Nutrition Report.

With all of the ways that diets, salt intake and noniodized options have changed over time, the importance of iodine nutrition may not be top-of-mind for the general public like it was a century ago. “Iodine deficiency is the leading cause worldwide of preventable developmental delay,” says Monica Serrano-Gonzalez, a pediatric endocrinologist at Hasbro Children’s and Brown University’s Warren Alpert Medical School in Providence, R.I. “People have kind of forgotten this is a risk.”

When iodine deficiency keeps the thyroid gland from making enough hormones, the body calls for more, which stimulates and enlarges the gland. That supersized gland, called a goiter, bulges at the neck. In the United States, goiters were extremely common a century ago in the Northwest, Great Lakes and Appalachians; together, the area became known as the “goiter belt.” Iodine naturally occurs in seawater and soil. But in the noncoastal northern part of the country, soils and water sources have little iodine, so the crops produced in these inland areas lack the nutrient.

An examining physician for the World War I draft reported in 1918 that 30 percent of almost 600 men from a northern Michigan county had goiters, some large enough to be disqualifying. A 1922 Michigan Department of Health study of schoolchildren in four counties revealed that close to 50 percent had goiters. “It would have been a really visible public health problem at that time,” Pearce says.

In response to the state’s iodine issues, the Michigan State Medical Society recommended the addition of the nutrient to table salt. The minimal expense for iodization meant this common kitchen staple would remain affordable. Companies introduced iodized salt in Michigan in the spring of 1924. By the fall, the Morton Salt Company, a major producer, was selling iodized salt nationwide. Over time, the goiters of the “belt” dwindled.

Salt iodization has been linked to rising IQ in the United States. For example, for men born in areas without natural sources of iodine, there was a significant jump in World War II Air Force acceptances, based on a qualifying exam, after widespread salt iodization. The increase did not occur among men born in areas with adequate natural sources of the nutrient, researchers reported in the Journal of the European Economic Association in 2017.

Adding iodine to salt was never mandated by the U.S. government. But for a long time, the voluntary approach — combined with how most people consumed salt and other dietary habits — did the trick to get people enough iodine. Today, different meal practices and available food options are tearing holes in the iodine safety net. “Iodized salt is important, but it may not be reaching everybody anymore,” Pearce says.

There are many more noniodized salt options today, with one analysis reporting that only 53 percent of table salt sold in U.S. stores is iodized. People get much of their salt from prepared, processed and packaged foods, which generally are made with noniodized salt. Milk can provide iodine, due to iodine-supplemented feed and the use of an iodine cleanser on cows’ udders. But the amount of iodine in milk can vary greatly, a recent analysis found, probably because industry practices can differ. Diets that restrict dairy may also put people at risk of consuming too little iodine.

The U.S. recommended dietary allowance for iodine for adults is 150 micrograms per day, which rises to 220 mcg for pregnant people and 290 mcg for those breastfeeding. The amount of the nutrient that iodized salt contains can vary, but will be listed on the container’s label. Based on a 2023 federal report on food iodine content, one teaspoon of salt has around 300 mcg of added iodine. (The American Heart Association recommends a limit of 2,300 milligrams of sodium per day; one teaspoon of salt has 2,400 mg of sodium.)

The American Thyroid Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend that pregnant and breastfeeding people, as well as those planning to become pregnant, take a daily supplement with 150 micrograms of iodine. Sufficient iodine is especially important in the first trimester of pregnancy, when a developing embryo relies solely on the mother’s supply.

When choosing table salt, “look for salt that’s iodized,” Pearce says. For people on a low-salt diet, it’s better to cut out packaged and prepared foods, Serrano-Gonzalez says. “We do want to have moderation with salt intake, but we need a little bit” of iodized salt, she says.

People have largely forgotten why it’s important to choose iodized salt, Pearce says. It’s not a decision that many people have to make globally: One hundred and twenty-six countries mandate salt iodization, while only 21 have voluntary polices.

An observational study of an iodine crisis in Ethiopia indicates the catastrophic consequences of losing the nutrient. The country adopted a salt iodization policy in the 1990s, and by 1995, 80 percent of households reported using iodized salt. But the country’s stock came from Eritrea, and in 1998, a conflict between the two countries closed the border and interrupted the supply of iodized salt. By 2005, the Ethiopian population was severely iodine deficient, with up to 40 percent of school-aged children and 36 percent of women of reproductive age developing goiters. Ethiopia’s government was able to reinstate mandatory salt iodization in 2012, and household coverage climbed to 85 percent by 2015.

But the severe iodine disruption during the war had a devastating effect on the children born and raised in that period. In rural areas, which had very low soil iodine levels, mortality among children in the first two years of life reached 25 to 30 percent, compared with 10 percent in other areas, researchers reported in Nature Food in September. The children from rural areas who reached secondary school scored lower on national exams. The study team took other effects of the conflict into account, to focus on the impact of the iodine nutrition emergency.

In the United States, the worst manifestation of iodine deficiency was the long ago “goiter belt.” “There needs to be public health education,” Pearce says, “so people understand again what the dangers are if we don’t have adequate iodine nutrition, and how to just ensure that they’re protected.”

#5703. Mars’s Most Mysterious Cloud Might Be Defying Textbook Cloud Physics

It may not be as obvious, but just like Earth, Mars has seasons. And every day of every summer, an 1,800 kilometer long thin white cloud appears to the west of Arsia Mons, a 20 kilometer high extinct shield volcano. At least for a few hours, before it dissipates until it reappears the next morning. Scientists have known about this feature, which they call the Arsia Mons Elongated Cloud (AMEC), for decades, but planetary scientists have been unable to explain it using traditional atmospheric models. But a new paper, published in Nature Geoscience by a research team led by Dr. Jorge HernÃ¡ndez-Bernal of the Sorbonne, suggests that the cloud could be the first documented real-world example of a cloud flash-freezing out of thin air.

That phenomena, known as homogeneous nucleation, has been theorized in text books for years. However, scientists have long thought of it as only a laboratory curiosity rather than something that naturally happens in nature. To understand why, it's first best to review the high school physics explanation of how clouds form.

On planets like Earth, when humid air rises, it expands and cools, simultaneously driving up its relative humidity. However, water vapor molecules are notoriously reluctant to clump together themselves. They have gigantic surface tensions that cause them to evaporate almost as soon as they form. Unless they have something to grab on to.

Video describing a NASA citizen science project to track Martian clouds. Credit - SciStarter YouTube Channel

That âsomethingâ usually takes the form of a particle in the atmosphere. Through a process called heterogeneous nucleation, the water vapor condenses onto microscopic airborne seeds known as condensation nuclei, which serve as the building blocks of clouds. On Earth, these take the form of anything from sea salt crystals to industrial pollution. Mars is notoriously dusty and routinely swept by global dust storms, so scientists naturally assumed that those dust grains supplied the condensation nuclei needed to grow the AMEC.

However, when scientists have run atmospheric simulations trying to reproduce AMEC using traditional cloud formation theories, they have come up short. They would either produce wide patches of haze, or no trailing tale reaching thousands of kilometers at all. Researchers could not explain how such a thin, intensely bright streak of water could remain stable across thousands of miles of open Martian desert.

If you read the textbooks closely enough, though, the scientists have long understood that homogeneous nucleation, i.e. cloud formation without a particle, is theoretically possible. In this scenario, water molecules collide with enough brute force to create liquid droplets or ice seeds without needing any preexisting molecule to coalesce around. But, it requires absurd levels of humidity, making it an interesting laboratory-only result to scientists who believed that there was always so much dust available in atmospheres that the heterogeneous nucleation weâre familiar with on Earth undercut the formation of any droplets formed by simply spontaneously forming.

The new study shows that the atmospheric environment surrounding Arsia Mons in the summer is actually strong enough to break that rule. The researchers used a combination of weather models and specialized microphysics packages from a software known as the Mars Planetary Climate Model, to show what specifically happens surrounding Arsia Mons in the summer.

First, there are notable westward winds that hit the shield volcano straight on, deflecting upward violently enough to generate a phenomenon called gravity waves - standing ripples in the atmosphere that represent gigantic pressure shifts. As air moves through these waves, it experiences extreme adiabatic expansion, and cools by about 30â in less than fifteen minutes, superchilling to below -143â.

In the summer, the Martian atmosphere is relatively dust-free, with only 0.1 dust grains per cubic centimeter. That means thereâs relatively little dust to soak up the vapor quickly, which then means the humidity in the area skyrockets to around ten million percent. That is exactly the type of environment where homogenous nucleation can happen. At this point, water vapor molecules hit a physical breaking point and spontaneously freeze into hundreds of ice crystals per cubic centimeter. In other words, they create an ice cloud. Thatâs where the wind takes over, blowing these newly formed ice particles to travel outside of the freezing pocket and into more than a thousand mile long pencil thin tail of cloud without dissipating.

Showing that this process can, in fact, happen in a planetary atmosphere opens up other possibilities for understanding where else it might occur. From the icy plumes of Enceladus to our own polar mesosphere, there are other places in the solar system that, at least in theory, could have the same absurdly high relative humidity levels with relatively low dust particle counts. But for now, this brilliant white streak on Mars is the only known natural example of an extremely rare cloud-forming phenomena. It just might not remain that way for long.

#5702. Are Tesla’s claims for its new ‘flying’ Roadster real, or is it just full of cold gas?

Tesla has finally re-unveiled its next-generation Roadster, originally unveiled in quite different form back in 2017. So since I did a technical breakdown of how it would work last time, I thought I’d give a quick update with what we know about the new version.

(Update: Now that various media videos are out showing a tour of the car, we’re updating this article with answers to some of the questions that were not answered in the product demo).

Back in 2017, the Roadster was unveiled as the car that would “give a hard-core smack down to gasoline powered cars.”

At the time, electric cars had already proven themselves with extremely quick acceleration numbers. But there weren’t many true electric sportscars (except, of course, the original Tesla Roadster from 2008, the car that started it all).

The next-gen Roadster was intended to change that, with a smaller, low-to-the-ground car built for speed, rather than comfort or efficiency. Of course, you’d still get some of those other goals as with any EV, but the idea was to win races, to beat gas cars around a track.

And then…. a decade happened. It’s been almost 9 years since that car was first shown off, and Tesla has not released it. Meanwhile, plenty of other companies have released their own electric sportscars, and set records in doing so.

The Rimac Nevera R hit 300km/h (186mph) a full 3.5 seconds faster than a Bugatti Chiron Super Sport, the Xiaomi SU7 Ultra – made by a smartphone company – set a Nürburgring record, and Porsche went even faster, electrically… but neither of those touch the Lotus Evija X, with the third-fastest time of any vehicle ever. And another Chinese company, BYD, showed off that its car can jump, an artifact of its active suspension system.

There are even some tricky, fully track-focused EVs that have beaten every car ever with truly videogame-like performance, and the ability to drive upside down – you can even buy it.

So the path Tesla intended to forge is looking less like an untamed wild, and more like a well-tread trail at this point. Maybe everyone hasn’t marched on it, but Tesla is no longer the first.

But Tesla has had the most impressive demo, you have to give it that.

In its demo, it made some wild claims about the car – and then backed them up. CEO ELon Musk said the car would be able to fly, and it did. It did a flip in the air as well. It accelerated from 0-60 in under 1 second, a number that no other car has gotten even close to. It drove upside down.

How did it do all these things, and what do they mean? Will they help the car beat every track record around the world, as Musk promised tonight? Well, it’s time for a quick primer on vehicle dynamics.

(For the purposes of this article, we’re going to treat everything that happened tonight as if it was real, not faked. While a lot of it looked surprising, we do know that fan cars are real, rocket boosters could increase acceleration, and we’ve seen flying machines that self-right with small gas thrusters, so it’s all plausible enough, even if the car’s movements in the air looked much quicker than you’d expect out of a car. Besides, it’s lazy to just say “it’s all fake” – we’d rather try to examine what seems plausible, and what doesn’t, and just use this as a jumping off point for a discussion about vehicle dynamics. So strap in.)

The first demo showed the Roadster driving up a hot wheels-like track, going fully vertical, and then upside down.

This was accomplished through a “fan car” system, like the McMurtry Speirling, the other car which drove upside down and was mentioned above. Tesla calls it a “multi-mode suction system.”

Fans on the vehicle are used to create a low pressure zone underneath the car, causing ambient air pressure to push the car against the surface it’s stuck to. This is enabled by skirts underneath the vehicle which seal off the vehicle underside so air can’t ingress into the low pressure zone.

This was described in a patent, and is a clever improvement on McMurtry’s system and previous fan car systems, as it allows the skirts to be adjusted so the car can be used in a wider array of circumstances other than smooth track surfaces. For example, at high speeds, the front and rear skirts are lifted and fans are used to maintain smooth airflow under the vehicle.

Each of the four fans can apparently pull ~20kW of power, so a total of 80kW, or 108hp, is being used just sucking the car to the ground.

The downforce numbers on Tesla’s display are incredibly high, and seem potentially unreal to me. There’s no need for 4,700kg of downforce on what is apparently a sub-1,700kg car (~3,700lb, that’s nearly a thousand pounds heavier than the original Roadster was).

In comparison to the McMurtry Speirling, that car is around 1,000kg and pulls around 2,000kg of downforce to do the things it does. So Tesla is not just running more ridiculous downforce numbers, but also running a larger ratio of mass to downforce than McMurtry’s car. It’s not completely out of the question that it’s running 4,700kg, but that number is still surprisingly large to me and I wouldn’t be surprised if some liberties were taken.

A hint can be seen in other statements about the Roadster’s capabilities – Tesla says it will be capable cornering at over 2G, which is plenty impressive enough, though the Speirling claims it can pull up to 3 lateral Gs in a corner. (high-performance cars can usually pull somewhere from 1-1.2 lateral Gs)

Also: it’s incredibly loud. At the event, Tesla had attendees who were hundreds of feet away wear earplugs as the car went up the ramp. That definitely defeats one of the benefits of EVs, and we wonder what it will sound like inside the car.

The next trick involved driving up the ramp, jumping off the end and then landing.

This uses the vehicle’s “cold gas thrusters” that have been placed around the car.

Tesla announced separately that the car would come with three trim levels: Performance, Thrust, and Rocket (and, apparently, there will be a “base” model at some point?). Rocket is the only one that is “air ready,” and has cold gas thrusters mounted around the vehicle, including underneath, which allow it to fly for short distances.

This is done by compressing air and holding it in tanks onboard the vehicle. The Thrust trim has 200L of storage, and the Rocket trim sacrifices the second seat for an additional 400L of storage (which does seem like it is theoretically enough energy stored to do the stunts Tesla did). The Rocket trim also adds one downward-pointing thruster at each corner, and two thrusters at the front for yaw.

Admittedly, I know less about rockets than I do about car dynamics, so I have little useful to say about how realistic it is to hold enough gas to produce enough force to do these stunts with a full-size vehicle. But I can tell you what benefits they would provide if they do work as Tesla says they will.

Tesla also showed off the car doing a “kickflip” over the rest of its vehicles, which uses the same technology as the jump did.

This made for an impressive demo, but offers no real performance benefit in terms of driving.

The fan car system provides additional downforce – this is because tires can only work so hard, and adding apparent weight to a car while maintaining its mass (yes, these are two different things) can make those tires work harder. This helps in basically anything you would want to use tires for – so, y’know, every aspect of vehicle performance.

But rockets that push the car upwards do not do any such thing. Those provide “upforce,” which is a word that nobody has uttered because it’s not something you want –  unless you’re trying to play a trick on Mark Webber. Pushing the car upwards just makes the tires work less, and hurts performance by a lot.

Worse, including the thrusters for this system adds mass and complexity. That means another thing that can break, and another thing that slows the vehicle down. As Musk said, Acceleration = Force / Mass, so if you add mass, you are decreasing acceleration.

So the jumping is just a party trick, and will not help with performance.

However, the side thrusters could add cornering capability. Downforce makes tires work harder, which is good, but another thing you can do is just not make the tires work at all. By shooting air out of the side of the car during a corner, you are adding lateral acceleration through something other than the tires, so even when the tires are maxed out in the amount of lateral acceleration they can provide, your jets are providing a little more.

(The car will also have steer-by-wire, which should help make cornering easier for a driver, allowing tight turns with less steering input at lower speeds)

So the jump is dumb, but the side thrusters could do something. Which leads us to the more important thrusters: on the rear.

Then Tesla showed a recorded 0-60 test utilizing rear thrusters, wherein the vehicle reached 60mph in 1 second.

Later in the show, Tesla did a live demonstration, and claimed that the car hit 60mph in 0.81 seconds, after some technical difficulties with the timer. Tesla later said that the top-spec version of the car would be able to hit 0-60 in 0.55 seconds with thrusters, suction, and its “over 1MW” powertrain active. The “Thrust” model does it in 0.85s, and the “Performance” model does it in an interminable 1.6s.

Cold gas thrusters strike again here, with four ports at the rear of the vehicle that push a huge plume of gas behind it. These produce 80 kilo-Newtons of force, a number that is used to measure rocket force but does not translate well to horsepower.

Much as described above, if you are at the limit of what tires can do, you can try to find force some other way. The thrusters push the car forward even when the tires can’t push any harder, which enables the car to essentially break the same physics laws that every other car is limited by.

The “fan car” system does help with forward acceleration too, the same way it helps with all other types of lateral acceleration. But the rockets out the back are what give it that extra oomph, allowing it to hit 60mph in under a second, and seemingly in only a few car lengths worth of distance.

Importantly, the 0.55s time includes a “one foot rollout,” where the timer doesn’t start counting until the car starts moving at least a foot.

That might matter more than usual here, because in the demo, Tesla clearly activated the thrusters well in advance of when the car started moving, as can be seen in slow-motion video shown during the event here.

If the thrusters take some time to “wind up,” then they’re less useful for certain types of acceleration tasks – and give back some of the benefits of electric propulsion, the instant torque. Hopefully Tesla has not just invented the electric version of turbo lag.

The car has an adjustable rear wing, which is perhaps the most “traditional” performance improvement on it.

These exist in many vehicles these days, and solve the primary problem of wings.

When people talk about “coefficient of drag” or Cd, which has become a popular stat to quote on an EV, everyone accepts that a lower number is better. If your car makes less drag, it’s more efficient, which means it can go farther with the energy it has. (cross-sectional area also counts – a large car with a low Cd can still lose more energy to drag than a small car with a lower Cd, because a larger car simply pushes more air around).

But drag sometimes offers a performance benefit. Formula One cars actually have extremely high drag coefficients – the most efficient EVs get close to 0.2 Cd, but an F1 car can have a drag coefficient of 0.7 or even 1.0 or higher.

This is because F1 cars actually use all that air for downforce. Instead of letting the air flow smoothly around the vehicle, they actively try to push the air upwards, which in turn pushes the car downwards (Newton’s third law of motion, equal and opposite reactions), which allows the tires to work better.

The problem is that when the car is going in a straight line, it doesn’t need that much downforce. At top speed, that downforce is just drag, which slows the car down.

So, active aerodynamics solves this problem. If you can put up a wing to push air down when you want it, then hide the wing so it lets the air fly by at higher speeds, you can have the best of both worlds. More downforce when cornering, and less drag at high speed.

The concept overall is called “aerodynamic efficiency.” How much downforce you can get through a certain method without adding more drag. A wing offers good downforce, but lots of drag – an active wing helps eliminate the drag downside. (and going back to the fan car system above, that offers a lot of downforce with little drag)

That’s not the only active wing on the vehicle – there’s a hidden front wing inside the front air intake which can redirect air underneath the vehicle, the panel behind the front wheel pops out (serving as a bargeboard), and the front hood conceals a hidden vent (similar in position to the cooling vent for the original Roadster). That’s a total of five active airflow surfaces at the front of the vehicle, where controlling air is even more important than at the rear.

At the original Roadster unveiling in 2017, Tesla said the car would have a 600 mile range. This made headlines, and people thought it was a big deal, or thought it was impossible that the car would have so much more range than everything else on the market.

But range numbers don’t really matter. It’s entirely possible to make an EV with a thousand mile range, but the problem is that car would be terrible. You’d have to spend too much space on batteries, they’d be too heavy, they’d be expensive, and you’d get diminishing returns because the more battery you add, the more battery you have to add to move the mass of that battery around, etc.

So 600 miles of range was kind of bad news for the Roadster. The main reason it had so much range is because EV batteries have a careful balance of many considerations – energy density, power density, cycle life, temperature response, charging speed, and so on and so forth.

At the time, Tesla’s chosen chemistry was rather high on energy density, but comparatively lower on power density. Energy is how much range you have in the battery, and power is how quickly you can get that energy out of it.

So, similar to needing to add more batteries to get more energy onboard the car, Tesla also had to add more batteries to get more power out of the car. If Tesla wanted to get the Roadster up to 1,000hp+, it would need a massive battery – and that was the point behind the “600 mile range.”

Now, we’re hearing that the new version of the next-gen Roadster will have a smaller battery, with Tesla only saying it will have a range of “over 300 miles.”

We don’t know if this is because the car will have less power than originally planned, or if Tesla has managed to get more power out of its cells… but it’s probably a bit of both.

It’s likely relying on its boosters and fan car system to pick up the performance slack and reach the absurd numbers Tesla is promising, and realizes that a gigantic battery is not as necessary once you have other ways to push the wheels around.

The location of the battery is of interest too – in Tesla’s photos, the seating position looks quite high, which suggests that the battery is low in the vehicle, as is the case on most of Tesla’s other vehicles. (Update: The battery is on the bottom, and is a 4680-format cell)

This offers a lower center of gravity, which helps with cornering. But it also means a higher seating position, which feels less “sporty.” The original 2008 Roadster actually had the battery placed behind the cockpit, leading vehicle balance to feel like a mid-engined car. The upcoming Porsche 718 EV has a similar configuration.

A low-slung battery will make the Roadster’s cornering feel more neutral, but perhaps less “fun” than a tail-heavy vehicle. Between that and the fan car system, it will probably feel like it’s on rails – though rear-wheel drive will make it feel like a proper sportscar.

Tesla does quote a “10-minute charge time” from 15-80% on an 800-1000V battery pack, which is faster than Tesla’s other vehicles, and puts it about on par with the Mercedes AMG GT. But it hasn’t told us how many kWh the battery will have. It does say that the car will be able to do 25-30 minutes at max power on a track, which is about as long as a normal track day session lasts anyway.

Edit: On further examination, a couple frames of the “kickflip” portion of the video show the underbody of the car.

This does not look like Tesla’s typical underbody, which is usually flat, as the battery is there. An underbody with lots of parts hanging off of it is bad for aerodynamics, so this is one of the benefits of putting a battery on the bottom of an EV.

This frame also leads us to the possibility that this car may not have had a battery in it at all. Which could help to explain the car’s fast movements in the air, if it was a thousand pounds lighter than a production vehicle might be… and also another reason that nobody was allowed near the kick-flipping cars to examine them closely. So I know I said at the start that I would take this whole thing seriously, but keep your conspiracy hats handy, boys and girls.

Tesla quotes top speed at “over” 230mph for the Rocket version of the car, but we’re skeptical that the rockets will have much effect at top speed. The Thrust model has a top speed of 230mph, and the Performance model has a top speed of 210mph.

At low speed, traction from the tires is the limiting factor for acceleration in today’s most powerful EVs, such that adding more power doesn’t help much if your tires can’t translate that to the ground – but the thrusters can get around that. But at high speeds, you’re limited more by the horsepower needed to overcome air drag, which goes up exponentially with speed. You still need special rated tires for high speed operation, but they are no longer your main limiting factor, air drag is.

So the benefit from thrusters seems less significant at high speeds, to me. You could use the rear thrusters for a boost when you reach top speed and get a few more mph at the end of a straight, but then as soon as you run out of gas or have to hit the brakes for a turn, you bleed that speed away. Whereas if you had hit the thrusters briefly during a turn, that could increase your exit speed, and then you carry that extra exit speed all through the straight – giving a better laptime benefit overall.

As for top speed, when Tesla claims the Rocket trim can go 230mph, but doesn’t quote a top speed for other trims, this could be due to some other factor, as I doubt the rockets have a lot to do with top speed.

Perhaps the Rocket trim is lighter because of the removal of the second seat (though it loses some of that mass reduction through the addition of tanks + useless downward-pointing thrusters), perhaps it comes with higher-rated tires or a more lenient software governor, or maybe it uses a different or larger battery than the other models. We just don’t know yet, but we hope to get more information soon.

(During the presentation, Musk also mused if the car should be able to reach at least half the speed of sound, which seemed to surprise his co-presenters, designer Franz von Holzhausen and engineer Lars Moravy – this would be ~380mph at sea level, which is unlikely, given the current record is 308.34mph, with an asterisk)

Another company, Dreame, a Chinese robot vacuum maker, also proposed using thrusters on an electric hypercar – but it was going to use solid rocket boosters rather than cold gas thrusters. Those would be one-time-use, and need to be recharged each time. It would have made 100kN of thrust and hit 0-60 in 0.9 seconds, but Dreame cancelled the project, which frankly seemed inevitable.

A cold gas thruster is a much better idea, since it doesn’t need to be recharged with solid rocket fuel each time.

But it does need to fill its tanks with pressurized air, which currently takes six or seven hours, though Tesla thinks it can get that number down to 30 minutes. This means you’ll only be using it on one lap – or maybe only on a few turns, since as best we can tell, the thruster system can only run for a few seconds at full power.

Tesla seems to think it will be enough to set track records, but that seems like it will already be possible via use of the fan car system. McMurtry is setting track records with its Speirling, and it doesn’t have any thrusters out the back or sides. (Both the Roadster and the Speirling are rear-wheel drive, despite that all-wheel drive might be better for record-chasing)

Finally, will you even be able to use them at all on anything other than a closed course? In today’s demo, the crowd was hundreds of meters away, which is a fair precaution for the first demonstration of a car doing a kickflip.

But it wasn’t just the kickflip that was concerning. The plume of gas sprayed out of the back of the car on acceleration, or on turning, seemed massive. It went tens of meters away.

So it certainly doesn’t seem like it should usable on public roads, as debris from the road would be kicked up into other cars, pedestrians, or whatever. Tesla says the “Rocket” is invite-only, and is “not approved for public road use.” But it says nothing under the “Thrust” trim entry, despite that it has the rear thrusters, which could cause plenty of damage.

Even on a closed track, you don’t want to be blowing tire marbles (discarded bits of rubber from tires) into a crowd beside the track, or knocking over marshals or braking boards or blowing all the hair off of too-adventurous squirrels that get too close to the track. It just seems impractical.

Heck, can a human even use these systems at all? For the demos, nobody was in the car. The movements in the air seemed incredibly rapid, much more rapid than you’d expect any several-thousand-pound object would be able to move in the air.

The speed of the acceleration displayed in the air would be at minimum disorienting to any average person. This is the sort of thing that fighter pilots train for years to do. And we’re just going to let people buy the car with Rocket mode and operate it, even as an “invite-only” product? Where? How? What happens if they have a medical episode? Will cars come with a G-suit to stop you from blacking out while operating flight mode? What happens to that compressed air in a crash?

All in all, now that we have a little more information about the capacity and capabilities of the thrust system, I’m going to put my money on the Performance model being the best yet-announced version of this car. Even though its acceleration numbers seem lower, and it has a lower top speed (probably software limited), it will save weight by not having the big thrust tank placed high in the vehicle. That tank isn’t going to help laptimes because you’ll only get a few seconds of thrust, then be dragging around a bunch of metal and tubes around the rest of the lap. The suction system will be much more effective for lowering laptimes, and that’s included on the Performance model.

Regardless of whether the demo was fake or not, I doubt this car ever sees the end of a production line. Musk probably filed months ago to sell additional Tesla shares in the week following this reveal. The only thing Tesla is selling consumers is hype. Self driving cars that aren't actually self driving. Self-driving cabs with the same problem. Humanoid robots with no customers. And now this.

The only products they've released of late that actually match consumer demand is the Model Y L and the Semi (well, they announced that production has started, anyways.)

But then, the question of practicality is somewhat moot. With the various mentions of SpaceX during today’s presentation, it seems clear that one major motivating factor behind Tesla’s recent product announcements is to push the idea of a SpaceX/Tesla merger, which would allow Musk to gain more power/money without actually accomplishing the goals required of him.

And besides, we have seen a reveal of a product that never actually made its way to public sales, which existed for the purpose of selling a merger before… so we wouldn’t count on that “2028, actually” shipping time promise.

So… there you go, that’s a quick reaction/explainer about the various abilities shown off, why they exist, and how practical they seem. #5701. The Best Things to Do in the Berkshires—and Where to Stay

Like much of Massachusetts, the Berkshires are deeply rooted in history. Great American names in art and culture—Melville, Hawthorne, Wharton, Rockwell—have called it home, and the creative spirit is truly alive and well here. I first visited the Berkshires in college to perform at what I can only call a very Western Massachusetts modern dance festival and have had the privilege of visiting the home of dear friends from Boston across many years and seasons since. Unlike the nearby Hudson Valley (too cool?) or Litchfield County, Connecticut (too tradwife?), where so many New Yorkers decamp from the city, a few days among the rolling hills of the Berkshires has always left me feeling completely reset.

Technically, Berkshire County is the westernmost slice of the state, though in practice you’ll probably find that application is slightly more generous. There are several larger towns to anchor your visit around: Great Barrington, Lenox, and Stockbridge in the west, North Adams in the northeast. Drive about an hour to Hudson, New York, in one direction or around two hours to Boston in the other. In between, you’ll find hiking and skiing, venerable cultural institutions, farm-to-table food and historic landmarks to keep you entertained, all set against a lush, pastoral backdrop. —Rachel Fletcher

Waterfront cabins at Prospect Berkshires are an idyllic respite for city dwellers.

One of the latest additions to the region, Prospect Berkshires, opened in 2025 in the delightfully sleepy Egremont, and is designed to embrace nature as its number one amenity. Forty-nine individual, Scandi-inspired cabins are situated along Prospect Lake, where swimming, kayaking and paddleboarding are provided for and encouraged. Made for the active traveler, saunas are peppered throughout the property—including two directly on the lake—as are a tennis court and a freshly finished pool.

Just down the road (and technically in New York state), Little Cat Lodge has a slightly more alpine-chic-meets-motor-lodge vibe. In nearby Richmond, Massachusetts, The Inn at Kenmore Hall is a boutique hotel in the form of a stately Georgian home, owned by the former head of menswear at J.Crew, designer Frank Muytjens and his partner, restaurateur Edward Scott Cole.

For something more traditional, the Red Lion Inn in Stockbridge is a well-known haunt to both tourists and locals that has been in operation since 1773. Though it was destroyed in a fire and then rebuilt at the turn of the 20th century, it has housed many storied guests since, including multiple US presidents, Judy Garland, and Bob Dylan. It also has the distinct honor of being the former workplace of Real Housewives of New York City alum and longtime Berkshires resident Dorinda Medley.

If you’re looking to be closer to the art action, Tourists Welcome in North Adams is just a six minute drive from MASS MoCA and three minutes to Williams College. Here, the iconic roadside motel is given a woodsy—also Scandi leaning—makeover, and the property recently expanded beyond its 46 rooms with the opening of two nearby private homes, one five-bedroom and one two-bedroom.

Lenox, with its many boutique hotels, is another great place to call home in the Berkshires. Each of the rooms at interior maven (and Berkshire native) Annie Selke’s inn 33 Main has its own artistic flair and personality, and touches like complimentary breakfast and cocktail hour in the parlor make staying here feel truly convivial. Locally based couple Kelly and Bryan Binder run the nine-bedroom Doctor Sax House, and while the refreshed wall dressings and plush bedding don’t show it, the property dates back to the Gilded Age.

An aerial view of the sprawling Housatonic River Valley

We’ll get to all the nature and history shortly, but first let’s talk about newness in one of my favorite arenas: shopping. This July, Walker Street Market, a new design gallery with works from more than 70 contemporary artists, opened right on one of the main drags in Lenox. I had the opportunity to attend the opening and was delighted to mull over delicious smelling candles, handmade Japanese ceramics, and mind bogglingly cool baskets from Underwater Weaving. Founded by former fashion designer Matthew Ames, the impeccably curated space features rotating collections from some of the most exciting makers around.

The Berkshire Galleries in Great Barrington is another great stop for antiquing, and the town's Main Street is well-suited for a leisurely afternoon of strolling and shopping. There are plenty of shops for vintage and new clothing; Fluff Alpaca stocks cozy sweaters and throws in addition to more tailored pieces like blazers and pants, and One Mercantile features an eclectic selection of linens, soaps, and locally made gifts; next door, the team operates SETT, a kitchen goods store with high-quality dining and serving wares. Take a break at Fuel, a popular spot for breakfast sandwiches and coffee, or enjoy a cone outside of SoCo Creamery, a local ice cream parlor with classic and less-common flavors (think Marzipan Date Orange Zest, and Lemon Basil).

A visit to the Berkshires would not be complete, in my opinion, without a visit to Tanglewood or Jacob’s Pillow for some live music or dance, respectively. MASS MoCA is also a must-see; the former industrial space, impressive in its own right, is now one of the largest museums in the United States and is venerated for its contemporary programming. Further south in Stockbridge, the Norman Rockwell Museum in the artist's hometown has a collection of almost 1,000 original drawings and paintings.

Fans of the Shakers won’t be disappointed. In Pittsfield, Hancock Shaker Village is a wholesome, family-friendly affair with farm animals included. In 2028, the region will also be home to the Shaker Museum, a second destination dedicated to the group’s pursuits in Chatham, New York. In the meantime, the Shaker Outpost is hosting seasonal exhibitions.

The natural beauty in this part of Massachusetts really can’t be understated. The landscape—with the Taconic Mountains, the Housatonic and Connecticut Rivers, and no shortage of woods—make for year-round splendor (the leaf peeping season is deeply underrated). The area also provides endless hiking and, in the winter, multiple skiing ranges for a little more active adventure. For one of the best views of the Housatonic River Valley (especially at sunrise), hike up Monument Mountain's three trails, each of which are under three miles long. Or to get a view of the Appalachian Trail, hike up Mount Greylock, which also happens to be home to the highest point in the state of Massachusetts. And in Stockbridge, the Charcoal Trail leads to Olivia’s Overlook for a stunning vantage point of Stockbridge Bowl lake and the surrounding area.

To enjoy art outdoors, guided tours are available on weekends from May through October at Chesterwood, a sculpture garden and studio that once belonged to Daniel Chester French, an artist who crafted, among many other pieces, the monumental Lincoln Memorial sculpture in Washington, DC. For the literary-inclined, author Edith Wharton’s Lenox home, The Mount, is open to visitors—the 45-minute garden tour is especially stunning in the spring.

Waterside dishes at The Cliff House will likely make your day, if not your entire trip.

Though the dining scene in the Berkshires is more quality than quantity, you’ll find options whether you’re looking for a casual roadside joint or a fine dining experience. If you’re starving for options, keep in mind that, depending where in the Berkshires you’re calling home, the many restaurants of Hudson and Stissing House in Pine Plains are also within an hour or so drive.

That the food is yummy and the vibes are immaculate at Prairie Whale is no mistake. One of its owners, Mark Firth, is a cofounder of Brooklyn Diner and the now sadly closed Marlow & Sons. The space feels like an old-school tavern that's always been there with its dark, ramshackle wood paneling and hodge podge vintage art collection, though in reality it opened in 2012. Expect excellent fries and a strong cocktail.

Come for the ice cream, stay for the baby cows—High Lawn Farm is a popular attraction for those with kids and no dairy intolerances. Bring some cheese and butter home for later. Speaking of cheese, Rubiner’s Cheesemongers in Great Barrington is the spot for grabbing your provisions. It’s also a favorite of local food icon Ruth Reichl. Its sister cafe, Rubi’s, offers no fewer than five different grilled cheeses and a solid roster of non grilled cheese sandwiches.

An outside view of The Cliff House, where food and nature meet to play

The restaurant at Prospect Berkshires, The Cliff House, is one of the more upscale options for dinner or a Sunday brunch. Arrive early to enjoy the lakeside view (which really is something to see), and dine on locally sourced ingredients prepared with minimal fuss. You’ll find hearty staples like a roast chicken, Faroe Island salmon, and pork as well as inventive cocktails.

No Comply, another newbie, is an alcohol-free restaurant with a skater spirit. Their creative egg dishes snagged them a spot on Bon Appetit’s “Best New Breakfast Spots” list in 2025 and the thoughtful dinner menu, served Wednesday through Saturday, makes even a veggie burger a special event.

There’s always a line for West Stockbridge’s No. Six Depot, a husband and wife-operated roastery that has been keeping the region caffeinated for over a decade; and a subscription to their coffee club is a souvenir that keeps on giving. Pair your morning latte with a croissant or three from the Lost Lamb, a Stockbridge patisserie run by a Le Cordon Bleu graduate.

The Berkshires might not be the first place that comes to mind for authentic Neapolitan-style pizza, but Pizzeria Boema has perfected the art of the super-crispy crust. Build your own pie or choose from one of the house classics, and don’t miss Monday Night Jazz events during the summer. For more comfort food, Great Barrington's Bistro Box, a seasonal pop-up with a cult following around its comfort food-centric menu, features BLTs, tacos, slushies, and more from April through November. In West Stockbridge, the Heirloom Lodge is a sleek fine-dining spot with farm-to-table fare and a focus on wines from family-run vineyards; chef and owner Matt Straus is the editor of literary food magazine Kitchen Work.
