#2697. Paul George draws NBA season's first fine for flopping after league reviews Celtics-Pistons game

Paul George had a historic debut with the Boston Celtics on Tuesday that later earned another notable footnote.

George, 36, tallied 38 points in Boston's 121-113 loss to the Detroit Pistons, setting a franchise record for most points scored in a Celtics debut. Additionally, he shot 7-of-12 on 3-pointers, passing Kyle Korver for eighth on the NBA's all-time 3-point shots made list.

Following the game, George drew the season's first fine for flopping on a fourth-quarter collision at midcourt with Detroit's Duncan Robinson. No call was made during the game, but the league issued a $2,000 fine after reviewing the play.

The postgame penalty was part of the NBA's emphasis this season to crack down on "STEM" violations — or Secondary, Theatrical, Exaggerated Movements attempting to draw foul calls.

"It's just something that we've seen that doesn't present well in-game, live or on TV, and it's not the fabric of NBA competition," NBA executive vice president and head of basketball operations James Jones said in a statement issued by the league. "We're going to take a really hard stance on flopping. It's something that we'll look at more so than anything."

NBA senior vice president and head of referee development and training Monty McCutchen clarified that the exaggerated effort to get a call is what will earn fines from the league, not necessarily the attempt to draw a whistle.

"A flop is not merely trying to entice a call. It has to be secondary, theatrical and exaggerated movement," he said. "A lot of these embellishments won't have that STEM — secondary, theatrical, exaggerated movement — and thus would be legal."

"Creating a perception of something that you know not to be true, that is separate and needs to be penalized," he added.

Under the new initiative, if a player is called for flopping in a game, he will receive a non-unsportsmanlike technical foul and the opposing team gets a free throw. But if the flop is not called during the game, as in George's case, the league can issue a decision after reviewing the play.

Players who continue to get punished for flopping will draw an increased amount for a fine and could be issued a one-game suspension after six or more flopping penalties.

George was last fined for flopping during the 2023-24 season while with the Los Angeles Clippers, according to Spotrac. During his previous 16 NBA seasons, he's been fined a total of $300,000. That does not include $11.7 million he was docked for being suspended for 25 games without pay last season for violating the league's anti-drug program.

#2693. Lula or Bolsonaro: Wall Street braces for two wildly different results in Brazil election

With the first round of Brazil's presidential election taking place Sunday, Wall Street is gearing up with starkly different market predictions depending on the outcome of the neck-and-neck race.

"The Brazil trade is: Does Lula win or does Bolsonaro win?" said Fernando Marengo, chief economist at Black Toro Global Investments.

Those names should sound familiar. Lula is 80-year-old leftist Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, who is running for a fourth term against 45-year-old right-winger Flavio Bolsonaro, son of former President Jair Bolsonaro. If neither candidate gets more than 50% of the vote, a runoff will take place Oct. 25.

In short, if Bolsonaro wins, Wall Street expects a rally in the country's bonds, currency and stocks.

As Bolsonaro has come from behind in the last few months, Brazilian stocks have moved higher along with his poll numbers. In a recent note to clients, JPMorgan noted that the MSCI Brazil "rose by 0.25% on average each day that Flavio gained in the polls."

Kalshi markets now show Bolsonaro favored to win 60% to Lula's 39%. Prediction markets are prohibited in Brazil, so they may not reflect local sentiment. In a note to clients, Aurora Macro Strategies senior advisor Richard Lapper said, "the balance has shifted toward Flavio over the past month, but not nearly as far as the prediction markets are pricing."

Bolsonaro is the favored candidate of the markets because he's promising more fiscal discipline, something many economists say Brazil desperately needs. Debt-to-GDP stands at 81.9%, up 10% since Lula took office.

"We need a 3-3.5% fiscal adjustment to stabilize the public debt in relation to GDP," said Leonardo Porto, Brazil head economist for Citi. And it can't just come from one-offs like privatization of state assets, he said. "Brazil needs a permanent fiscal adjustment."

That means cutting spending or raising taxes — either of which will be difficult. Roughly 90% of Brazil's budget is mandatory, some of it required by the constitution. At 32%, Brazil's tax burden is already the highest in Latin America, according to the OECD, and its prospects for growth are low.

But there's a lot to be gained if Bolsonaro wins and manages to implement a "robust reform agenda," said JPMorgan.

The firm looks to what happened under his father Jair when he was in power from 2016 to 2020. Bolsonaro Sr. managed to pass pension reform, which saved hundreds of billions of dollars. It imposed a minimum retirement age of 65 for men and 60 for women. Previously, men could retire at any age after working for 35 years, and women could retire at any age after working for 30 years. On average, the male retirement age was 56, and 53 for women.

During that period of reform, JPMorgan said Brazil's 2-year yields fell almost to 4.7%, and the equity market gained 130%.

If Brazil enters another period of reform, JPMorgan analysts say interest rates could decline to their neutral level, 6% in real terms, 10% in nominal terms, and "we would be thinking about the MSCI Brazil upside potential between 21% and 41%." They believe the forward P/E could move from a current level of 8.6 to as high as 13.3, last seen in 2020.

The currency outcome is "bimodal," said JPMorgan, with USD/BRL moving to 5.50 if Lula wins and 4.90 if Bolsonaro wins.

The entire lower house, and one third of the upper house are also being decided in this election. The composition of the legislature will be a key factor regarding the ability to achieve reforms.

Black Toro's Marengo points out that other recent victories by pro-business candidates in Latin America have led to big upside moves in the countries' stocks, bonds and currencies. He notes Colombia's risk premium compression "was about 200 points, and it was one of the stock markets that rose the most — something similar to what happened in Peru." Marengo cautions some of the move is already priced in in Brazil.

As with all emerging markets, a key risk is rising global interest rates, and for Latin America in particular, the El Niño weather phenomenon which could lead to crop damage for agricultural exporters.

#2692. Reported fraud losses among older Americans hit $3 billion in 2025 as big losses surge: FTC report

Fraud losses reported by older Americans jumped in 2025, driven largely by an increase in victims losing sums of at least six figures, according to a new report from the Federal Trade Commission.

Americans ages 60 and older reported losing about $3 billion to fraud last year, a 25% increase from $2.4 billion in 2024, according to the FTC's annual report to Congress, released Monday, on fraud among older adults. That figure is up 400% from $600 million reported lost in 2020.

Just 6% of older fraud victims reported losing $100,000 or more last year, but those losses accounted for 70% of the age group's total reported losses. The number reporting losses of that magnitude rose nearly sixfold to 6,769 in 2025 from 1,136 in 2020.

Additionally, because many scams go unreported, the FTC estimates actual losses in 2025 could have been anywhere from $13.3 billion to $119.5 billion. In 2024, the estimated range was from $10.1 billion to $81.5 billion.

"The numbers and the increases are staggering," said Bob Blancato, national coordinator for the Elder Justice Coalition, an advocacy group for ending elder abuse. "Part of it is that the perpetrators are always ahead. They seem to be one step ahead of everybody."

The rising losses come as scammers increasingly target Americans' life savings by impersonating banks, businesses and government agencies, according to an August 2025 analysis by the FTC. The schemes often involve fabricated emergencies that convince victims to transfer money to supposedly protect their accounts from being compromised.

The volume of scams has increased with the use of artificial intelligence. AI-generated scams are also increasingly difficult to detect and easier to produce, according to the FBI.

In many impersonation schemes, criminals pose as representatives of banks or government agencies and falsely warn that a consumer's money is in danger of being stolen. Victims are then instructed to transfer funds, mail cash or deposit money into crypto ATMs, supposedly to protect their assets.

However, investment scams were the costliest fraud type reported by older Americans last year, with losses exceeding $1 billion, a 42% jump from 2024, the FTC's new report shows. These scams often begin with social media ads promoting investment opportunities or messages from people posing as financial advisors, according to the FTC. Victims may then be directed to fraudulent trading platforms.

Government impersonation scams ranked second in total reported losses among older consumers of $448 million, followed by business impersonation scams, which resulted in $422 million in reported losses.

The growing financial toll comes as Congress considers legislation intended to provide additional protections and relief for people who lose money to scams.

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 temporarily restricted victims' ability to claim their fraud losses in certain cases. as a deduction on their tax return. Prior to that legislation, fraud losses were generally deductible. The provision was scheduled to expire in 2025, but President Donald Trump's "big beautiful bill" law enacted last year made that change permanent.

While investment fraud losses may be deductible, according to an IRS memorandum issued in March 2025, money lost to other scams — such as some impersonator or romance scams — is not, experts said.

In addition, if the victim tapped a tax-deferred retirement account such as a traditional 401(k) or individual retirement account as part of the fraud, income taxes may be due on the distribution. And if the victim is younger than age 59½, an early-withdrawal penalty of 10% may be imposed.

"You get scammed out of $100,000 and then you're paying taxes on that $100,000," Blancato said. "That's crazy."

The bipartisan Tax Relief for Fraud Victims Act, which was passed by the House in September in a 408-17 vote, would eliminate the deductibility restrictions and waive the 10% penalty if it applies, among other provisions. The bill was referred to the Senate Finance Committee. It's uncertain whether it will gain traction in the upper chamber before this session of Congress is over.

Separately, the Financial Exploitation Prevention Act, which cleared the House in June by a vote of 414-2, would allow certain financial institutions such as mutual fund companies to temporarily delay transactions when they reasonably believe a customer is being financially exploited. It was referred to the Senate Banking Committee; whether it will get a floor vote is unknown.

Already, many banks and other financial institutions ask accountholders for a "trusted contact," which is someone who can be contacted in certain circumstances, including when financial exploitation is suspected. That person may be able to intervene to prevent the fraud by reaching out to the accountholder. The Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, or FINRA, requires brokerages to make a reasonable effort to add a trusted contact to accounts, although investors are not required to provide one.

If you suspect an older adult in your life is being victimized, it's important to approach the situation with care, experts say.

"It's usually best to start with an open, nonjudgmental, compassionate approach," said Marti DeLiema, an associate professor at the University of Minnesota and associate director of education for the school's Center for Healthy Aging and Innovation.

"You never say 'you're being scammed,' because they'll throw their guard up," DeLiema said. "Scammers will try to intentionally isolate the target from potential interveners. They'll say don't tell anyone about our relationship or don't tell anyone that you won the lottery."

If the money is already gone, it's important not to blame the victim, DeLiema said. "We do that to scam victims all the time. We say, 'What were you thinking? How could you be so foolish?'" she said.

"We really need to move away from the shame, because I guarantee you, they're already doing that to themselves," DeLiema said. "They're already pointing the finger inward."

From AI companies financing data center projects to France's heavily indebted government, credit markets are flashing warnings as investors grow warier of borrowing risks.

AI-related bonds have been selling off as markets raise the cost of capital amid concerns of a financing crunch. Countries like France face a similar crunch. Pressure is building in bond markets as investors demand more compensation to finance public debt. Recent mass protests and rioting in France have heightened the market's concerns.

"Markets may no longer want to underwrite massive capex, heavy leverage, and uncertain future cash flows, and that same shift is widening French sovereign spreads as investors demand more compensation to hold long‑duration, debt‑financed obligations from a strained state,"  FedWatch Advisors CIO and founder Ben Emons wrote on his "French Neoclouds" Substack post.

CoreWeave, Oracle and SoftBank are at the heart of the AI infrastructure buildout, spanning data centers, cloud services and financing.

Their five-year CDS spreads offer a window into investors' anxiety about the cost of it all and eventual payoff. Higher spreads mean more expensive default protection and can signal greater concern about a borrower's creditworthiness.

CoreWeave has experienced some of the sharpest increases in default-protection costs. Its five-year CDS spread reached 1,065.84 basis points on Oct. 21, according to FactSet, reflecting heightened concerns about the debt-heavy company's ability to finance its expansion.

At the same time Oracle's spread rose to 308.25 basis points on Oct. 21, up from 141 on Jan. 2. S&P Credit Ratings downgraded Oracle's debt to the lowest level of investment grade in July, citing the expanding infrastructure business raised the overall credit risk.

Softbank is a major AI investor with a large stake in OpenAI and investments in data centers and power infrastructure, including an $87 billion commitment for data centers in France. Its five-year CDS spread stood near a four-year high of 432 basis points on Oct. 20, up from 284 basis points on Jan. 5.

France isn't in a full-fledged debt crisis, but markets are signaling unease with the cost of protecting against a sovereign default rising above that of Italy's, which has also increased.

France's five-year credit default swap spread reached 89.8 basis points on Oct. 21, up from 29.74 basis points on Jan. 2, according to CNBC's analysis. Italy's spread increased to 67.1 basis points on Oct. 22 from 28.33 on Jan 2. France's budget uncertainty and debt burden have become a focus, raising concerns that the risk could spill over to other countries in Europe.

Germany's spread also rose but remained much lower at 12.15 basis points on Oct. 22, compared with 7.47 at the start of the period.

Thierry Wizman, global FX and rates strategist at Macquarie Group, pointed to France's mounting debt burden and persistent fiscal deficits as key drivers of rising default-protection costs.

"The key drivers are the large amount of French sovereign debt (120% of GDP) and the inability to reduce the deficit," Wizman told CNBC. In comparison to France, German government debt is 65% of the country's GDP.

Investors are also watching the spread between French and German bonds, considered the European benchmark of creditworthiness, which has also surged to 1.6 percentage points. France's 10-year bond yield also topped 5% for the first time since 2002. The spread between the 10-year yield on French OATs and German Bunds is the extra payment investors demand to hold French bonds instead of German.

While the Franco-German spread is relatively wide, the Eurozone saw far larger gaps in borrowing costs during the Eurozone debt crisis of the early 2010s.

#2658. The Lions’ heavy offense will stretch the Packers’ thin defensive line

You've heard this phrase a lot in the NFL over the last decade: Nickel is the new base. In some ways that's true, but it's really more like "nickel is the new base, when the opposing offense wants it to be."

Most NFL teams still match their defensive personnel to what opposing offenses throw out there, and the Green Bay Packers are no different. In general, defenses want to play as many cornerbacks and nickelbacks as the opposing offense has receivers.

So when offenses are in 11 personnel (three receivers, one back and one tight end) sets, defenses tend to play Nickel — which allows for a slot defender to "match" a slot receiver on the field. So far in 2026, the Packers have played 291 of their 292 snaps when they've faced 11 personnel offenses in Nickel (or Dime) sets — 99.7 percent.

When opposing offenses play heavier looks, be it multiple backs, multiple tight ends or even sets with extra offensive linemen or defensive linemen, NFL teams still match that with "base" personnel (a 3-4 in the Packers' case) an extra body in the box.

This year, Green Bay defensive coordinator Jonathan Gannon's defense has played 75.9 percent of their snaps against these personnel packages (12/13/21/22) with a base 3-4 defense. No third receiver? That means no slot defender on the field. That's the basic math being done league-wide.

So is nickel the new base? Really only when the offense is playing 11 personnel. This, obviously, varies a lot team-by-team.

For example, last week the Packers faced the Dallas Cowboys, who have played 73 percent of their snaps in 11 personnel in 2026, the third-highest rate in the NFL. Against Green Bay, for whatever reason, they averaged 1.0 running backs per play and 1.1 tight ends per play — essentially living in 11 personnel full-time, even more so than their season-long averages.

From my perspective, I feel like the Packers caught a break. Green Bay, which almost always carries five interior defensive linemen on a 48-man gameday roster, was only able to carry four big bodies for the game because of Javon Hargrave and Jonathan Ford going on the injured reserve. Rookie Chris McClellan had to take over the starting 3-4 nose tackle spot in "base" looks for the first time, in place of Ford, and the only defensive lineman on the sideline when the starters were out there for limited 3-4 defense looks was Anthony Campbell, who had played all of nine career NFL snaps coming into the game. On top of that, Green Bay's Will linebacker position is a rotation between Isaiah McDuffie and Ty'Ron Hopper right now, as starter Edgerrin Cooper is now on the injured reserve.

Campbell ended up playing 32 defensive snaps for the Packers in Week 6, even with the Cowboys playing mostly 11 personnel looks that mostly kept Green Bay to playing two defensive tackles at a time for most of the game. With how the Packers' lack of depth at the position played out on the defensive line, Dallas should have tried to wear out Green Bay's lack of numbers with multiple tight end looks (with slot defender Javon Bullard sidelined because of personnel matching) and tried to win that game in the second half against an exhausted front. Instead, Bullard played six of every seven snaps against the Cowboys.

The Packers dodged a bullet, but that doesn't mean that they're out of the woods. This week, they'll play a Detroit Lions team that likes to play a much heavier form of offense than Dallas. The Lions only play 11 personnel 58 percent of the time, a below-average rate in the NFL. They like to use multiple tight ends, or even linemen (both offensive or defensive) instead of that extra slot receiver.

They don't use multiple backs (average 1.0 backs played per play), but they play a second tight end (or extra lineman) close to every other play (1.5 tight ends per play).

It will be interesting to see if the Packers call up one of their two practice squad interior defensive linemen, David Blay Jr. or Mazi Smith, to add some depth to the game-day roster this week, but if Green Bay doesn't, they'll have to make it through with four linemen for a second straight game. This time, though, they won't be playing against a team that just wants to play with a full-time slot receiver, they'll be going against an offense that wants to play heavy offensive looks about every other play, the same looks that have forced an extra defensive lineman on the field for more than three-quarters of the Packers' defensive snaps in those situations throughout the season.
