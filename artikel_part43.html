The rich, tempting aroma of coffee may be benefiting your mood more than you thought.

According to a small study published in Nutrients on September 9, coffee aroma may help you manage stress and boost your mood.

While the researchers noted that these benefits may be linked more to conditioned responses and expectations than to physiological changes, this research also suggests that smelling coffee may help improve alertness, even without that hit of caffeine.

Rod Mitchell, registered psychologist with Emotions Therapy Calgary, who was not involved in the study, explained to Healthline that, “smell is wired more directly into the emotional brain than other senses.

“I think the bigger factor is learning — ordinary conditioning. A regular coffee drinker has paired the coffee aroma with a break, warmth, and relief thousands of times, so the smell alone starts the settling the body has been trained to expect,” he said.

However, while the new research suggests that the aroma of coffee may offer benefits for stress management, it shouldn’t replace professional treatment for anxiety disorders.

The researchers used a group of 20 healthy adults, ages 20–29, for the study. Psychological and physiological measurements were taken from each participant both before and after a brief exposure to the aroma of coffee.

Each experiment included freshly brewed coffee placed around 50 cm (19.6 in) from the participants. No additional odor source was purposefully introduced into the room, which was ventilated before each experiment.

Each participant was exposed to the aroma for five minutes while breathing normally.

The researchers used the Profile of Mood States (POMS) to assess the participants’ mood immediately before and after exposure to coffee aroma. They also analyzed heart rate using a portable monitor and electroencephalographic (EEG) activity.

They found that total mood disturbance (TMD) decreased. They also observed reductions in tension-anxiety, anger-hostility, fatigue, confusion, and depression-dejection. However, after adjusting for multiple comparisons, the reduction in anger-hostility wasn’t statistically significant.

These findings are largely consistent with previous research suggesting that the aroma of coffee may have a positive effect on mood.

“The interesting part of this study is what the coffee aroma moved — the smell caused the students’ moods to settle, not perk up,” Mitchell told Healthline.

“Tension and fatigue decreased while energy stayed flat. What this study can’t say is what [caused] the settling, since the students sat doing nothing for five minutes beside fresh coffee, and nobody sat beside hot water,” he added.

The EEG-derived relaxation index rose from about 0.74 preexposure to 0.78 postexposure. The change was modest but consistent across all participants. There were no significant changes in heart rate after exposure.

However, the authors noted that these findings can’t be conclusively attributed to the effects of coffee aroma itself.

The study didn’t include a control group, which means that the participants’ change in mood may have been influenced by the calm environment, rest, repeated testing, expectations, or adaptation to the laboratory setting.

While coffee aroma is an accessible option, it isn’t as well-studied as established aromatherapy scents. It may be best to view it as a complementary approach.

Essential oils like lavender and orange oils have stronger evidence for anxiety and stress reduction.

Lavender oil has been shown to significantly reduce stress.

Studies have also shown that inhaling orange oil can help reduce feelings of anxiety.

If you want to incorporate the aroma of coffee into your routine, there are various ways you can do so.

“Let your coffee breaks be actual breaks. The smell already signals a pause, so let it be one. Before the first sip, give the cup a full minute, phone face down,” said Mitchell.

You should keep in mind that this study was based on the scent of coffee, not drinking it.

Mitchell told Healthline that, “A candle delivers the smell without the ritual it was learned in. A coffee aroma candle burning while you answer emails is unlikely to have the same effect as five slow minutes beside a real cup.”

BLOOD CLOTS, ALSO known as coagulation, are a natural healing process we’ve all experienced. When you cut yourself, blood clots—platelets, proteins, and cells stuck together—over the injury to keep you from bleeding excessively. Once the injury heals, your body naturally dissolves the clot.

A BLOOD CLOT can form anywhere in your body.

Sometimes, blood clots form in your blood vessels or arteries without an injury, and they don’t naturally break down. Blockage of blood vessels and restrictive blood flow can cause a life-threatening condition called thrombosis.

“Thrombosis is responsible for about one in four deaths worldwide,” says Erich De Paula, MD, PhD, vice dean and associate professor of hematology at the University of Campinas School of Medicine in Brazil.

They get the nickname failed stars because they form like stars from collapsing clouds of gas and dust but fail to gather enough mass to trigger the fusion of hydrogen to helium in their cores, the process that powers actual stars.

In public, the two presented a harmonious image of a high society couple. In private, tensions plagued their marriage, including over religion (the Mesmers were Catholic, while Griffith was Protestant).

At home, Griffith was paranoid and menacing. Despite publicly supporting the Temperance movement, he was secretly an alcoholic, and his drinking worsened his behavior. Griffith’s paranoia reached a fever pitch after a narrow brush with death in 1891.

While the Griffiths were in a carriage on their way to the grave of Tina’s recently-deceased mother, someone fired a gun at them from another carriage, grazing Griffith’s head. The attacker, who fatally shot himself before he could be apprehended, turned out to be an investor from the failed ostrich farm, who had been ruined and owed Griffith back rent.

In 1896, despite his private troubles, Griffith was at the height of his power. Ever-ambitious, he sought a way to immortalize himself. He was also flush with cash, having sold off most of the Briswalter tract acquired from Tina, but still owed a tax debt that he hoped the city would forgive. That Christmas, the Griffiths donated more than 3,000 acres of Rancho Los Feliz to the city of Los Angeles for the formation of a public park, to be called, of course, Griffith Park.

While Griffith spoke of wanting to give back to the community and create “a place of rest and relaxation for the masses,” generosity may not have been his only motive. Critics called the huge donation a tax scheme, and pointed out that Griffith could afford to part with the land. Not only did he have Tina’s fortune, he had already rented out the most profitable parts of the Rancho Los Feliz, and what remained was no good for purposes like farming.

The more his influence grew, the more unstable Griffith became. According to Tina’s later testimony at Griffith’s trial, he pulled a gun on her for the first time in May 1903, when she threatened to leave him during an argument. “He is very proud and did not want the public to know” of their marital disputes, she said.

A few months later, the Griffiths stayed at the Hotel Arcadia in Santa Monica with their only child, 15 year-old Vandell. Tina hoped that a family vacation would calm Griffith’s nerves, but Griffith was on edge. He repeatedly asked servers in the hotel restaurant to change his food and drink, believing it had been poisoned.

On the last night of the trip, Vandell Griffith went to the beach with the hotel owner’s son, leaving his parents alone in the hotel room. While Tina was packing, Griffith approached her with a gun in his hand. He forced her to kneel and started asking her about the death of Andre Briswalter years earlier.

Apparently, Griffith now suspected that the Mesmers had poisoned Briswalter, and planned to poison him next. Griffith then asked Tina if she had ever been unfaithful. She assured him that she had not, and that Briswalter’s death had been natural, but he fired off a bullet at point-blank range.

Tina leapt from the window and rolled to safety, breaking her shoulder in the fall. Hotel staff soon found her and took her to a doctor. A surgeon who attended her injuries later reported that “if the bullet had struck Mrs. Griffith one-sixteenth of an inch below where it did, it would have gone through the eye socket and into the brain.”

Tina lost her right eye, and due to the risks of infection and blood loss, doctors were not sure whether she would survive. Miraculously, she did.

Police began searching for Griffith immediately, but it was three days before they found him at a nearby bar in the midst of an alcoholic bender. The subsequent trial became a massive media scandal. Tina took the witness stand wearing a veil and black glasses to hide her injuries.

When she entered the courtroom, “she looked at [Griffith] casually as though he were someone she had never seen,” the Los Angeles Times reported, while Griffith remained motionless, listening intently. Tina testified for an hour before fainting from exhaustion, returning later that day for a second round of questioning. Only then did the extent of the Griffiths’ private problems become public knowledge.

Griffith’s attorneys argued that he was not in his right mind; instead, his drinking had made him delusional, a case of “alcoholic insanity.” Griffith was fined and sentenced to just two years in prison.

Tina was granted a divorce on grounds of cruelty, one of the few legal justifications for divorce in 1903. She also received a cash settlement and full custody of Vandell, but she was unable to receive half of the estate, as she had asked for.

The couple’s properties had long been under Griffith’s name only, including what remained of Tina’s inheritance. As one of her lawyers told the Los Angeles Times, the law prevented her from obtaining “any approximation of what really should be hers.” However, Tina was left with enough to live on, and Griffith agreed to pay for Vandell’s future education as long as he didn’t attend a Catholic university.

After the trial, Tina moved in with her sister. Though she stepped away from city development, she devoted herself to Catholic charities and social events. She was apparently a big fan of whist, a four-player card game. She never remarried and continued to be known as “Mrs. Griffith” until her death in 1948 at the age of 86.

When Griffith emerged from San Quentin in December 1906, he appeared to be a changed man. He gave up drinking and devoted himself to the cause of prison reform, as well as improving facilities for visitors in the park that bore his name. He also came up with an idea for a second “Christmas gift” to rehabilitate himself in the eyes of the Los Angeles public: a free science center and an outdoor theater.

However, the city was now reluctant to associate with him. As one newspaper op-ed stated, Los Angeles was “neither so poor nor so lost to [a] sense of public decency that it can afford to accept this money.”

Griffith died of liver disease in 1919 without seeing this dream become reality, but he bequeathed the funds for the Observatory and the nearby Greek Theatre to the city in his will. The project would finally move forward more than ten years after his death.

It was Griffith and Tina’s son, Vandell Griffith, who oversaw the construction of the Observatory as president of the Los Angeles Park Board. (Vandell would later become a city police commissioner, and was described in a 1951 newspaper as “a dead honest man.”)

When the Griffith Observatory opened in 1935, it was an instant success, part of a new wave of interactive museums that made astronomy and other sciences accessible to the public. And it all goes back to a man who destroyed his own reputation. It’s a strange chapter in the history of Los Angeles, and a reminder that the legacies people leave can be complicated.

#4266. Could a Human Being Really Survive Whalefall?

Our product picks are editor-tested, expert-approved. We may earn a commission through links on our site. Why Trust Us?

IN DANIEL KRAUS’S novel Whalefall, a teenage scuba diver is swallowed alive by a sperm whale in pursuit of a giant squid. While that might seem like a farfetched tale, reminiscent of Jonah being swallowed up by a whale in the bible, it actually happens more than you think. In 2025, a kayaker was sucked up by a whale near Chile and in 2021, a lobster fisherman was swallowed while diving to check his traps near Provincetown, Mass. Both survived, having been rather quickly spit back up by their respective seabound captors, but it begs the important question: Is Whalefall’s premise more of a cautionary tale than it is a total fantasy?

That’s certainly one of the questions the creators of Whalefall’s new cinematic adaptation want viewers to ask themselves. Starring the very busy Austin Abrams (who last month appeared in Zach Cregger’s Resident Evil) as poor Jay Gardiner, the diver who ends up in the whale, the film bounces back and forth narratively from the cetacean’s stomach to life with Gardiner’s recently deceased father (Josh Brolin), whose remains Jay was seeking when he was swallowed up.

It’s a complicated story that asks more than just “how will this dude get out of this whale,” and yet, the story and struggle behind Gardiner’s eventual (spoiler alert!) escape is captivating. Could someone really survive being sucked into a whale’s guts and not just their mouths? And what’s actually inside a whale, if not just a big empty cavern and a couple of rowboats, a la movies like Pinocchio?

Ramsey Avery, Whalefall’s Production Designer, has put a whole lot of thought into both questions, mulling over everything from how to accurately depict what a whale’s stomach lining would look like to the mechanics of a whale’s multiple stomach sphincters. (And there are multiples!) Men’s Health talked to him about the practical challenges of building camera-ready whale guts, the realities of life inside a constantly diving sperm whale, and whether he thinks he’s ready to fight his way out of one should a future swim go sideways.

MEN’S HEALTH: Where did your brain go when you first heard you got the Whalefall gig?

RAMSEY AVERY: We dove immediately into a lot of research, and the thing you discover fairly quickly is no one has really stuck a camera down the mouth of a sperm whale to see what that all looks like inside there. We have plenty of photography of porpoises and orcas and dolphins, which gives us something we can compare to, though, and then we reached out to a couple of cetacean experts.

Dr. Joy Reidenberg was our key contact and we just started asking a bunch of questions about size and shape and what happens in each of the stomachs. There are four stomachs in a whale and each has a different function. There are some interesting things about sperm whale anatomy, too, as opposed to say a blue whale or a humpback whale, which make it possible that a human could actually get swallowed and then survive the experience for at least a bit.

Another interesting thing is that sperm whales don't chew. They have teeth, but mostly they're like antlers on an elk. They're for display and dominance battles. Sperm whales kind of suck their food in instead. They create this compression chamber with their tongue that draws squid in.

Also, because sperm whales have evolved to eat giant squid unlike pretty much every other mammal, their larynx has been shifted off to the side rather than in the center so that there’s room for the big squid to go through. That's why, if you look at a sperm whale, its blowhole is on the left side of the head rather than centered like all the other whales and dolphins.

From the autopsies and dissections that Dr. Joy and others have done, we figured out the basic size, shape, and colors of a whale stomach. That meant I looked at a lot of really interesting dissection videos and photography first thing in the morning quite often. That was really how we tried to dive in, because for the director, for the studio, and for the producers, it was really critical that it be believable.

MH: It sounds like you’d have to do a lot of reverse math, like “If we need to fit a six-foot actor inside this stomach, proportionally we’d need a whale that’s this big”?

RA: You are exactly right. For whatever reason, most of the whales that wash up on coasts are either female or juvenile, but we do have a lot of records from whaling days about the sizes of male whales. We were able to take a look at the dimensions of those whales’s internal organs and mouths and project them out. And as it turns out, if you wanted to be able to put a person in a stomach that was about six feet tall and about eight feet long—which was the smallest we felt we could do and actually be able to get a camera inside there with the actor—that meant that the whale had to be about 80 feet long... which is right about the length of the largest sperm whales on record. So, it makes sense!

MH: How many models did you make, and how did you represent the differences between stomachs?

RA: The first stomach, where most of the movie takes place, is where a sperm whale chews its food. There's no digestion going on in there. Because they don't have teeth, their stomach actually does the chewing, so it's a muscular compressive stomach.

We do get into another stomach, which is where digestion starts. That's when you start getting into the acids. For the movie, we had to create part of the outside of a whale and its mouth, which we could put in a tank with water so that we could have both our hero actor and his stunt doubles be able to perform appropriately against a real set piece. We started there and then we looked at how the back of the throat and the esophagus works, how that all connects back in, and what the shape language of that is. We ended up creating a couple of set pieces for that to make it all work.

For the main stomach, we started off with a very simple mockup. We got a bunch of upholstery foam, roughed out the size of what we wanted to do, hung it on a rig, and started to put people inside of it to see how it moved. Is it big enough? What's the shape like? How do we get a camera inside?

A whale’s stomach has two entrances and exits. For the first stomach, there’s an entrance from the esophagus and an exit into the second stomach, and those entrances and exits are called sphincters. I have to say, I've used the word “sphincter” more in the last year than I ever imagined I would over the course of my entire life. But the first thing we figured out is that our sphincters could not sit right behind the actor's head, because you didn't really want the audience looking at the equivalent of a butthole for the entire movie. So we had to figure out how to adjust the anatomy of the stomach, both for the visual of it because we want our audience to be able to go along for the ride, but also to determine the action because when you read the script, it reads like the stomach is a long tunnel with an entrance at the front and the exit at the back. But in a real sperm whale stomach, both of those sphincters are up near the top of the stomach. We ended up going with that because it’s better for the action, better for the visuals, and more real.

But to get all of that to work, we had multiple things that the stomachs had to do, and we had multiple ways we needed to get cameras in there. Sometimes we had to put two or even three cameras in this little tiny space, so we did a whole test process again, carrying on with our mockups. Every time we did a mockup, we did a camera test, and we developed it further and further and further and got more specific with the shape language.

MH: Were all the sets four-walled enclosures?

RA: Yeah, they were 360 degree sets, holding water inside, and you could climb in and out of the sphincters. Those could function as camera ports, too, but we made other camera ports as well to be able to get some shots from different angles. With all the folds inside a whale stomach, you can hide those ports relatively simply, too.

MH: Did the movie already have science experts on staff when you came on or did you have to track down your own?

RA: Because the movie is based on Daniel Kraus's book, he’d already done a lot of that work to make sure everything felt real. So he put us in contact with his experts and then we reached out a little further to other sources. For example, we found a person who was an expert in whale sound to ask what type of sounds a whale would or could make.

We know that the inside of that first stomach is filled with methane and unbreathable gasses, because there have been times—and Dr. Joy actually had this happen to her—when a researcher cuts into a whale’s stomach and it’ll explode because of all the gasses building up inside. So, we know that you couldn't really breathe for any period of time inside that stomach and that's a key element to our story, because it sets a timer. Jay only has X number of minutes of oxygen in his tank—and that's a real thing.

MH: Where did you end up having to stretch the truth?

RA: The whale does a lot of things, and we don’t really know whether or not that’s how a whale would react with something like that in his stomach that he can't seem to swallow. We can make guesses, but we don't know, because that’s not something that can be studied.

The fact is that if you're studying a whale, odds are it's dead or it's on the verge of dying, because that's the only way you can do the work. You can't see a whale’s insides while it's still living in the ocean. There's a lot we can't know.

MH: It’s incredible how much we still don’t know about oceanic creatures. Like, we still don’t know where Great White sharks are born.

RA: It's funny because we’re learning more and more and more now, though, and even in the time that we were doing this project we got the first real imagery of a giant squid.

That's another thing: By the time a giant squid gets to the surface—which happens very rarely—they're dead and they've deflated. How they function is a mystery to us. But just in the last year or so, footage of a whale eating a giant squid came out, so we’re still learning things.

MH: Speaking of giant squid: How did you decide what else to put in your stomachs? What would be inside a hungry whale?

RA: Because of the autopsies that have been done on whales, we know the types of things that are in their stomachs. That second stomach where the digestion happens is where squid beaks get left behind because the acid in there isn't enough to digest them. That’s also where ambergris comes from: The leftover parts and bits of squid that aren't completely digested and that get kind of fused into this weird material.

The water itself was a special effects issue. We had to figure out the right viscosity and the right opacity of the water to make it feel like it would be inside a stomach. Wētā Workshop created a bunch of bits and pieces and flotsam and jetsam, like they built all of the beaks and then did multiples in various levels of digestion. They built the squid as well, which was a practical effect. We did this sequence with Jay fighting the squid that you see in the trailer, and that’s all practical props we then augmented and extended with visual effects.

MH: Having done all this work, how confident are you that, if you got sucked down by a sperm whale today, you could survive? Have you put yourself in the shoes of your lead?

RA: There are a gazillion pictures of me in various components of the stomach, the mouth, and the esophagus, because I didn't want to ask Austin [Abrams] or the stunt doubles to do anything I wasn't willing to do myself. There are even pictures of me crawling in the mouth of the whale in my wetsuit in the tank. In terms of actually surviving inside a whale, though, one of the things we were able to sort out is that Austin [Abrams] is a relatively slight person, so he might fit in with his tanks better than I would fit in with my tanks. I'm not sure I would actually go down the throat, because I'm a bigger guy. But at least I’ve also learned the methodologies that he learned to try and get out.

#4261. New weather model may finally offer a reliable six-week forecast

Published Oct 14, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

Even after decades of meteorological advances, weather forecasting remains infamously difficult. Today’s most trusted models are rarely accurate beyond a two-week window due to a constant cascade of atmospheric and climatic variables. These unpredictable meteorological interactions are the foundations of chaos theory. The interdisciplinary field studies how even the world’s most predictable systems can still produce completely unpredictable results, and was first developed by MIT mathematician Edward Lorenz during the 1970s.

“He came up with this theoretical two-week limit where beyond that, we just can’t predict weather because the noise in the system grows so much that it overwhelms any signal you start with,” Judah Cohen, a climatologist also at MIT, explained.

According to Cohen, many climate researchers continue to view Lorenz’s two-week hurdle as insurmountable. And that assumption has largely proven true for nearly 50 years. Even today’s dynamical simulations and artificial intelligence models begin to break down when attempting to predict conditions two-to-six weeks in the future—a window known as subseasonal forecasting. Nicknamed the “predictability desert,” this range runs into slow-moving, highly variable forces like this year’s super El Niño event. Once those factors enter the equation, forecasting becomes more of a roll of the dice l than anything concrete. However, Cohen has had his suspicions about chaos theory’s limitations.

“I never really believed that completely,” he said. “In theory, the atmosphere can create an infinite number of patterns, but it likes to fall into these favorite patterns over and over again.”

To try and break this forecasting barrier, Cohen and his colleague Lester Mackey have spent years developing an AI system that not only minimizes forecasting errors, but learns from its inevitable mistakes through a method called postprocessing. With postprocessing, the AI improves by comparing the inaccuracies with existing historical data, and then adjusts from there.

Cohen’s team recently submitted three versions of their model featuring the postprocessing system the European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). Widely known as the world’s best forecasting hub, the ECMWF also hosts seasonal competitions that pit the latest prediction models against one another. Most of these systems have relied on historic climate averages to reach their conclusions, but this was the first time a group submitted a program with postprocessing capabilities.

Cohen and his colleagues reported their results in a study published today in the journal Nature. Their AI postprocessing integration blew the existing forecasting models out of the water. For the two-to-six-week subseasonal window, researchers doubled overall prediction accuracy. Such massive improvements could soon help the farmers and energy providers who already rely on subseasonal forecasts, but the benefits may even save lives.

“It also improves predictions of extreme weather events, whether it’s flooding or cold spells or something else,” said Cohen. “It’s really important to predict both, but extreme events might be the most impactful part of this for society.”

Many researchers have long presumed subseasonal forecasting to be a dead end, but the latest leap may clear the way to even bigger advances in the field.

“People have questioned if it’s even worth investing in models at all for this time range,” said Cohen. “[But] we consistently beat climatology averages with our models.”

#4259. Are You Eating Enough Whole Grains to Lower Your Heart Disease Risk?

  • A large meta-analysis suggests that eating more whole grains may offer substantial cardiovascular benefits.
  • The findings show that every additional 48 grams of whole grains consumed per day was associated with improvements in body weight, waist circumference, total cholesterol, and inflammatory markers.
  • The greatest cardiometabolic benefits appeared at 60 to 100 grams of whole grains per day, or 4 to 6 servings.
  • Whole grains, such as oats, brown rice, whole wheat, and farro, offer cardiovascular and metabolic health benefits as part of a balanced diet.

Whole grains have long been touted for their heart health benefits. Even a box of Cheerios boasts the trademark heart-healthy claim.

Now, researchers say that loading up on whole grains may offer even greater cardiovascular benefits. 

A large meta-analysis of 87 trials found that roughly 4 to 6 servings of whole grains per day offer substantial benefits for cholesterol, inflammation, blood pressure, and metabolic health. 

The findings, published on September 15 in the European Heart Journal, indicate a “likely causal” relationship between higher whole grain intake and reductions in cardiovascular risk factors.

The benefits, the researchers say, start at around three servings of whole grains per day. That’s a bowl of oatmeal (1 serving) and two slices of whole wheat bread (2 servings).

This suggests that the current recommendations of 2 to 4 servings of whole grains per day set by the U.S. Dietary Guidelines for Americans are sufficient.

However, adding a few more servings to your diet each day could be a boon for heart and metabolic health.

Still, most U.S. adults fall short of getting enough whole grains in their diets, with only 2.9–7.7% consuming the recommended amount each day, meaning that most people aren’t getting enough dietary fiber.

Cardiovascular benefits start at 3 servings per day

The meta-analysis examined randomized trials and identified a dose-response relationship between whole grain consumption and multiple health markers. 

For every additional 48 grams of whole grains consumed per day, participants experienced reductions in:

  • body weight
  • waist circumference
  • total cholesterol
  • interleukin-6 (IL-6), a protein involved in inflammation

For most outcomes, the greatest benefits were observed at approximately 60 to 100 grams of whole grains per day by dry weight, or about 4 to 6 servings.

However, most of the trials analyzed lasted about 8 weeks, so the long-term effects of these improvements are not yet known.

While the findings come from randomized controlled trials, a strong form of evidence, the research is not definitive. 

The observed changes were generally modest, but even modest improvements in cardiovascular risk factors can be meaningful, particularly when whole grains are consumed as part of a healthy, balanced diet. 

“The findings reinforce the broader evidence supporting whole grains as part of a heart-healthy diet,” said
Cheng-Han Chen, MD, board certified interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA. Chen wasn’t involved in the study.

Chen said the researchers’ 4-serving recommendation provides a useful, practical target for heart health, but noted that everyone’s nutritional needs are different.

“I would not interpret these findings to mean that everyone needs to reach a specific number of servings to improve their heart health. Rather, they reinforce the importance of making whole grains a regular part of our diets and choosing them consistently over refined grains whenever possible,” Chen told Healthline.

How whole grains may protect heart health

Whole grains may support cardiovascular health through several mechanisms. 

As a key source of dietary fiber, whole grains also contain several important nutrients that support heart health.

“Dietary fiber is likely an important part of the picture, but it is probably not the only factor,” Chen said. “Whole grains contain fiber as well as a variety of vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and other plant compounds that may work together to support cardiovascular health.”

Michelle Routhenstein, a heart-health dietitian for Entirely Nourished, wasn’t involved in the study, but explained that whole grains contain a combination of heart-healthy nutrients, such as:

  • beta-glucan fiber
  • magnesium
  • potassium
  • B vitamins
  • polyphenols
  • healthy fats
  • phytosterols (derived from wheat germ)

Chen added that dietary fiber may also help support blood sugar management, healthy cholesterol levels, and a healthier gut microbiome.

“Whole grains may influence several of the established risk factors for heart disease, including cholesterol, blood pressure, blood sugar, body weight, and inflammation,” he said.

“Rather than attributing the cardiovascular benefits of whole grains to one particular nutrient, I think of them as a nutrient-dense food group that can positively influence several pathways involved in cardiovascular health.”

Of course, plenty of research supports the cardiovascular and metabolic benefits of whole grain consumption.

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis found that replacing refined grains with whole grains can improve total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, hemoglobin A1c, and C-reactive protein.

A 2025 meta-analysis reported reductions in systolic or diastolic blood pressure, although results across studies were somewhat inconsistent.

Whole grains may also help lower IL-6 levels, a marker of inflammation. Elevated IL-6 is associated with inflammation and increased cardiovascular risk, making reductions in this marker potentially relevant to heart health.

Whole grains may also support weight loss and healthy weight management.

While these improvements may benefit cardiovascular and metabolic health, experts note that whole grains aren’t a magic bullet.

“Whole grains are one component of an overall heart-healthy lifestyle,” Chen said.

“In addition to incorporating whole grains regularly, I recommend focusing on a balanced diet that includes plenty of fruits and vegetables, lean sources of protein, and healthy fats while limiting highly processed foods, added sugars, and excessive sodium. Regular physical activity is also important, as are maintaining a healthy weight, not smoking, getting adequate sleep, and keeping conditions such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and diabetes under control.”

How many servings of whole grains should you eat?

Refined carbohydrates account for around 42% of total calories in the average American diet. Experts recommend choosing unrefined whole grains over empty, processed carbs when possible.

Routhenstein said that many people could benefit from getting more whole grains in their diet, but not everyone may need 6 servings of whole grains per day.

“What matters most is the replacement of refined grains for whole grains, rather than simply adding more food to the diet,” she said.

For meaningful cardiometabolic benefits, you might aim for around 3 to 6 servings of whole grains per day. For context, a single serving is about 16 grams of whole grains, such as:

  • one slice of whole wheat bread
  • 1/2 cup of cooked brown rice or oatmeal
  • 2 cups of air-popped popcorn

Starting with simple swaps, such as choosing oatmeal over refined cereal or brown rice over white rice, can help you get more whole grains in your diet.

Here are a few practical ideas to help you get started:

  • oatmeal instead of sugary cereal
  • brown rice instead of white rice
  • whole wheat bread instead of white bread
  • farro or quinoa instead of refined grains
  • air-popped popcorn instead of chips

Routhenstein recommended choosing palatable, tolerable, and easily accessible whole grains.

“This can look like oats, bulgur, whole wheat pasta, whole grain bread, quinoa, and farro,” she said. “If you are gluten-free, you can choose options like quinoa, brown rice, amaranth, and gluten-free oats.”

Remember that substitution is key. Simply adding more grain-based foods to your diet may not provide the same health benefits as replacing refined grains with whole grains.

“The goal isn’t to make one perfect dietary choice, but to build healthy habits that can be maintained over the long term,” Chen said. “Small, consistent changes like choosing whole-grain bread, brown rice, oatmeal, or whole-grain pasta instead of refined alternatives can add up and contribute to better cardiovascular health over time.”

#4252. Sled dogs have stubby legs for a reason

Published Oct 14, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

They’re some of Earth’s most powerful athletes, traversing thousands of miles of rough terrain in sub-zero temperatures, through blinding snow, and biting winds. Sled dogs have been helping humans travel across the planet’s coldest regions for centuries, hauling supplies, people, and equipment across the snow and ice. In the process, this important role that goes above and beyond simply being “man’s best friend” could have a visible impact on their evolution.

Sled dogs may have evolved shorter legs to help them pull loads more efficiently. Currently working sled dogs and those that lived about 2,000 years ago have six percent shorter leg bones than similar species, according to a study published today in the journal iScience. These findings could help biologists trace the origins of sled dogs and understand more about how dog-powered transportation spread over the past 9,000 years.

“Sled dogs have played an indispensable role in communities in the Arctic,” Mitya Kletzerman, a study co-author and an archeologist at the University of Haifa in Israel, said in a statement. “Without dogs, people couldn’t move as efficiently to hunt, relocate as conditions changed, or travel between communities to exchange knowledge and share new ideas. Understanding how the practice evolved could give us new insights into how people adapted to life in the Arctic.”

As of now, our earliest archeological evidence associated with dog sledding comes from 9,000-year-old sled parts and dog remains uncovered in 2017 on Zhokhov Island (present-day Russia). Sledding equipment like this is rarely found in the archaeological record, so it has remained difficult to pinpoint when and where the practice first emerged or spread.

Earlier studies have shown that work associated with humans can leave traces in an animal’s skeleton. Reindeer used for racing, carrying loads, and pulling have limb bone differences compared to those that do not pull. Kletzerman and her team were curious if sled dogs have also developed particular skeletal traits that have helped them adapt to all of the physical demands of pulling a sled.

In the study, the team developed a biomechanical model to help them understand how a dog’s body handles the work of pulling a sled. The model showed that one effective way to pull a sled loaded with material is for a dog to lower its center of gravity. This increases the canine’s ability to generate pulling force.

“When people or animals pull a load, it tends to tip them backward. A lower or more forward position of the center of mass counteracts this effect, allowing greater pulling force,” Kletzerman explained.

They then tested whether modern sled dogs have skeletal proportions that can lower their center of mass. They analyzed bones in 60 different modern canids, including sled dogs, wolves, spitzes, and mongrels. They then compared the lengths of five leg bones. Three in the forelegs (the humerus, radius, and ulna) and two in the hind legs (femur and tibia).

After accounting for overall skeletal size, they found that sled dogs have relatively shorter radius, ulna, and tibia bones than their non-sled pulling counterparts. These bones measured about six percent shorter on average.

“It’s unclear how this trait developed, but selective pressure may have preserved it, because it gave sled dogs an advantage over other dogs,” Kletzerman said.

To see if this characteristic already existed in ancient dogs, the team analyzed 82 ancient dog bones and three skeletons. The three skeletons were from ritually buried dogs uncovered from three archaeological sites associated with ancestral Inuit populations in present-day Chukotka, Russia, and date to 2,000 to 500 years ago.

Nearly 89 percent of the bones were consistent with the modern sled-dog pattern of shorter leg bones, as were all three ritually buried dogs. According to the team, this suggests that the skeletal pattern may be an effective way to identify that canine remains belonged to a sled dog, even when archaeological remains are incomplete or do not have any direct evidence of sled equipment.

“Dogs have been working alongside humans for thousands of years, but we’re only beginning to understand how that partnership shaped them,” Kletzerman concluded.

#4251. Women, Young Adults Experience Fatigue More Often, Says CDC

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Fatigue is common among adults in the United States, according to a report published on September 3 from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS).

The NCHS found that 71.5% of adults reported experiencing fatigue on at least some days, with 10.7% reporting it on most days.

Frequent fatigue, meaning most days or every day, was reported by 16.6% of adults.

Fatigue is more than just feeling drowsy or tired. It is an extreme sense of tiredness and lack of energy that can interfere with your daily activities and may not improve with sleep.

“The findings are concerning, but not surprising. Many Americans are trying to balance work, family responsibilities, financial pressures, and ongoing uncertainty about the future,” said Matt Glowiak, PhD, and chief addiction specialist with Recovered. Glowiak wasn’t involved in the study.

The report didn’t look at the underlying causes of frequent fatigue. However, experts say stress is one of many factors that can contribute to fatigue.

The NCHS report used data that were collected in the 2024 National Health Interview Survey to estimate fatigue symptoms among adults ages 18 and older.

It found that women were more likely than men to experience frequent fatigue, with 20% reporting it. Younger adults were also more likely to report frequent fatigue, with 19.5% of those ages 18–34 experiencing it.

“Women and young adults often face unique pressures that may contribute to fatigue. Many women balance multiple caregiving, professional, and personal responsibilities, while young adults are navigating major life transitions, financial uncertainty, and high social expectations,” said Glowiak.

“Both groups may also be more aware of and willing to acknowledge their experiences with mental health and well-being, which can influence how fatigue is recognized and reported.”

The report also found differences in frequent fatigue by race and ethnicity:

Frequent fatigue was also more common in certain regions, with 17.7% in the South and 17.5% in the Midwest reporting it.

Frequent fatigue was also more common in rural areas (20.5%) than in urban areas (14.5%).

Compared with people without frequent fatigue, those who experienced frequent fatigue were more than three times as likely to have symptoms of depression and twice as likely to report cognitive difficulties and anxiety symptoms.

People with frequent fatigue also found that their work and social lives were impacted. Work limitations were reported by 36% of those with frequent fatigue.

When it came to social activities, 16.2% reported some difficulty with social activities, while 12.8% reported a lot of difficulty or said they couldn’t engage in these activities at all.

The largest proportion of people (45.3%) with frequent fatigue reported that their symptoms lasted some of the day, while 34.5% reported symptoms lasting most of the day, and 20.2% reported symptoms that lasted all day.

The largest proportion (46.8%) also reported that their fatigue was “between a little and a lot.” Others described their fatigue as “a lot” (38.8%) and “a little” (14.5%).

Fatigue can be a symptom of other health conditions or a side effect of medications. However, it can also be connected to stress.

“The possible causes of fatigue, both physical and mental, are vast. Even a single person may experience multiple triggers for their fatigue,” said David Cutler, MD, board certified family medicine physician at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study.

Chronic stress can lead to prolonged changes in stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline, which may contribute to fatigue.

The most recent Stress in America Survey by the American Psychological Association (APA) found that a majority of adults felt isolated, left out, and more stressed about the future than before.

Glowiak agreed with this and added, “When stress becomes chronic, fatigue can become a common part of daily life rather than an occasional experience.”

Fatigue during menopause stems from a complex mix of metabolic, hormonal, and psychosocial changes that can disrupt the body’s energy balance.

Of all the symptoms that can worsen during the course of menopause, fatigue is clinically significant because it can be disabling.

It is also one of the most common and distressing symptoms.

One study found that 85.3% of postmenopausal women and 46.5% of perimenopausal women reported physical and mental exhaustion.

You may want to consider speaking with a healthcare professional if your fatigue lasts more than a couple of weeks or if it begins to interfere with your daily activities.

It is also a good idea to check with your doctor if fatigue is severe or comes with other symptoms, such as unexplained weight loss, shortness of breath, or fever.

Glowiak told Healthline that managing fatigue begins with identifying potential contributors.

Cutler agreed and added that identifying the cause requires a careful, detailed understanding of all aspects of a patient’s life and medical history.

Cutler also added that it is important to validate a person’s experience of fatigue and recognize that the symptom is real. Supportive treatment is important while the diagnostic process seeks to identify an underlying cause.

“Once the source is better understood, people can take intentional steps to address it, such as setting boundaries, improving sleep habits, seeking support, or making lifestyle changes. Awareness is important, but meaningful change often requires action,” Glowiak said.
