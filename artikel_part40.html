The pioneering computer scientist Margaret Hamilton, who died on Sept. 30 at age 90, played a key role in putting astronauts on the moon. She was a problem-solver extraordinaire, and it all started in early childhood.

Considering the name "daddy longlegs" unfair to female arachnids, young Margaret decided to call half of them "mommy longlegs." Wondering why the neighborhood baseball players were all boys, she determined to join the team herself.

As she grew up in the 1940s and '50s, Margaret noticed a similar problem with various professions. Why were doctors, judges and scientists almost always men? The solution: she would study hard in school and prepare herself for any job she might care to do.

It's no surprise that a born problem-solver would gravitate to computers. In the late 1950s, the new technology offered mind-bending possibilities, and this college math major saw a chance to put her skills to use on a cosmic scale.

Hamilton had long been a stargazer, thanks to a philosophical father who taught her to love the night sky. In 1965, after stints in meteorology and satellite tracking, she became interested in a Massachusetts Institute of Technology project that designed in-flight computers for NASA's moon mission.

The team, of course, consisted solely of men. But, just as with her neighborhood baseball team, Hamilton refused to accept limitations for herself or anybody else. She kicked open the door and quickly rose to director of software engineering.

With her knack for devising novel solutions to seemingly insoluble problems, no one was better suited for the job. Working with the era's primitive technology, Margaret thought through the various mishaps that could occur on a spaceflight and designed the software to handle every contingency.

The epochal Apollo 11 moon mission of 1969 put her planning to the test. As astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin neared the moon's surface in their lunar module, an error overloaded the onboard computer's processing unit. Mission Control faced the prospect of aborting the landing, but Margaret's ingenious programming allowed the system to prioritize essential tasks while canceling the inessential ones. The lunar module touched down without a hitch, and Armstrong and Aldrin became famous.

Sadly, Margaret Hamilton did not. She continued to work behind the scenes, contributing to the five additional lunar landings as well as Skylab and the space shuttle. In 1976, she struck off on her own to start a software company. She might have lived the rest of her life in obscurity if not for a fateful black-and-white photograph from 1969.

This image began to attract attention on the internet in the 2010s. It showed Margaret wedged between a coatrack and a blackboard at MIT, standing beside a towering stack of her printed code from the Apollo missions. Bespectacled and smiling, she radiates the confidence of a scientist who had stormed the boys' club and conquered the universe.

I was one of those fascinated by the photograph, and I contacted Margaret about the possibility of profiling her in a children's book. She initially resisted, uncomfortable with being the center of attention. But she came to see the appeal of a book about herself that she could read to her great-grandchildren, so that they might be inspired to break barriers themselves one day. She warmed to the task of telling her life story and even sent me examples of her Apollo code, which Lucy Knisley incorporated in the illustrations for "Margaret and the Moon: How Margaret Hamilton Saved the First Lunar Landing."

With the 2016 book and movie "Hidden Figures" calling attention to women's overlooked contributions to the space program, Hamilton soon had to get used to being the center of attention. She received a Presidential Medal of Freedom and achieved LEGO immortality in the "Women of NASA" set. In 2019, to mark the 50th anniversary of the first moon landing, Google used reflected moonlight to create a massive portrait of Margaret in the Mojave Desert. Rarely has so humble a soul received so grand a tribute.

The world will miss Margaret Hamilton, but, just like her great-grandchildren, we will always have her example to inspire us. She showed that, no matter how daunting the obstacles, there are few problems we can't solve.

TL;DR: Birdeye has launched Peak, a subscription terminal combining digital-asset market data, fundamentals and portfolio analytics. Its pitch centers on consistent data across venues, rather than another isolated trading dashboard.

Institutional crypto researchers have another terminal to evaluate. Birdeye launched Peak on Wednesday, bringing several of its data and analysis services into one professional workspace.

The company’s October 21 announcement describes coverage spanning centralized and decentralized venues, with ETF flows, filings and private-funding information alongside on-chain data. Access is sold by seat and organization.

The official product page lists token supply and unlock information, holder concentration, wallet-level portfolio analysis, protocol metrics and institutional indices. A command-based interface lets users move between different objects and views, while a research layer called Peak AI is intended to surface connections across datasets.

Putting several data feeds into one screen can save time. It does not automatically make the figures comparable.

For a researcher examining an asset, circulating supply, fully diluted valuation and an upcoming unlock each describe something different. A wallet transfer might be a sale, a custody move or an internal reorganization. A large volume figure can also look more useful than it is if the methodology does not distinguish between types of activity.

Those are the practical problems an institutional terminal must solve if it is to support decisions beyond simple price monitoring. Users need to trace a number to its source and understand the assumptions behind it.

Birdeye says owning its on-chain indexing infrastructure lets it reconcile records and apply consistent definitions. That is the company’s explanation of its approach, rather than an independent audit of every metric delivered by Peak.

The product has not been performance-tested for this article, and the launch release does not establish that an AI-generated connection between datasets is a verified investment conclusion.

For prospective users, the relevant test will be whether the terminal makes research reproducible: can a colleague reach the same result, identify the same inputs and explain why the number changed? Peak’s launch puts that question in front of institutional desks with a new product built around it.

#4445. Dataline raises $10.6 million for decentralized data network for AI agents, plans ICO

Dataline plans to hold a public token sale through Uniswap's auction platform and launch its token by the end of the year.

Dataline, a decentralized data network for AI agents, said it has raised $10.6 million in seed funding and plans to hold a public token sale.

Lattice and Samsung-backed Dispersion Capital co-led the round. Symbolic Capital, Coinbase Ventures, Anagram, and Robinhood co-founder and CEO Vlad Tenev also participated, Dataline co-founder Nuo Shi told The Block.

Other unnamed investors included a former head of the CFTC and founders or partners from Venmo, Anchorage Digital, NEA, General Catalyst, Jump Capital and FalconX.

The round closed in April and was raised through a simple agreement for future equity, or SAFE, with a token warrant, Shi said. She declined to disclose the valuation.

None of the investors received a board seat, observer role or advisory position, Shi added.

Dataline provides data that AI agents can use and pay for when needed. It covers onchain as well as offchain data, Shi said. Developers can connect their AI agents to these data sources through one platform. Dataline also uses AI to score the data based on how reliable, recent, and consistent it is, helping agents decide whether to trust the data before using it.

"For example, a stablecoin priced at $0.97 could be a bargain or the start of a depeg, and a simple price feed / API can't tell the difference," Shi said. "Dataline pairs the pricing data with context on peg stability and cross-venue outliers, so bots, smart contracts, and people know which one they're looking at."

Shi named Chainlink, Pyth, The Graph and Grass as other data platforms in the market, but said Dataline was built natively for AI agents.

Dataline is already live and covers around 50 data sources, Shi said. Dataline's first "AI Data Launch" group includes Hyperliquid, Polymarket, Kalshi, Base, Robinhood Chain, BNB Chain, Sui and TON. Developers building on Base and Robinhood Chain will receive free access to Dataline, the startup said.

Dataline plans to launch its token by the end of this year, Shi said, noting that the token will be used for payments and access across the network. Users will also be able to lock tokens for continued access to data feeds instead of paying a regular subscription.

Dataline also plans to hold a public token sale on Base using Uniswap's Continuous Clearing Auction, or CCA, system. The startup is working with Uniswap and expects to announce the details next week, Shi said.

Dataline wants the sale to be the largest Uniswap CCA held on Base, according to Shi. She declined to share how much the sale aims to raise, how many tokens will be sold, or other terms.

Once Dataline's token launches, data providers will need to stake tokens when submitting data, Shi said. If the customer accepts the data, the provider gets paid and receives the tokens back. Providers offering regular data feeds will keep tokens locked as a quality guarantee, Shi said.

If a customer rejects the data, AI will review it first, followed by a network vote if needed, Shi said. Providers whose data fails the review will lose their tokens, which will be added to the reward for another provider, she added.

Dataline plans to make money by taking a cut of data payments, but has not started charging fees yet, Shi said. Its foundation also plans to earn money by providing its own data.

Shi said Dataline will use the funding to add more data sources, improve data verification, and expand into areas such as robotics and AI training data.

Dataline was founded in 2025 after winning a BNB Chain AI hackathon, Shi said. Its team is mainly based in Europe and North America. Shi did not disclose its headcount or hiring plans.

A New York federal judge has sentenced Leonardo de Jesus Zuluaga Duque to 160 months in prison in a case involving cryptocurrency, drug proceeds and transfers through the US banking system.

The US Attorney’s Office for the Southern District of New York announced the sentence on Wednesday. Judge Mary Kay Vyskocil also imposed five years of supervised release and ordered forfeiture of $14,751,846, according to prosecutors.

The office described Zuluaga Duque as a Colombia-based crypto broker with a supervisory role in the network. It said he and others helped launder approximately $14.5 million in Tether derived from drug trafficking between May and November 2023.

Prosecutors’ account describes proceeds converted into Tether, passed to an unlicensed money-transmission business and exchanged for US dollars. US shell companies then transferred funds to Colombian companies before the money was converted into pesos and distributed.

The token transfer was one link in a chain that also depended on companies, currency conversion and banking access. The proceeds ultimately reached recipients in local currency, prosecutors said.

Monitoring a blockchain address alone would not necessarily explain the commercial purpose of an associated bank transfer. Conversely, a bank record might show the receiving company without revealing the earlier token movement. Reconstructing the entire route requires connecting those pieces.

The approximately $14.5 million laundering figure describes the Tether transactions attributed to the group. The separate forfeiture order sets the amount Zuluaga Duque must surrender under the sentence; adding those figures would overstate the proceeds described in the case.

The prison term exceeds 13 years. Afterward, Zuluaga Duque faces the additional five-year period of supervised release announced by prosecutors.

Later in the day, both banks and crypto weighed in on the dispute once again.

The GENIUS Act said that issuers cannot directly pay interest on stablecoins, but there has since been debate on whether third-party platforms, such as Coinbase, can. The issue was hotly debated during negotiations for a broader crypto bill called the Clarity Act, which has so far failed to progress in the Senate.

The initial OCC rule incorporated the GENIUS approach in that it bans issuers from directly paying interest on stablecoins. The proposed rule also created a presumption that certain arrangements could be unlawful if issuers had certain types of business relationships with third parties. However, the final rule drops that presumption about third parties.

The OCC's final rule preserves the ability for crypto platforms to offer yield, despite "Congress's clear intent to protect local lending," The American Bankers Association CEO Rob Nichols said in a statement on Wednesday.

"Those gaps risk undermining the distinction Congress drew between payment stablecoins and traditional bank deposits, threatening the funds banks use to make the loans that drive the U.S. economy," Nichols said. Layer 2 scaling solutions help blockchains like Ethereum process more activity without forcing every transaction to happen directly on the base chain.

Early blockchains were monolithic. They tried to do everything in one place: run transactions, store data, and agree on the rules of the network.

That design is simple, but it creates limits. Every node has to keep up with the same shared system. If a blockchain increases throughput too aggressively, it may become harder for ordinary participants to verify the chain. If fewer people can verify the chain, decentralization and resilience can suffer.

This is often described as the blockchain scalability trilemma: the tension between scalability, decentralization, and security.

Ethereum’s challenge is not only to make transactions faster. The harder challenge is to scale activity while keeping the network verifiable, open, and resistant to capture. That is why Layer 2 scaling became central to Ethereum’s roadmap.

Layer 2 scaling solutions are systems built around a base blockchain, or Layer 1, to increase capacity and reduce costs.

For Ethereum, Layer 1 is the Ethereum mainnet. It provides settlement, security, and a shared source of truth. Layer 2s handle more transaction activity away from the main chain, then connect back to Ethereum through data posting, proofs, bridges, or settlement.

Ethereum is the trust foundation. Layer 2s are the scaling systems that help more activity happen around that foundation.

Layer 2s do not all work the same way. Some focus on lower fees. Some focus on proof systems. Some focus on application-specific performance. The important question is not only whether a Layer 2 is fast, but what assumptions it makes around data, sequencing, bridges, and security.

Layer 2 is a broad category. The main designs include rollups, and older approaches such as Plasma or state channels. In Ethereum today, rollups are the central model.

Optimistic rollups assume transactions are valid unless someone challenges them.

They process transactions off-chain and post results back to Ethereum. If a transaction is wrong, a fraud-proof process can be used to challenge it. This model can lower costs and increase throughput, but it depends on a challenge window and honest participants watching the system.

ZK rollups use validity proofs to prove that transactions were processed correctly.

Instead of waiting for someone to challenge a bad transaction, a ZK rollup submits cryptographic proof that the state transition is valid. This can create stronger verification properties, but it also introduces proving costs and technical complexity.

Optimistic vs ZK Rollups: Key Differences

Optimistic and ZK rollups both help Ethereum scale, but they use different verification models.

Core idea: Optimistic rollups assume transactions are valid unless challenged. ZK rollups prove transaction validity upfront.

Proof model: Optimistic rollups use fraud proofs. ZK rollups use validity proofs.

Cost profile: Optimistic rollups benefit strongly from cheaper data posting. ZK rollups benefit from cheaper data, but also depend on proving costs.

Finality model: Optimistic rollups often include a challenge period. ZK rollups can verify faster once proofs are generated.

Complexity: Optimistic rollups are often easier to implement. ZK rollups are more cryptographically complex.

Main trade-off: Optimistic rollups depend on challenge windows and monitoring assumptions. ZK rollups introduce proving overhead and technical complexity.

Neither model is simply better. Each makes different trade-offs. Optimistic rollups can be practical and compatible with Ethereum tooling. ZK rollups provide upfront validity guarantees, but they require advanced proving infrastructure.

Layer 2 scaling solutions exist because Ethereum blockspace is limited and demand is high.

The main benefits are lower fees, faster activity, and more room for applications to grow.

Layer 2s reduce the need for every user transaction to compete directly for Ethereum Layer 1 blockspace. By batching transactions and posting compressed data or proofs back to Ethereum, rollups spread costs across many users.

They also make new kinds of applications more practical. Games, consumer apps, social products, payments, and high-frequency DeFi all become easier to build when transaction costs are lower and throughput is higher.

Scaling is not only about making today’s transactions cheaper. It is about unlocking what can be built when the network has more capacity.

Rollups move execution away from Ethereum’s main chain, but they still need data to be publicly available.

This is the data availability problem: how do we make sure everyone can still see the data needed to verify a rollup’s state?

If rollup data is missing, users and validators may not be able to reconstruct what happened. The rollup could claim a state is valid, but others would not have the information needed to check it.

A rollup can post data directly to Ethereum. This gives strong security, but Ethereum’s data space is limited and expensive.

A rollup can also use an external data availability layer such as Celestia or Avail. These networks specialize in storing and proving data. They can offer lower costs and higher throughput, but the rollup now depends on that DA layer staying honest, live, and available.

Ethereum-aligned systems such as EigenDA create another option by connecting data availability to Ethereum’s broader security ecosystem.

The result is a growing data availability market where rollups choose between cost, throughput, security, reliability, and trust assumptions.

Data Availability Sampling, or DAS, helps nodes check that data is available without downloading every byte.

Instead of downloading a full dataset, nodes randomly sample small pieces. If enough independent samples are available, the network gains statistical confidence that the full dataset is available.

Erasure coding adds redundant pieces so missing data can be detected and reconstructed, while cryptographic commitments let nodes verify that sampled pieces belong to the published dataset.

Encode: the data is split and given redundancy.

Sample: light nodes randomly fetch small pieces of the block.

Verify: if enough samples succeed, the network assumes the data is available with high confidence.

This makes it possible for smaller devices to help check much larger datasets.

EIP-4844 introduced blob transactions to Ethereum.

Before EIP-4844, rollups posted much of their data through calldata. This worked, but it was expensive because rollup data competed with other Ethereum activity.

Blobs created a separate, temporary data space for rollups. A blob is a temporary container for rollup data. It keeps data available long enough for verification, but it does not store everything permanently on Ethereum.

EIP-4844 also created a separate blob fee market. Gas fees pay for computation. Blob fees pay for rollup data space. This helps separate rollup data demand from normal Ethereum execution demand.

Proto-danksharding is the first stage of Ethereum’s longer-term data-scaling roadmap. Full danksharding is intended to expand blob capacity and distributed data verification.

A sequencer is the actor or system that orders transactions for a rollup.

Many rollups today rely on a centralized or semi-centralized sequencer. This can provide fast confirmations and a smoother user experience, but it also concentrates ordering power.

Shared sequencers are designed to coordinate transaction ordering across multiple rollups. Instead of each rollup using a separate ordering system, several rollups can use a common sequencing layer.

This matters for cross-rollup composability. If users and applications move between rollups, the order of transactions matters. Shared sequencing can help with cross-rollup trades, liquidity movement, bundled transactions, and more reliable workflows.

But shared sequencing introduces new questions. Who governs the shared sequencer? How are fees distributed? How is MEV handled? What happens if the shared sequencing layer fails?

Restaking allows staked ETH to help secure additional services beyond Ethereum itself.

In a normal staking model, validators stake ETH to help secure Ethereum. Restaking extends that idea. Validators or restakers can opt into additional services and accept extra duties in exchange for extra rewards.

These services are often described as actively validated services, or AVSs. They can use restaked security instead of bootstrapping a new validator network from scratch.

This can make it easier for new infrastructure to launch, including data availability systems, sequencing services, or other middleware.

The trade-off is risk. Restaking can create more rewards, but it can also create more slashing exposure and operational complexity. Shared security can also mean shared risk.

Risk caps, operator vetting, insurance funds, and circuit breakers can limit some of that exposure, but they cannot remove the possibility that a failure spreads across connected services.

Decentralized sequencing spreads transaction-ordering power across many participants instead of relying on one sequencer.

This matters because sequencing is one of the main centralization points in many rollup systems. A single sequencer can be fast, but it can also censor transactions, delay transactions, or extract value from ordering.

MEV, or maximum extractable value, is value that can be extracted by choosing or changing transaction order. MEV does not disappear just because activity moves to Layer 2.

MEV-aware ordering uses transparent auctions, randomization, rule-based ordering, or other mechanisms to manage ordering power more openly.

Decentralization also creates a latency trade-off. One sequencer can confirm transactions quickly, while a distributed group needs time to agree. Some designs combine fast local ordering with broader consensus to balance responsiveness and credible fairness.

The goal is not to pretend MEV disappears. The goal is to design systems where ordering power is visible, constrained, and less dependent on one trusted operator.

Layer 2 scaling improves capacity, but it does not remove trade-offs.

Sequencer centralization: one sequencer can improve speed, but it can also create censorship and ordering risks.

Bridge risk: users often depend on bridges to move assets between Ethereum and Layer 2 networks.

Data availability assumptions: a rollup using Ethereum data has different assumptions from one using an external DA layer.

MEV and ordering risk: transaction ordering can affect execution quality and fairness.

UX fragmentation: users may need to understand networks, bridges, gas tokens, and withdrawal times.

Shared infrastructure risk: shared sequencers, DA layers, and restaking-backed services can connect failures across many systems.

The right question is not only “which Layer 2 is cheapest?” It is also “what assumptions does this Layer 2 make?”

Several Layer 2 networks are widely used in the Ethereum ecosystem. This is not a ranking, and the best option depends on the user, application, and security needs.

Arbitrum is a major optimistic rollup ecosystem used for DeFi and general-purpose Ethereum applications.

Optimism is another major optimistic rollup ecosystem and is known for the OP Stack, which supports a broader network of chains.

zkSync is a ZK rollup ecosystem focused on validity-proof-based scaling and Ethereum-aligned application development.

Starknet is a ZK-based scaling network that uses STARK proofs and its own developer environment.

These examples show the range of Layer 2 designs. Some emphasize Ethereum compatibility. Some emphasize proof systems. Some emphasize ecosystem coordination.

Ethereum’s scaling roadmap is rollup-centered and increasingly modular.

The base chain is not trying to process every user transaction directly. Instead, Ethereum acts as the trust foundation while rollups and modular systems handle more execution and application activity.

In this model, rollups process transactions, data availability layers publish and prove data, blobs reduce rollup data costs, shared sequencers coordinate activity, restaking-backed services extend security, and decentralized sequencing improves resilience.

The future of Ethereum scaling is not one chain becoming infinitely large. It is a network of specialized layers that work together.

That creates new capacity, but also new questions. How much should rollups depend on Ethereum? Which data availability assumptions are acceptable? How decentralized should sequencers be? How should MEV be managed?

Those questions define the next phase of Layer 2 scaling.

Editor's note: Japan launched its bold MMX sample-return mission to the Mars moon Phobos on Oct. 19) at 3:41 p.m. EDT (1941 GMT). Watch a replay of the liftoff and read our launch story here.

Japan's mission to Mars is one of the most ambitious in spaceflight history. It aims to become the first spacecraft to return to Earth from Mars orbit, carrying with it the first-ever samples from the Red Planet system.

The mission, known as Martian Moons eXploration (MMX), is a first-of-its-kind endeavor to study the origin of Mars' moons Phobos and Deimos. The robust undertaking includes a landing on Phobos to collect the samples — which will likely be the first from the Mars system to return to Earth — and a search for evidence that could shed light on events during the formation of the solar system billions of years ago.

MMX launched on an H3 rocket from Japan's Tanegashima Space Center on Monday (Oct. 19) at 3:41 p.m. EDT (1941 GMT; 4:41 a.m. local time in Japan, Oct. 20). After a 10-month journey to reach Mars, the spacecraft will spend about three years studying Phobos, deploying a rover and landing to collect its samples. The mission will then shift its focus to Deimos for a series of flybys before departing for Earth in November 2030. If all goes according to plan, the MMX sample-return capsule is scheduled to land in Australia in 2031.

The MMX spacecraft is comprised of three main parts, as well as a small rover. After release from its H3 launch vehicle, a propulsion module will provide the boost to put MMX on course for Mars. MMX's main exploration module carries the microwave-size IDEFIX rover and most of the mission's scientific instruments, while the return module houses the sample-return capsule.

Japan's Mitsubishi Electric built MMX's three main modules, with the French space agency CNES (Centre National d'Études Spatiales) and Germany's DLR (Deutsches Zentrum für Luft-und Raumfahrt) jointly developing IDEFIX. The little rover is named for a dog in the 1960s French comic "Asterix," in a nod to the country's first satellite, Astérix, which launched in 1965.

IDEFIX carries a suite of cameras, as well as the Raman Spectrometer for MMX (RAX) and miniaturized radiometer (miniRAD) instruments to analyze Phobos' surface composition and temperature.

Most of MMX's scientific instruments are housed on the exploration module, like NASA's Mars-moon Exploration with Gamma rays and Neutrons (MEGANE, pronounced "meh-gah-nay") device, a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer built by the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory (APL), and France's MIRS (MMX InfraRed Spectrometer) instrument.

The exploration module also houses the Japanese-built TENGOO (TElescopic Nadir imager for GeOmOrphology) tight-zoom camera, wide-angle OROCHI (Optical RadiOmeter composed of CHromatic Imagers) multispectral camera, laser altimeter (LIDAR) and Mass Spectrum Analyzer (MSA). The return module, meanwhile, carries Japan's Circum-Martian Dust Monitor (CMDM) and Interplanetary Radiation Environment Monitor (IREM).

MMX will arrive in orbit around Mars in August 2027, at which point it will shed its propulsion module and transition into a quasi-satellite orbit (QSO) around Phobos that allows the probe to fly in a sort of formation with the small, asteroid-sized moon as they both circle the Red Planet.

The spacecraft will spend its first few months in the Martian system observing Phobos from different altitudes, using its TENGOO and OROCHI cameras, LIDAR and MSA to map the moon's surface, study its composition and select potential landing sites.

IDEFIX will be released in late 2028 or early 2029, during the first of two MMX landing rehearsals, at about 130 feet (40 meters) above Phobos. Due to the 14-mile-wide (22 kilometers) moon's extremely low gravity, the rover will be able free-fall to the surface damage-free (hopefully), right itself after a few bounces and begin exploring to gather data on the terrain and surface conditions.

MMX sample collection could begin as early as December 2028, the acting director of NASA's Planetary Science Division, Lindsay Hays, said during a mission briefing on Oct. 15. The spacecraft will attempt two collection landings, with its exploration and return modules descending and returning to orbit as one vehicle.

On Phobos' surface, two different sampling mechanisms — the Corer Sampling Mechanism (C-sampler) and Pneumatic Sampling Mechanism (P-sampler) — will extract and collect at least 10 grams (0.35 ounces) of material for stowage inside the spacecraft's return capsule before MMX lifts off again for orbit. After its first touchdown and sample collection, MMX will return to QSO near Phobos before descending to a second landing site for a similar attempt and subsequent return to orbit.

MMX will end its observations of Phobos after about three years focused on the Martian moon, and will transition into a higher, resonant orbit around Mars in 2030. That course will repeatedly intersect Deimos' path for a series of flybys to collect and compare data with those from Phobos observations as scientists aim to investigate the moons' origins.

Near the end of 2030, the exploration module will separate, leaving the return module to fire its engine out of Mars' gravitational influence and begin an eight-month journey back to Earth with the collected samples. As MMX closes the last stretch of void during its approach to Earth in 2031, the sample-return capsule will separate and enter the atmosphere for a targeted parachute landing in Australia's Woomera region, where its contents will be recovered for laboratory analysis by Japan and its mission partners.

Those samples, and the data collected by MMX, could tell scientists a lot — about the moons' composition, geography and, perhaps most importantly, how they came to exist in the first place. There are two leading theories for Phobos and Deimos' origins, both of which point to very different versions of ancient Martian history.

"Phobos and Deimos are interesting, but not just in their own right. Their presence is a key piece of the larger story of the Mars system," said Tom Statler, MMX program executive and scientist in NASA's Planetary Science Division, during the Oct. 15 mission briefing. "By uncovering the moons' origin, we'll gain new insights into the history of both Mars and Earth, especially those events that shaped both planets billions of years ago and helped create the worlds we see today."

Both Phobos and Deimos are relatively small and irregularly shaped, and bear several resemblances and spectral characteristics to primitive D-type asteroids — ancient bodies thought to preserve material dating back to the solar system's formation about 4.6 billion years ago. Those similarities suggest Phobos and Deimos may have formed elsewhere in the solar system and were later captured by Mars' gravity.

Their near-circular, equatorially-aligned orbits around the Red Planet, however, somewhat complicate that theory. For example, a captured asteroid would be much more likely to have entered an elongated and tilted orbit akin to the direction and velocity of its original approach, scientists say. A competing hypothesis, therefore, offers a much more violent, but somewhat familiar, narrative for the moons' origins.

An enormous collision with ancient Mars could have blasted material into orbit to form a debris disk from which the two moons eventually coalesced. Such a process, which would be similar to the formation of Earth's moon, could explain their circular, equatorial orbits.

The contradicting geological and orbital observations for Phobos and Deimos collected to date have prevented scientists from definitively establishing one hypothesis over the other. MMX aims to provide an answer.

"It's two totally different lines of evidence that point in two different directions. That's what makes it an interesting puzzle," Statler said. Several theories have also been presented for hybrid scenarios combining the two, he added.

"There's all kinds of different ways, all kinds of different combinations of those two basic scenarios," Statler explained. "But that's the puzzle from the start, the two seemingly contradictory lines of evidence that are pointing us to what … the real history was, but we don't know what it is yet."

Each theory for where Mars' moons came from predicts different chemical characteristics for their surfaces. Presumably, captured asteroids would preserve chemical signatures of material originating elsewhere in the solar system, potentially rich in volatile elements like hydrogen. Moons formed through a planetary impact, however, would contain a mixture of Mars and impact material, altered by the intense heat of such a violent collision.

Determining which scenario is more likely could reveal whether Mars experienced a massive moon-forming collision or acquired its moons from elsewhere in the early solar system. Either answer could offer new insights into the planet's evolution and the events that shaped the terrestrial planets like Earth billions of years ago, mission team members say.

"At this point, we know nothing about the composition of Phobos," David Lawrence, MEGANE principal investigator at APL, said during the Oct. 15 briefing. "We have information about mineralogy, but no elemental composition. And so, elements are really a key way to get at some of these foundational questions."

MMX's scientific suite has been carefully calibrated to make those determinations. The cameras and sensors on the MMX exploration module's TENGOO, OROCHI and LIDAR instruments will map Phobos' terrain, geological features and variations in surface materials from orbit, to provide clues about the moon's formation and evolution. Complementing those measurements, MIRS is designed to detect and classify Phobos' surface minerals and search for organic compounds to further differentiate its possible origin as an asteroid or ancient chunk of Mars.

The RAX spectrometer and miniRAD instrument aboard IDEFIX will add localized context to those observations by examining surface mineralogy and their thermal properties up close.

Back in orbit, NASA's MEGANE instrument will measure Phobos' elemental composition through gamma-ray and neutron spectroscopy that can scan tens of centimeters beneath the moon's surface for signs of volatile elements. Higher abundances of hydrogen, chlorine, sodium and potassium, for instance, would favor the asteroid-capture hypothesis, while lower abundances of those compounds could point to the heat and chemical deterioration associated with a planetary impact.

"In Japanese, 'megane' means eyeglasses," Lawrence said. "So we like to think of MEGANE as being an instrument that will look at Phobos with gamma ray and neutron glasses."

When compared to the samples MMX will return to Earth, MEGANE's findings could help scientists determine if volatile depletion found on Phobos reflects the intense heat of a post-impact formation or billions of years of subsequent erosion from the natural effects of space exposure.

MSA, meanwhile, will measure charged particles around Phobos, searching for evidence of subsurface ice and investigating how solar wind exposure has altered the moon's surface. It will also study atmospheric particles escaping from Mars. CMDM will measure dust around Mars, including particles potentially ejected from its moons by impacts.

Comparative observations of Deimos after MMX's final departure from Phobos will help establish whether the moons share similar compositions and, potentially, a common origin.

Once safely returned to Earth, laboratory analysis of the Phobos samples will allow scientists to examine chemical composition and mineralogy with a precision beyond that of MMX's onboard instruments. Tests like isotopic analyses — measuring subtle differences in the atomic makeup of elements — can help identify the samples' origins by comparing their chemical fingerprints with those of Martian rocks and asteroid material.

JAXA (Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency) scientists think some returned material could even originate from Mars itself, deposited on Phobos after ancient asteroid impacts blasted rocks off the Red Planet's surface, potentially holding evidence of its geological past.

The JAXA-led mission represents interagency partnerships with NASA and the European Space Agency (ESA), which are providing hardware and communications support through the Deep Space and Estrack networks.

NASA's contributions to MMX also include MEGANE, the Honeybee Robotics-built P-sampler, and an approximately $87 million investment through the end of the mission. NASA is funding the United States scientists on the MMX team, who will, alongside NASA's astromaterials experts, "be fully involved in the examination and scientific analysis of the Phobos samples when they are delivered to Earth," Hays said during the mission briefing. "We get all of the value and all of the scientific participation at a lower cost total [than funding the entire mission]."

For its role, NASA will receive at least 15% of the returned samples, with materials to be maintained at the agency's Johnson Space Center in Houston for analysis and long-term preservation. To that end, MMX's scientific impact could extend far beyond the mission's conclusion.

"The Phobos samples will be historic," Statler said, with the potential "for future research to answer the questions we don't even know yet how to ask."

DATA, the blockchain startup backed by Andreessen Horowitz's a16z crypto and formerly known as Story Protocol, is shutting down its blockchain and moving to Sui after securing a $30 million investment from the network's foundation.

The move comes after spending years trying to bring intellectual property onchain. But after raising $140 million and building its own blockchain, the company found that changing how the world's intellectual property is licensed was harder than expected.

"We were trying to boil the ocean, and it was a very saturated and entrenched ocean," Andrea Muttoni, CEO of DATA Foundation, told CoinDesk in an interview.

That realization helped drive Story’s rebrand to DATA Foundation in June, when it shifted its attention to the market for AI training data. Four months later, the company is now narrowing its focus further to tracking the origins and usage rights of data used to train artificial intelligence models.

When the Bellevue, Washington-based startup was founded in 2022, the original plan covered everything from music royalties to patents and trademarks. The idea was to let owners register their intellectual property on a blockchain and set terms for how others could use it. The problem, Muttoni said, was that many of those industries already had systems in place, while large rights holders had little interest in making their intellectual property available for permissionless licensing.

"We were kind of spreading ourselves way too thin across so many different types of IP," he said.

However, after that, the team began to question whether it still needed to maintain an entire blockchain, given that most of its work centered on a single product: Trace, which is a registry that records where training data comes from, who contributed it and what rights buyers have to use it.

Also, running a blockchain comes with costs, including engineers, validators, audits and infrastructure providers.

"Does it still make sense to run our own L1, or should we focus most of our energy on the protocol, on the product, on Trace?" he said.

That line of thinking led to the decision to shut down its blockchain and move to Sui.

Muttoni did not disclose how much of Story's original $140 million in funding remains, but said its existing investors, including a16z crypto, are still involved.

Now, Muttoni is positioning the new focus as a continuation of the first rebrand rather than another change in direction.

The company's new strategy grew partly out of its experience with Poseidon, an AI data company incubated by the Story team that collects and processes data for training AI models.

Muttoni said the team found a market where buyers often had little way to check whether the datasets they were purchasing were original, properly licensed or even exclusive. In one case, vendors offered Poseidon supposedly exclusive datasets that were already available from other sellers.

"We were at one point given our own data set back to us," he said.

DATA is trying to address that problem through its product, Trace.

For example, when someone uploads a video or voice recording via a participating data marketplace, Trace creates a receipt for that file. The record includes details such as when it was submitted, the terms the contributor agreed to and any later licensing or payment events.

Kled, a marketplace that pays people to contribute data, accounts for much of the activity. Muttoni said the platform is adding between 3 million and 4 million contributions a day, including older records being brought onto Trace.

The figures show how much data is being registered, though not necessarily how much has been sold. DATA did not disclose how many records have generated licensing revenue.

The move comes as more crypto projects shift toward AI, betting on growing demand for training data and tools to track its origins. Muttoni estimates the AI training data market could be worth hundreds of billions of dollars.

For now, Muttoni said, the goal is not to compete with data marketplaces but to provide a record that buyers and sellers can check. "The main goal for now is being the infrastructure for, let's say, the auditability and provenance of data," he said.

He didn't rule out DATA eventually operating its own marketplace, but didn’t provide any further details.

One is Walrus, a decentralized storage platform that can hold copies of the original files registered through Trace. Another is Seal, which can encrypt those files and restrict access to buyers with the right permissions.
