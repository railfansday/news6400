#3900. Chinese Cars Cost Way Less To Build Than European Ones. Here’s Why

Chinese cars are catching on for a reason, and it’s not super hard to see why. Chinese brands are making big inroads globally among value-oriented consumers in search of a deal. These cars are generally very good, especially for the cheaper-than-average prices they’re offering. Of course, this has sent Europe into an existential crisis, as the Chinese brands threaten the future of its automotive industry. Some insist that this is merely political policy from Beijing to underprice cars and weaken global industry.

Not so fast. A recent analysis from financial consulting and market analysis company AlixPartners examined China’s rise on a deep level. The firm shows that financial subsidies or incentives aren’t the full explanation for China’s superiority in the EV space. AlixPartners found that there’s a roughly 35% production cost difference on average between Chinese EV models and European ones. At the end, the analysis emphasized the importance for European manufacturers to fundamentally change how they all operate in order to maintain relevance and profitability.

“A Chinese-branded EV made in China costs about $20,000 to produce versus $31,000 for a European equivalent,” the study said. Why? Well, there’s a combination of reasons, some larger than others, but all add up to structural advantages in how Chinese companies are managed and how these companies utilize their manufacturing advantages, and not really just subsidies.

For starters, the study says that Europe’s battery manufacturing and raw material sourcing are too reliant on external inputs, which is a structural barrier to high costs.

Essentially, the study asserts that from top to bottom, making and sourcing a battery is just too pricey in continental Europe. Its largest source of raw materials is from battery recycling, but the capacity for such recycling is below where it needs to be for it to be sustainable for battery production. European energy prices are way higher than those in the U.S. and China, too, only further driving up the price of production. AlixPartner’s suggested solution is essentially subsidies and incentives for manufacturers to continue to establish and run battery production plants, even subsidizing energy costs when the plants are running.

Also, the concept of “China Speed” is one of the biggest advantages Chinese brands have against most of the Western world. In essence, Chinese car companies have reduced and accelerated decision-making timetables compared to industry norms. Some big decision-makers have far fewer layers of management, and testing is often virtual, with physical in-person validation happening at the very end.

This speed and confident decision-making showed up all through China’s EV development. Whether it be from software-defined vehicles or physical platform and ergonomic development, these brands are just so much more agile, able, and willing to put new technologies into practice. Of course, sometimes China has been accused of moving too fast, as the race-to-market strategy has resulted in some blunders that could likely have been caught with more extensive testing. For example, Lynk & Co’s software bug could accidentally turn off the lights via voice commands while driving. The resultant accident from the Lynk & Co Z20 went viral on Chinese social media, and other Chinese EV drivers learned that their cars, some made by other brands, were susceptible to the same problem.

AlixPartners’ study ends with a word of caution: Tariffs only buy time to catch up, and they won’t thwart China’s competitive advantage. Sure, European localization of Chinese EVs will erode some of the cost advantages that Chinese brands have with their home supply chains, but the structural advantages of how these companies are run will continue. Western firms need to reinvent themselves if they want to keep up.

Overall, AlixPartner’s paper is a fascinating look into some of the things I’ve seen a glimpse of on the ground in China. Insisting that China’s dominance in EV manufacturing is more complicated than simply throwing everything on government policy; rather, it's a big country with smart people running big companies in incredibly wise ways.

#3893. Treefrog ‘fingerprints’ are on their eyeballs

Published Oct 22, 2026 1:06 PM EDT

Our fingerprints, patterns on whale flukes, and fur patterns on cheetahs are examples of the natural ways we can tell one individual animal from another. For an Amazonian treefrog called the egg-eating spiny-backed frog (Osteocephalus oophagus), we need to look into their eyeballs. Fine black radial lines cross their golden irises, and form a pattern similar to our fingerprints. A study recently published in the journal Herpetozoa details the discovery.

The project began with study co-author Mélissa Peignier, a behavioral ecologist at the University of Bern in Switzerland, admiring frog eyes.

“At the time, I was doing my PhD, studying Allobates femoralis in the field in French Guiana,” Peignier said in a statement. “My first reaction was simply something along the lines of, ‘Wow, what beautiful eyes!’ That initial curiosity gradually turned into a genuine interest in the species, and I realised how little we actually know about them.”

There was also an ethical component. One of the standard ways to tell amphibians apart from one another is toe-clipping, where a section or all of a single toe is removed. This procedure can affect their survival, physiology, and behavior.

“Other studies have used toe-clipping, which involves removing some of the digits. Personally, I was uncomfortable with this approach, especially given that these are arboreal frogs that rely on their limbs to climb trees.”

Looking for other markings could offer a more humane solution and a way to observe the animals natural behavior unimpeded.

For this study, fieldwork took place over three rainy seasons (2021 to 2023) on a river island in the Nouragues Nature Reserve in French Guiana. They identified 103 individual frogs over the course of the study.

During their later encounters, the team took pictures of each animal’s eyes without touching it and then compared the images with a reference library. This way, the recognised frogs were left undisturbed and their eyes appeared in their more natural state. The identifications were then independently verified using Wild-ID, a software package that analyzes photographic markings in animals.

They found highly distinctive iris patterns. Within one season, between 56 and 80 percent of the frogs were recaptured and reliably re-identified. According to the team, this indicates that the patterns on their eyes remain stable enough to monitor the frog in the short-term. Photographs could even be taken of frogs while in their mating embrace (called amplexus) without disrupting them. The team hopes that this method will spread.

“In a world where biodiversity is declining at an alarming rate, it is essential that we minimise our impact on the animals we study,” added Peignier. “If we can reliably identify individuals using naturally occurring features, we may be able to reduce the need for more invasive marking techniques while still collecting the data needed to answer important scientific questions.”

While the reliability of the eye markings will vary between species, and should be done case by case, the team sees considerable promise for amphibians with complex irises. And working with them offers its own benefits.

“Honestly, the frogs themselves are incredibly beautiful and surprisingly chill, which makes working with them a real pleasure,” Peignier said.

#3886. Rattlesnake bite venom is based on their personality

Published Oct 22, 2026 1:42 PM EDT

Rattlesnake venom is a complex blend of necrotic toxins designed to quickly disrupt blood coagulation and destroy tissue cells. This effective biological weapon comes at a major premium, however. Snakes must regularly replenish their venom reserves, which means they need to dole out just the right amount with each bite. Injecting too little venom into prey may allow their next meal to escape, while delivering too much can leave a rattlesnake defenseless against its own predators.

But that doesn’t mean rattlesnakes adhere to a one-bite-fits-all policy. According to researchers at California’s Loma Linda University, the amount of venom routinely varies by individual reptile. Their recent findings published in Frontiers in Ethology indicate these dosage ranges aren’t randomly distributed, and that snakes and their venom are much more complicated than many people realize.

“This study gives us a better understanding that reptiles, such as rattlesnakes, are behaviorally complex and have personalities like humans do,” explained Loma Linda University biologist and study co-author Chelsea Martin.

Martin and her colleague, William Hayes, focused on the preferences of the Pacific rattlesnake (Crotalus oreganus helleri). On three occasions over a four-week window, the team offered 48 snakes the chance to sink their fangs into a membrane-covered beaker. Previous experiments mainly focused on the frequency of bites, but Martin and Hayes also measured the amount of injected venom in each instance. They then compared the data across snakes.

“The results were striking in their consistency,” the study’s authors wrote in an accompanying Phys.org rundown.

The team also reported measured behaviors like aggressiveness, bite willingness, and the amount of venom amount were statistically significant and repeatable across many of the snakes. Additionally, their study further debunks the lingering myth that larger snakes actually produce less venom.

“Larger snakes expended more venom than smaller snakes, as expected given their greater capacity for venom storage,” explained the biologists, while noting that actual delivered levels still occasionally varied regardless of size.

The study’s authors emphasize that their findings shouldn’t be held against the rattlesnakes themselves. While often feared, the snakes generally do everything they can to avoid confrontations with larger animals like humans. Bites are almost always the result of an unintentional encounter in the wild rather than an overly aggressive serpent. If anything, it’s people who aren’t paying attention to their surroundings, and not the other way around.

“One practical lesson remains unchanged: No rattlesnake should be approached or handled on the assumption that it will behave predictably,” the authors said. “Consistent individual differences do not make any particular snake safe.”

You'll be clambering up enormous trees, lighting campfires beneath mushrooms, and befriending or evading troublesome larger supernatural creatures known as "Väsen".

I caught a briefing for Trollheim: Gnomes earlier this month, and while I worry this is generic fare on some level, I'd sure like to get lost in those procgen woods. I'm also interested in the game's eight-player multiplayer, in that it seemingly creates opportunities for hide-and-seek – the game's building system affords scope to camouflage your dwellings against the litter, moss and mold of the forest floor.

Off-leash are a new studio founded by some fairly seasoned people. Trollheim game director Jojje Dalunde spent nine years at Battlefield creators DICE, while company CEO Jakob Munthe is a former executive producer at Paradox Interactive. Munthe's most recent credits include Vampire: The Masquerade – Bloodlines 2, which I guess isn't necessarily reassuring, but hey, that game didn't turn out too terribly in the end. Art producer Josefin Lindgren has done stints at Payday household Starbreeze and Unravel developers Coldwood Interactive. I can definitely make out a thread between Unravel's homespun countryside and Trollheim's pungent array of organic terrain and building materials.

Trollheim: Gnomes is the first project in a narrative universe founded by film-maker Björne Larson. He and other members of the Gnomes team are piping their own childhood memories of Nordic woodland lore into the game. "When my parents divorced, I lost the concept of home, until my grandmother took me into these forests we grew up in, telling me about the gnomes and trolls and fairies who lived there and thrived there," Larson told journalists during the briefing. As you might expect from that personal reflection, Gnomes isn't a combat-led game, though it begins with scenes of strife: the Sprýg Gnomes are driven out from Midgård by a corrupted Oden, forced to relocate to the wildwoods of Trollheim. The non-player denizens of the woods can be threatening – hungry trolls may go after your food, for example - but the Väsen are broadly characterised as "neighbours", not enemies. Your job is to find your niche alongside them.

You might be able to placate grumpier Väsen with the help of other players. During the briefing, the developers gave the example of snaring a troll with rope trap, calming the beast with some flute music, and then offering them a tasty stew. The game's gifting system strives to make an art out of reviving downed players and NPCs: you'll need to identify and prepare an appropriate remedy, though Off-leash didn't go into detail here. As for player specialisation: there are no classes, so it's all about what you craft and wear.

The environment itself is pretty enchanting. I guess Obsidian's Grounded is the immediate comparison, but Grounded doesn't have trunks this overwhelmingly tall. A lot of work has gone into capturing both the scale of the canopy and the minutiae of twigs, berries, fungus and boulders. There are babbling brooks and moonlit lakes that are crying out for a birchleaf canoe (I'm not sure boating is a thing in the game). There are undulating mossy carpets that look like they'd wrench the ankles of any human hikers, but seem pleasant enough to scramble over when you're the size of a champagne cork.

Sadly, the inclusion of elements familiar from other open world and survival games tramples some of the charm. That's an impression based on hands-off play, mind. When I told the devs I found the building system a bit of a letdown, they argued that the small scale and level of creative control add up into something distinctive.

The gnome architectural designs look lovely in themselves, and again, I'm keen on the prospect of concealing them in amongst the roots of spruce and pine. Or constructing nests linked by ropeways, high up in the branches. I don't think there's PVP, as such, and I'm not sure the game's NPCs can be duped by camouflaged exteriors, but I like the idea of tribes of players hiding out from each other, or leaving intricate trails to their lairs. If you're an especially insular species of gnome - a proper Svirfneblin - you can play the whole thing solo and offline.

A Russian satellite just took a very close look at an American spacecraft in low Earth orbit.

A few days ago, the snooping satellite Kosmos 2612 rendezvoused with ICEYE-X36, a commercial craft that observes our planet using synthetic aperture radar (SAR).

The close encounter, which occurred between Oct. 15 and Oct. 19, was reported by Marco Langbroek and other satellite trackers, with Langbroek calling it "a worrisome cat-and-mouse game" in orbit. The rendezvous has garnered a lot of attention in space circles, because it was non-consensual and startlingly close; at one point, the two spacecraft may have been separated by just a few meters. What did Russia hope to gain from it?

Before tackling that question, we need to set the stage a bit. For example, it's important to note that inspections aren't unheard of in the final frontier. Russia, the United States and China have all maneuvered satellites into close proximity of other nations' hardware in recent years.

Most of this activity has taken place in geostationary orbit, which lies 22,236 miles (35,786 kilometers) above Earth. At this altitude, orbital velocity matches our planet's rotational speed, allowing spacecraft to "hover" over one patch of Earth continuously. GEO is therefore a popular destination for spy, communications and weather satellites — and for spacecraft designed to shadow and assess them.

Kosmos 2612 is operating in low Earth orbit (LEO), which is part of the reason its activities have created such a stir. Neither China nor the U.S. has conducted an uncooperative rendezvous in LEO, as far as we know. But Russia has, according to Victoria Samson, chief director of space security and stability for the nonprofit Secure World Foundation.

"They've done a few," she told Space.com. "Kosmos 2543 got within about 30 kilometers [19 miles] of USA 245 in January 2020, and then Kosmos 2558 got within about 70 kilometers [43 miles] of USA 326 in August 2022. And they've done repeated approaches to 326."

Kosmos 2612 got much closer to its target than those other inspectors did, of course. And there's another big difference: USA 245 and USA 326 are classified spy satellites managed by the U.S. National Reconnaissance Office, while ICEYE-X36 is a private Earth-observing craft.

There are about 80 ICEYE spacecraft in LEO at the moment. They image our planet using SAR, which can peer through clouds and gather data both day and night. #3852. Bat mothers’ hearts beat faster when they hear their pups

Published Oct 23, 2026 12:00 AM EDT

A single bat colony contains hundreds or even thousands of the winged mammals. But with less-than-ideal eyesight and so many neighbors, how do mothers identify their pups amid the extremely crowded conditions? Writing in a new study published today in the journal Frontiers in Ethology, researchers say it often comes down to the subtle, unique vocalizations emitted by their individual offspring. To assess their responses, the team relied on an even more delicate biological signal—the mother bat’s own heartbeat.

The investigation began when biologists at Japan’s Doshisha University noticed a surprising—but consistent—behavior among the Japanese house bats (Alionoctula abramus) in their laboratory colony. Whenever a pup wandered too far from its roosting area, only its mother raced to the rescue.

“We knew that pups emit isolation calls—acoustic distress signals—when separated. But seeing this strict behavioral selectivity made us wonder: what is happening inside the mother when she hears these calls?” recalled study co-author and Doshisha University biologist Kazuki Yoshino-Hashizawa.

To learn more, Yoshino-Hashizawa and his colleagues monitored four mother bats who each recently birthed a litter of three pups. Some of the litters only contained females, so they decided to exclude the young males from the study to avoid sex-based variations. At two days old, the team recorded clips of the female pups’ tiny isolation calls. These function like the cries from a human baby, and each vocalization has its own unique tones.

However, Japanese house bats grow quickly. The new generation started making echolocation calls at about eight days old, and they ceased isolation calls after around 12 days. After roughly one month, they began flying like adults and set off on their own within the colony. The researchers then recorded their adult echolocation calls, as well.

Around 40 days after the pups’ birth, the team isolated each mother and played them the various audio clips while monitoring their heart rates using an ECG machine. As a control, they also played the recording of a random adult male bat. The study’s authors quickly saw that a mother bat’s heart rate sped up when they heard cries of their own pups, but not the pups that belong to other bat moms. Surprisingly, the mother bats’ heart rates boosted the most from recordings of the older, independent pups. Researchers theorize this might relate to the bats’ memory of more recent calls, but that idea requires more study to confirm.

Still, don’t get overly sentimental. The team also recorded strong heart rate reactions when the mothers heard calls from the random adult male. It’s possible the bats interpreted the sounds as a threat, but the team stopped short of categorizing the reactions as good or bad.

“‘[Heart rate measurement] cannot distinguish between positive emotions, such as maternal motivation, and negative ones, such as stress or alarm,” Yoshino-Hashizawa added.

Instead, the study authors suggest heart rates may be a solid starting point when trying to analyze bat behaviors and responses to their everyday environment. At the same time, a mother bat’s heart certainly seems to patter a bit more for her pups.

“[This] suggests that these vocalizations instantly physiologically prime the mother to engage in maternal behavior,” explained Yoshino-Hashizawa.
