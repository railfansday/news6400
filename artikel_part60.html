#3446. "We made a very difficult decision": Australian indie showcase Freeplay reject antisemitism claims after delisting a Jewish sponsor who was allegedly accused of being a "zionist"

Unnamed speaker criticised Unpacking dev's inclusion

The organisers of Australian independent games showcase Parallels have rejected accusations of "prejudice" and say they've received "violent threats", after a Jewish developer had her sponsorship of the event refunded. The developer in question is Wren Brier, creative director at Witch Beam. She claims an unnamed speaker booked for the event has accused her of being a "zionist", following an article about her work from an Australian Jewish publication.

On 8th October, Brier announced that Parallels organisers Freeplay had refunded and delisted her sponsorship, following complaints about her involvement from the anonymous speaker. According to Brier, the speaker called her a "zionist", apparently based on the presence of Witch Beam's 2021 puzzler Unpacking in a round-up of Israeli and Jewish-made videogames from The Jewish Independent, published on 5th October to mark this year's Melbourne International Games Week. Brier herself does not identify as Zionist and does not live in Israel, having left the country as a teenager.

There is much heated disagreement about what "Zionism" involves. Many protesters of Israel's vicious and inhuman war on Gaza define Zionism as a violent ethnonationalist project, comparable to European fascism, but the term has a long history that encompasses the desire for safety and security among Jewish people amid virulent antisemitism in other countries, before and after the Holocaust. I am not Jewish, and my understanding of these things is threadbare, but one thing we can say for sure is that being Jewish does not make you a Zionist. To conflate the two is actively antisemitic.

"I am Jewish," Brier wrote in her Bluesky thread from 8th October, which was posted a few hours before the Parallels event began. "I was born in Israel. I emigrated to Australia when I was 18. I did not serve in the military. I have never expressed any anti-Palestinian views. I really wanted to believe that 'zionist' wasn't a dogwhistle for 'Jewish' but I don't know what else it could be here.

"No one bothered to check whether the accusation was true," she went on. "The first I heard of any of this was this morning, when Freeplay told me they were refunding my donation and removing me as a sponsor because my mere name being listed 'has created an untenable environment' for their speakers."

Brier says she was not aware that Unpacking had been featured in the Jewish Independent piece. She credits the game's inclusion to the fact that it contains three Jewish cultural items - a dreidel, a menorah (pictured above on the table), and a hamsa, among hundreds of other objects the player is asked to unbox and arrange. "That was enough to make a speaker accuse me of being 'zionist', which in turn was enough for Freeplay to remove me as a sponsor, literally on the day of the event," Brier wrote.

"I've always loved Parallels, I've even participated in it myself and showed Unpacking there way back in 2018," she continued. "But I don't think I'll ever be able to support it or Freeplay again. I know at least one Freeplay board member has quit the board over this, too. This is discrimination, pure and simple."

Other game developers have come forward in Brier's support. "For the record, I had heard via-via murmurs of this and strongly objected to describing you as Zionist, and vouched for your person several times," wrote former Vlambeer co-founder Rami Ismail in response to her Bluesky thread. "I'm so sorry things played out like this. This is indeed deeply distressing."

Former Splash Damage writer Ed Stern added: "Wren, this is so stupid and absurd I don’t even know where to start."

Recently founded industry news site Vertical Slice have since obtained a copy of the email to Brier, informing her that her sponsorship had been refunded. "We value you as a member of the Freeplay community," it reads, "but unfortunately your involvement in Parallels has created an environment that is untenable for a number of our speakers, and it is very important to us that Parallels 2026 goes ahead. Freeplay has mixed feelings about this very difficult decision, and we sincerely wish our speakers had raised these concerns earlier so we could engage with you and look for a solution."

According to This Week In Videogames, meanwhile, two Freeplay board members in total have resigned over the decision to exclude Brier. TWIV further report that this year's Parallels event included several statements of support for Palestinians in Gaza, with addresses from Freeplay board member Joel Davidson and Origame Digital's Naphtali Faulkner, developer of the recent post-colonial horror Penguin Colony. Among the games featured at the event was Dreams on a Pillow, created by Palestinian developer Rasheed Abueideh, who was not present at the event, but recorded a video segment.

Supporters of Palestinians regularly call for a boycott of products made in Israel, and of projects or companies such as Microsoft that have business connections with the Israeli state and military. Unpacking does not appear to have any such links: according to Brier, it was made by developers based in Queensland, Australia, with funding from state body Screen Queensland. As for Brier's sponsorship of Parallels, she was not present at the event itself, but sent her studio logo for inclusion on the sponsor list.

Hitherto, Freeplay and Parallels have been affiliated with the Melbourne International Games Week, itself organised by the state cultural body the Australian Centre for the Moving Image. But according to a spokesperson, the ACMI "acted immediately to pause its support" after learning about Brier's delisting. “This was a clear breach of the Games Week code of conduct, which prohibits discrimination on any basis including sex, sexual orientation, gender, race, religion, disability, or any other protected attribute," the spokesperson told This Week In Videogames, noting that Freeplay are an independent entity and that they did not consult with the ACMI, before delisting Brier.

Now, finally, Freeplay themselves have come forward with a statement about the situation, which was sent to RPS among other outlets, following a request for comment yesterday. The statement doesn't identify the originator of the "zionism" claim.

"For 22 years, Freeplay has been a diverse, inclusive and welcoming place for people of many different backgrounds and perspectives to get together, chat about games and our lived experiences," it reads. "We can’t express how much we value our community, sponsors and donors and all of the support we have received over the years.

"Recently, the board decided to refund and remove acknowledgement of a donor from Parallels 2026," they go on. "The donor has been a valued member of the Freeplay community for many years. We regret the manner in which aspects of this process occurred and the upset this has caused within our community. We will be seeking further discussions with all affected parties to hopefully achieve a better outcome for the situation moving forward.

"The public discussion has included serious allegations about the motivations behind our actions," the statement acknowledges. "We reject suggestions that Freeplay acted out of prejudice towards any person or community. We made a very difficult decision under significant time pressure with the understanding that Parallels 2026 was at risk of not going ahead."

"We understand many of you would like clarity on the events from us," Freeplay write. "Given the sensitivities of the situation, and our responsibility to everyone involved, we won't be commenting on private communications or the various claims that have been made publicly. This week, members of our board have received violent threats, which we have reported to the police. We know that feelings in our community are running high. We ask that if you choose to engage online, please do so with care and respect for everyone involved."

#3444. I Tried a Smart Band That Measures Body Temperature During Workouts, and I'm on the Fence About It

The market for smart bands is getting pretty crowded. It’s hard to beat the Fitbit Air on value or the Whoop on performance analytics, and anything in between struggles to make itself stand out. But the Speediance strap caught my eye for an unusual feature it includes: a sensor that claims to measure your core body temperature during workouts.

The only other device I’ve seen make a similar claim is the Core 2 thermal sensor, which I wore for a summer’s heat training and found it valuable and useful (though not without a few caveats). A Speediance rep told me their device works the same way as the Core 2: It has a sensor for skin temperature, and another for heat flux, which can estimate how much you’re warming up over the course of a workout.

Core 2 requires that you pair its device to a chest strap heart rate monitor because its algorithm can only relate data from the sensors to your core temperature if it knows how your heart rate is responding. It also asks you to wear the sensor on the chest strap, so that it’s as close as possible to your actual core temperature. Speediance doesn’t have those requirements; you can get temperature estimates while wearing the device on your wrist and without any extra equipment.

To test Speediance’s claims, I wore both devices together for several workouts and compared the heat strain each device says I was under. While the Core 2 device is geared toward heat adaptation, Speediance uses heat data mostly to tell you when you’re done warming up and when your performance may be suffering due to heat.

The Speediance app shows your “heat load” during workouts, right on the same screen that shows your heart rate, time, and running mileage. At the beginning of a workout, you’ll see a label that says you’re insufficiently warmed up. Once your body temperature rises by about 0.5 to 1 degree Celsius (about 1 to 2 degrees Fahrenheit), you’re deemed to be in the “optimal performance” zone. A numerical strain index increases as you get warmer; above 5.0, you’re told you’re in “performance decline,” and above 7.0, you’re in “heat overload.”

For comparison, the Core 2 sensor uses a slightly different scale, or at least has different cutoffs: Between 1.0 and 2.9, you’re increasing your heat training load, and from 3.0 to 6.9 you’re more effectively increasing that load. Above 7, you’re not recommended to train because risks of overheating are too high.

It’s fall as I was testing the Speediance strap, and the weather was beautiful, so I wasn’t able to test this feature in any extreme temperatures. I did notice that, in a strength workout in my garage, my warmup was supposedly over before I had barely done any work. And I hit the “performance decline” level of heat load toward the end of several relatively cool runs.

To get a better sense of how the Speediance band does at measuring temperatures, I took it out alongside the Core 2 for an hour-long trail run on an unseasonably warm day (it was in the high 70s), and compared the heat load readings at various points during the run.

The numbers didn’t match up directly; the Speediance band always read several points higher than the Core 2 sensor, and seemed to show more ups and downs. But both climbed more or less together. I don’t think I’d say the Speediance band is as accurate—and in any sense, I don’t have a gold standard to compare to—but it seems like it could certainly be useful in monitoring how your performance varies with temperature in hot weather.

If you’re going to get a Speediance band for its temperature features, you’re probably also going to use it for general activity and sleep tracking. On that front, I have plenty of good news. It’s comfortable to wear, with a soft band that feels a bit nicer than Whoop’s. I often completely forgot I was wearing it.

The app has a lot of nice little features. You get a circular visual of your day, showing when you slept versus when you exercised. My only complaint here is that the background is a generic visual that suggests you’re looking at a graph of daytime and nighttime, but it’s not lined up with the actual times of sunrise and sunset. Aligning those times with the visual would do a much better job of showing how your habits are (or aren’t) matching up with your circadian rhythms.

The band and app measure all the usual things, and display the data well: My HRV, skin temperature, and resting heart rate are all reported as vital signs, and I can easily see how they’ve changed over the past week, month, and year. A “TRIMP” number shows up among my daily metrics, which I know refers to the same thing that other apps call cardio load. A little obscure in the naming, but a good metric to track. The app suggests a bedtime, and its sleep tracking seems relatively accurate (not counting the time it read a dentist appointment as a nap—although, to be fair, I was lying still for half an hour).

But I’m disappointed by a few things. One annoyance is that, since Speediance is primarily a home gym company, you can’t log a strength workout without creating an exercise routine. (I ended up logging my strength sessions as “mixed cardio” instead.) If you want to share your exercise data on a graphic, you get stock images of Speediance equipment for backgrounds.

And I was never able to properly sync my workouts to Strava. I got a message in Chinese after attempting the connection, which a translation app told me means “the binding is successfull!” and the settings in my app told me in English that Strava was connected. Still, nothing ever showed up on my Strava page. That’s a bummer, both for everyday use if you use Strava, and for me personally since I could have used Strava to download more specific data from my workouts than I can get from just viewing workouts in the Speediance app.

At $159, I’m on the fence. The temperature features of the Speediance strap could be useful for anyone who wants to keep track of how heat affects their workouts, especially in the summer. It doesn’t track heat adaptation, so it can’t replace the Core 2 sensor, but I do appreciate that Speediance is offering something more than just another heart rate measurement.

But the glitches (especially the lack of Strava connection) are a dealbreaker for now, and I wasn’t able to get as much information as I’d like about how well it measures heart rate. If I could overlook those, I don’t think this band is particularly worse than the Polar Loop or the Garmin Cirqa, which both cost a bit more. But I certainly can’t say it’s better than the Fitbit Air or the Whoop. Perhaps with time, this will be a better device, but at this point, I’m more inclined to watch for improvements than to recommend it as is.

The Goodreads mobile app has a built-in scanner that uses your device's camera to scan either the book cover or barcode. There are some books that are worth reading multiple times, and Goodreads allows you to track each reread as a separate activity so you don't overwrite your original entry or affect your reading challenge stats. If you care about nitty-gritty reading stats, make sure you choose the specific edition of the title you're reading. Different editions have different page numbers, and you can also select audiobook versions if you're listening rather than reading to keep those pages from being counted in your totals. Goodreads counts all books read within a calendar year toward your annual Reading Challenge, but there may be titles you don't want included in your year-end stats. #3428. "It just seemed arbitrary, and not very smart": EA was never a responsible owner for Bioware, but the developer never seemed to learn from their mistakes

19 years after buying Bioware, Electronic Arts have closed the veteran RPG developer. But as saddening as this is, bringing an end to series such as Dragon Age and Mass Effect, EA have never had an easy relationship with the studio.

Looking through old interviews with developers who worked across Baldur's Gate, Neverwinter Nights, Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic, Dragon Age, Mass Effect, and Anthem, the corporation immediately changed the atmosphere of the studio and never seemed to understand what exactly they had bought, or even why.

However, another thread of the conversations is that from project to project, Bioware repeated the same mistakes, getting years into projects before working out what they were trying to make. Sometimes they could pull an exceptional game out of this mess, but other times it led to costly missteps like Anthem.

Back when I was running TechRadar Gaming, features editor Jeremy Peel worked with Jon Bailes to produce the oral history of Mass Effect. I'm hideously biased, but it's an excellent account of that game's development. It was during the RPG's creation that EA acquired Bioware, and developers on the game immediately noticed a change.

"What I noticed once EA came in was how the language changed at work, and started adopting more corporate-isms, like the language of corporate America," audio design lead Steve Sim recalled. "At one time if you needed help on a project, you'd say, 'I need more guys,' or 'I need more people to help me out with this.' And then it turned into, 'I need more resources.' So the biggest change I found was just the change in the language, especially amongst producers and that type of crowd. They were assimilating into the Borg, it seemed."

It's the kind of language that you will find through the open letter published by EA's chief operating officer David Tinson and chief studios officer Cam Weber. The first reference to Bioware's closure is tied up in the euphemism "Saying no to some things we're passionate about". Doing so, they say, "allows us to focus our teams and resources so we give our biggest opportunities everything they need to succeed."

Also speaking in the Mass Effect oral history, director of technology and Bioware co-founder Trent Oster describes the immediate costcutting EA enacted on taking over the studio. "When we were acquired we were all looking forward to it like, 'Yeah, we're owned by EA, they're going to give us a budget, we finally don't have to keep constraining ourselves and crushing things down and cutting corners.' And one of the first things they did was like, 'OK, based on your budgets, we need to cut across the company 10%.' And we were like, 'Oh, so we're not getting more, we're getting less.' That was a bit of the EA feeling. We call it the EA butter knife. It just seemed arbitrary, and not very smart."

The corporate cost cutting Oster describes took place nearly 20 years ago, but it continued throughout EA's ownership. Most recently, in 2023 when 50 staff were laid off to “shift [Bioware] towards a more agile and more focused studio”. Then in 2025, more staff were laid off, this time it was described as "taking [the] opportunity between full development cycles to reimagine how we work at BioWare".

It becomes particularly clear with Anthem, Bioware's launched-but-quickly-shuttered live-service game, how EA's influence manifested at the studio. Interviewed in the Summer Eternal Anthology, long-time Bioware writer David Gaider explains the design concept for the game seemed to be formed from executive demands. "The nearest I could tell, working in a corporate environment, was that it was a sifted-down list of bullet-point requirements, that no single person ever took responsibility for," Gaider explains. "It just kind of happened like that. There needs to be this project, so it was willed into existence, but nobody would say that before this project is actually kicked off, someone needs to take responsibility for the vision."

Mark Darrah echoes this in his own interview in the anthology. "Casey pitched the exact thing [EA execs] wanted to hear," he says. "Always online, live service, infinite revenue. Multiplayer." Darrah describes it as "a near-perfect pitch deck." But, he also questions whether "Bioware is the company to make a live service looter shooter."

However, while those ideas may have filtered down from EA and then been pitched back to them by Bioware leadership, it meant that work would begin on a project long before it had crystalised into a clear premise. "The idea was that they could kick the project off, and that the designer, the design department, or the creative director, particularly, would then create the vision," Gaider says. "But at that point, the project's already happening. So each time it felt like trying to catch a bronco bull who's already out and running and bucking, and you're just trying to keep up."

For that reason, Gaider claims "Anthem was doomed from the start."

Development on Anthem began in 2012, but when Gaider joined the project 18 months later, it was struggling. "The team on it was just going around in circles," Gaider says. They didn't know what they wanted." Hudson then left Bioware in 2014 and Darrah says "they basically ran with no leadership, for two, three years." In 2017 Hudson returned and Darrah was brought over from the team working on the next Dragon Age project. Over the next two years Anthem took shape before it eventually released in 2019, but, in doing so, it meant the Dragon Age team was also then left without leadership for the next two years.

"My biggest regret," Darrah says, "is I kind of feel like I should have told EA 'no' when they asked me to take over Anthem. I should have just let whatever was going to happen, happen at that point." He adds that leaving "Dragon Age running without an executive producer from 2017 to 2019 was bad. That was a huge mistake."

Darrah says he doesn't feel like he had a choice, however, because "EA was very good at making Bioware feel like things were very dire." Though, he points to the fact that Hudson's replacement as Bioware's general manager, Gary McKay, was able to delay Dragon Age: Veilguard through multiple financial years. "That tells me either he's magic, which is possible, or it was never quite as 'gun to our head' as we thought it was," Darrah says.

(Bear in mind Darrah was being interviwed ahead of Bioware's closure and he's referring to events in the past five years.)

However, while pressure from EA may have significantly impacted the development and direction of Bioware's games, it appears that the studio repeated the same mistake for decades. Gaider, Darrah, and the developers that contributed to the Mass Effect oral history, all say Bioware consistently got deep into projects without having a vision of what they were trying to build.

"When we started, Dragon Age's big premise was 'It's D&D, but it's not'," Gaider says in his Summer Eternal interview. "We'll just file the ID numbers enough to make it our own. We went through a long period of going around in circles on Dragon Age." Edwin's covered this as a push and pull between teams that thought they were making Conan, others thinking it was Lord of the Rings, and still others thinking they were making a new Diablo.

Gaider believes the only reason EA allowed Bioware to complete the original Dragon Age was because the team "were already halfway through development, and [EA] considered it sunk cost. They said, 'Well, you've got to finish this.' But it was clear that Electronic Arts considered Dragon Age, our initial vision, to be too old fashioned, too slow, and too ponderous."

Gaider says Mass Effect had the benefit of Casey Hudson, who was able to pull together a single vision for the project much sooner. Though this may be something of a grass is greener situation, because the developers on Mass Effect describe being on the game for three years before it started coming together and even then it was only because they had to prepare something for E3.

"It was the first time we pulled together playable moments in the engine that really proved to us what the game was capable of," Hudson says in the oral history. "Until you pull things together, it's all sort of theoretical, and you think you know what game you're making. But once you have that moment in time, where everything's pulled together, you know exactly what the game needs to be."

It was only after the vertical slice was completed, lead designer Preston Watamaniuk says, "that the team understood what we were building and was able to then work autonomously, without as much hands-on direction."

This approach continued through the years. In 2012 Bioware began work on Mass Effect: Andromeda. Three years into development they scrapped the plans and tech they were using to procedurally generate worlds switching to a more traditional style of RPG, making the bulk of the game in its final 18 months. In 2015 they started work on a their next Dragon Age project, which was shelved in 2017 in favour of the project that would become Veilguard, but that would then take another seven years to complete, with a live service component first adopted from Anthem and then dropped after Anthem's failure.

It looks to have still been a problem for the studio with its current Mass Effect project. First announced back in 2020, initially as part of the "N7 Day" and then followed up with a teaser trailer at The Game Awards, the new Mass Effect was still described in EA's open letter as in "early-development". Developers who worked on the cancelled game are saying they believe they were "making something special", and aimed build on what they saw coming out of Larian and CD Projekt Red, but six years is a long time to still be in early development and sounds a lot like the project could have fallen into the "going around in circles" Darrah described of Dragon Age and Anthem.

I am saddened by Bioware's closure. The team have put out incredible games over the decades, and it sounds like they were never a natural match for EA's executives or the corporation's goals. However, it also sounds like the studio have continued to face the same challenges on projects year after year without really learning how to avoid them.

Still, it would have been much better that they be allowed to spin off and become independent or be sold to another publisher who knows what the talented team can create, than to be closed down after years of staff layoffs and project cancellations.

#3422. How to Enable Global Privacy Control in Every Browser (and Why You Should)

Security-wise, most browsers are an open door, happy to reveal your screen size, operating system, language preferences, or GPU model when a website asks. But telling those websites to stop selling your data to third parties is still more confusing than it should be.

Although about a dozen U.S. states—including California, Delaware, New Jersey, and Texas—now require businesses to honor consumers’ privacy preferences online, most browsers do not offer a native opt-out option that lets you signal those preferences by default. Now, the California Opt Me Out Act, which goes into effect on Jan 1, 2027, is making it mandatory for browsers to do so.

Still, for now, native support for a global privacy opt-out remains inconsistent among web browsers. TSome privacy-forward browsers like Brave, DuckDuckGo, and Mozilla Firefox enable this feature by default, while others, like Chrome or Edge, require you to install third-party extensions such as the EFF Privacy Badger. Since coverage and setup vary wildly, I’ll walk you through the exact steps to enable this feature on every major browser.

On a technical level, GPC combines an HTTP header (Sec-GPC) with a JavaScript property (navigator.globalPrivacyControl) that your browser sends with every website fetch request to let website owners know that they are not supposed to share or sell your data.

Unlike previous iterations like Firefox’s Do Not Track, GPC issues a specific request that’s backed by leading data protection legislation, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), making websites and businesses more likely to honor them. With over 150 million users and more than 66,000 participating websites, the GPC project has garnered support and participation from the Mozilla Foundation, The Washington Post, Automattic, Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF), The New York Times, and others. It has even been made into an official work item at the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C).

When it comes to GPC support, web browsers fall broadly into two camps. There are privacy-focused platforms like Brave Browser, DuckDuckGo, and Firefox that offer native support using a built-in toggle, or just enable it by default. Then there are mainstream browsers like Chrome, Edge, and Safari that offer limited support through third-party extensions, or sometimes none at all. Here are the exact steps to follow to enable Global Privacy Control on Firefox, Chrome, Edge, Brave Browser, and others:

Being a privacy-first Chromium-based web browser, Brave has GPC enabled by default on Windows, macOS, and Android. However, due to certain platform limitations on iOS, Brave can only enable the feature through the JavaScript API and not the HTTP header on iPhones.

You don’t need to do anything to enable GPC on Brave Browser, but there’s an easy way to confirm whether it’s active and running:

Launch Brave. In the address bar, type in “brave://flags/#brave-global-privacy-control-enabled” and hit Enter.

Confirm that the Settings page reads Default or Enabled, not Disabled.

To disable GPC on your browser, navigate to Settings > Brave Shields and Privacy to find the corresponding toggle.

DuckDuckGo, like Brave Browser, enables GPC by default on Windows, macOS, Android, iOS, and iPadOS. There’s no built-in toggle to disable it, which tells you just how seriously this browser takes its privacy implementation. (There's even a DuckDuckGo Search & Tracker Protection extension you can add to Chrome, Edge, and Firefox to enable GPC on those browsers, too!)

If you’d like to confirm that GPC is on when you’re using DuckDuckGo, there’s an easy way to do that:

Open a new tab on DuckDuckGo Private Browser and visit the Global Privacy Control website.

Check the notification banner at the very top to see if it reads “GPC signal detected.”

To avoid breaking certain websites on Android, iOS, and Mac devices, DuckDuckGo only sends the GPC signal to websites that are known to respect it. However, you still get full coverage on Windows or if you use the Chrome extension.

Firefox has offered GPC support since version 120. However, it’s not enabled by default, and you need to hunt for a specific toggle to switch it on.

Click on the menu button on your Firefox app, then navigate to Settings.

Access the Privacy & Security tab from the panel on the left.

Keep scrolling until you see Additional protections. On Android version 122+, look for a section that says Enhanced Tracking Protection.

You should see a checkbox next to an option that reads “Tell websites not to sell or share my data.” This is what you’re looking for.

Make sure you check the option to enable GPC, then relaunch Firefox.

As of this writing, Google Chrome does not offer a built-in toggle or native support for GPC. However, you can install a third-party browser extension like the Privacy Badger by EFF or DuckDuckGo Search & Tracker Protection to enable Global Privacy Control very easily.

Search for Privacy Badger on the Chrome Web Store.

Once you find it, click Add to Chrome. Make sure to enable any required permissions for the extension to work too.

That’s it, GPC is enabled on all sites now. You don’t need to fiddle around with the settings since everything comes pre-configured.

As with Chrome, Microsoft Edge users must also rely on third-party extensions to enable Global Privacy Control. And like before, you can choose between Privacy Badger or the DuckDuckGo extension to activate this feature.

Open the Edge Add-ons store and look for Privacy Badger. EFF’s website lists both Edge and Edge for Android as supported browsers for the extension.

GPC is now on by default. You don’t need to configure anything else.

First, GPC is a signal. It merely informs websites of your privacy preferences, but does nothing to actively block or prevent tracking or sharing of your data. Second, GPC is narrower in scope because it only covers selling or sharing your data with third parties, not collecting or storing data for a website’s own use.

When you’re enabling Global Privacy Control instead of using an anti-tracking app or ad blocker, you’re trading coverage and effectiveness for convenience. You’ll have to just trust that websites will honor your privacy preferences, which many don’t, even when they are required to. For example, Disney just settled a $2.75 million lawsuit with the California AG’s office because it didn’t honor opt-out requests for logged-in users on every device like it was supposed to.

I'm not even all that much of a BioWare enthusiast. I'm not sure I'm in mourning, exactly. Partly that's because videogame studios are big corporate abstractions, unworthy of our silly human emotions; the story here is that a bunch of people have lost their livelihoods, after their employers triumphantly thrust themselves into unbelievable debt. And partly it's because, as with any studio of over 30 years standing, BioWare was a much-caulked and jury-rigged ship of Theseus – paddling around in the doldrums of 2026, I'm not entirely sure what I should be mourning, any more.

Reportedly, there were around 50 staff left at BioWare, mostly labouring away on Mass Effect 5, when EA closed them this week. A lot of those who worked on the studio's formative games, including the very first Baldur's Gate, are long departed. The past decade especially has seen a steady exodus of BioWare talent, alongside a barrage of "veteran" start-ups and spiritual successor projects, many of which have sadly now faded and folded.

Within that arc, my own appreciation for BioWare has waxed and waned. I honestly can't remember enough of Baldur's Gate 2 to have strong opinions, though I overall acknowledge its brilliance. I probably felt closest to the studio during the heyday of the original Mass Effect trilogy. The original's Vigil scene still gives me shivers, and who could forget the prodigious act of fan service demolition that was Mass Effect 2's opening, to say nothing of its legendary "Suicide Mission" (I left Mordin behind).

I will always carry a candle for Dragon Age: Inquisition – a generous and unwieldy return to save-the-world-dom after Dragon Age 2's leaner city story, which I cherish for its beautiful Tarot art, its bustling hub castle, and its spread of rambunctious and difficult companion characters. I greatly enjoy Ferelden as a setting, all the different yarns the various races spin about the world's becoming. Still, I bounced right off Dragon Age: The Veilguard's on-the-nose, corridor-like design, despite Nic's guarded exhortations.

Anthem? I was keener on that than a lot of you, but I remember it mostly as a conflicted yet promising aviator with nowhere to go – so much BioWare flourish, floundering through a swamp of live service. I thought Mass Effect: Andromeda was a wide, shallow bowl of lukewarm space-gruel lent relish only by its launch bugs. There are other BioWare games I simply haven't played – Neverwinter Nights (well, apart from the five minutes I managed, before it became clear my computer at the time really couldn't run it) and its celebrated modding toolkit, the Orientalised fable Jade Empire and, whisper it, almighty KOTOR.

As for my experience of BioWare as a games journalist, it's pretty brief and polarised. On the one hand, spending three hours with a preview build of Inquisition in a London warehouse, alongside such guiding lights as former PCGamer scribe Chris Thursten, is probably as close to heaven as I'll get before my inevitable deathbed recantation. The thing is, you don't have to have enjoyed, or even played many BioWare games to understand the difference they've made, and why the newly private EA's closure of the studio is such a kick in the guts, however presaged by years of interference, mismanagement, and neglect. As regards North American and European sci-fantasy incarnations, at least, BioWare have always been the RPG's sleeping sentinel. They wrote the genre around themselves, producing and revising a vocabulary over decades that so many other designers, writers and modders are in conversation with – whether courting comparison, or critiquing and even despising.

BioWare are why D&D RPGs took off. They're the creators of by far the most influential adaptation of the tabletop game's forbidding ruleset. They are the studio you probably think of, when you think of party-based stories with branching plots. They are the blockbuster joint you think of, rivalled only by Final Fantasy and more recently Larian, when you think of companions who deserve the starring role.

Even their stumbles have thrown open ground for other writers, designers and artists to investigate: that main-and-supporting-band antagonism between critical path and sidequest, for example - between yet another chronicle of destined heroes averting some great evil, and the huggable humour of playing go-between for a knight who's too shy to hit on somebody. How to reconcile the grandiosity with the closeness is the trad fantasy RPG's founding dilemma. They popularised in-game romances and, yes, sex scenes that generally feel like the convincing result of hours of careful narrative distillation, and occasionally like a helping of cheesecake unlocked by pawing away at a hidden skilltree.

They are the studio who got us tallying up colour-coded Good and Bad points to shape both protagonist and endgame - so tacky, but undeniably striking as a vehicle for stark shifts and flavourful writing, as demonstrated in reverse by the omnivorous wishy-washiness of Mass Effect: Andromeda. Their morality systems are a place from which to start thinking about other, less videogamey, more organic and conflicting entanglements of Morality and System. They introduced a particular species of videogame celebrity with Commander Shepard, and they presided over a very particular stage in the evolving friction between creators and "audience" with Mass Effect 3's much-revised ending. While I'd made up my mind to stop covering EA games, following the Saudi-led acquisition, I was curious, if not optimistic, to see what they might have done with Mass Effect 5.

Above all, BioWare were the wizards of words. They had less-good scripts but I don't think they ever penned a bad one. Looking back over their games is dizzying and deafening, like opening a door in a dark, crumbling house to reveal a crowd of revellers - demons and courtesans and krogans and fops and berserkers, painted glorious shades by secret firelight and dancing in a ring.

So many words! How many words? Tens of millions, surely. Words that conjure words, raising them from the dust: Go for the optics, Chatika! Go for the eyes, Boo! Words seemingly drawn from some personal well of pain and tenderness, easily reduced to authorial self-commentary, that stick in my mind where so much else about the ageing fantasy has lost its charm. He died of the Wasting in a Denerim ward... those last weeks, I read to him. I had to take his hand to turn the pages, and I couldn't tell if he was too weak, or if it was the old game. He smiled at that, his big girl… Don't let anyone tell you how to mourn. And when someone says 'move on', you take their hand and say, 'my choice.'"
