#4199. Irish central bank fines Coinbase Ireland $3.6 million over fund safeguarding failures

Coinbase Ireland was hit with a 3.25 million-euro ($3.6 million) fine from the country’s central bank over alleged breaches of its safeguarding and outsourcing obligations.

The Irish division of cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase Global COIN failed to establish an adequate outsourcing governance framework, the Central Bank of Ireland (CBI) said on Thursday.

Safeguarding and outsourcing requirements underpin the protection of customer funds. Companies are obliged to keep them adequately funded and monitored and set up procedures to report to the regulator when material changes occur.

“They placed excessive reliance on the U.S.-based Coinbase Group as an outsourced service provider to implement safeguarding processes and controls [and] failed to adequately safeguard customers’ funds, both through inadequate system design and insufficient control testing,” the CBI said.

When crypto-friendly bank Silvergate was on the verge of collapse in 2023, Coinbase instructed 30 customers to deposit funds into an alternate account held by Coinbase at another U.S. bank, the company said in a statement following the CBI’s announcement.

The funds were therefore held alongside those belonging to customers of another Coinbase entity, a breach of safeguarding requirements.

Coinbase also referred to euro withdrawals from U.S. or U.K.-based customers being paid from Coinbase Ireland’s safeguarding account, and coding errors leading to the account being underfunded by an average of 0.54% in 2023 and the first half of 2024.

“No customer suffered any financial loss as a result of these matters, and no customer was ever unable to access their funds,” Coinbase said.

The penalty arrives almost one year after the CBI fined Coinbase Europe 21.4 million euros for breaching its anti-money laundering (AML) obligations between 2021 and 2025.

#4198. Want Studio-Style Pilates at Home? Start With These Editor-Tested Reformers

Our top picks for every kind of at-home Pilates setup, from compact machines to studio-worthy reformers.

If you’re a pilates princess, you already know what a reformer is, and you’re well acquainted with the many ways it delivers a low-impact, strength-building workout. However, if you’re a newbie, here’s a quick crash course: A reformer is a specially designed machine, originally developed by Joseph Pilates, that consists of springs for resistance, a moving carriage, a foot bar that you push or pull, and pulleys to hold onto or hook to your feet when performing pilates exercises.

“I’d describe the reformer as a machine designed to help create an understanding of your body with resistance,” says Sam Miles, owner and co-founder of Good Time Pilates in New York City. “It gives real time feedback so you can find sensation through the shortening and lengthening of your muscles. In short and easy terms: an assist to finding your muscle engagement!”

There are unique benefits to using a reformer that are hard to replicate in a pilates mat workout, or really any other type of workout. But that doesn’t mean you have to be advanced to start using one. “The modifications to increase or decrease challenge make it a truly universal machine for any body and any fitness level, which is very unique and just awesome,” Miles says. “You can rehabilitate with a reformer, strengthen muscles, stretch and find release work, it’s the best!”

Pilates reformers are available in different types: classical and contemporary. The classical reformer stays true to Joseph Pilates’ original design; the machines are made of wood, and have springs that all have the exact same resistance level. Purists are going to want to consider these machines.

Contemporary reformers are an evolutionary riff on the original and often incorporate springs of varying resistance that can be adjusted. The dimensions also usually vary a bit from the OG classical reformer design.

While it used to be that reformers were only found in Pilates studios, more and more women are investing in a reformer as an addition to their home gym setup. There are also less intense reformer models on the market that are specifically designed for at-home use, and these generally come in at a fraction of the cost of a classical or studio-grade contemporary reformer.

After testing a bunch of options, we narrowed down our picks to a variety of machines, including commercial-grade reformers and pared-down, reimagined reformers that are made specifically to take up less space and be more affordable.

#4197. Romantic Hypocrisy: Perceiving and Practicing Infidelity

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Infidelity is a significant threat within exclusive romantic relationships that, in many cases, can lead to dissolution. But how do you define it? Most couples would agree there are definite examples of cheating, and then there is the grey area. Text messages, browsing singles websites, and expressing interest in (attractive) others are examples of behaviors that might be viewed as ambiguous in terms of interest and intent. The significance of interpretation is important within romantic relationships, considering the potential for misunderstanding. Research explains.

Researchers Ashley E. Thompson and Lucia F. O’Sullivan examined the way partners engage in inconsistent explanations of infidelity in a piece aptly entitled “I Can But You Can’t.”[i] Using online surveys with 810 adult participants, they compared opinions about infidelity given by participants regarding their own behavior and the behavior of their partners. People judged their own behavior more permissively than the way they viewed the behavior of their partners, yet only in certain areas. They were more permissive in the areas of emotional/affectionate and technology/online behaviors, but not when viewing behavior that was categorized as sexual/explicit or solitary.

Many study participants admitted to being unfaithful, especially within the categories of emotional/affectionate and technology/online. Thompson and O’Sullivan explored some of the reasons behind the differences in the ways partners viewed the same behavior—depending on who committed it. They note that generally, individuals tend to judge the behavior of others more severely than their own, a tendency known as actor-observer bias. This self-serving bias blames others and attributes one’s own bad behavior to external factors, situational or contextual, yet attributes the bad behavior of others to internal reasons, such as stable personal or dispositional characteristics. They give the example of someone blaming one’s own low exam score on the situational factor of a poor instructor, while explaining someone else’s low score on a dispositional factor such as low intelligence.

Within romantic relationships, Thompson and O’Sullivan found that individuals classify certain behaviors as infidelity and then report participating in them themselves. Their study included a sample of 810 individuals, 328 men and 482 women, with a mean age of 34.4 years. Most of them lived in the United States, almost 90% were heterosexual, and most were in a committed relationship.

The measures included four subscales. The Sexual/Explicit Behaviour subscale included explicit physical contact such as oral sex and sexual intercourse. The Technology/Online Behaviour subscale related to explicit technology-facilitated communication, including messaging, browsing a singles website, and webcam masturbation. The Romantic/Affectionate Behaviour subscale included displaying romantic interest or intimate activities, including sharing secrets, having a casual dinner, and providing emotional support. The Solitary Behaviour subscale covered behaviors people typically do alone, such as masturbating or viewing pornography.

Regarding areas of inconsistency, Thompson and O’Sullivan reported that differences in judgments between self and others revealed a self-enhancing bias and greater willingness to downplay one’s own negative behavior, especially when it was more ambiguous, in order to maintain a positive self-image. They found this actor-observer difference in judgments of technology/online and emotional/affectionate infidelity behaviors, not solitary or sexual/explicit behaviors.

Regarding solitary behaviors, Thompson and O’Sullivan noted that a substantial number of participants viewed them as suggestive of infidelity to some extent because they involved arousal or attraction to someone else, but did not view them as serious violations of monogamy expectations. Yet as a practical matter, a significant number of partners would view them as distasteful and undesirable, perhaps even to the extent they would rethink a relationship.

This research highlights the importance of discussing fidelity expectations early on in a relationship to avoid misunderstanding and prevent incompatible pairings. Healthy, sustainable, long-term relationships start with good communication and are sustained through mutual respect, love, and commitment.

The condition causes the cornea to thin and bulge outward into an irregular cone shape instead of staying round.

This can cause blurry or distorted vision, increased sensitivity to light, glare and nighttime halos, frequent or rapid changes in eyeglass prescription, and eye irritation or redness.

“I didn’t know anything about it before I went to the doctor, nor did I really know that I had truly blurry vision or, like, distorted images and stuff,” Curry told Healthline. “It was kind of just the way I saw things, and for whatever reason, I didn’t ask the questions or go have an eye screen and all that until our general manager for the Warriors, his name’s Bob Myers, [pointed it out].”

A small puncture wound through a 75,000-year-old Neanderthal shoulder blade may be the earliest evidence of death by spear on humanity’s family tree. Given forensic details such as the entry angle and overall damage, researchers believe the injury was made as an intentionally fatal “heart shot” that killed the ancient hominin within moments. The discovery is detailed in a study published today in the journal Scientific Reports.

In addition to the grisly injury, the nearly translucent bone itself makes this evidence even more striking.

“It’s extraordinary to have a perfectly preserved scapula from 75,000 years ago. These bones are so thin that you often don’t get complete scapulae from modern burials,” explained University of Cambridge archaeologist and study co-author Emma Pomeroy. “We were able to lift it out in one piece from under almost eight meters [roughly 26 feet] of rock and sediment.”

Pomeroy and her colleagues excavated the remains from inside Shanidar Cave, an archaeological site in Iraqi Kurdistan famous for containing evidence of ritualistic Neanderthal burials. The roughly 0.6 inch, nearly circular perforation on the bone was likely made with a wooden spear, since a stone or flint tip would create a flatter injury and leave behind microscopic mineral shards. The individual who inflicted the fatal injury drove the spear downward through the victim’s left chest cavity from the front, piercing major arteries and the lungs before creating the hole in the left scapula.

Remnants of a wooden spear weren’t found near the Neanderthal’s remains, which is understandable since the material often decomposes so quickly. That said, prior archaeological digs have revealed similar prehistoric wooden weapons dating as far back as 400,000 years. Pomeroy’s team believes a rounded spear carved from wood almost certainly caused the killing blow.

“Holes left by recreations of spears from this time are an excellent fit for the one we found in the Neanderthal scapula. That early technology absolutely works for this wound,” said Pomeroy.

Although it’s possible that a mishap like a hunting accident was the cause of death, Pomeroy argues the cumulative details suggest something far more purposeful.

“The position of the wound and the force required to push it right through the Neanderthal’s body bear the hallmarks of an intended kill shot directed towards the heart,” she added.

Another major injury the victim sustained before the possible stabbing supports this hypothesis—and complicates the story. In addition to the scapula, archaeologists documented a pair of left ribs, a right rib fragment, a nearly complete right hand, and seven teeth. Collectively, the remains indicate the Neanderthal was about 45 years old at the time of death. However, dental damage reveals the individual suffered a severe blunt-force blow to the front of the face at least a year before death. This injury split a lower front incisor lengthwise and even rotated some teeth inward.

“There’s no doubt that [they] would have had difficulty communicating and been in absolute agony for a long period of time,” said Pomeroy.

Despite this blow to the mouth, the Neanderthal survived for months or even years. This was only possible because others must have cared for the individual, including duties as simple as preparing softer foods to eat. In this sense, there is a distinct possibility the spear wound was a final, merciful act by someone close to them. According to Pomeroy, the prehistoric scene showcases both the highs and lows of what it means to be a human.

“On the one hand, we have evidence of a terribly violent end,” she said. “On the other, this was clearly an individual who was cared for during their lifetime.”

#4188. How Fear of Vulnerability Keeps Mental Illness Hidden

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma

My brothers came to every one of my court appearances. They watched me at my worst as I was charged and punished for my actions while in a manic psychosis. But when I transferred to a state psychiatric hospital, they all but disappeared.

More than once, my mother has asked them why. Why did they stand by her through the criminal phase but remain silent through the slower, uglier work of recovery? I don’t know is the answer she hears. Her friends long offered a variation of the same refrain. On an early morning call last year, one close friend confessed: I don’t know enough about it, and I don’t want to say the wrong thing.

Since defense mechanisms, by definition, work outside our capacity to be aware of them, I don’t know may be an honest admission: we can struggle to see what’s stopping us from engaging, from asking a question that makes another feel seen. Stanley Cohen calls this implicatory denial: we accept the facts of what happened but not what they ask of us.

My brothers know the diagnosis and lived the horrors of its history, especially when my manic cruelty turned on their families. Still, I wonder why they turn away from what the knowledge would ask of them, or how it might implicate them. To be fair, I can’t read my brothers’ minds, and fear is not the only reason people keep their distance; anger, grief, and the urgencies of their own hectic lives surely play a part. But fear is a reason we rarely name.

They are not alone. For 10 years, when anyone asked what was wrong with me, I gave the same answer. While my I don’t know started in a brain that couldn’t see its own illness, theirs unfolded in selves reluctant to look. I suspect what united both was the same fear of vulnerability.

Vulnerability wears many masks. When psychiatry speaks of vulnerability, it typically means diathesis: one’s genetic and temperamental risk to develop a mental disorder. But in everyday terms, being vulnerable means fearing that others will see you as needy, wrong, scared, or unknowledgeable. And psychiatry is far less comfortable exploring this terrain of emotional exposure.

This fear of vulnerability perpetuates what my mother and I have started to call the fog and the silence of mental illness. The fog is anosognosia, a broken neurological instrument of self-knowledge rather than denial. It is an internal blindness that kept me from getting help for a decade. The silence, an external blindness, operates on two levels: the interpersonal silence of social stigma and the systemic silence of a fragmented mental health care system that never educated my family about my diagnosis or gave them a role in my care.

The mechanism that keeps mental illness hidden through the fog and the silence is not vulnerability but the defensive armor we put on to avoid it. Armor holds both blindnesses in place: it thickens the fog from within and amplifies the silence from without.

Carl Jung had a name for what you could call the fog’s accomplice: the persona, which is the face we present to the world. Mine was the intellectual hero, which was built on the conviction that I would rather be thought ambitious than exposed as afraid. When an AB in calculus contradicted the image of invulnerability fused to my intellectual identity, I vowed never again to place myself where anything could betray it.

What the persona can’t admit is relegated to what Jung called the shadow, which is where my bipolar illness found refuge, since being sick or weak had no place in the hero’s story. “When one’s conception of who one is gets stranded in time,” a leading psychologist of anosognosia, Xavier Amador, notes, “one can’t help ignoring or explaining away any evidence that contradicts it.” The illness removed my instrument of insight, and the persona ensured I would reject everyone else’s.

For its part, the interpersonal silence of stigma has its own engine: the fear of not knowing, which is how implicatory denial plays out in everyday life. It is what my mother’s friend meant when she said she didn’t want to say the wrong thing. Learning about bipolar disorder means first admitting you don’t know, and perhaps you don’t want to know, so silence feels safer than revealing it. Mental illness functions as our society’s collective shadow: the discomforting possibility we keep at a distance because it could happen to any of us.

My brothers’ armor did not form without cause. The threats I made were real, and they created real injuries: armor naturally forms around real wounds, which is why it can be so difficult to take off. Accepting that I have changed since then would require them to become vulnerable in the face of that harm, and open to the realization that their account of events is incomplete.

The mental health care system has its own armor of specialization and siloing. While I was treated by psychiatrists, psychologists, neurologists, and social workers, no one practiced the kind of slow medicine that could see my case whole. Narrow clinical expertise is a structural way of never having to admit I don’t know. As Arthur Kleinman explains, patients need to know “that you, the caregiver, are also willing to reveal your own vulnerability.”Recovery, for me, has been a story of embracing my vulnerability: owning both the illness and the harm, across a decade of my life, rather than exiling them. Jung described the task each of us faces as learning to “reconcile himself with his own nature—how he is to love the enemy in his own heart and call the wolf his brother.” We can encourage the same reconciliation in those who love us. It begins with curiosity. My mother once did not know the word anosognosia, or much about bipolar disorder, or how to connect with a son who couldn’t hear her. She began by reading, asking questions, and speaking into the silence of ignorance. Where I don’t know closes the door, curiosity pries it open. Curiosity requires exposure to the very vulnerability the silence conceals. For a brother, a friend, or a clinician, it may require them to confess ignorance, feel uncomfortable, risk being wrong, or modify an interpretation of the past where they found sanctuary. Curiosity can break the silence with the same three words this article opened on, followed by two more: I don’t know. Tell me.

Olympic gold medalist Simone Biles is the most decorated gymnast in history. But the beloved icon is taking a break from talking about the balance beam and vault to discuss a personal health condition that she lives with alongside 31 million fellow Americans: eczema.

“A couple years ago, I started noticing flaking and itching on my scalp, especially on the crown of my head. And at first, I thought it was a lot of dandruff, and then I was worried it could be something contagious,” Biles told Healthline. “I’ve also dealt with patches of dry skin on my arm, but it comes and goes.”

Recently, she noticed her symptoms getting worse, especially when stressed from training.

“I would definitely be in the gym, finding myself waiting for bars or for beam and just scratching and scratching and [people] would be like, ‘Simone, can you stop scratching?’ and I’m like, ‘Oh, yeah, my bad, sorry,’” said Biles.

When her hairdresser noticed scaly patches on her scalp, Biles began searching online for answers and trying home remedies, including combs, shampoos, and oils.

“[Self-fixes] weren’t really helping, and I didn’t go to the dermatologist immediately because I was busy in the gym training,” said Biles. “I also felt ashamed and embarrassed, so I started speaking out about it at Paris Olympics.”

After making a TikTok video about her symptoms, she was inspired to see a dermatologist who diagnosed her with the skin conditions seborrheic dermatitis and atopic dermatitis (also known as eczema).

“[A] lot of what she is communicating really mirrors what we’re seeing in patients and what we know about these conditions,” Angela Lamb, MD, Bile’s dermatologist, told Healthline.

While eczema is a group of chronic inflammatory skin conditions, Lamb said stress can trigger flares, as well as extremes of weather, especially for those with atopic dermatitis.

“And then there’s just individual triggers, so patients really need to listen to their bodies, find out what moisturizer would work well for their skin, find out if you have seborrheic dermatitis on your scalp,” said Lamb. “[Know] what are the products that tend to be flaring it or what you do that makes it better or worse.”

How Biles found relief from her eczema symptoms

Biles says she found relief for her symptoms with Zoryve, a once-daily, steroid-free, topical prescription medication that comes in both foam and cream forms. Biles uses both forms: the foam for her scalp, and the cream for her skin.

“I feel like it fits in easily to my lifestyle…you can apply it to your scalp, and it’s really, really lightweight, and then for my eczema, it’s a cream, and it’s non-greasy,” said Biles.

In addition to helping her feel more comfortable, she said that receiving effective treatment eased the embarrassment she experienced around the appearance of her skin.

“[Going] on a lot of shoots and a lot of sets, [I was] definitely more self-conscious about my parts or if I was wearing braids — the flaking, what that looks like on camera — so I would just try to cover it up with oils and all of that stuff,” she said.

To share her journey, Biles partnered with Zoryve’s maker, Arcutis Biotherapeutics, to launch its Skin to Believe In campaign. The initiative aims to break the stigma around chronic skin conditions that can make people feel self-conscious, isolated, or embarrassed.

“I know people are going through similar things, thinking it’s dandruff or thinking it’s contagious,” Biles said. “[You] don’t have to silently suffer. Own it. Go see a trusted professional, and get the relief that you deserve.”

Because every medication has potential side effects, Lamb said it’s important to talk with your doctor about a medication’s potential risks and benefits.

“This one, the side effects are in a very low percent of patients, which can include some nausea, headache, as well as an allergic reaction, but you’ll discuss that with your physician, and in this case, dermatologists are the main ones writing for Zoryve,” said Lamb.

She said it’s encouraging that nonsteroidal medications exist as options for patients with skin conditions. She explained that Zoryve contains roflumilast, a PDE4 inhibitor that helps reduce inflammation involved in eczema.

“So I’m very excited [because] I think the landscape of how we’re treating many conditions is really changing as we understand the science and the core of what drives it better,” said Lamb. “I think that’s been the largest breakthrough in the last many years in healthcare…understanding root cause is better, which can lead us to better treatments.”

How Biles reduces stress to help manage her eczema

Because Biles believes stress can contribute to her eczema flares, she practices a wellness routine that includes spending time with her girlfriends to get their nails done or a massage, and taking baths at night to wind down.

“[But] honestly, without training every single day, I don’t feel as stressed,” she said. “[Everyone] can find different ways to alleviate their stress. And it looks different for everyone.”

As she approaches her 30th birthday, Biles says she’s learned to prioritize her physical and mental well-being.

“I feel like now I know that there is help out there if I do get stuck or I notice something, and I feel like maturity has helped a lot with that,” she said.

“And I just feel like being an ambassador and having awareness for so much has opened up the doors for me to say, ‘Hey, I’m not feeling okay. Can I please do this instead?’ Or, ‘Oh, I need to speak to my therapist twice a week because I feel like I’m stuck in a rut right now.’ So I feel like [aging] has given me space and… confidence to do that.”

#4177. Super El Niño influencing ‘strange uptick’ in illegal Galápagos iguana trafficking

Published Oct 15, 2026 2:23 PM EDT

Marine biologists are sounding the alarm on behalf of the Galápagos Islands’ marine iguana (Amblyrhynchus cristatus). Without tightened regulations on the often abused exotic pet trade, researchers warn that a recent “strange uptick” in illegal animal trafficking combined with this season’s Super El Niño is further endangering the already vulnerable reptiles living on the Eastern Pacific archipelago.

“They are already shrinking in size and facing mass mortality, either by starvation or by trying to swim longer distances in search of food,” Juan José Alava, a marine ecotoxicologist at Canada’s Simon Fraser University, explained in a recent letter published in Science.

The marine iguana family encompasses at least 11 known subspecies, each specially evolved to live on the Galápagos Islands. They are the only reptiles in the world capable of foraging underwater for macroalgae, which makes up the majority of their diet. Although iguana colonies remain relatively stable, the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) still lists them as Vulnerable due to predators, mounting pollution issues, and cyclical El Niño events. Ten of the 11 recognized subspecies are also locally classified as either Critically Endangered or Endangered. Sale of iguanas is banned under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES), but a recent uptick in illegal trafficking along with increasingly dramatic climate shifts is adding more stress to the situation.

Ecuadorian authorities have caught people attempting to smuggle iguanas multiple times this year. In May, officials discovered at least a dozen animals inside handbags at an airport that were bound for illegal markets. Law enforcement found another 32 tied up inside a suitcase intended for similar buyers in August. Many trafficked iguanas won’t survive the stress and rough handling, but even rescued reptiles face challenges. Because subspecies are evolved to live on specific islands, ecologists must test each rescued iguana’s DNA to determine where to return them.

Smuggling hasn’t been seen within the Galápagos Marine Reserve itself, but Alava and his colleagues theorize that smugglers are capturing iguanas near Ecuador’s coastal shorelines. Traffickers then obtain CITES permits that falsely declare the reptiles are captive bred in other nations, even though no such authorized facilities actually exist. Often, smugglers are only engaged in the industry out of financial necessity or coercion.

“Artisinal fishing communities from Galapagos and coastal Ecuador, who are facing severe socioeconomic hardship and expanding organized crime activities, may feel pressured to participate in this international black market,” the letter’s authors wrote.

Conservationists are now urging a multifaceted approach to protect the animals. CITES representatives need to close existing permit loopholes, while the Ecuadorian government should focus on simultaneously breaking up organized crime and providing economic aid to struggling communities.

“Maintaining global ocean observing systems is essential, and extreme marine event forecasting and response capabilities are improving,” the authors wrote. “But responsibility and action remain fragmented across environmental [organizations], fisheries, and emergency management, and public health agencies.”

#4172. 3 Strong Signs Your Partner Has an Obsessive Personality

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Passion is a valued personality trait. We are inspired by people who wear passion on their sleeves, and we do our best to bring passion into our own endeavors. When it comes to love, having a passionate relationship is often viewed as the pinnacle of romantic experience.

One study found that “zest,” or the ability to approach situations with excitement and energy, is top on the list of helpful character strengths when approaching everyday challenges. Other research has found passion to have strong ties to life satisfaction.

However, psychologists will tell you there’s a fine line between passion and obsession. For one, they both qualify as “hot” emotional states—characterized by intense emotions such as alertness, excitedness, and elation as opposed to “cool” emotions like relaxation, calm, and serenity. The same traits that lead someone to be the inspiring person who goes all in on the things they care about can, in certain cases, turn toward mania, rumination, insecurity, and impulsiveness.

Here are three things to keep an eye on if you fear your romantic partner or someone you care about might be acting obsessively, and what you can do about it.

When a person’s range of interests suddenly narrows down to a single preoccupation, it could be a sign of obsessive behavior. Whether it's a hobby, activity, or even thoughts focused on one subject, the shift from versatility and breadth to narrow fixations may indicate an unhealthy level of obsession. This intense concentration might lead to neglect of other essential aspects of life.

To address this, you can encourage your partner to experiment with a broad range of activities and interests. Suggest revisiting abandoned hobbies or discovering new ones together. Open communication about the shift in focus is crucial. Understanding the reasons behind this narrowed attention can pave the way for finding a healthier balance.

Obsessive individuals might fixate on specific actions or words, replaying them in their minds and out loud incessantly. This fixation could range from positive experiences to perceived slights or misunderstandings. This tendency to get “stuck” on certain aspects of their relationship may lead to persistent questioning, seeking reassurance, or even holding onto grudges.

To ease their stubborn stance, you can start an open dialogue about their feelings and try not to become upset by the focus of their obsessive thoughts, even if they are accusatory. If the fixation persists, seeking professional guidance from a therapist may help you and your partner.

When an individual becomes excessively focused on someone or something, their own well-being may take a backseat. This neglect is evident when self-care routines, such as proper nutrition, adequate sleep, and regular exercise, start to decline. Obsessive thoughts or behaviors may consume so much time and energy that maintaining personal health becomes secondary.

What you can do here is initiate conversations about self-care and well-being. Encourage them to maintain a balanced and healthy lifestyle. Support your partner in prioritizing self-care routines and find time for activities that promote their overall well-being. If needed, consider seeking professional help to address the underlying issues contributing to the neglect.

While passion is a vibrant and essential aspect of any relationship, recognizing when it tips into unhealthy obsession is crucial. Open communication, understanding, and a willingness to seek professional help can be instrumental in navigating these situations. Remember, fostering a healthy and balanced connection requires mutual respect and a shared commitment to emotional well-being.

#4168. Jason Wood Did Everything Right to Live Longer. That Was the Problem.

Following the healthspan playbook to the letter could create this troubling new syndrome.

IT SEEMED LIKE it was going to be a perfect summer day. Jason Wood, an office manager at a tech company in Boulder, Colorado, was on a much-needed vacation in Wyoming with his husband, Matt, who worked for an architecture firm. First stop had been Fort Collins, to hike around the Horsetooth Reservoir, with its crystal blue water reflecting the sky. Next they drove to Cheyenne, where the plan was to catch the Independence Day fireworks at the local rodeo that evening. They went to dinner at a classic American restaurant in downtown Cheyenne. Matt ordered a shrimp pasta dish. Wood ordered the hummus platter, but he wanted to make a substitution. He asked the waiter if he could please have fresh vegetables—carrots, cucumber, celery sticks—instead of pita bread. The waiter told him they didn’t have any fresh veggies, so he wouldn’t be able to substitute.

“Just that one thing, that was enough,” says Wood, a slim man of 40 years, with close-cropped hair and a light goatee. “I looked at Matt and was like, ‘I’m done with this trip. I hate it here.’ ”

Before they’d left home, Wood had extensively researched restaurants with options that fit his food rules. Nothing that would contaminate his body and shorten his life. No meat. Organic foods only: chickpeas, low-fat yogurt, and Malk, the only almond milk Wood touched. Absolutely no bread. But all the places he’d researched were closed for the Fourth. He spent an hour in the hotel looking for another restaurant he thought would fulfill his requirements. And then, boom: no fresh vegetables.

What he was doing seemed like a good thing, especially since he’d been heavy as a kid. In 2018, after dropping from 265 pounds to below 200, he was empowered to do more. In addition to following strict food rules, he hit his apartment gym twice a day for 60-minute sessions: cardio in the a.m., weight training at night. Then came the weekly intravenous drips, plus D vitamins, B vitamins, various nutrients, whatever he thought would improve his overall health. He’d lose his temper if he couldn’t train at a specific time or eat the way he wanted. Any hobbies that kept him from working out fell away. He visited his doctor every few months to order another round of blood work. He lived in constant fear of breaking his routines, so he stopped hanging out with friends.

As Wood went into panic mode over dinner, Matt delivered a verbal body check: You’re obsessed with food and diet and wellness. All you ever talk about is supplements or how often you’re going to work out. It’s not fun to be around you.

Wood, through his tears, picked up the pita and ate a few bites. But he knew, sitting there at that restaurant in Cheyenne, that he was sick—and had been for a long time.

TODAY THERE’S A new term to describe the way Wood altered his life in the name of extreme health: longevity fixation syndrome. Doctors and therapists are now exploring ways to help people with the condition—and bracing for an onslaught—as more and more behaviors and products are marketed as longevity hacks. David Popiel, MD, a primary care physician who works for Private Medical, a concierge health care clinic network, tells me that over the past decade, he’s seen more people admitting they have an unhealthy obsession with their own longevity.

For Wood, the control he was trying to exert on his physiology had become toxic. Everything—friends, vacations, his spouse—was a roadblock. At restaurants, he couldn’t follow his food rules. On vacations, he couldn’t get to the gym. “Up until that point, there was never an inkling of doubt that what I was doing was the wrong thing or the unhealthy thing,” he says. “And then that day [in Cheyenne] hit, and I realized that this was no way to live.” But Wood would have to find his own path out, balancing his quest for a long life with doing what was best for his health—and not knowing, for years, that there was a difference between the two.

LONGEVITY FIXATION SYNDROME is like other compulsions: It overtakes your thoughts and behaviors until it exacts a negative toll on every aspect of your life. It’s not unlike orthosomnia, the detrimental obsession with achieving perfect sleep metrics, or orthorexia, a pathological interest in healthy eating habits. But with longevity fixation syndrome, the target of the obsession is what gets sold to men at the gym, at the grocery store, on every bro podcast, and by every looksmaxxing influencer right now. If you miss a gym session or have to stay up late or can’t get fresh veggies instead of pita bread, it’s not only your health taking a hit—your lifespan is in trouble.

Unlike anorexia nervosa or obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), however, longevity fixation syndrome isn’t listed in the DSM-5 or ICD-11. (Orthosomnia and orthorexia aren’t, either.) Dirk Stemper, MD, a psychotherapist in private practice in Germany, describes longevity fixation as a unique preoccupation. “Mostly it’s a phenomenal element on the surface with an underlying obsessive-compulsive disorder,” he says. But because it lacks a formal designation, doctors tend not to recognize it as a problem. Guys themselves pass it off as “just being healthy.” That leads to years of being miserable or making others miserable and not finding treatment—and not getting it covered by insurance even if you do.

Jan Gerber, a serial founder with a PhD in business and the CEO of Paracelsus Recovery, a boutique mental health clinic in Zurich, coined the term “longevity fixation syndrome” in early 2026. About 5 to 10 times every month, Paracelsus fields calls from people claiming they are longevity-obsessed. “We’ve been noticing this for at least a couple of years,” says Gerber. “It’s not just with our patient group, but also with the referrals and inquiries we get.”

Although the term might be novel—foreign, even, to some longevity specialists—the degree to which it’s a perceptible problem is not. “One patient described it to me as like a drip of a sink you’re hearing in the middle of the night that you cannot stop,” says Roberto Olivardia, PhD. A leading clinical psychologist who focuses on eating disorders and body image issues in boys and men at his practice in Lexington, Massachusetts, he explains that he’s seen this phenomenon for years.

Olivardia and Dr. Popiel don’t seem surprised that more patients they speak with these days are fixated on longevity. In a life filled with uncertainty, taking care of our health is one thing a person can control—to the point of compulsion. “I was losing who I was as a person because everything was defined by this health and wellness regimen that I put into place,” Wood says. “I didn’t really know who I was anymore.” At the height of Wood’s issue, he was spending about $750 every month on his longevity program. Olivardia mentioned a story about an ordinary guy who spent so much on staying healthy that he couldn’t keep up with his mortgage.

There aren’t any hard criteria that indicate when discipline around longevity is turning into compulsion. But according to Olivardia, there are clues. For instance, spending hours a day reading about longevity, or feeling so much anxiety during meals out with friends that you’d rather avoid seeing your buddies. Another telltale sign is feeling anxious or stressed if you can’t get to the doctor or check your vitals. You find yourself giving up everything for the sake of trying to live longer.

The $250 billion global longevity industry itself deserves part of the blame. Its products are amplified by podcasters like Gary Brecka, Dave Asprey, and Mark Hyman, MD, and wellness brands like Hims, Lifeforce, and Viome that advertise on social media. “Convince a healthy person that aging is a disease, and every birthday turns into a symptom. Then sell him the panel, the peptide, the patch, the protocol,” says Jordan Shlain, MD, founder and chairman of Private Medical. “By the time these folks reach us, they’re already exhausted from babysitting 40 biomarkers and a supplement shelf that rivals a pharmacy.”

GERBER DESCRIBES THE people who call his clinic as mostly men in their 40s, many of whom fit the profile of the Silicon Valley founder: very driven, very competitive, and very knowledgeable about the latest health trends. Paracelsus can’t see all of them. It’s a residential treatment center with an in-house maximum capacity of three patients at any one time, although the clinic does outpatient aftercare as well. The fee is more than $100,000 a week. Other guys get help for a lot less, using whatever health insurance resources they have to find an affordable therapist.

There is, however, a factor that Gerber has seen consistently among the longevity-obsessed, regardless of how fat their wallets are. “Some kind of trauma is the one common denominator,” Gerber says, before rattling off a short list of what he means: emotional neglect; someone close to a person, like a friend or a parent, dying; a personality disorder. Traumatic experiences aren’t the only driver, but people who have experienced trauma previously are at higher risk of developing anxiety, depression, eating disorders, and, yes, a preoccupation with longevity.

“Trauma just creates a lot of unpredictability, feelings of helplessness, uncertainty, and loss of safety,” says Jason Nagata, MD, an associate professor at the University of California, San Francisco, and an expert in eating disorders in boys and men. “As a coping mechanism, they want to try to maximize things that are in their control, like food, or exercise, or biohacking.”

That tracked for Wood. He was 11 when his father died of colorectal cancer; when he turned 19, his mother died of metastatic brain cancer, after having already survived three bouts of lung cancer. Wood became an orphan before he was legally allowed to drink—although that didn’t stop him. (He’s sober now.) He drifted in and out of homelessness, at one point living at a Red Roof Inn for weeks on end. He was supposed to inherit the family paving business at age 25, but with his parents dead, his older siblings sold it off. Wood says all of this to me in a cool, calm manner—the complete opposite of how he was that night in Cheyenne. There’s no warbling tone, no trace in his voice of any of the pain he felt as a younger man. No tears. But he looks away for a moment, then back at me, and leans in before describing how he experienced his day-to-day moments. “Life felt like it was spiraling out of control,” he says.

Then, when he turned 29, he found blood in his stool and had his own colorectal scare. A colonoscopy revealed three polyps close to turning cancerous. “It really made me feel like my body was out to get me, and I wanted to take complete control of the situation and try to fight back,” Wood says. “Seeing both my parents die young, I was already very aware that we have an expiration date, and I wanted to make sure I pushed mine out as far as I possibly could. I thought that if I slip up even once, I’m going to die at a young age, too.”

AFTER HIS HUMMUS reckoning in Wyoming, Wood approached his primary care physician, who diagnosed him with OCD, anxiety, and an unspecified eating disorder, tacking on post-traumatic stress disorder, too, because of his experiences with the deaths of his parents. In other words, he told Wood that his fixation was a form of obsession, and that he might be so focused on longevity because of past trauma. But then all he did was tell Wood to go home and Google some therapists. “I couldn’t believe that,” he tells me, an incredulous tone rising in his voice. “If I had a heart problem, you’d send me to a cardiologist.”

The impulse was right even if the delivery was off. After researching about 25 different therapists, Wood connected with Sean Stevens, a licensed professional counselor based just outside Denver who seemed to understand what Wood was going through. Currently, the best treatment for longevity fixation looks a lot like how doctors might treat patients with orthorexia, orthosomnia, or other compulsive conditions. In his sessions with Wood, Stevens employed cognitive behavioral therapy, a form of talk therapy that focuses on helping a person challenge negative thoughts and engage in practical activities. Wood appreciated that Stevens talked to him about his fear of death and his fear of losing control over his health. Then it became about taking active steps to curb that fear. Wood would venture out with friends for pizza. Stevens would ask him: “Did your health suffer in a meaningful way just because you had an evening out?” After several months, the treatment strategies started becoming habits.

Yet not everyone agrees on what, exactly, is being treated when it comes to longevity fixation syndrome. Since the line between disorder and discipline is hard to draw, some in the longevity industrial complex question whether this is really pathology. Biohacker and influencer Bryan Johnson cheekily panned the entire idea of longevity fixation syndrome in comments on Facebook, X, and LinkedIn. (“I’ve been sober from dying for five years now,” he wrote. “I’m trapped in a prison of health. Please help.”)

He argues that a fixation on health isn’t necessarily damaging. “Other fixations are more serious,” he says. When it comes to an obsession with money, for instance, “do we think it causes people anxiety? Do we think it causes them to neglect their health, their family, their own mental well-being?” Underpinning Johnson’s “Don’t Die” movement is the idea that nothing is more important than the act of living. But Wood started to question whether the extreme rules he was living under were really a way to live life at all.

WOOD AND STEVENS met twice a week for six months initially. Stevens was compassionate and patient but also pushed Wood outside his comfort zone, which for years had been bordered by the rigidity of his longevity regimen.

But Wood struggled to shed his obsession. “I had to separate myself from all of those things that used to define me. For a long time, it was kind of like figuring out who I am again and how I fit into this world,” says Wood. He would sneak off to the pharmacy to check his blood pressure. Even though Wood now had to monitor his glucose levels to manage his diabetes, he would do it far more than he needed to every day, and then claim that his doctor was the one who wanted him to check his levels that frequently. Wood would even try to fool Matt into thinking that he was eating foods that he never would have touched at the height of his longevity fixation.

Pancakes, especially, caused Wood “a great deal of anxiety,” he says. After all: They’re made of “forbidden” flour. But they were one of Matt’s favorite foods, and so, early on in his recovery process, Wood made an effort—in appearance, at least. Whenever pancakes hit his plate, he would cover his mouth with a napkin while chewing, spit out the contents, and then hide the napkin in his lap until he could dispose of it. “I would throw it in the garbage and try to make it look like I ate it,” he says. “And there were a couple times I got caught and called out on that.”

Through his counseling sessions, Wood began to think a little differently about how he had been controlling his health. Stevens asked him to experiment with trying different behaviors to test whether Wood’s health beliefs were really accurate. For instance, if he tried taking a rest day after an intense gym session, he was encouraged to ask himself: Are you any less healthy? And if you think the answer is yes, what’s your proof? “A lot of therapy is helping people figure out what their own recipe is as opposed to having this rigid way of thinking,” Stevens says.

Eventually Stevens gave Wood an assignment: Write about himself, walking through every event in his life that brought him to the brink that day in Cheyenne. About a year into his recovery, Wood started a blog and began sharing his experiences on social media. He sat down one day to write a post, but when he looked up, he had written 50,000 words, a memoir he self-published in 2022 called Starving for Survival.

“I was able to realize that I was worth so much more than just what my diet plan was or my exercise plan was,” he tells me. That allowed him to disconnect his health routine from the idea that it was the one thing keeping him from meeting the same fate as his parents. “It was really scary at first, but every day that I do it, I feel like I gain a little bit more trust in myself and in my body,” he says.

OVER TIME, HE threw away the pulse oximeter. He threw away the scale in the house. Now, the only tracking device he has is a continuous glucose monitor for his diabetes. He gets his blood work done once a year and says he looks at the results only if the doctor points something out. The IV drips are gone. He still goes to the gym, but that’s to keep up his tennis game and to keep his muscles in good working order for when he goes rock climbing. He’s vegetarian for ethical reasons now, but eating doesn’t send him into a tailspin. His new habits, he says, “are total freedom, because now I don’t have to obsess about all this stuff. I’m not constantly thinking about it.”

Wood and his husband now live near Grand Rapids, Michigan. They made the move in 2023, a few months after Wood became the director of community engagement at the National Association of Anorexia Nervosa and Associated Disorders in Chicago. He started a men’s support group for guys struggling with the same stuff he did: worries about dying early and disordered relationships with food and their bodies. Wood is there to share his own story, reframe the questions, and remind these men that longevity is good, as long as it doesn’t consume you.

“TO ME, HEALTH has been redefined,” says Wood. “For so long, health was focused on food and fitness and body. And now I’ve realized that my social health, my mental health, my spiritual health—all of those different things are part of my overall well-being.”

This July, he and Matt took a trip to Washington, D.C., in advance of the city’s semiquincentennial celebrations. For dinner, they went to a Mediterranean restaurant. Hummus was on the menu. This time, Wood didn’t make any substitutions and ordered the pita bread. He didn’t feel guilty. He didn’t worry about it. He ate it all.

The eating window is followed by fasting for the remaining hours. Common approaches include 16:8, which involves 16 hours of fasting and 8 hours of eating, and early TRE, which involves eating earlier in the day.

The researchers used data from 124 adults across five TRE trials lasting between 3 and 6 months. At baseline, participants had an average eating window of about 15 hours and at least one component of metabolic syndrome.

Their food and beverage intake was tracked using an app.

Participants were assigned either to a TRE group or to the control group. The control group participants maintained their habitual intake, received nutritional counseling, or were given guidance on healthy eating.

Those in the TRE group limited their eating windows to 8, 10, or 12 hours, with no restrictions on dietary quality or energy intake.

After 3–6 months, the researchers found that participants assigned to an 8-hour eating window lost about 3.7 pounds (1.7 kg) more than those assigned to a 12-hour window and about 5 pounds (2.3 kg) more than those in the control group.

However, there was no statistically significant difference in weight loss between the 8-hour and the 10-hour windows. The 8-hour window also showed no significant impact on blood pressure, glucose, or cholesterol.

“The findings are interesting and add to the evidence that shortening the daily eating window may help with weight loss, but I would be cautious about concluding that eight hours is the optimal window,” Dalia Perelman, MS, RDN, CDCES, research dietitian at Stanford Medicine, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline.

“One finding I find particularly interesting is that people who shortened their eating window more relative to their usual pattern tended to lose more weight. #4155. Insomnia Isn’t Just Exhausting — It May Raise Your Risk of Stroke, Dementia

  • A recent review has found that insomnia may be associated with an increased risk of stroke and hospitalization.
  • The researchers also found evidence of an association between insomnia and depression, suicidal behaviors, and dementia. 
  • These findings add to growing evidence that insomnia may be linked to health risks beyond poor sleep and impaired daytime functioning.

Insomnia may do more than leave you tired. A review published in Sleep Medicine Reviews on September 7 has linked it to a higher risk of stroke, depression, and hospitalization.

Insomnia is characterized by difficulty falling or staying asleep despite having an adequate opportunity to sleep and typically involves impaired daytime functioning.

“Sleep is such an important health factor when we think about overall well-being,” Joseph You, MD, cardiologist with Manhattan Cardiology in NYC, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline.  

He noted that sleep deprivation and poor-quality sleep have long been linked to adverse cardiovascular events and other health problems.

Insomnia linked to 26% higher stroke risk

Insomnia is the most common sleep disorder in adults. Chronic insomnia affects approximately 22% of the general population.

“Chronic insomnia is generally characterized by ongoing sleep difficulty despite having an adequate opportunity to sleep, rather than simply having an occasional bad night,” noted Christopher Yi, MD, board certified vascular surgeon at MemorialCare Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study. 

To examine the association between insomnia and stroke, the researchers identified nine studies involving more than 1.3 million people. Six were included in the meta-analysis, which found that insomnia was associated with a 26% higher risk of stroke.

It is important to keep in mind that association does not equal causation. However, several factors may help explain the link between insomnia and stroke.

Persistent sleep disruptions can trigger overactivation of the sympathetic nervous system. 

The sympathetic nervous system is part of the body’s involuntary nervous system that triggers your “fight or flight” response in stressful or dangerous situations.

“Being too wired can impair sleep, and impaired sleep, in turn, can make us too wired. A state of higher adrenaline and cortisol raises blood pressure, blood sugar, and increases inflammation, all of which have serious health effects,” said Alex Dimitriu, MD, double board certified in Psychiatry and Sleep Medicine, and founder of Menlo Park Psychiatry & Sleep Medicine, who wasn’t involved in the study. 

The review authors noted that these changes can not only raise blood pressure and heart rate but can also increase vascular tone while reducing heart rate variability. Continuous cortisol release can also promote hypertension, systemic inflammation, and disrupt glucose and lipid metabolism. 

Together, these changes may promote plaque formation and compromise vascular health, potentially contributing to a higher risk of stroke.

“I would emphasize that this study demonstrates an association and does not prove that insomnia directly causes stroke or other cardiovascular events,” cautioned Yi.

“There was significant variability among the studies, and factors such as underlying medical conditions and the use of sleep medications could influence the results. The authors themselves caution that an independent causal role for insomnia has not been established.”

Insomnia can increase the risk of hospitalization by 28%

The researchers also looked at five studies examining the association between insomnia and hospitalization. The studies included mostly older adults and ranged in size from 1,101 to 484,707 participants.

Three of the studies, involving 19,745 participants, were included in a meta-analysis. Researchers found that people with insomnia had a 28% higher likelihood of hospitalization for any reason.

The researchers noted that insomnia may be a key marker of poor health and may exacerbate the severity of underlying health conditions.

When to seek help for insomnia

Dimitriu said a good indication that it may be time to seek help is when sleep problems begin to affect your life, health, or happiness. If you are unable to sleep at least 6–7 hours when given the opportunity, or regularly feel tired or sleepy during the day, he recommends speaking with a healthcare professional.

You recommended seeking help as soon as you begin to suspect you may have insomnia. Doing so may also help identify other risk factors or lifestyle habits that could be affecting your sleep and overall health.

People with insomnia may be more likely to have depression

The researchers reviewed six meta-analyses examining the association between insomnia and depression and other mental health disorders.

Although most were rated as having low or critically low methodological quality, the findings consistently linked insomnia with a higher risk of depression.

According to the researchers, chronic insomnia can lead to daytime fatigue, attention issues, and reduced executive functioning. These factors can increase rumination, negative thinking, and maladaptive coping strategies, which are all risk factors for depression and anxiety.

Separately, two meta-analyses found that insomnia was associated with a higher risk of suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and suicide death.

Sleep disturbances are among several risk factors for suicide, including independently of depression.

This isn’t to say that insomnia causes depression or suicidal behaviors, only that it may be one among various risk factors.

Insomnia linked to higher risk of dementia

The researchers reviewed five meta-analyses examining the association between insomnia and dementia. The analyses included between 5 and 39 studies and ranged from 36,005 to more than 3.1 million participants, with follow-up periods ranging from 2 to 40 years.

Overall, most of the analyses linked insomnia with a higher risk of dementia. Three also found that insomnia was associated with a higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease.

The researchers noted several possible explanations for the association. Sleep disturbances may contribute to inflammation and impaired clearance of substances such as amyloid-beta and tau from the brain, processes that could potentially contribute to neurodegeneration.

However, these findings show an association between insomnia and dementia and do not establish that insomnia causes dementia.

#4153. Jung in Azeroth: The Psychology of WoW Forever

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

On November 4, Blizzard Entertainment releases World of Warcraft (WoW): Forever, a reimagining of the game’s original 2004 world. The announcement emotionally stirred something in me: a homesickness for a place that never existed, yet has always been real to the psyche.

So, I am going back to Azeroth, likely to the dismay of the Horde players I plan to turn into sheep through magical spells. Playing the beta, I’ve noticed how quickly my body remembers the slow walk through an enchanted forest at dusk, or how relaxing it is to hear the music shifting as I cross into a new zone.

In my practice, clients often mention gaming the way people confess a guilty pleasure. However, I love these conversations; what we choose to play, and how we play it, can tell us a great deal about what the human soul is reaching for.

Some of psychology’s greatest thinkers took play seriously, in theory and in practice. After his painful rupture with Freud, a disoriented Jung returned to a childhood game: building a small village of stones by the lake. That play, he later wrote, opened the door to the inner images that shaped his life’s work.

Other psychoanalysts from Freud to Winnicott have written about play as well; it may be one of the oldest formative experiences we share across cultures, and play itself can be a form of liberation.

Jung described archetypes as forms without content: inherited patterns that shape how we perceive and respond, and that surface as images in myth, dream, and story. World of Warcraft's classes are almost a field guide to them.

Notice, too, that no one finishes a dungeon alone. A group needs someone to protect, someone to heal, and someone to strike. Psyche works in much the same way: differentiated parts, each limited on its own, and yet becoming whole only in relationship.

Campbell called it the call to adventure: the moment the familiar world grows too small and something beckons from beyond its edge. Modern Western life offers few initiatory structures; we are rarely asked to leave home, face real danger, find companions, and return changed.

The original World of Warcraft offered a safe, structured version of exactly that. You begin small, often gathering herbs and chasing wolves near a village, then venture into more dangerous lands and discover you cannot go far alone. WoW Forever promises to keep that slow road, and I suspect the slowness is the point; psyche does not individuate on a deadline.

Having spent much of my career with grief and dying, I find the way World of Warcraft handles death quietly profound. When your character dies, you awaken as a spirit in a distant graveyard, in a world drained of colour. To return, you walk alone back to where you fell, or accept a Spirit Healer’s resurrection and come back weakened for a time.

It is one of the oldest human patterns: descent and return. Death here is a passage, and it must be walked. Many people I work with know this terrain well: the grey world after a loss, the long road back, and the weakness that lingers once you arrive.

Every player chooses a side: Alliance or Horde. The game even garbles the other faction’s speech, so you literally cannot understand them. It is hard to imagine a cleaner image of projection: What we refuse to recognize in ourselves, Jung taught, we meet outside ourselves as an adversary.

Players who eventually cross over factions may experience a small revelation: Orcs can have honour, and humans can have cruelty. This is a beginner’s taste of shadow work.

Excessive play can also become a place to hide. I have sat with people for whom the game had quietly replaced the life it once enriched. Jung distinguished passive fantasy, which carries us away, from active imagination, where we engage the images consciously and let them change us.

Is this expanding my life, or replacing it? In Campbell's telling, the hero's journey is not complete until the hero returns home with their boon. What has the game given you—courage, belonging, adventure, etc.—that wants to come home into your waking life?
