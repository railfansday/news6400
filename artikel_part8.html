#6050. The Tiny Chair Has Always Had Big Ideas

The Children’s Wishbone Chair is a design icon made for someone too young to know it’s an icon. The fun-size version of the 1949 classic, released by Carl Hansen & Søn in 2024, maintains the signature curved back and Y-shaped support of the original design as well as the chair's meticulous, 100-step production process.

Ligne Roset’s Mini Togo, introduced in 2007, shrinks Michel Ducaroy’s foam lounge chair for five-to-12-years old. Soon to follow was a child-size version of Mies van der Rohe’s Barcelona chair and Faye Toogood’s Roly-Poly. Paola Lenti premiered a full Kids Collection of downsized sofas, poufs, chairs, and tables at Milan Design Week in 2025. This year, Hamburg’s Museum für Kunst und Gewerbe places these launches within a much longer history. “Mini Furniture: Chairs for Children,” on view through the first of November, gathers more than 130 children’s chairs from two centuries, including high chairs, miniatures of adult classics, and furniture meant to support children’s motor, social, and creative development.

A chair is one of the first objects that asks a child to arrange their restless body to fit adult architecture. And across two centuries, children’s chairs have been used as a means to carry out competing adult agendas: They encourage the toddler to sit properly, do things independently, or use their imaginations.

By the late 1800s, Thonet had added child-size cane seating and high chairs to its catalog. A 1931 English model in the MK&G exhibition bears the straightforward name Correction Chair. It was used to straighten posture and enforce discipline in homes and schools; a small chair meant to prepare children for the larger, meaner world.

On the other end of the spectrum we have Erich Dieckmann’s 1925 children’s ladder chair, also included at MK&G, which doubles as a set of steps for reaching higher shelves. Montessori philosophy, meanwhile, preaches the virtues of the “prepared environment”—a child-centered space designed to encourage independence. Here, low, lightweight furniture allows children to move objects and begin activities without asking an adult for help. n each case, the furniture changes scale not simply to accommodate a smaller body, but to give the child greater independence.

Designers eventually accounted for the reality that a seated child rarely stays seated for long. Charles and Ray Eames’s molded-plywood Elephant—as seen in this modular home outside Madrid—designed in 1945, could be sat on, ridden, or treated as an animal. The idea that a child’s chair can do more than offer a place to sit continues to shape new designs. When industrial designer Henry Julier developed his children’s chair for USM, the Swiss furniture maker known for its modular system of steel tubes and ball joints, he couldn’t simply shrink an existing design. He had to find child-friendly proportions using those same parts. "At that scale, sitting, climbing, leaning and playing can become pretty interchangeable,” he says. The finished chair behaves more like a stool, while its “back” doubles as a handle that makes it easier for adults to move.

For many of the adults buying them, miniature chairs are design objects as well as children’s furniture. Lora Appleton, founder of children’s design gallery Kinder Modern, has seen interest in collectible children’s furniture grow as design-conscious parents, influencers, actors, and other high-profile families embrace living stylishly with children. In designer Abigail Turin’s Carpinteria kitchen, for example, children’s Panton chairs share the room with Eero Saarinen’s Tulip chairs—child-size furniture absorbed into the family’s design vocabulary rather than confined to a separate children’s world.

"Naturally, the purchasing decision is made by an adult, and many parents appreciate children’s furniture that integrates harmoniously into the overall aesthetic of the home,” says Knud Erik Hansen, CEO and third-generation owner of Carl Hansen & Søn. While children's furniture has "often been viewed as something transitional," to be discarded when the youngster grows, he says, the Children's Wishbone is designed to become an heirloom that can carry memories between generations.

A mini Jens Risom chair sits in front of custom laminate intarsia cabinetry in this San Francisco home by AD100 interiors firm The Archers.

Scaled down versions of adult designs, while beautiful, aren't always practical, Appleton says. Unlike furniture conceived around children’s needs, such as the simple bent-plywood pieces architects historically designed for schools, an adult icon made smaller can cost nearly as much as the original to produce, be outgrown quickly, or lack the stability and proportions needed for a child to use it independently. “Often, it’s just cute, but not functional,” she says. A miniature chair can succeed as an image while failing as a chair, eventually becoming, in Appleton’s experience, a catchall for clothes and miscellaneous bedroom debris.

Appleton evaluates children’s furniture according to safety, size, function, and play-ability: Can the child move it without help from a grownup? Can small fingers get caught? Will it remain useful as dexterity and interests change? She has watched children turn a miniature Panton chair on its side to support a fort, hide behind its curves, and wedge stuffed animals into its crevices. When she tested prototypes with her son, he often crawled beneath them and ignored the expected use entirely. Those detours would often be the inspiration that produced new design ideas.

Every tiny chair begins as an adult proposal for what it should be. It may be meant to teach posture or independence, invite play, cultivate an appreciation of craft, or eventually become an heirloom. The child makes a counterproposal: Turn it sideways and build a fort. The best tiny chairs leave room for that possibility.

#6037. Is An Apple A Day Actually Good For You? A Dietitian Weighs In

The classic snack supports your health—but with a few caveats.

An old proverb says one apple a day keeps the doctor away, but does nutrition science back that up? For most people, yes: An apple is a healthy way to add fruit to your diet and help you meet the recommended daily amount. But a few caveats—like apple variety, washing, and digestive sensitivity—are worth keeping in mind.

Current dietary guidelines recommend two servings of fruit per day, and since an apple is about one serving, it can help you meet those needs. But most types of apples are high in fiber and natural sugar, so eating too many could mean problems for your gut health, according to Lauren Manaker, M.S., R.D.N.

Let’s dig into the science to learn how to safely add apples to our diets.

“Apples offer a range of health benefits, including supporting heart health, supporting immune function, and reducing inflammation,” Manaker says. She credits vitamin C with supporting the immune system and flavonoids, a type of antioxidant, with helping lower the risk of heart disease.

While nutrient content varies by apple type, a medium Golden Delicious is mostly water. A single apple contains roughly 96 calories, 23 grams of carbs (17 of which come from sugar), and about four grams of fiber. That fiber, especially soluble fiber like pectin, can help regulate blood sugar and support gut health.

“Eating an apple every day can be incredibly healthy, as apples are a nutrient-dense fruit full of fiber, vitamins, and antioxidants,” Manaker says. “Since most people don't eat the recommended servings of fruits and veggies daily, making a habit of having an apple a day can be a good thing.”

Just don't forget to wash them. “Eating unwashed apples may pose a risk of pesticide exposure, so washing them thoroughly is recommended,” she notes. Apples often appear on lists of produce with higher pesticide residues, such as the Environmental Working Group's Dirty Dozen. Of course, washing is recommended for all produce, and conventional (versus organic) apples still provide nutritional benefits.

“While apples are healthy, over-consuming them in excessive amounts could lead to excess calorie intake or digestive issues due to their high fiber and natural sugar content,” Manaker says.

For most people, it's hard to overdo it enough to cause harm. However, she cautions that too much fiber and natural sugars could cause mild discomfort for those with IBS or more sensitive stomachs, and people managing blood sugar may want to pair apples with protein or fat.

One apple per day is a healthy choice for most people, but rotate fruits, wash produce, and adjust portions if apples trigger digestive symptoms. Your doctor—and your gut—will thank you.

Recent breakthroughs in pig-to-human xenotransplantation have successfully prevented hyperacute rejection of kidney, heart, liver and lung xenografts, marking a milestone long considered a critical barrier. However, these major advances also brought new challenges into focus, as immune-mediated graft injuries including antibody-mediated rejection, T cell-mediated rejection and innate immune activation have been observed in most xenotransplantation procedures to date. Detection and management of these injuries remain a critical bottleneck, as conventional histopathology and standard clinical monitoring lack the sensitivity required to identify subclinical injury early enough to guide timely intervention. In this Review, we synthesize recent progress in xenotransplantation and position multimodal precision diagnostics as a pivotal strategy to address the remaining immunological and clinical barriers to widespread clinical implementation.

Or you can cut the line, and go straight to the experts. That it looks quite unlike anything that came before it means that it doesn’t have simple answers to simple questions: Is this an everyday watch? A dress watch? Something for serious collectors or splashy newcomers? What makes the OF37 special is that it refuses to be any one of those things.

Ancient DNA from skeletons buried in a historic cemetery in Quebec is changing people's understanding of their Indigenous ancestry. Contrary to popular belief, there may be little truth to stories of intermarriage between Indigenous and French people during the early days of colonization, according to a new archaeology study published Oct. 5 in the journal PNAS.

"Family stories of an Indigenous ancestor are very common" in Quebec, study first author Diane Martin-Moya, a biological anthropologist at the University of Quebec in Trois-Rivières in Canada, told Live Science in an email, and "commercial genealogy and DNA tests have amplified this." But distant or claimed Indigenous identities can actually undermine the rights, resources and recognition of Indigenous nations, she said.

To learn more about the founders of Quebec, Martin-Moya and co-authors investigated Notre-Dame Cemetery, which was established in Montreal in 1683 and used for Catholic burials until 1796. Based on previous studies of the skeletons in the cemetery, experts disagreed on whether people of Indigenous, European, African or mixed ancestry were buried there. To settle the question, Martin-Moya and her colleagues sequenced DNA from 45 people buried in the cemetery and compared it with the genomes of people from Europe, West Africa and Indigenous groups in the Americas.

"The ancient DNA showed that all 45 individuals we analyzed had French European ancestry, with no sign of recent Indigenous or African admixture," Martin-Moya said of the research, which she conducted while at the University of Montreal.

These findings suggest that people descended from Indigenous and African populations may have been marginalized in early Montreal, challenging the popular narrative of cooperation and intermarriage, she said.

"Our sample is small and comes from a single cemetery, so this does not show that such unions never happened," Martin-Moya cautioned. "It is, however, consistent with genealogical and genetic studies suggesting that Indigenous ancestry among present-day French Canadians is very limited."

The apparent lack of genetic mixing among European, African and Indigenous populations in this cemetery group "provides a perspective on population relationships during the colonial period, one where perhaps nongenetic interactions were present but no biological relationships were found," Raquel Fleskes, a molecular anthropologist at Dartmouth College who was not involved in the research, told Live Science.

The DNA helped the researchers find several genetic lineages of people that spanned multiple generations. But the graves were scattered across multiple areas of the cemetery, suggesting that burials were not organized into family plots. This finding suggests that, in the 17th century, people in Montreal may have been burying their dead in a way that was not defined by strict biological kinship ties, Fleskes said.

Martin-Moya stressed that the results of the new study do not necessarily apply to all of colonial Montreal.

"Not identifying recent Indigenous or African admixture in this sample does not imply that Indigenous or African-descended people were absent from the city," Martin-Moya said.

In addition, "biological ancestry cannot by itself establish Indigenous belonging," as it "is rooted in kinship, community and relationships with land, not in blood or DNA," she added.

Changes in the use of the cemetery over time also may have influenced the findings, as reburials and later urban development disturbed or destroyed earlier graves.

"Those buried inside the church, mostly clergy and wealthier residents, were more likely to be exhumed and moved," Martin-Moya explained, while the city's poorer residents were buried outside the church and further buried beneath the expanding city. This means that the skeletons in the study may have been the remains of lower-class French colonists.

Archival records show that some Indigenous people were buried in Catholic cemeteries in Montreal as early as the 17th century, Martin-Moya said. "Over time, though, Indigenous communities were increasingly separated from the colonial town through land dispossession and the relocation of missions away from urban centers," she explained. "Their dead would most likely have been buried in those communities. Enslaved people, both Indigenous and African, were often buried on their enslaver's property or in cemeteries for the poor."

The results of the study don't mean that Québécois have no Indigenous ancestors or that relations between French settlers and Indigenous peoples were only hostile, Martin-Moya said. Those relationships were complex and included alliances, trade, conflict and, sometimes, family ties.

"What our results suggest is that when such ancestry exists, it needs to be put in context," Martin-Moya said. "A single ancestor who lived more than 300 years ago does not say the same thing about a person today as being raised within an Indigenous family, community or nation."

#6004. Pumpkin Basque Cheesecake

In my opinion, a Basque cheesecake is the best kind of cheesecake to make. No water bath, no worrying about the top cracking, and no waiting to let the whole thing cool means that you get all of the lovely cheesecake rewards without any of the stress. It embraces all of the imperfections that can happen with cheesecake, but promises an ultra-silky interior with a crust that adds an extra caramelized flavor. This pumpkin version packs in warm spices and an extra drizzle of caramel on top, creating a stunning dessert for any fall holiday or potluck. The best part? You can make it ahead of time—basically, it’s about as stress-free as an impressive dessert like this can get.

Line your springform pan with parchment paper. Scrunch the parchment up into a ball and then straighten it back out. This helps the parchment hold its shape and stay in the pan better. Place the parchment paper overlapping in opposite directions so that all of the sides are well covered with a bit of overhang.

In the large bowl of a stand mixer fitted with the paddle attachment, beat cream cheese and sugar on medium speed until creamy and no lumps remain, about 2 minutes. Add pumpkin and beat until combined. You want to make sure you aren’t seeing any bits of cream cheese in the now orange mixture. Keep beating until everything is smooth. Make sure to scrape down the sides and bottom of the bowl as well.

Add eggs and beat until incorporated, then scrape down the bowl and add egg yolks and vanilla, beating until incorporated, 1 minute more. Add flour, pumpkin pie spice, and salt and beat on medium-low speed until just combined. Add cream and beat until incorporated, about 2 minutes. The mixture should be smooth and pretty loose at this point.

Pour the batter into the lined pan. It will be very full, and that’s okay. The parchment helps hold it in. It will puff up while baking, but should overflow.

Bake the cheesecake until the top is very dark and puffed, the edges are slightly set, and the center is still quite jiggly. This should take about 60 to 70 minutes. Then you’re ready to top with caramel and serve!

The cheesecake keeps well in the refrigerator. It can be kept in the refrigerator for up to 5 days.

#6003. This Trick Makes Cutting Winter Squash So Much Easier

A little heat makes a big difference when it comes to cutting a stubborn squash.

I love winter squash, but I’ve never been fond of the effort required to get some varieties onto the dinner table. Butternut squash is manageable enough, but a stubborn acorn or Hubbard squash can make even the sharpest chef’s knife feel inadequate. And while I have no objection to a little prep work, I’d rather spend my time cooking dinner than trying to negotiate with a vegetable.

Of course, the reward is usually worth it. Roasted squash is one of fall’s greatest gifts, and there’s no shortage of ways to put it to work in soups, sides and even desserts. But the effort (and fear of accidental finger amputation) is off-putting. Then I came across a video demonstrating a simple way to make cutting winter squash easier, and I put it to the test.

The technique itself isn’t new or complicated. What caught my attention was how little effort it took to dramatically simplify the process of preparing an otherwise stubborn squash.

In the video, the creator places a whole squash in a 400ºF oven for 20 minutes before cutting it.

Afterward, her knife passes through the squash with remarkably little resistance, and she removes the seeds in a single scoop. It’s an impressive demonstration, even though the principle behind it is familiar enough: Heat softens vegetables, and winter squash is no exception.

The question is whether 20 minutes is enough to make a meaningful difference without cooking the squash all the way through.

To find out, I picked up two similarly sized Danish squash—a classic type of acorn squash, recognizable by its dark green, deeply ridged exterior and sweet, golden flesh. Like other types of winter squash, it has a tough rind that can make cutting it in half more challenging than its relatively modest size might suggest.

I left one squash raw and baked the other for 20 minutes in a 400º oven, piercing the skin first to let steam escape. After letting the roasted squash cool long enough for me to handle it, I cut both in half using the same knife.

The difference was immediately obvious. Cutting through the raw squash required much more pressure, while the partially roasted one offered much less resistance. Removing the seeds was easier, too, and the softened flesh was still firm enough to be cut into wedges without falling apart.

Admittedly, putting a squash in the oven before cutting it raises a reasonable question: Why not just roast it all the way through? The answer depends on what you’re making. If you’re planning to scoop out the flesh for soup or puree, you might as well go all the way. But if your recipe calls for wedges, cubes or slices that need to hold their shape and brown in the oven, you need to cut the squash before it’s fully cooked.

Those exposed surfaces are what allow roasted squash to develop its beautifully browned, caramelized edges. And if you’re making a squash gratin, a roasted squash salad or a soup that begins by sauteing cubes of squash with onions and aromatics, the shape and texture of those pieces matter.

This technique doesn’t save time from start to finish. You still have to preheat the oven, roast the squash and allow it to cook before cutting into it. What it does save is the physical effort of cutting through a dense, raw winter squash, which can be complicated if you struggle with hand strength or find vegetables tough to manage. You’ll still want a sharp knife and a stable cutting board, but there’s something to be said about a little less resistance.

Danish squash worked beautifully for me, but the technique certainly isn’t limited to acorn varieties. Other hard-skinned winter squash like kabocha, Hubbard and buttercup can benefit from a brief period in the oven before cutting. Butternut squash is another good option, especially if you’re working with a large one that needs to be halved and cubed.

The size of the squash matters, too. Twenty minutes at 400º may be enough to make a noticeable difference in a smaller squash, but a large variety may need more time. The goal isn’t to cook it until it’s tender; it’s to soften it just enough that your knife can pass through more easily. If I don’t have to test the limits of my chef’s knife, I’ll happily take it.
