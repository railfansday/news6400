#747. Resignation of Bärbel Bas shines light on harsh era of German politics

German Labor Minister Bärbel Bas has stepped down, citing her need to grieve a loved one and leave behind the harsh realities of government. So how did German politics get so heated?

For a brief moment, a sense of quiet sympathy could be felt in Berlin's often unforgiving political arena: On Monday morning, Bärbel Bas, Germany's labor minister and the co-leader of the center-left Social Democrats (SPD) announced her resignation from all government and party positions. The 58-year-old politician cited a personal tragedy as the reason for her decision: "Following the death of my stepdaughter, I have come to realize that I need time to work through my grief," Bas wrote in a statement.

Bas also wrote that sustained criticism from the conservative governing parties, the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU), had contributed to her decision to step down. In her statement, she wrote that she had been forced to "conclude that public attacks on me, unfortunately also from within the ranks of our coalition partner, continued to intensify during this personally difficult time."

The labor minister had recently been responsible for implementing complex changes to Germany's public pension system — including increasing the retirement age — and had been urged by some conservative politicians, in rather harsh terms, not to delay the project any further.

According to a report in the Süddeutsche Zeitung newspaper, one remark in particular appears to have weighed heavily on her: During an off-the-record briefing with journalists, Chancellor Friedrich Merz, of the CDU, reportedly criticized the pace of her efforts despite knowing that she was working through a personal tragedy.

Did Bas feel so overwhelmed by pressures both personal and political that she chose to step down rather than continue to endure them?

Mirco Liefke, a researcher with the Institute for Media and Communication Studies in the Department of Political and Social Sciences at the Freie Universität Berlin, told DW that unfairness and harshness have always been part of politics. However, he added: "What I do think is new is the potential for escalation and the speed that social media have brought to public debate. Along with that has come an incentive for individual actors to adopt ever more radical positions."

There are few public spaces left in which politicians can speak with the expectation that their remarks will not be reported. Merz is said to have made critical remarks of Bas during an off-the-record briefing whose contents were not intended for publication.

Though he was fully aware of her personal circumstances, Merz reportedly said Bas was barely visible in public and failing to put forward proposals of her own.

"It has effectively become a business model to leak information that is meant to remain confidential and thereby generate attention," Liefke said.

A spokesperson for Merz declined to confirm or deny the report.

Felix Banaszak, the co-leader of Germany's center-left Greens, has known Bas fior years, with both hailing from Duisburg, in the state of North Rhine-Westphalia, albeit born a couple of decades apart.

"Toughness and ruthlessness are nothing new in politics," Banaszak told DW. "But we still need to find a different way of doing things if we do not want to keep wearing people down."

Banaszak said the SPD was partially responsible for the treatment of Bas. "The fact that Bärbel Bas encountered so little understanding for her personal situation from within the ranks of the coalition partner, and that apparently no one in the SPD was willing to step forward, defend her, and take her out of the line of fire, is simply not a good sign," he said.

"It would be naive to pretend that the attacks on a man would have unfolded in a similar way," he added.

Liefke offered a similar view: "Women politicians are subjected to an extraordinary degree of misogynistic and sexualized abuse — and they face a level of public exposure that is entirely new."

The debate over how Bas and her grief should have been treated soon took on some of the same characteristics as the attacks that had preceded her resignation. Manuela Schwesig, the SPD state premier of the northeastern state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, described the attacks by the CDU/CSU as "inhuman," drawing sharp criticism from many conservative politicians. Merz publicly expressed his regret over the labor minister's resignation and wished her all the best.

Liefke said this was another tremnd in German politics. Once a politician decides to step down, he argues, public expressions of concern are often far more intense than they used to be and come from all sides: "This is a facade of concern," he said. "There is something ritualistic, almost reflexive, about it. People react this way because they are following another major trend: Emotionalization."

The attacks on Bas are just one example of the increasingly harsh tone of political debate. Formal reprimands in the Bundestag have risen sharply, particularly since the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) entered parliament in 2017. High-profile resignations have also made headlines, including that of Kevin Kühnert, the SPD's former secretary-general.

Kühnert stepped down in 2024. In April 2025, however, he told Die Zeit newspaper that he had felt constantly threatened by far-right extremists. Political debate, he said, is showing "an increasing lack of respect for the views of those who think differently."

Liefke said politicians once managed to maintain two distinct modes of communication: one public and one private. "When you think of earlier generations of politicians, what comes to mind is the distance they maintained, the relative lack of emotional display," he said. "Take Helmut Schmidt, for example. He did not openly grieve over things that came crashing down on him. At least, he did not show it publicly." Schmidt, of the SPD, served as chancellor from 1974 to 1982.

"Today, politicians such as Bärbel Bas benefit from drawing on their own life stories when they say, 'I come from a working-class background and worked my way up,'" Liefke said. "That can work to their advantage — but it also makes them far more visible, and far more vulnerable, as individuals."

Bas, who served as president of the Bundestag from 2021 to 2025, intends to retain her parliamentary seat. Life is likely to become quieter for her now as other politicians move into the spotlight, both in public and, perhaps, in private as well.

#744. Alaska’s monster tsunami sent water 1,580 feet up a mountain

On the evening of Aug. 9, 2025, passengers on the Hanse Explorer finished taking selfies and videos of the South Sawyer Glacier, and the ship headed back down the fjord. Twelve hours later, a landslide from the adjacent mountain unexpectedly collapsed into the fjord, initiating the second-highest tsunami in recorded history.

We conduct research on earthquakes and tsunamis at the Alaska Earthquake Center, and one of us serves as Alaska state seismologist. In a new study with colleagues, we detail how that landslide sent water and debris 1,580 feet (481 meters) up the other side of the fjord – higher than the top floor of the Taipei 101 skyscraper – and then continued down Tracy Arm. The force of the water stripped the fjord’s walls down to bare rock.

It was just after 5 o’clock in the morning on a dreary day, and fortunately, no ships were nearby. In the months after, some cruise lines started avoiding Tracy Arm. However, the conditions that led to this event are not at all unique to this fjord.

Landslides are common in the coastal mountains of Alaska where rapid uplift, caused by tectonic forces and long-term ice loss, converges with the erosive forces of precipitation and moving glaciers. But a curious pattern has emerged in recent years: Multiple major landslides have occurred precisely at the terminus of a retreating glacier.

Though the mechanics are still poorly understood, these mountains appear to become unstable when the ice disappears. When the landslide hits the water, the momentum of millions of tons of rock is transferred into tsunami waves.

This same phenomenon is playing out from Alaska to Greenland and Norway, sometimes with deadly consequences. Across the Arctic, countries are trying to come to terms with this growing hazard. The options are not attractive: avoid vast swaths of coastline, or live with a poorly understood risk. We believe there is an obvious role for alert systems, but only if scientists have a better understanding of where and when landslides are likely to occur.

The Tracy Arm landslide is a powerful example.

The landslide occurred in August, when warm ocean waters and heavier precipitation favor both glacier retreat and slope failure. The glacier below the landslide area had experienced rapid calving – large chunks of ice breaking off and falling into the water – and it had retreated more than a third of a mile in the two months prior. Heavy rain had been falling. Rain enters fractures in the mountain and pushes them closer to failure by increasing the water pressure in cracks.

Most provocative are the thousands of small seismic tremors that emanated from the area of the slide in the days prior to the mountainside collapsing.

We believe that this combination of signs would have been sufficient to issue progressive alerts to any ships in the vicinity and homes and businesses that could have been harmed by a tsunami at least a day prior to the failure – had a monitoring program existed.

Escalating alerts are used for everything from terrorism and nuclear plant safety to avalanches and volcanic unrest. They don’t remove the risk, but they do make it easier for people to safely coexist with hazards.

For example, though people are still killed in avalanches, alert systems have played an essential role in making winter backcountry travel safer for more people. The collapse at Tracy Arm demonstrates what could be possible for landslides.

We believe that the combination of weather and rapid glacier retreat in early August 2025 was likely sufficient to issue an alert notifying people that the hazard may be temporarily elevated in a general area. On a yellow-orange-red scale, this would be a yellow alert.

In the hours prior to the landslide, the exponential increase in seismic events and telltale transition to what is known as seismic tremor – a continuous “hum” of seismic energy – were sufficient to communicate a time-sensitive warning for a specific region.

These observations, recorded as a byproduct of regional earthquake monitoring, warranted an “orange” alert noting immediate concern. The signs were arguably sufficient to recommend keeping boats and ships out of the fjord.

Our research over the past few years has demonstrated that once a large landslide has started, it is possible to detect and measure the event within a couple of minutes. In this amount of time, seismic waves in the surrounding area can indicate the rough size of the landslide and whether it occurred near open water.

A monitoring program that could quickly communicate this would be able to issue a red alert, signaling an event in progress.

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s tsunami warning program has spent decades fine-tuning rapid message dissemination. A warning system would have offered little help for ships in the immediate vicinity, but it could have provided perhaps 10 minutes of warning for those who rode out the harrowing tsunami farther away.

There is no landslide monitoring system operating yet at this scale in the U.S. Building one will require cooperation across state and federal agencies, and strengthened monitoring and communication networks. Even then, it will not be fail-proof.

Alert systems do not remove the risk entirely, but they are a better option than no warning at all. Over time, they also build awareness as communities and visitors get used to thinking about these hazards.

Many of the most alluring places on Earth come with significant hazards. Arctic fjords are among them. The same processes that create this hazard – glacier retreat, steep terrain, dynamic geology – are also what make these landscapes so compelling. #736. NASA reveals 16 stunning galaxies hiding cosmic chaos

Some galaxies glow with sweeping spiral arms, while others are shaped by violent collisions, powerful black holes, and explosive bursts of star formation. A newly released collection of 16 images from NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory and other telescopes reveals the extraordinary diversity of these cosmic structures.

Like precious gemstones, galaxies come in many shapes and sizes, each with characteristics that tell a story about its history. Astronomers generally classify them into three major groups. Spiral galaxies, including our Milky Way, have winding arms extending from their centers. Elliptical galaxies typically contain older stars and are often thought to form through galactic mergers. Irregular galaxies have less orderly structures and can display a wide variety of cosmic activity.

Much as gemstones preserve clues about the geological processes that shaped Earth over billions of years, galaxies hold evidence of the forces that have shaped the universe. Studying these distant systems helps astronomers piece together the Milky Way's history and better understand what may await our galaxy in the distant future.

NASA Combines Chandra, Webb, and Hubble to Reveal Galactic Secrets

The new gallery features 16 images that bring together observations from some of the world's most powerful telescopes. Each includes X-ray measurements gathered by Chandra during its decades of operation in space.

These observations are combined with data from NASA's James Webb and Hubble Space Telescopes, IXPE (Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer), the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, NuSTAR (Nuclear Spectroscopic Telescope Array), and additional observatories operating on Earth and in space.

X-ray observations are especially valuable because they reveal extreme activity that other types of light can miss. Chandra can detect enormous clouds of gas heated to millions of degrees by powerful winds from massive stars, material expelled by supermassive black holes, and debris from stellar explosions. These processes also help spread chemical elements through space, including elements that ultimately become part of planets, the air we breathe, and our own bodies.

By combining X-rays with observations at other wavelengths, astronomers can see more of what is happening inside galaxies. The resulting images reveal how these vast systems form stars, interact with their surroundings, and change over billions of years.

Spiral Galaxies Reveal Explosive Star Formation

Several images highlight the remarkable activity taking place inside spiral galaxies. Messier 33 and NGC 3938 are viewed almost directly from above, allowing astronomers to examine their sweeping arms and identify regions containing energetic pairs of stars and the aftermath of stellar explosions.

Other galaxies reveal how their internal structures can help drive the birth of new stars. NGC 1672 and NGC 1385 are barred spiral galaxies, meaning they contain elongated central structures made of stars, gas, and dust. These bars can channel material toward the inner regions, providing fuel for intense episodes of star formation.

NGC 4725 demonstrates another way galaxies can produce new stars. Its observations reveal how an earlier encounter with another galaxy can disturb gas and stimulate star formation.

The collection also includes dramatic side views of NGC 4631 (the Whale Galaxy) and Messier 82 (the Cigar Galaxy). These galaxies feature enormous halos and powerful winds of gas heated to millions of degrees. Driven by intense stellar explosions, these outflows can carry material thousands of light-years into space, spreading important chemical elements into the regions between galaxies.

Supermassive Black Holes Blast Energy Across Galaxies

Some of the most dramatic features in the gallery come from the enormous black holes lurking at the centers of galaxies.

When these black holes actively consume surrounding material, their central regions become extremely energetic. Known as active galactic nuclei, these bright and powerful galactic cores can launch jets of particles and enormous outbursts that influence conditions across their host galaxies.

Centaurus A provides a striking example. Observations from Chandra and IXPE reveal a powerful stream of high-energy particles shooting tens of thousands of light-years into space from the galaxy's central region.

In Messier 106, jets launched from the central supermassive black hole interact with and heat surrounding gas. This activity produces unusual spiral structures that differ from the familiar arms normally associated with spiral galaxies.

The famous Sombrero Galaxy (Messier 104) offers another remarkable view. A supermassive black hole sits within its immense central concentration of stars, while Chandra's X-ray observations reveal a widespread halo of gas heated to millions of degrees and the hot remains of stars. These features surround the galaxy's prominent bands of dust, providing a deeper look at the energetic environment hidden within its elegant appearance.

Violent Galaxy Collisions Offer a Glimpse of the Milky Way's Future

Not every galaxy evolves peacefully. Some undergo enormous transformations as gravity pulls neighboring galaxies together, distorts their structures, or strips away the gas needed to create new stars.

Arp 143 captures the aftermath of a particularly dramatic encounter. A direct galactic collision produced an expanding ring, similar to ripples spreading outward after an impact. The disturbance also triggered waves of new star formation.

Other images show the chaotic consequences of galaxies merging. NGC 3256 and II Zw 096, the latter containing a powerful burst of star formation hidden behind thick dust, provide examples of the violent interactions that were especially common in the early universe.

These collisions also offer clues about the Milky Way's possible future. Our galaxy is expected to merge with the neighboring Andromeda galaxy billions of years from now, and observing other galactic mergers helps scientists understand how such an encounter could unfold.

The gallery includes several other unusual systems. NGC 1569 serves as a nearby example for investigating the intense episodes of star formation that occurred in young galaxies billions of years ago. NGC 660 displays a rare "polar ring" structure, with a ring of stars orbiting around the galaxy's poles rather than following its main plane.

Messier 90 illustrates a different kind of galactic transformation. As it travels through the Virgo Cluster, the galaxy is losing the gas it needs to form new stars. Its movement through the cluster strips away this material, potentially changing how the galaxy evolves.

Together, these 16 cosmic portraits show that galaxies are far from static collections of stars. They are constantly influenced by gravity, stellar explosions, black holes, and interactions with neighboring systems. By revealing these processes in remarkable detail, NASA's Chandra and its partner telescopes are helping astronomers understand the forces that shape galaxies, including our own.

#723. Scientists reach the ultimate quantum limit on a surface

Scientists at the Max Planck Institute for the Science of Light (MPL) have achieved a breakthrough in quantum physics, developing a way to study individual molecules on a surface with extraordinary precision. For the first time, researchers have demonstrated that molecules attached to a surface can reach the ultimate quantum limit of optical coherence, overcoming a major obstacle that has long restricted molecular quantum research.

The findings, published in Science, could open new possibilities for investigating how molecules interact with surfaces and developing advanced quantum technologies.

Why Surfaces Have Been a Major Obstacle in Quantum Physics

Many emerging quantum technologies depend on extremely small objects, such as individual atoms and molecules, that interact with light. Known as quantum emitters, these tiny systems can produce single particles of light called photons, store quantum information, and help establish quantum connections between distant systems. These capabilities are important for future applications in quantum computing and secure quantum communication.

To study a single quantum emitter, scientists must keep it stable and confined to one location for extended periods. Traditionally, researchers have accomplished this by trapping atoms or molecules in a vacuum or embedding them inside solid materials.

Placing quantum emitters directly on a surface, however, offers an intriguing advantage. Scientists could potentially manipulate individual molecules by "touching them" with an extremely sharp probe. Techniques such as scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) already use tips with atomic precision to investigate matter at incredibly small scales.

The difficulty is that surfaces are rarely as clean and stable as quantum experiments require. Contaminants from the surrounding environment can accumulate on them, creating disturbances that interfere with the delicate quantum behavior of nearby atoms and molecules. Until now, researchers had struggled to preserve the quantum optical properties of individual emitters attached to surfaces.

Scientists in MPL's Nano-Optics Division have now found a way around this problem.

A Clever Crystal Cleaning Method Solves the Problem

The research team, led by Prof. Vahid Sandoghdar, director at MPL and head of the "Nano-Optics" Division, developed an unusual cleaning method that takes advantage of a natural property of organic crystals.

At room temperature, the crystal material gradually evaporates through a process known as sublimation, in which a solid transforms directly into a gas without first becoming a liquid.

The researchers placed a small crystal inside a vacuum chamber equipped for extremely low temperatures, called a cryostat. As the crystal's uppermost layers slowly evaporated, they carried away unwanted contaminants, leaving behind a remarkably clean surface.

The team then cooled the crystal to just a few degrees Kelvin above absolute zero, the lowest theoretically possible temperature. This extreme cooling halted further sublimation and helped maintain the pristine surface.

Finally, the scientists used a tiny, specially fabricated oven to evaporate molecules onto the cold crystal, allowing them to settle on the clean surface.

Molecules Reach the Ultimate Quantum Limit

With this exceptionally stable environment in place, the researchers could investigate whether the molecules retained their fragile quantum properties.

Dr. Alexey Shkarin, a researcher in the Nano-Optics Division at MPL, explained: "The quality of quantum emitters can be evaluated by their coherence times, which indicate how long they keep their quantumness."

Coherence time describes how long a quantum system can maintain the properties needed for quantum behavior. The longer this coherence persists, the better the emitter can perform in applications that depend on precise interactions with light.

However, there is a fundamental physical restriction on how long coherence can last. Known as the Fourier limit, this boundary is determined by the time required for a quantum emitter to release its energy into its surroundings.

In an unstable or 'noisy' environment, disturbances can cause coherence to disappear hundreds or even thousands of times faster than this theoretical limit allows.

The scientists discovered that molecules deposited on their exceptionally clean crystal surface consistently reached the Fourier limit. This demonstrated that the surrounding environment was sufficiently quiet and stable to preserve the molecules' quantum coherence for the maximum physically permitted duration.

It is the first time researchers have achieved this fundamental limit with molecules attached to a surface.

New Possibilities for Controlling Individual Molecules

The breakthrough also allowed the team to investigate how a crystal surface influences the molecules resting on it.

Further experiments revealed that interactions with the surface can cause molecules to align in particular directions and alter their energy levels. The researchers also found indications that the surface might influence the molecules' shapes or the ways they vibrate.

These effects could provide valuable clues about how matter behaves at the molecular scale, while also offering new opportunities to control quantum systems.

"Our future work will focus on combining this method with AFM and STM to gain local nanometer control over individual quantum emitters," says Vahid Sandoghdar.

By combining their ultra-clean surface technique with powerful microscopy methods, the researchers hope to manipulate individual quantum emitters with precision measured in billionths of a meter.

Such capabilities could reveal previously inaccessible details about interactions between molecules and surfaces, while laying the groundwork for engineering new quantum states of matter and advancing future molecular quantum technologies.

The group known as OPEC+ announced Sunday it would cut oil production by more than 1.6 million barrels a day starting in May, running through the end of the year. The news sent both Brent crude futures, the global oil benchmark, and WTI, the US benchmark, up about 6% in trading Monday.

The production cut announcement also had an immediate impact on gasoline futures, which will be passed onto US drivers much more quickly than the spike in oil prices. RBOB, the most closely watched wholesale gasoline price, was up about 8 cents a gallon, or about 3%, in morning trading.

“I think OPEC is reawakening the inflation monster,” said Tom Kloza, global head of energy analysis for OPIS, which tracks gas prices for AAA. “The White House has to be shocked and major-time pissed. It certainly alters the calculus for a while.”

The national average for US gas prices stood at $3.51, on Monday, according to AAA. Kloza said he could see it getting up to $3.80 to $3.90 in relatively short order thanks to the move by OPEC.

“We’re not going to get back to $5 a gallon. I don’t think we’re even going as high as $4,” he said. But he said by the end of the summer US drivers could be back above year-earlier prices, especially if there is a hurricane or other storms affecting production along the Gulf Coast.

The average US regular gas price a year ago stood at $4.19 a gallon in the wake of Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and the disruption that caused to world’s energy markets. Prices eventually reached a record $5.02 a gallon on June 14, before starting a slow but steady decline over the course of more than three months during which the average price fell every day. The decline was partly driven by the release of oil from the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve, and partly by concerns that there could be a US or global recession that reduced the demand for gasoline.

#715. Just 3,000 steps a day may help slow Alzheimer's, scientists find

A new study suggests that even low levels of physical activity could protect the brain from Alzheimer’s disease – but not in the way scientists expected.

The researchers tracked almost 300 older adults with early brain signs of Alzheimer’s for nine to 11 years using pedometers. They found that physical activity didn’t reduce the toxic amyloid plaques that most Alzheimer’s treatments now target.

Instead, in people who already had these plaques, physical activity reduced the accumulation of misfolded tau proteins in specific brain areas. These proteins appear later in Alzheimer’s disease and are more closely linked to cognitive and functional decline. These signs of dementia were reduced by almost half in more active participants.

Benefits appeared at just 3,000 steps – roughly half an hour of walking at a moderate pace. The optimal range was 5,000 to 7,500 steps daily, after which the effect plateaued. More steps didn’t necessarily mean greater protection, which suggests a realistic target for older, sedentary people, rather than the often-cited 10,000 steps.

The study had limitations, however. It involved a fairly small group of mostly white, well-educated people in the US, and it didn’t take other lifestyle or health factors into account. Also, there may be other protective mechanisms of walking at play. But it does support other research suggesting that being physically active may lower the risk of dementia.

A UK study of 1,139 people over 50 found that those who were moderately to vigorously active had a 34-50% reduction in dementia risk when followed over eight to ten years. Among those who developed dementia, staying active reduced their memory decline, particularly in older women.

A larger 2022 UK study tracked 78,430 people for seven years using wrist accelerometers. It found a 25% reduction in dementia risk with just 3,800 steps daily, rising to 50% at 9,800 steps.

However, people who walked more also had better cardiovascular health – lower cholesterol, better sleep and blood pressure and reduced diabetes risk. Since these heart and stroke risk factors also increase dementia risk, the picture is complex.

Healthy habits often go together. People who exercise are more likely to eat well, not smoke, look after their heart health and have fewer financial stresses. This makes it hard to know which factor is having the biggest effect. The researchers tried to account for this, but because these habits are so closely linked, it’s difficult to say that exercise alone is responsible.

However, there is a strong case for this as there are multiple ways exercise might support the brain: improving cardiovascular health, increasing blood flow and boosting chemicals that promote brain-cell connections.

One such substance is irisin, a hormone produced by muscles that acts on almost all faulty brain mechanisms associated with Alzheimer’s, including inflammation. This and other chemicals, such as BDNF, associated with exercise, provide plausible biological pathways for how physical activity might directly influence brain health beyond its cardiovascular benefits.

But the relationship might work in reverse, too. People may become less active because of early Alzheimer’s symptoms. Those with hearing problems, for instance – itself a dementia risk factor – often report barriers that make them stop being active before other dementia symptoms appear.

Reduced activity then accelerates memory decline. This creates a vicious circle. Early disease symptoms – such as not hearing – can affect self-esteem and reduce engagement in physical activity, which in turn worsens cognitive decline.

Brisk walking might be particularly beneficial. A small trial of 15 people with mild to moderate Alzheimer’s who did Nordic walking (an enhanced walking technique that uses poles to work your upper body as well as your legs) maintained brain function over 24 weeks, with some functions even improving.

The 15 who received only standard care showed decline or no improvement. Though small, the trial suggests that even people already diagnosed with Alzheimer’s might benefit from increased physical activity, including brisk walking.

Getting outside, particularly in nature, may be especially beneficial for preventing dementia – possibly because it improves mood and sleep while reducing isolation – all dementia risk factors. The combination of physical movement, natural light exposure and social interaction when walking outdoors may create multiple protective effects that complement each other.

The challenge now is helping people overcome barriers to outdoor activity, such as safety concerns, fear of falling, or simply preferring the comfort of the sofa – particularly during wetter, colder months. But the evidence suggests that even a few minutes of walking could make a difference, and that modest, achievable targets – a half-hour stroll rather than a marathon training regime – may offer substantial protection against cognitive decline.

#711. Who was Eva Mamlok? Berlin square renamed after Jewish anti-fascist resistance fighter

She started taking action against Hitler as a teenager and never stopped until her death in a concentration camp. Berlin's Blücherplatz is now being renamed after her.

In the history of German resistance to Nazism, some names have become symbols: Sophie Scholl, Claus von Stauffenberg, Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Georg Elser. Yet many others have remained largely forgotten.

Among them is Eva Mamlok, a young Jewish woman from Berlin whose acts of defiance began in her teenage years and continued until her murder in a Nazi concentration camp.

Now more people will get to know her story as Berlin renames Blücherplatz, a square in the district of Kreuzberg, Eva-Mamlok-Platz on October 16.

"Eva Mamlok was a very remarkable person. As a young Jewish woman from Berlin, her spirit of resistance was particularly striking," said cultural researcher Alexandra Weltz-Rombach, who has worked extensively with the historians' network "Gruppe Eva Mamlok."

Mamlok was born on May 6, 1918, into a Jewish family in Berlin. They ran a wholesale wine business, and the family belonged to the vibrant Jewish middle class that formed an important part of Berlin's social and economic life during the Weimar Republic.

But Eva came of age just as Germany was descending into dictatorship. When Hitler became chancellor in 1933, she was only 14 years old. She was also a member of the Socialist Workers' Youth (SAJ). Following the suppression of the left-wing political youth organization later that year, she became active in the underground resistance.

According to testimony from Inge Berner (1922-2012), the sole survivor of Mamlok's later resistance group, Eva climbed onto the roof of a department store on Blücherplatz as a teenager and painted the slogan "Down with Hitler."

"She was caught doing that, though she wasn't arrested at that particular moment — that didn't happen until a year later," noted Weltz-Rombach.

Mamlok first appeared in official records in November 1934, after she was arrested for taking part in commemorations for socialist leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht at Berlin's Friedrichsfelde Cemetery.

Authorities determined that the 16-year-old held "hostile views toward the state" and placed her in "protective custody until further notice" — a euphemism the regime used to justify imprisonment without trial. She was sent to the women's concentration camp at Moringen in Lower Saxony, where she remained until May 1935.

After her release, she continued her anti-Nazi activities despite the increasing danger, facing persecution both as a political dissident and as a Jew.

In these underground circles, she met Pieter Siemsen (1914–2004), and they remained a couple until he emigrated to Argentina in 1937. The only known surviving photograph of Eva comes from his personal archive.

She had a daughter, Tana, in 1939. The father's identity is unknown, however. The child was placed in a Jewish orphanage in Berlin and deported in 1942 to the Auschwitz-Birkenau extermination camp, where she was murdered.

Meanwhile, because she was Jewish, Mamlok was forced to perform compulsory labor at the Berlin firm F. Butzke Schrauben-Industrie in 1940. There she met other women who shared her opposition to Nazism.

Together they formed what later became known as the "Eva Mamlok Group," an informal resistance network of Jewish and non-Jewish women. They distributed anti-Nazi leaflets and wrote slogans critical of Hitler.

They also shared banned books. "They were books that are today part of Germany's educational canon," from Thomas Mann to Lion Feuchtwanger, said Weltz-Rombach. That the Berlin Central and Regional Library (ZLB), Germany's largest public library, stands on the new Eva-Mamlok-Platz is a fitting symbol of this act of resistance and the importance of books, she added.

Mamlok and others in her group were arrested for distributing this forbidden literature. On January 13, 1942, she was deported from Berlin to the Riga Ghetto in occupied Latvia.

According to Inge Berner's testimony, Mamlok remained active in the resistance there too. For example, she arranged for a contact still in Berlin to send her a cake with a miniature camera hidden inside it to document persecution in the ghetto.

In October 1944, she was sent to the Stutthof Nazi concentration camp, where she died in December of that year.

Blücherplatz was named after the Prussian field marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, whose most renowned achievement was leading his army against Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813 and the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

Several places, including streets and parks, have been named after him in Germany.

"There is no shortage of Blücher memorial sites," said Weltz-Rombach. "And perhaps it's also time to put an end to this old Franco-German enmity — which is, of course, inherent in the name Blücher, given its Prussian tradition — and to establish, in its place, a different form of remembrance."

The square also happens to be the site of the Carnival of Cultures, a multicultural street festival and parade held every year to celebrate Berlin's global diversity.

Having first heard about Mamlok when a Stolperstein commemorating her was laid in 2011, Weltz-Rombach has been researching her biography for the past few years. The Berlin-based filmmaker, producer and curator has also been sharing the resistance fighter's story through various projects, including an exhibition in 2024 and a podcast series with young Berliners.

Since Mamlok was only a teenager herself when she became politically active, the workshop participants can easily relate to her fate.

These teenagers learn about Mamlok's experiences at the actual locations where she was active, helping them imagine what it would be like to be arrested and imprisoned for six months without trial for doing things that normally wouldn't be considered crimes — such as simply expressing an opinion publicly or laying flowers at a commemorative event, explained Weltz-Rombach.

"Above all, she is, of course, a role model — an example of how one would like to act oneself, and how courageous one would like to be."

"[Women's] resistance was often of a more everyday nature, rather than planting bombs," said Weltz-Rombach. Their stories offer examples of "forms of resistance that have only recently received more attention." Other examples include a 2024 exhibition and the launch of a database dedicated to female resistance fighters.

Even though many of the eyewitnesses are now dead, Weltz-Rombach believes there are still many more of these stories that deserve to be brought into the spotlight: "There are definitely many names we do not know, that are waiting to be further researched."

#702. NASA solves a strange mystery hidden inside asteroid Bennu’s boulders

NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission encountered an unexpected mystery when it arrived at asteroid Bennu in 2018. Instead of the relatively smooth terrain that earlier observations had suggested, the spacecraft found a rugged landscape dominated by enormous boulders, with very few areas suitable for collecting samples.

"When OSIRIS-REx got to Bennu in 2018, we were surprised by what we saw," said Andrew Ryan, a scientist at the University of Arizona's Lunar and Planetary Laboratory, who led the mission's working group responsible for analyzing the physical and thermal properties of its samples. "We expected some boulders, but we anticipated at least some large regions with smoother, finer regolith that would be easy to collect. Instead, it looked like it was all boulders, and we were scratching our heads for a while."

The discovery was especially confusing because of what astronomers had previously learned about Bennu's ability to retain heat. Measurements from NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope in 2007 indicated that the asteroid's surface warmed and cooled rapidly as it rotated through sunlight and darkness.

Scientists describe this property as low thermal inertia. Materials with low thermal inertia, such as beach sand, quickly gain heat in sunlight and lose it once the Sun disappears. Large, solid rocks behave differently. Much like concrete, they typically hold on to warmth and release it gradually.

Bennu's boulder-covered surface appeared to contradict those earlier measurements, leaving researchers with a puzzling question: How could an asteroid covered in massive rocks cool so quickly?

Hidden Cracks Reveal the Secret of Bennu's Boulders

Observations collected by OSIRIS-REx while surveying Bennu offered an early clue. The asteroid's boulders might contain far more empty space than scientists had assumed, allowing them to lose heat faster than ordinary solid rocks.

When samples from Bennu were returned to Earth, researchers finally had an opportunity to examine that possibility directly.

Ryan and his colleagues used several laboratory techniques to investigate tiny rock fragments collected from the asteroid. Their findings, published in Nature Communications, confirmed that the rocks were indeed highly porous. However, porosity alone could not fully explain Bennu's unusually rapid heat loss.

The researchers discovered another important feature. Many of the rock particles contained extensive networks of cracks running through their interiors.

To investigate how these fractures influence the movement of heat, researchers at Nagoya University in Japan examined the samples using a technique called lock-in thermography. This method uses a laser to heat a tiny area of a sample while measuring how the heat spreads through the surrounding material, much like ripples expanding across the surface of a pond.

"That's when things became really interesting," Ryan said. "The thermal inertia measured in the lab samples turned out to be much higher than what the spacecraft's instruments had recorded, echoing similar findings obtained by the team of OSIRIS-REx's partner mission, JAXA's (Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency) Hayabusa-2."

The difference between laboratory measurements and spacecraft observations suggested that the size and internal structure of Bennu's rocks were essential to understanding their behavior.

Because the returned samples were much smaller than the enormous boulders covering Bennu, scientists needed a way to determine how heat would move through the material on a much larger scale.

NASA Uses 3D Scans to Look Inside Asteroid Rocks

At NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston, researchers prepared the samples for detailed X-ray imaging while taking special precautions to prevent contamination.

Inside a glove box filled with protective nitrogen gas, team members sealed individual rock particles in airtight containers. They then transported the containers to a laboratory for X-ray computed tomography, or XCT scanning. After imaging, the particles were returned to the glove box without being exposed to Earth's environment.

"The sample goes into its own 'spacesuit,' gets a CT scan, and then comes back to its pristine environment, all without having any exposure to the terrestrial environment," said Nicole Lunning, lead OSIRIS-REx sample curator within the Astromaterials Research and Exploration Science division at NASA Johnson and one of the study's co-authors. "We can image right through these airtight containers to visualize the shape and internal structure of the rock that's inside."

The scans gave scientists a detailed view of the samples' interiors, revealing structures that could not be examined simply by looking at their surfaces.

"X-ray computed tomography allows us to look at the inside of an object in three dimensions, without damaging it," said study co-author and NASA Johnson X-ray scientist Scott Eckley.

Each scan also produced a permanent three-dimensional digital record of the particle's exterior and internal features. These records were added to a publicly accessible database, allowing the samples to be studied without repeatedly handling the original material.

Ryan's team then incorporated the X-ray scan data into computer simulations designed to track how heat travels through asteroid rocks. By extending the calculations from individual particles to the much larger boulders found on Bennu, they were able to reproduce the low thermal inertia observed by the spacecraft.

A Discovery That Could Change How Scientists Study Asteroids

The results resolved a key mystery about Bennu's unusual surface. Scientists had previously considered whether the asteroid's boulders might be extremely porous, with a soft, fluffy, or even sponge-like internal structure.

Although the rocks do contain substantial pore space, the researchers found that their widespread internal fractures were equally important to understanding how they retain and release heat.

"It turns out that they're really cracked too, and that was the missing piece of the puzzle," Ryan said.

The discovery has implications beyond Bennu. Astronomers frequently rely on measurements of how an asteroid absorbs and releases heat to estimate the physical properties of its surface. Until now, interpreting those observations has been difficult without actual rock samples to examine.

Ron Ballouz, a scientist at the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory in Laurel, Maryland, and the study's second author, explained that the findings provide a valuable connection between measurements made remotely and the true internal structure of asteroid material.

"We can finally ground our understanding of telescope observations of the thermal properties of an asteroid through analyzing these samples from that very same asteroid," Ballouz said.
