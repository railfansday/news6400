#650. Your aging brain may be trading memory for something more valuable

Growing older is often associated with memory loss and declining mental abilities. However, scientists are questioning whether these changes tell the whole story. Rather than simply becoming less capable, the brain may be adapting to a new stage of life in which accumulated knowledge, wisdom, and understanding become more important than remembering every detail.

Researchers at the University of Arizona have proposed a different way of understanding cognitive aging. Drawing on findings from molecular biology and brain imaging, they suggest that the changes occurring in older brains may reflect a natural stage of human development rather than deterioration alone. Their work, published in Perspectives on Psychological Science, offers a new perspective on why thinking and memory change with age.

The researchers call their proposed explanation the adaptive aging hypothesis. It suggests that the brain gradually adjusts its priorities to make better use of a lifetime of accumulated experience. This could help explain why older adults often have valuable strengths in teaching, mentoring, storytelling, and passing knowledge to younger generations.

"If we look at normative aging as a story of just decline, we are really missing the boat," said study coauthor Fabian-Xosé Fernandez, an associate professor of cognition and neural systems at the University of Arizona. "The older brain is optimized for different things than the younger brain, predominantly the sharing of stored expertise and social relationships."

How the Brain Shifts From Memory to Meaning

Two brain regions play important roles in how people remember and interpret their experiences. The hippocampus helps preserve specific information about events, including their details and circumstances. The frontal cortex, meanwhile, is more involved in extracting broader meaning, recognizing patterns, and understanding the central message of an experience, sometimes called its "gist."

According to the researchers, the balance between these systems changes during healthy aging. As people grow older, the brain tends to place less emphasis on activity in the hippocampus and rely more on the frontal cortex. This shift may allow the brain to focus more on interpreting information through existing knowledge rather than recording every new experience in precise detail.

"The fact that you see this across people and animal models suggests there is an evolutionary driving force," Fernandez said. "One could argue you are developing your entire life, and development occurs in lockstep with the needs of your brain. When you're younger, the hippocampus turns on because you don't have a knowledge base or know how the world works. A kid absorbs information like a sponge because they are building an archive of information. In early life, the balance is tilted toward recollecting declarative memories with precision, detail, and context."

Young brains need to gather enormous amounts of new information because they are still building a foundation for understanding the world. By later adulthood, however, people have already developed extensive stores of memories, concepts, and experiences. Connecting new information to what they already know may become more useful than retaining every individual detail.

The researchers suggest that this changing balance reflects a shift in how the brain processes experience. Instead of concentrating primarily on collecting detailed memories, it increasingly emphasizes understanding relationships, identifying underlying meaning, and applying previously acquired knowledge.

Why Evolution May Favor Wisdom in Older Adults

Evidence of similar changes in animal models raises the possibility that this transition has an evolutionary explanation. Fernandez suggests that the aging brain may be adapting to help individuals preserve and share the knowledge they have acquired throughout their lives.

From this perspective, the ability to draw on decades of experience could be especially valuable for making judgments, guiding others, and transferring important information between generations. Older adults may therefore play a distinct role in supporting younger people through mentorship, teaching, and storytelling.

"Given those goals, it makes sense that you aren't interested in nitty-gritty details," Fernandez said. "You are interested in safeguarding the vast knowledge you already have. Evolution may be saying that if you make it long enough, there's a need to transmit this information, so we put into motion biological changes that enforce and protect your semantic and autobiographical memories."

Under this hypothesis, changes in memory and thinking are not necessarily signs that the brain is functioning less effectively. They may instead reflect a different set of priorities, with greater emphasis on preserving meaningful knowledge and using it to understand new situations.

Why Healthy Brain Aging Is Different From Alzheimer's

The adaptive aging hypothesis could also provide scientists with another way to examine the differences between normal cognitive aging and Alzheimer's disease.

Although both involve changes in memory, Fernandez emphasizes that Alzheimer's should not be understood as ordinary aging taken to an extreme. The biological processes involved are fundamentally different.

"When I was in grad school, people thought about Alzheimer's as a more intense version of cognitive aging, but that is just not the case," Fernandez said. "They are completely different."

During typical aging, the brain's gradual shift away from relying heavily on the hippocampus and toward greater involvement of the frontal cortex generally unfolds between the fifth and seventh decades of life. In Alzheimer's disease, however, abnormal processes involving proteins such as amyloid and tau may interfere with this transition.

One important distinction involves activity in the hippocampus. While healthy aging is generally associated with reduced emphasis on this region, Alzheimer's can involve unusually high levels of hippocampal activity. Rather than supporting the normal progression of cognitive development, disease-related changes may disrupt the brain networks responsible for memory and understanding.

"In normative aging, hippocampal activation is deemphasized, which is probably what should be happening in a 70-year-old. But in many cases of Alzheimer's, it turns out the hippocampus is hyperactive," he said. "This beautiful developmental transition is completely disrupted in Alzheimer's disease by pathological processes that mess with episodic memory and interfere with circuits that allow you to think about your own life, lexicon, and vocabulary."

These disruptions can affect the ability to recall personal experiences and access stored knowledge, including words and their meanings. Understanding how these changes differ from the adaptations associated with healthy aging may help researchers develop a clearer picture of normal brain development and neurodegenerative disease.

Embracing the Hidden Strengths of the Aging Brain

The researchers argue that society may need to reconsider what successful brain aging looks like. Encouraging older adults to process information exactly as younger people do may not always be the most productive approach.

Fernandez suggests that placing excessive emphasis on remembering precise details could work against the brain's changing priorities. "For many individuals, the weight of how you process information should probably not be so hippocampal-centric by the sixth or seventh decade."

Instead of judging older adults primarily by abilities that tend to weaken with age, the researchers encourage greater recognition of the capabilities that may become especially valuable later in life.

Creating more opportunities for social interaction could be one way to support these strengths. Relationships between older and younger generations may be particularly beneficial because they provide opportunities to exchange experiences, share expertise, and preserve knowledge that might otherwise be lost.

"People see older individuals and look at them as not being on par with younger individuals across a lot of metrics," Fernandez says. "The way I look at it is that you've got someone with a brain that has been active for several decades and has seen a lot. There is tremendous value there, and we don't do a good job of unlocking it."

The central message of the adaptive aging hypothesis is that growing older may involve more than a gradual reduction in cognitive abilities. Although some mental skills become less reliable with age, the brain may also be adapting to make better use of the knowledge and experience accumulated over a lifetime. Recognizing those strengths could change how society understands aging and the contributions older adults can make.

#649. 'Not in our DNA' - will 3v3 change the future of English football?

Shortly after England's disappointing defeat by Argentina at this summer's World Cup, boss Thomas Tuchel offered his view on why the Three Lions are struggling to win major tournaments.

"I think ball possession plays a crucial role," he said.

"It's maybe not in our DNA like it is in the Spanish DNA or Argentinian or Brazilian DNA to take the ball, control the game and the ball, which is also a big problem."

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), in scientific terms, usually refers to the master blueprint for life - the hereditary material in humans and almost all other organisms.

In football, the DNA of a team starts out at grassroots level, where young players are shaped and developed.

This season, the Football Association (FA) has implemented a change in youth football that could well result in a change of the DNA of the English game.

For a long time, it was considered normal for children of all ages to play 11 versus 11 matches on full-sized pitches, but in 1999 the FA introduced "mini-soccer" for under-10s.

The idea was that the small-sided variation of association football would provide youngsters with more opportunities to get a touch of the ball, and therefore be more involved than they were in large-sided games.

Up until the end of last season, the smallest variation of the game that served as an introduction to football for children under the age of seven was 5v5.

Last month, that was reduced further to 3v3.

This variation of the small-sided game does away with goalkeepers and there are also no referees - the idea is that fewer players in a "team" means those three will get more touches of the ball, and score more goals.

It is the result of two years of research by the FA in conjunction with Liverpool John Moores University, where over 400 games from under-6 to under-14 level were analysed.

"All of the research done demonstrates that those players having more touches of the ball are more engaged in the game," John Folwell, the FA's head of grassroots coach development, told BBC Sport.

"They are also making more decisions and are engaged in more physical activity, so that is the thinking behind it.

"There are no goalkeeper and no substitutes, so everyone is active in the game. They are not sitting on the side watching their mates play, or they are not standing in goal watching their friends play. They are actually involved in the game."

With the new format having only been introduced a few weeks ago, it will take some time before teams and coaches have been able to fully adapt.

The smaller-sized format can pose logistical issues, with more games and more coaches and volunteers needed to oversee them.

BBC Breakfast recently visited Collingham FC in Nottinghamshire to see how the coaches there were adapting to the new format.

"We have three pitches going on at once and all it needs is one parent to be at work and you are trying to keep an eye on three games and nine children at once," said Michael Bland, coach of Collingham FC's under-7s.

"It is the self-officiating, dangerous tackles flying in and trying to teach the kids what a safe tackle is."

Mick Goodwin has been a youth football coach since the 1990s, so has seen it evolve from 11-a-side games to the small-sided format it is now.

"My concern is you will get one dominant player in the 3v3 where the not-so-good players will not get a touch of the ball," he said.

"What we've seen is the dominant player has been shooting from the halfway line.

"We can absolutely see the concept, I just think it needs a bit of finessing and it will be fine."

The guidelines for football for children under seven is that the sport should not be about tactics or competitiveness, but about enjoyment - that is what the FA believes the new format will provide.

At Collingham FC, that was generally the consensus among those who played, and their parents.

"They are open goals and you can score more," said one child, while another added: "There's less players so it is easier to dribble around them."

One parent, Dean, said: "Before [my son] was just in goal but now he is learning to pass to other players, block stuff and be a better defender as well."

Another parent, Hannah, added: "My son has an older brother so he is used to playing in the more traditional format, so for him it has taken a bit of adapting."

One of the more criticised aspects of the new, smaller format is the lack of goalkeepers, with some concerns over how that might impact their development.

The 3v3 format only lasts for 12 months, so goalkeepers do return from under-8 level when the game progresses to 5v5.

"When you make decisions like this, there are obvious trade-offs but we don't believe it is going to be detrimental to the development of future goalkeepers," Folwell added.

"Obviously playing 3v3, players can't use their hands but they are still going to be developing skills of blocking, sliding across, needing to get between the ball and the goal.

"They will also improve their ability on the ball, which we know for goalkeepers now is an increasing technique they need to have.

"We don't believe that goalkeeping is an early specialisation position. There are lots of examples of players who have come through and ended up being goalkeepers at a later age - England goalkeeper Hannah Hampton, for example, first played outfield for a long time."

Whether the new format improves the development of English footballers will not be clear for a few years yet.

But England are far from alone in opting for 3v3 at under-7 level, with the format already part of the youth structure in Germany, the Netherlands, Norway and other countries.

Indeed, it is a format that is growing in popularity across the world.

"It is the first year of adoption and obviously these things can take time to implement and for people to accept change," Folwell said.

"What we have seen is that once people have experienced it they tend to be a convert and understand why we are doing it, because the children really love it."

#646. Ukrainian President Zelenskyy speaks at EU summit amid spat with Donald Trump

EU leaders reaffirmed support for Ukraine at the Brussels summit. But souring relations with the US over Russia and Starlink and a big hole in the budget will make for a difficult winter for Ukraine.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy was applauded by EU leaders when he appeared at this week's summit in Brussels. But getting what he needs from Europe will be tough, and a public dispute with US President Donald Trump has put him in a very difficult position.

Speaking to press at the summit, he said that "Putin wants to destroy us" and that Ukraine must prepare for a hard winter.

Experts view the upcoming cold season as a rough one for Ukraine as well.

"Ukraine is arguably entering the most difficult winter since [Russia's] full scale invasion," Olena Prokopenko, a senior fellow at the German Marshall Fund of the United States, a nonpartisan think-tank, told DW.

Three painful topics were on the table at the summit for discussion between Zelenskyy and EU leaders: US relations with Russia, Starlink ― and money.

Zelenskyy's visit to Brussels comes at a time when he needs European support more than ever. That's because his relationship with the US is worsening. Worries are growing in the EU and Ukraine that the US is growing more sympathetic to Russia.

President Trump told US news channel Newsmax: "I think I find Zelenskyy to be more difficult to deal with than [Russian President Vladimir] Putin."

Trump struck a deal to buy Russian diesel on October 12, which signifies a step back from sanctions placed on Russia and a blow for Ukraine.

When Zelenskyy ordered Ukrainian forces to conduct fresh strikes on Russia's oil refineries, Trump labelled the Ukrainian leader's decision as "a disgrace."

At the press conference at the summit on Friday, Zelenskyy told reporters that he knows that US-built missiles "are being sent to the Middle East, so it is no secret that we are not a priority."

He is asking Europe instead to supply the military power Ukraine needs to get through the winter.

Since the beginning of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the US has been the biggest military donor to Ukraine. But the relationship between the two countries has become more difficult since the start of Donald Trump's second term in the White House.

Ukraine uses Starlink, a satellite Internet service developed by tech billionaire Elon Musk. The country also uses Starshield, the military-grade version, for battlefield communications, mid-range strikes on Russian-occupied territory and interceptor drones, though not for long-range strikes in Russia.

At the summit, Zelenskyy spoke of his "deep and unpleasant" surprise upon finding out that Ukraine was experiencing disruptions to the systems. He said this has hobbled Ukraine's ability to defend itself.

Both Elon Musk and the White House say they are not responsible for the slowdown.

The EU's foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas told the press at the summit before the talks began: "Starlink is really necessary for Ukraine and unfortunately in [the] short term Europe can't replace this capability."

Prokopenko said that this was the reason "why Ukraine's most credible medium-term solution here is to continue relying on the US. And the EU is critical in this context in terms of backing Ukraine's requests for the US administration to keep those technologies on offer" for Kyiv.

Speaking to journalists at the summit, Zelenskyy said the speed of Starlink had still not been fully restored but had improved. He said Ukraine is cooperating with various countries, including France, to search for a partial solution.

Zelenskyy urged EU leaders Friday to keep backing Kyiv financially as well, asking for nearly $80 billion (€70 billion) more next year.

"This is a realistic amount and I ask you to find this support," Zelenskyy said. "It will help us hold Russia back."

The EU gave Kyiv an approximately $100 billion (€90 billion) loan last year, making the EU Ukraine's biggest international backer. But the Ukrainian government is saying it needs more.

"The war is becoming objectively more expensive, and the Ukrainian economy is increasingly affected by the Russian strikes, leading to a drastic decline in tax and customs revenues," Prokopenko said. "So the needs will continue growing."

So will Ukraine get what it needs? Closing the summit, President of the European Council Antonio Costa said that Ukraine "remains a priority" and asked the European Commission to come up with a proposal to pull together more funds for the country in 2027.

Essentially, it looks as though the EU will try to find a way to meet Ukraine's cash demands ― though it is not yet clear how.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen promised the EU will "continue to lead" but urged international partners to step up as well.

Zelenskyy will leave Brussels without concrete promises or numbers, but with renewed commitments for support from Europe.

The Ukrainian president concluded his press conference with an emotional statement: "We will keep living and fighting with dignity."

#641. Scientists discover a bizarre Jurassic “squid” unlike anything ever seen

Two extraordinarily rare fossils found in Wyoming have revealed a strange new species of squid relative that lived approximately 160 million years ago, during the Jurassic Period.

Named Wyoteuthis linsterorum, the prehistoric creature belonged to an extinct group of marine animals known as belemnites, which were related to modern squid and octopuses. What makes this species particularly remarkable is its unusually thick, barrel-shaped internal skeleton, earning it the distinction of being the "chunkiest" or "fattest" belemnite ever identified.

An international research team from the United States, United Kingdom, and New Zealand, including Dr. Dean Lomax, an Honorary Research Fellow at The University of Manchester, detailed the discovery in the scientific journal Papers in Palaeontology.

An Unusually Thick Jurassic Squid Relative

Belemnites are among the most frequently discovered fossils in the world. They are best known for their distinctive, bullet-shaped internal skeletal structures, called rostra or guards, which are often the only parts of the animals that survive in the fossil record.

Wyoming's Sundance Formation, which preserves evidence of an ancient inland sea that once stretched across much of the western United States, has yielded millions of these fossils. According to Wyoming paleontologist and study coauthor Jessica Lippincott, they are commonly nicknamed "squid butts" in the region.

Most belemnite fossils from Wyoming have a narrow, bullet-shaped appearance, typically measuring between 5 and 10 centimeters in length and just 1 to 2 centimeters across. The newly identified species is dramatically different. Its rostrum measures approximately 10 centimeters long but reaches an extraordinary 6 centimeters in width, making it the thickest known example of its kind.

Scientists estimate that the living animal, including its arms, could have reached a total length of approximately 60 centimeters, or about two feet.

Rare Fossils Reveal a Surprising Evolutionary Secret

Wyoming paleontologist Bill Wahl, who has devoted much of his career to searching for fossils across the state, recognized that the unusual specimen deserved closer examination. He said the belemnite "stuck out like a sore thumb" compared with the countless others found in the region.

Wahl assembled an international team to investigate the fossils, bringing in specialists including belemnite expert Alexey Ippolitov, a PhD student now based at Victoria University of Wellington in New Zealand.

The researchers used computed tomography (CT) scanning to investigate the fossils' internal structures without damaging the specimens. These detailed scans revealed an unexpected connection to an ancient belemnite family that scientists had believed disappeared millions of years before this animal lived.

Ippolitov said he was "deeply surprised" to encounter such an unusual specimen from a group that has already been studied extensively. Belemnites have been the subject of paleontological research for more than 200 years, making the identification of such a radically different species especially remarkable.

The creature's unusual proportions may also offer clues about how it lived and hunted.

Ippolitov explains: "A possible explanation is that their relatively large size gave them an advantage when hunting co-occurring smaller belemnites of the genus Pachyteuthis. Modern squid, after all, are hardly picky when it comes to prey: they readily hunt not only other species of cephalopods, but sometimes even their own kind."

A Fossil Discovery Nearly 30 Years in the Making

Although the species has only now been formally described, its discovery began almost three decades ago.

The first known specimen was found in the late 1990s by Dr. Burkhard Pohl, founder of the Wyoming Dinosaur Center. While examining fossils, Pohl noticed a belemnite that was considerably larger than any he had previously encountered.

Despite its unusual appearance, the specimen remained unstudied in a museum collection drawer for many years.

A breakthrough came when fossil collector Cliff Linster discovered a second specimen near Ten Sleep, Wyoming. He donated the fossil to the Wyoming Dinosaur Center in 2019, providing researchers with another example of the mysterious animal.

With two specimens available for comparison, scientists finally had enough evidence to recognize and formally describe an entirely new species.

The genus name Wyoteuthis means "a squid from Wyoming," while the species name linsterorum recognizes Cliff Linster and his family for their contributions. Linster discovered one of the only two known examples and ensured it was available for scientific research.

Lippincott emphasized how extraordinary the discovery was, even for researchers familiar with Wyoming's abundant belemnite fossils.

"After spending years fossil hunting in Wyoming and collecting countless fossils of the common Pachyteuthis belemnites here in Wyoming, this one is unlike anything I have ever seen! It goes to show that we are still finding new species of fossils all the time, and people who rockhound or collect invertebrate fossils can also contribute to science."

Honoring a Fossil Hunter's Remarkable Legacy

Sadly, Cliff Linster passed away in June before the research was published. He had been aware that scientists were investigating his discovery and was enthusiastic about seeing the unusual fossil receive formal scientific recognition.

The Linster family already had an impressive history of fossil discoveries, including their association with the famous dinosaur Bambiraptor.

For British paleontologist Dr. Dean Lomax, the discovery also carries considerable personal meaning. Lomax, an Honorary Research Fellow at The University of Manchester and an 1851 Research Fellow at the University of Bristol, first worked alongside Cliff and his wife, Sandy, while excavating dinosaur fossils as a teenager.

He said: "I met Cliff and Sandy in 2009, when I spent a week digging up a dinosaur bonebed on their property in northern Montana. I have fond memories of that dig, listening to Cliff share his passion for fossils, including this belemnite. I'm honored to help name this fossil after Cliff. It's bittersweet that he didn't get to see it published, but it's wonderful that we can immortalize him and his family name in the history of paleontology. The find is incredibly rare, which might be due to some ecological adaptations to the environment at the time. Perhaps its rarity is a result of narrow specialization."

Lomax has extensive experience studying Wyoming's prehistoric animals, including collecting fossils and excavating plesiosaurs and ichthyosaurs from the Sundance Formation. He is also the author of the recently published book "The Secret Lives of Dinosaurs," which features fossils from Wyoming.

The discovery highlights how even well-studied fossil groups can still hold major surprises, particularly when unusual specimens are preserved and made available for scientific investigation.

Both known fossils of Wyoteuthis linsterorum are now on display at the Wyoming Dinosaur Center in Thermopolis, Wyoming, where visitors can see the extraordinary remains of one of the Jurassic seas' most unusual squid relatives.

The findings are published in the international journal Papers in Palaeontology.

#630. Scientists discover a surprising link between vitamin C and cancer survival

Vitamin C plays an important role in keeping the immune system healthy, helping wounds heal, and protecting cells from damage. However, people with precancerous blood disorders and blood cancers often have persistently low vitamin C levels. Researchers have been investigating whether correcting this deficiency could improve their health and treatment outcomes.

New findings from the phase 2 EVITA clinical trial suggest that vitamin C supplementation may offer important benefits for people with precancerous blood conditions and early-stage blood cancers. The randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study was conducted by the Van Andel Institute-Stand Up To Cancer® Epigenetics Dream Team.

The results, published in the American Cancer Society journal CANCER, revealed that patients who received 1,000 mg of oral vitamin C every day for one year experienced fewer adverse events during the study and its follow-up period. The supplement also changed the levels of certain immune signaling molecules, known as cytokines, in ways associated with more favorable outcomes. These changes suggest that vitamin C could influence inflammation-related processes involved in the growth and progression of abnormal blood cells.

Although vitamin C did not significantly affect the rate at which abnormal blood cells expanded, the researchers identified an encouraging result. An exploratory analysis showed that participants who received vitamin C were significantly more likely to be alive after approximately three years of follow-up than those who took a placebo. Scientists emphasize that this potential survival benefit must be verified in a larger phase 3 clinical trial.

Vitamin C Shows Promise for Patients at Risk of Leukemia

"We are tremendously excited and cautiously optimistic that our results could one day translate into a simple way to improve care and outcomes for people with pre-leukemia disorders," said Peter A. Jones, Ph.D., D.Sc. (hon), a distinguished professor at Van Andel Institute. Jones is also a co-corresponding author of the research and co-leader of the VAI-SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team.

"Our approach reflects a growing emphasis on cancer interception -- intervening before cancer becomes established. We are working to build on the trial findings by exploring how exactly vitamin C interacts with pre-cancerous cells, including the idea that it may help restore normal epigenetic function in blood cells at risk of becoming leukemia."

The international research effort was led by Kirsten Grønbæk, M.D., Ph.D., a leading hematologist, professor at the University of Copenhagen, and member of the VAI-SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team.

"These exciting preliminary findings lay the groundwork for a larger clinical trial to explore vitamin C as a possible way to improve patient outcomes," said Grønbæk. "It is too early to make definitive recommendations based on our results, but we are hopeful that a more expansive study will provide further insights."

First Clinical Trial Tests Daily Vitamin C for Precancerous Blood Disorders

The study enrolled 109 patients with either clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) or myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). Both conditions can develop into acute myeloid leukemia (AML), an aggressive form of blood cancer that is often difficult to treat. The likelihood of developing CCUS and related blood disorders rises with age, making these conditions particularly common among older adults.

Currently, no approved treatment can prevent CCUS from progressing to leukemia. Although stem cell transplantation is an option for treating MDS, many patients cannot undergo the procedure because of age or other medical conditions. None of the people enrolled in the trial were receiving cancer treatment when the study took place.

To determine whether vitamin C might offer benefits, researchers randomly divided participants into two groups. One received 1,000 mg of vitamin C by mouth each day for 12 months. The other received a placebo designed to be indistinguishable from the supplement in appearance, taste, and smell, but containing no vitamin C.

Neither the participants nor the clinical staff knew who was receiving vitamin C until the study concluded. This double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled approach is considered the gold standard in clinical research because it reduces potential bias and allows investigators to assess treatment effects more objectively.

The research was carried out at four hospitals: Copenhagen University Hospital-Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen University Hospital-Herlev, Aalborg University Hospital, and Keck Hospital of the University of Southern California.

What makes EVITA particularly notable is that it was the first randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to investigate oral vitamin C in patients with conditions that can precede leukemia or with lower-risk myeloid cancers. Earlier studies had primarily examined vitamin C administered intravenously and had focused on solid tumors rather than precancerous blood disorders or early-stage blood malignancies.

EVITA instead evaluated a daily oral dose comparable to that available in many over-the-counter vitamin C supplements.

More Than Half of Patients Had Low Vitamin C Levels

Blood samples collected throughout the study allowed researchers to track participants' vitamin C concentrations and determine whether supplementation corrected deficiencies.

At the beginning of the trial, more than half of the participants had insufficient vitamin C levels. Among those assigned to the supplementation group, blood concentrations returned to normal after they began taking vitamin C.

For patients living with conditions that could eventually develop into leukemia, the possibility of an accessible intervention is especially important. In many cases, physicians have little choice but to monitor the disease and watch for signs that it is progressing.

"The standard approach for patients with pre-cancer blood disorders is to monitor their blood cell counts over time. This is, understandably, very frustrating -- no one wants to hear that they have a condition that increases leukemia risk but there's nothing that can be done other than watch and wait," said Casey O'Connell, M.D., FACP, the Lawrence and Jane Kelly Chair in Hematology at the University of Southern California and lead clinician at USC's trial location.

"The EVITA trial took on this challenge by exploring vitamin C's potential to address the underlying factors that drive leukemia development."

How Vitamin C Could Influence Cancer Development

Scientists have investigated whether vitamin C could play a useful role in cancer treatment for more than seven decades. For much of that time, the evidence remained uncertain because studies produced inconsistent findings and employed different experimental methods.

During the past decade, however, improved research technologies have revealed that vitamin C has important functions beyond its familiar roles in nutrition and immunity. In particular, scientists have uncovered its involvement in controlling gene activity and guiding normal cell development.

One of the most important discoveries concerns TET2, an enzyme that helps prevent cancerous growth by controlling whether particular genes are switched "on" or "off." Vitamin C supports the activity of this enzyme, which helps maintain normal patterns of gene regulation.

Mutations affecting TET2 are frequently found in leukemia and related blood disorders. When the enzyme's normal function is disrupted, abnormal cells can gain an opportunity to multiply and potentially develop into cancer.

Experiments using cancer models have provided additional evidence of vitamin C's possible protective effects. These studies found that supplementation could interfere with cellular changes associated with cancer and slow the progression of leukemia. Notably, the strongest effects appeared when vitamin C was introduced before cancer had become established.

This research helped inspire the idea of using vitamin C as a form of early intervention, potentially targeting the biological changes that precede the development of leukemia.

Before launching EVITA, the VAI-SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team conducted a pilot clinical study in 2016 to determine whether taking vitamin C orally could meaningfully raise its concentration in the blood.

The initial findings suggested that oral supplementation was a practical approach. Encouraged by those results, the researchers launched the phase 2 EVITA trial in 2017.

"This early work was critical because it offered a foundation from which to develop the present trial," said Stine Ulrik Mikkelsen, M.D., Ph.D., the study's first author and a clinician researcher in Grønbæk's laboratory.

"Vitamin C is important because it is involved in a range of biological functions, including the activity of enzymes that regulate normal cell function."

Researchers are continuing to examine the EVITA data to better understand the biological processes that might explain why patients receiving vitamin C showed improved survival.

Although the findings offer a promising direction for future research, additional clinical studies will be essential to determine whether vitamin C supplementation can reliably improve outcomes for people with precancerous blood disorders and early-stage blood cancers.

#622. Astronomers uncover new secrets of the Universe’s first five minutes

Scientists have measured the amount of helium produced during the Universe's first five minutes with unprecedented precision, offering a clearer picture of the conditions that existed shortly after the Big Bang. The achievement, made by an international research team that includes scientists from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, represents a major advance in precision cosmology.

The findings, presented in five papers published in The Astrophysical Journal, are based on 130 hours of observations using the Large Binocular Telescope. By analyzing distant galaxies, the researchers reduced the uncertainty in this fundamental measurement to just 0.5%, making it three times more precise than previous estimates.

The improved accuracy provides scientists with a powerful new tool for investigating the origins of the Universe and testing the Standard Model of physics. Such fundamental tests have a long history of advancing scientific understanding and contributing to technological breakthroughs.

"This is a physics experiment on a grand scale and one of the biggest findings in my entire 40-year career," said Evan Skillman, University of Minnesota College of Science and Engineering Distinguished Professor in the School of Physics and Astronomy. "It's a fundamental number that tells us specifically about the conditions of our Universe in its first five minutes. It has diagnostic power that speaks directly to the Standard Model of Physics."

According to the Big Bang theory, the Universe began expanding from an extraordinarily hot, dense state approximately 13.8 billion years ago. Three major lines of evidence support this explanation: the continuing expansion of the Universe, the cosmic microwave background radiation, and the amounts of light elements such as helium and deuterium that formed during its earliest moments.

Although cosmic expansion and the microwave background have been studied extensively, measurements of primordial helium have historically received less attention. Determining its original abundance more accurately allows researchers to investigate the physical conditions that prevailed when the Universe was only minutes old.

To achieve their new level of precision, the team departed from the conventional method of estimating primordial helium. That approach involves observing large numbers of galaxies and extrapolating the results to determine how much helium existed before later generations of stars altered their chemical composition.

Instead, the researchers selected 15 exceptionally pristine, small, distant galaxies. These galaxies have undergone relatively little chemical evolution, making them valuable cosmic "time capsules" that preserve clues about the Universe's early composition.

Powerful Telescopes Deliver Record Precision

The team used sophisticated spectrographs developed at Ohio State University to examine more than 10 helium spectral lines and 15 hydrogen lines simultaneously. Studying these signals together made it possible to identify and correct subtle sources of measurement error that earlier studies could largely overlook.

Such small effects become especially important when researchers are trying to reduce uncertainty to less than 1%. Accounting for them was essential to achieving the new 0.5% precision.

"The MODS spectrographs took 12 years to build from conception to first light on sky," said Richard Pogge, College of Arts and Sciences Distinguished Professor of Astronomy at Ohio State University. "This is the kind of project we designed them to do, and to see them deliver is enormously satisfying. It's not every day you can help build instruments that measure something fundamental about our Universe."

Beyond refining estimates of the Universe's earliest chemical composition, the new measurement provides an important test of the Standard Model of physics. The amount of helium created shortly after the Big Bang depends on the physical conditions at that time, including the types of elementary particles present.

Using their precise helium measurement, the researchers were able to calculate the number of neutrino families in the early Universe. Neutrinos are extremely light subatomic particles, and their behavior plays an important role in scientists' understanding of fundamental physics.

The results therefore do more than strengthen existing evidence for the Big Bang. They also offer a more precise way to check whether established physical theories accurately describe the Universe during its first few minutes.

"We promised a half-percent uncertainty in our proposal, and we got there," added Skillman. "In the unpredictable world of science, that doesn't happen very often."

The international collaboration brought together scientists from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, Ohio State University, Gonzaga University, Northwestern University, The University of Texas at Austin, Indiana University, University of California Santa Cruz, University of Illinois, Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, and TRIUMF.

#615. China's influence in the gaming industry is growing

Home to more than 700 million gamers, China is the world's largest gaming market — but it's also one of the most tightly regulated. The effects reach far beyond China, impacting players around the world.

In a dark underground hideout, dim light spills from a handful of oil lamps. A comrade fiddles with a rifle. A red flag hangs on one of the roughly hewn stone walls, bearing a hammer and sickle on a white star — the symbol of the ChineseWorkers' and Peasants' Red Army. Weapons are scattered on the ground. One is picked up, and soon the action moves into the snowy forest, where gunfire erupts among the trees.

Such a scene could fit in a new installment of "Call of Duty," the popular first-person shooter game that transports players to battlefields past, present, and future. But it is actually from "The Defiant." The game is currently being developed by the Chinese studio Hoothanes Technology in Hangzhou. It tells the story of Chinese forces fending off the Japanese army in the 1930s and 1940s.

Developer Connor Xu tells DW the weapons featured in the game are unique. They were used only in Asia and are therefore unfamiliar to many Western players, he says. The same goes for the story, which Xu says has never before been told in a video game.

"The Defiant" is also designed to appeal to an international audience. In just a few years, China has emerged as a major player in the global gaming industry despite strict regulations, censorship and a former ban on video game consoles.

More than 700 million people in China regularly play video games. According to market research company Newzoo, most consider gaming an important part of their lives. Many use games to stay in touch with friends.

"Games make life more interesting," developer Xu says. "I can try different roles, different stories, different characters, because we only have one life to live, but we can play a role in another story. That's really attractive, I think, to Chinese users."

People in China primarily play games on PCs and smartphones, while consoles are much less common. There are historical reasons for that: While gamers in Europe and North America debated whether PlayStation or Xbox was the better console in the early 2000s, the devices were not officially sold in China between 2000 and 2014. Chinese authorities said the restrictions were intended to protect children and teenagers from becoming addicted to gaming.

Minors in China are currently allowed to play online games for no more than three hours a week. There are no such limits for adults, but gaming is subject to strict controls. Anyone who wants to play must register using their real name and a personal identification number. That effectively allows the state to track who plays what and when, as well as whom they communicate with.

There is also a gray area that the Chinese government appears to tolerate. Some players log in using VPNs, giving them access to games that are not officially available in China. Some younger players use their parents' accounts to get around the limits on gaming time.

Despite its strict controls on underage gamers, China has become increasingly receptive to video games in general, having recognized the industry's enormous economic potential. Early on, games released for Chinese players often appeared to be copies of Western titles. At the same time, Chinese tech companies such as Tencent and NetEase invested in numerous Western gaming companies.

One distinctive feature of the Chinese market is its licensing system. "Unlike a lot of the other big video games markets in the world, China has a tightly regulated and licensed official video games market," George E. Osborn tells DW. The journalist advises NGOs and governments on video games and spoke at the Gamescom congress. In his book "Power Play," he describes how authoritarian states use video games to pursue political objectives.

The rule for all games in China is simple: No license, no sales. "That system essentially forces developers who want to release their video games to go through multiple layers of approval from China's Ministry of Propaganda, Ministry of Culture," Osborn says.

Games are prohibited from containing material that threatens China's unity, sovereignty or territorial integrity, among other restrictions. Pornographic content and gambling are also banned. Games may not incite crimes that threaten national security or spread information authorities believe could disrupt public order.

"The whole point around this criteria is that it's deliberately designed to be pretty vague," Osborn says. Whether a game violates the rules is a matter of interpretation. Sometimes games are altered even after release. In the 2025 action role-playing game "Wuchang: Fallen Feathers," for example, enemies based on famous Chinese figures from the Ming Dynasty suddenly could no longer be killed. Instead, they would collapse from exhaustion.

The Chinese action role-playing game "Black Myth: Wukong" became an unexpected international hit in 2024. By then, it was clear Chinese developers could do more than cater to the domestic market — they could create successful games for a global audience as well. The game was inspired by the 16th-century Chinese literary classic "Journey to the West."

Osborn sees nothing wrong with bringing Chinese culture to audiences around the world. The question, he says, is how much the state actually intervenes in the games themselves. "I think in general, video game players are actually quite deeply skeptical of these kinds of things. They are generally very good at spotting when someone might be selling to them or someone might be propagandizing. And so I think if it's too obvious, it won't work," he says.

Because of the licensing system, Western developers also have to comply with government requirements if they want to sell their games in China. And they do want to, Osborn says, because the market is too big to ignore. "But I think when you start thinking about the potential impact this has on creative standards around the world, I think it's really important that developers ask themselves the question of what will we or won't we compromise on here?"

He fears the real danger lies in self-censorship. That could lead developers, for example, to leave queer characters out of their games. That would represent a major step backward, he says, because games help many people explore their identities.

China is not the only country where games are modified to comply with national regulations. In Germany, for example, symbols that violate the country's constitution, such as the swastika, may not be shown in games unless the game in question explicitly contributes to the country's culture of remembrance. Depictions of extreme violence also have to be removed from German versions.

Glorifying violence is prohibited in China, too, yet Xu's team is still allowed to develop a war game. How does this add up? Xu explains it this way: "I think in this game, the most important thing is not violence. The most important thing is to tell history."

Xu says the team is committed to delivering a historically accurate product. Whether it succeeds will be for historians to judge.

#611. Hurricane warning maps are improving, but a dangerous problem remains

An updated hurricane forecast map is making it easier for people to identify areas threatened by storms, but new research reveals a concerning limitation. Even when people can recognize where hurricane watches are in effect, they may not feel more at risk or become more inclined to take precautions.

Researchers at Florida State University examined whether changes to the National Hurricane Center (NHC) forecast graphics improve public understanding of hurricane threats. The study involved faculty from the School of Communication within the College of Communication and Information and the Department of Geography.

The researchers evaluated an experimental graphic that expands the traditional hurricane forecast cone to include tropical storm and hurricane watches and warnings for inland areas. The familiar cone has historically placed greater emphasis on coastal regions, even though dangerous storm conditions can extend far from the shoreline.

The findings revealed mixed results. Although the revised graphic helped people without specialized weather knowledge recognize inland areas under hurricane or tropical storm watches, it did not significantly influence how dangerous they believed the storms would be or whether they intended to take protective measures. Participants also showed no improvement in identifying areas covered by storm warnings.

The updated format was developed by the NHC in 2024, and the findings were published in the American Meteorological Society journal Weather, Climate, and Society.

"The National Hurricane Center has made a number of thoughtful changes to improve how hurricane information is communicated," said Assistant Professor Elizabeth Ray, the study's lead author. "We had a simple question. Do those changes actually help people understand what the graphic is telling them about the risk they face?"

Why People Misunderstand Hurricane Forecast Maps

To investigate how the public interprets hurricane forecasts, the research team surveyed residents of Florida and Georgia. Participants were shown either the conventional hurricane cone graphic or the newer experimental version that includes additional information about inland storm threats.

People who saw the revised map were more successful at identifying locations under hurricane or tropical storm watches. However, that improvement in recognizing watch areas did not lead to greater concern about the potential danger or stronger intentions to take safety precautions.

The results also highlighted a persistent misunderstanding surrounding the familiar hurricane "cone of uncertainty." Many people incorrectly interpret the width of the cone as an indication of a storm's physical size, rather than understanding what the graphic is intended to communicate about the uncertainty in its projected track.

"For years, research has shown that people often mistake a wider cone for a bigger storm," Ray said. "Our findings suggest that even with an updated graphic, that misconception can be hard to shake."

Why Better Hurricane Warnings May Not Be Enough

The NHC introduced its expanded forecast graphic in 2024 to help people recognize that hurricane dangers are not limited to communities along the coast. Flooding, tornadoes, and destructive winds can threaten areas well inland, making it important for residents beyond coastal evacuation zones to understand their potential exposure.

By adding inland hurricane and tropical storm watches and warnings to the traditional forecast cone, the updated graphic provides a more complete picture of where dangerous conditions could develop.

However, the study suggests that adding useful information to a forecast does not automatically make people more aware of their personal risk or more likely to respond appropriately.

The researchers stressed that their findings do not call into question the quality of the NHC's weather predictions, which they consider highly accurate. Instead, the results underscore the difficulty of presenting complex meteorological information in a way that people can readily interpret and use when making safety decisions.

"Our question wasn't whether the science is good," said co-author Patrick Merle, professor and director of the School of Communication. "The science is excellent. The question is how we communicate that science so people who aren't meteorologists understand the risk they're facing.

If people find information credible but still don't understand it well enough to take protective action, then communication has fallen short. The goal is to empower people with information they can use to stay safe."

The findings point to the importance of looking beyond a single forecast image when preparing for a hurricane. Although the cone can provide valuable information about a storm's expected path, it cannot communicate every hazard that a particular community might experience.

The researchers recommend using hurricane forecast graphics alongside local weather predictions and detailed information about possible impacts. Understanding the specific threats to an area, as well as the protective steps residents should consider, can provide a more useful basis for making decisions.

"Our findings are a reminder that one graphic can only tell you part of the story," Ray said. "Don't rely on the cone alone. Pair it with clear, local information about what impacts to expect and what actions to take. That gives you a fuller picture of what the storm could mean where you live and what to do next."

The study received funding through an internal Florida State University SEED grant. The research was also informed by discussions with the NHC, which supplied the experimental forecast graphics evaluated by the team.

Bradford Johnson, an assistant professor in the Department of Geography, contributed as a co-author.

#607. OpenAI says GPT-5.6-Sol helped find the PHP flaws now shaking the internet

OpenAI says its AI models helped uncover the critical PHP security flaws that have websites across the internet scrambling.

In a blog post on Wednesday, the company said it had spent the past two months leading a coalition of PHP maintainers, security researchers, and security companies to deal with the vulnerabilities, now nicknamed "PHP2SHELL." In its own Wednesday blog post, the PHP foundation thanked OpenAI, among other industry partners and cybersecurity researchers.

The newly exposed flaws could allow attackers to take over servers running PHP, the programming language behind a large share of the web. OpenAI called the bugs one of the most impactful security problems in recent memory.

According to OpenAI, security firm Calif found one of the critical flaws using the company’s GPT-5.6 Sol model as part of Patch the Planet, an OpenAI security program. Security company Wiz also discovered and reported both vulnerabilities on its own, OpenAI said. PHP’s maintainers led the work on the fixes. OpenAI, Wiz, Cloudflare, and other partners contributed research and helped prepare protections before the flaws went public.

OpenAI President Greg Brockman added more detail in a post on X. He said PHP was the first programming language he learned, which gave him a personal connection to the work.

According to Brockman, the bug had existed in PHP for 14 years. A smaller version of it was found in 2024, but wasn’t treated as serious at the time. Calif found a version of the bug in August, and OpenAI’s own team independently found a more complete version in September.

The coalition planned to disclose the flaws on Oct. 22, Brockman said, but details leaked early, and PHP’s maintainers released the fixes a day sooner. He said the two months of preparation still mattered, because the fixes were complicated and an earlier leak could have caused far more damage.

OpenAI said it also worked with U.S. government agencies on the response, including the White House’s Super Intelligence Force, the Office of the National Cyber Director, and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency.

OpenAI says PHP won’t be the last case of this kind. The company said it has already found several other serious vulnerabilities and expects similar discoveries to rise sharply in the coming months. Brockman pointed to a separate remote code execution flaw that Node.js, another widely used web technology, disclosed on Wednesday.

This year, new AI models like GPT-5.6-Sol and Claude Mythos have led to a rapid rise in bug discoveries, pushing some zero-day bug initiatives to shut down entirely, as Mashable reported previously.

Brockman wrote that the current way of handling bugs like these is "slow and manual," and won’t scale as AI gets better at finding them. OpenAI is now asking the security community for feedback on a new process for coordinating disclosures. One idea that floats is having governments or other authorized defenders constantly scan the internet for vulnerable systems. They would warn site owners when they find one and, in extreme cases, patch the systems themselves.

OpenAI also acknowledges the risk in its pitch. The same AI capabilities that helped find these bugs could help attackers build exploits faster. To put more of those tools in defenders’ hands, the company says it has committed $1 billion toward subsidized access to its security tools for organizations that protect essential services. It is also releasing a tool that helps site operators check whether they’re running a vulnerable version of PHP.

The patch scramble has been very visible. Wikipedia went down Wednesday morning, and the Wikimedia Foundation confirmed to Mashable that the outage was tied to the PHP vulnerabilities. The foundation said it had taken other systems offline as a precaution until they could be safely patched.

Other popular sites have also gone down or posted maintenance notices. According to Downdetector, sites having trouble in the midst of the PHP issues include Asana, GOG, Duolingo, Capital One, Bitbucket, Moodle, Quest Diagnostics, Final Fantasy XIV, War Thunder, and the Internet Archive. Most haven’t said whether their problems are connected to PHP, but, again, the programming language runs much of the internet.

Even outside of the response from the cybersecurity arm of the U.S. government, other world leaders are getting involved too. Japan’s prime minister made an unusual public appeal on X, urging anyone who runs a website to act that night, according to a translation of the Japanese-language post.

The PM asked site owners to check whether their public websites use PHP and to install the fix as soon as it was released at 11 p.m. Japan time on Oct. 21, which matches PHP’s 10 a.m. ET release.

For most people, the impact should be limited to some sites being temporarily unavailable. Regular users don’t need to install anything, since the fix is up to the companies running the sites.

#606. Paris Saint-Germain sparkle on the pitch while the city of light burns around them | Barney Ronay

PSG, pet project of the state of Qatar, expresses the dualism of the city, a place of deprivation where the centre is perfect

Those who sought to bring down the Republic will have to answer for it.

Tell you who would have loved this? Who would have been utterly engrossed scrolling the hashtag #Paris on Tuesday night as two things happened simultaneously, the harrowing of the Barcelona defence at the Parc des Princes, and the harrowing of Paris itself via tales of city-centre thuggery and end-of-days political doom‑mongering.

The big man, Guy Debord himself. The Society of the Spectacle, you say? Actually, I think we’re past that now. Hold the society bit. We’ll just take the spectacle.

The great Parisian Situationist philosopher wasn’t really one for managed fun. But it was impossible not to be bowled over by the extraordinary pre-match show for Paris Saint‑Germain versus Barcelona, Champions League Matchday Three.

The wild pyrotechnics, the plumes of smoke, the full-wrap light show, the lime-green robo‑text spelling out every player’s name on the pitch. Although not the Barcelona names, sadly, for anyone with an unrealised desire to see the words “ANTHONY GORDON” projected in vast letters for the delight of a shrieking crowd.

Admittedly, the Halloween‑themed tifo banner showing a masked Paris Saint-Germain serial killer-type literally butchering the Barcelona badge to death with his bloodied chainsaw was perhaps a bit much. As were the Barça Puta signs, which are now reportedly the subject of a complaint to Uefa.

But it was a wild and all‑consuming spectacle, annihilating every other impulse in your head to a green thought in a green shade. And in the process capturing the profound sense of cognitive dissonance in the city.

Just walking around the streets of central Paris earlier in the day generated its own mild whiplash. On the face of it this was still the same city of flâneurs and tourists and everyday bistro business, and not, repeat not, a civil war drama or some kind of emergent military caliphate.

But the sense of dread was also tangible. You still got the occasional crunch of glass underfoot. Here and there charred bikes and scooters had been politely piled to one side. Even close to the Parc, away from the main hotspots, shopkeepers could be seen emerging warily into their doorways at the sound of a police siren.

By Tuesday night, a total of 2,513 people had been taken into police custody after the events of the weekend. At least 420 rental bikes had been destroyed or maimed. Liberated high-value retail goods were already being sold on Vinted, a commendable show of entrepreneurial energy from the maligned younger generation.

Not that you’d suspect these two worlds had any kind of overlap, lost in the booming triumphalism around PSG’s 4–2 win, one of the most beautifully luminous football matches you’re ever likely to see. Or indeed reading the coverage of an event that seems to have been treated in France like its own clear, clean space, floating above the flames, all the chat about the brilliance of Monsieur Dynamite, Désiré Doué, the wonderful television audiences on Canal+ TV, the whole of European football “kneeling before the machine” (are we sure this isn’t to do with anarchic rejection of social control?). “Paris est insubmersible!” was the headline in Le Parisien. But is it?

Remember when football used to reflect the world? Or just provide clues, a commentary, some kind of metaphor? This is no longer the case. At a time when even the most thunderous of commentators is struggling to summarise all this in suitably trite and damning terms, it was hard to avoid the feeling Paris Saint-Germain, the pet power football project of the state of Qatar, expresses this dualism perfectly.

Sometimes you just have to let football tell you things. “People who want to cause trouble in the city centres next Saturday will be prevented,” Emmanuel Macron announced on Tuesday evening, speaking, as it happens, from Doha, where he met Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad al-Thani, ultimate owner of PSG.

Macron is said to be considering deploying the French military to keep the peace on the streets before the next planned protests, although even here he may have to ask Qatar’s permission if he wants to deploy any of the French troops currently deployed working its missile systems, or embedded in the Doha security services.

The key event Macron is tooling up for is this Saturday night, 24 October, the date the gilets jaunes social media feed has set for an even greater swell of disorder. And again this will be football adjacent, with PSG playing Lyon at home on Sunday, a top‑of‑the-table capital‑city clash played out against whatever wreckage is left, however many thousands of Dot bikes are savaged, whatever guerrilla skirmishes are still playing out in the Marais.

What does this really have to do with football and the Ici C’est Paris brand appropriated by a state outreach project? Well, what are these demonstrations anyway? There have been many theories put forward as to what exactly is happening here, what is the spark, the kindling, the fire behind this disorder. School protests were the original line. Others have now roped in some kind of nebulous omni-cause, Instagram-activism, perhaps even a barbarian takeover, the decline of the west, savagery at the gates, of the type Donald Trump pretends to see.

Do you need a single reason? Take a look around. Modern life is pretty alarming on a minute‑to‑minute basis. Disaffection, alienation, a sense of being transformed in real time into a passive economic consumer unit, a set of eyeballs, an avatar of consumption. French youth unemployment is close to 20%. Robots nobody wants are being lined up to take jobs that don’t exist. Why don’t these young people want to join a youth club? Why don’t they sign up for the Liberal Democrats? Why are they enjoying empowered disorder? Maybe because nothing is yours around here, not even the things thrust into your brain as digital distractions. So why not grab something when you can? Debord would have called this “a vicious circle of isolation”, lonely crowd, technologies based on isolation.

On the other hand, what is PSG in Paris, other than the perfect expression of this? It often seems to surprise people that celebrations of PSG victories end up with riots and burnt-out cars. But Paris is both a beautiful and deeply dysfunctional city, a place of make‑believe and deprivation, where the centre is a perfect, performative relic, the suburbs shunted out to the ring roads, a life lived inside a box, noses pressed up against the glass, peering through your binoculars at the city of someone else’s love.

Paris is the divide between overclass and churn expressed in a single city. It’s everything you might have had but never will. And PSG represent this like no other entity, a football club owned as an act of outreach by a staggeringly rich inherited monarchy, weaponised passion, borrowed culture, engineered spectacle, deeply cynical but also deeply seductive.

Why are the Qataris splurging hundreds of millions into this project? Because they just really like high-pressing positional football? Qatar is the indentured labour state that employs Colin Kaepernick to promote its outreach projects. It will hymn its connections to the footballers of the banlieue, which is true and real and valuable, but not because of anything this political project wants or needs other than public relations.

It is in its own way a perfect human lab experiment, synthesised beauty, collectivism, with a cold, cold hand at its heart. And this is why there weren’t two versions of Paris in evidence on Tuesday night, just the same one, the dark side and the shiny side, an engine of the same divisions, the same kind of violence, right down to the sight of kids in PSG training gear dodging the police or taunting the barricades at the Place de la République.

And now we have Sunday night to come, more Ici C’est Paris, more PASSION. What kind of background will it be played out against? Does it matter under the lights? Will anyone notice?

Le Monde has described these events as a very basic test of the republic, its ability to bend, to remain essentially itself, the state’s willingness to pit itself against its own people. Football, as ever, holds these answers already. PSG’s Qatari owners have spent the past 15 years flaunting this city like a global luxury brand, extending its reach, creating its own ideal of conspicuous consumption, spectacle, triumph, son et lumière, bread and roses. As ever, if you want to know how and why, football has the answers.

#601. How big is Russia's threat against Germany?

Experts believe that, so far, Germany has been ill-prepared — both militarily and mentally — for Russian provocations. They also warn against the idea that ending support for Ukraine would make Germany safer.

Warnings keep mounting about Russia's threat to Germany. On October 12, Sinan Selen, president of Germany's domestic intelligence agency, the BfV, said that Russia's covert attacks and destabilization efforts against Germany and other European countries are "not a temporary development but a long-term hybrid attack."

Even if the war in Ukraine were to end, said Selen, the Russian threat would not disappear. "For Russia, this is about completely redrawing the map of Europe and redefining its own role within Europe and in global politics."

He added that Russia's aggression would continue to be felt not only in Ukraine, but also in the Baltic states, in Poland and in Germany.

German Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt has described the threat situation as one in which "our country is not at war, but we are the daily target of hybrid warfare." Russia's goal, he said, is not to defeat Germany militarily "but to subdue us politically and socially."

Security experts, however, are not so sure that Russia is only interested in exercising political and social influence. Last year, political scientist Carlo Masala told the political talk show Maischberger that "Russia is preparing for a major war." And he didn't mean just a cyberwar. Inspector General Carsten Breuer, the Bundeswehr's highest-ranking officer, agreed.

For now, though, Russia remains tied up in its war against Ukraine. According to NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, there is no immediate military threat to NATO territory. But according to military experts, European intelligence agencies, and the German Defense Ministry, Russia will have the personnel and equipment to wage a conventional war against NATO by 2029. The Russian defense industry is running at full speed, and Moscow plans to expand its armed forces to 1.5 million soldiers.

At the moment, Russia has other objectives, believes Stefan Meister, an expert on Eastern Europe at the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP): "I don't think Russia has any interest in a direct military conflict with NATO right now. It wants to test NATO and also display its capabilities," he tells DW.

An example of Russia's hybrid war might be the failed attack with an explosive-laden drone against a Ukrainian transport aircraft at Leipzig-Halle Airport in August. Soon after, the German government stated that it was certain Russia was behind the attack.

Security expert Nico Lange told DW at the time that Germany was very lucky the explosives did not detonate: "Honestly, it was pure coincidence and not the result of German defensive measures."

As for why Germany was targeted specifically, Lange said: "I believe that Germany has always been a primary target of Russian influence and hybrid warfare. Russia believes that Germany is the key to European decision-making. Germany is now leading the way in supporting Ukraine."

Moscow swiftly responded to the German government's accusation with an angry denial. Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov said it was "the beginning of a real war," though there was no immediate further escalation.

Nevertheless, there is always the risk that things could spiral out of control following a Russian provocation and subsequent response, according to Stefan Meister: "Accidentally shooting down an aircraft that they were trying to intimidate would trigger a counter-reaction. But I don't believe that Russia is currently aiming for a direct war but rather for provocations, polarization, and sowing uncertainty among the population."

Not everyone shares the assessment that Russia poses a threat to Germany — some believe that the threat can be averted.

Despite the war in Ukraine, politicians from the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the populist left-wing Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) want to improve relations with Russia, resume Russian energy supplies and end support for Ukraine.

AfD co-leader Tino Chrupalla said in early September, "People are fed up with this escalation against Russia, with the billions being squandered there on a system that is actually funded by German taxpayers' money."

The message appears to have resonated among the population, especially in eastern Germany. The AfD saw strong gains in all three state elections in September. In Saxony-Anhalt, it became the strongest party by far and may even run the government there soon, which would be the first time that a far-right party has held power in Germany since the end of WWII.

The CDU's security policy expert Roderich Kiesewetter disputed the claim made by the AfD and BSW that ending aid to Ukraine would mean greater security and more money for German schools and infrastructure: "Spending on education, social policy, and infrastructure is only possible if we live in freedom and democracy — and, above all, in peace and security. Anyone who believes that we'll have more money for social programs if we stop supporting Ukraine and let Russia have its way is either gravely mistaken or is making themselves a tool of Russian propaganda."

The perceived security threat has led the German military, the Bundeswehr, to fast-track significant modernization through substantial investment. But it is struggling to find enough recruits for its planned expansion. Many young Germans have taken to the streets to protest against plans to reintroduce conscription. Defense Minister Boris Pistorius faced criticism from inside his own party, the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD), for saying that Germany must become "capable of waging war." Many found the statement to be too militaristic.

Is Germany prepared for a military threat? "Not at all. Germany is not well prepared militarily or in the civilian sector, and the population isn't mentally prepared for it either," said Meister. "Just look at the demonstrations against conscription and the military that continue to take place, even though Germany needs a draft. For decades, we've grown up in this culture of pacifism, in a comfort zone. I believe there is very limited societal resilience, but above all, we are not at all mentally prepared for war."

Chancellor Friedrich Merz and his conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) are under increasing political pressure — especially from the far right — to scale back support for Ukraine. But Merz never tires of emphasizing that Ukraine will continue to receive support under his chancellorship, calling it a matter of Germany's security. He recently visited Kyiv once again and, together with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, surveyed the destruction caused by Russia.

But this also means that the threat from Russia will continue and even intensify, says Stefan Meister: "I believe we will see a massive increase in attacks such as the one in Leipzig, of provocations involving drones. We will see attacks on infrastructures and possibly also on specific individuals within the military-industrial complex. I believe this is the real, concrete danger facing Germany in the coming months."
