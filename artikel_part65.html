We'll have to wait and see how this show ultimately takes root (see what I did there?), but the possibilities are endless for our first introduction to a new live-action Poison Ivy.

BENGALURU, INDIA, October 22, 2026 - eMudhra Limited (BSE: 543533 | NSE: EMUDHRA), a global digital trust company, today announced that its Board has decided to voluntarily withdraw from the global SSL/TLS certificate business.

Mr. V. Srinivasan, Chairman, eMudhra Limited referred to the recent incident relating to its global SSL/TLS certificate and indicated that eMudhra handled the recent incident diligently, revoking the affected CAs within 14 hours of discovery and disclosing promptly. There were no end entity certificates issued out of the affected CA’s. Its infrastructure was not compromised and no data was breached. All physical and logical security controls remained intact.

He added that global SSL/TLS is a small part of eMudhra’s business contributing to about 1% of the global revenue. Due to increasing data sovereignty & compliance requirements, complexity and cost associated with this business is increasing over the years. This, paired with the fact that some of the large entities issue the SSL/TLS free of cost, the business potential and RoI of this line of business also has considerably reduced for eMudhra when measured against our cumulative revenue base.

However, the level of social media amplification around this incident has brought scrutiny that is out of proportion to SSL/TLS’s place in our business. In view of this, after detailed deliberations our board has concluded that withdrawing from global SSL/TLS is the right decision for eMudhra and its shareholders. For many certifying authorities, SSL/TLS is the whole business. For eMudhra it is one part of a much broader digital solutions portfolio. We will put our energy where we create the most value for customers with Sharpened Focus on Core Enterprise Solutions and trust services other than SSL/TLS.

Our G1 RSA roots will continue to serve customers until this transition is complete as we work with browser programs and our customers to enable this transition. eMudhra will reach out to affected customers to replace any certificate free of cost from partners through the transition. Revocation and certificate status services will keep running until every issued certificate has expired or been replaced. eMudhra will complete its remaining commitments to the browser root programs, including a full root-cause report.

eMudhra’s global SSL/TLS infrastructure runs separately from its other operations. The company’s Global digital signature certificate (DSC), eSign, and other signing services, including those in India, its enterprise PKI Solutions (emCA, CertiNext platforms), emSigner eSignature Suite, and its identity and authentication solutions (SecurePass, emAS) are not affected and continue to operate as normal.

If you have personal conversations with ChatGPT that you don't want others to see, you can now add an extra layer of security for those specific chats. The new Locked Chats feature allows you to hide sensitive conversations behind a four-digit account PIN (or biometric authentication on mobile), meaning others with access to your device or screen can't read them.

Locked chats are retained in your chat history—unlike temporary chats, which are private conversations that aren't saved by default—so you can return to or continue them at any point. However, once locked, they are hidden from your regular chat history, pinned chats, and regular conversation search. To access them, you have to go to Projects > Locked chats and enter your PIN or device unlock method (such as Face ID or Touch ID).

To start a locked chat for the first time, open an existing conversation that you want to keep private and open the options menu. Select Lock chat > Lock chat and create or enter your account PIN. This moves the conversation to your locked chats project. You can repeat this with any existing conversation or start a locked chat from scratch under Projects > Locked Chats by entering your PIN or authentication method and typing a message to launch the chat. When you're done, select Lock now to end access to locked chats and hide them from the sidebar.

Note that if you don't already have an account PIN set up, you should be prompted to do so the first time you attempt to lock a chat. Select Create a PIN, enter your four-digit code, and enter it again to confirm. You can also enable biometric authentication in Account PIN settings. If you reset your account PIN at any point, all locked chats are permanently deleted and cannot be recovered.

Locked chats are available on ChatGPT Free, Go, Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise, and Edu plans on both mobile and desktop apps. The feature is not available for ChatGPT for Healthcare. There are other limitations, too: For example, temporary, archived, and group chats can't be locked, nor can those with shared links, scheduled and background tasks, or meeting recordings. OpenAI says locked chats are rolling out gradually, so they may not be available to all users yet.

While locked chats do add a layer of privacy in terms of how conversations appear on your device, they are not suddenly encrypted or otherwise protected from being retained or used by OpenAI. As the support page notes, locked chats are not encrypted end to end or placed in a separate encrypted vault, and locking a chat does not affect other privacy settings, such as Improve the model for everyone (which can be turned off in Settings > Data controls) or remove information ChatGPT already knows about you.

#3193. Clayface Rotten Tomatoes Score: How Does It Compare To Superman And Supergirl?

James Gunn's crowd-pleaser "Superman" was a promising way to kickstart the burgeoning DC Universe, and the recently concluded HBO series "Lanterns" has proved similarly successful. "Supergirl," on the other hand, was a bit of a hiccup, to say the least. Now, we have "Clayface," a body horror origin story for a lesser-known Batman villain. Surprisingly, given the subject matter and a distinct lack of hype leading up to the film's release, "Clayface" has debuted to decent reviews and has been significantly more well received than "Supergirl."

The film isn't going to be the kind of critical triumph that establishes James Gunn and his DC Studios co-head Peter Safran as purveyors of premium comic-book based entertainment that can hold its own against the most celebrated dramas Hollywood has to offer. But it has managed to nudge the Tomatometer to a respectable 73% based on a healthy collection of 75 reviews. The "Clayface" critic score is just two percentage points shy of a "certified fresh" rating as of this writing, but that could still change. What's more, it's enough to prevent DC's horror flick from earning the dreaded green splat, which is more than can be said for "Supergirl."

Though it's arguably one of 2026's most underrated movies, the Milly Alcock-led comic book outing only managed a comparatively miserable 52% on Rotten Tomatoes. It also made just $126.5 million on a budget of between $170 to $186 million. All in all, then, it was the DC Universe's first significant failure, and thus far, "Clayface" has at least managed to garner more impressive reviews. It hasn't, however, managed to outdo Superman himself.

Though the animated series "Creature Commandos" preceded it, "Superman" was the first major DC Universe release — and it was a hit, earning $618.7 million on a $225 million budget and an 83% "certified fresh" critic score on Rotten Tomatoes. James Gunn's take on the DC hero was a major change from the gloomy days of the DC Extended Universe, which had been infused with Zack Snyder's dreary sensibility ever since 2013's "Man of Steel." By introducing us to David Corenswet's lovably goofy Supes in a bright movie that wasn't afraid to embrace hope and optimism, Gunn reinvigorated the Man of Steel for a new generation. Critics seemed to like it, too.

Given that, "Clayface" was always going to struggle to outdo Gunn's movie. For one thing, it's an R-rated body horror from master of the genre Mike Flanagan and "Speak No Evil" director James Watkins. That immediately limits its appeal among the masses. But the initial reactions to "Clayface" were positive, and now the Rotten Tomatoes score similarly bodes well.

While /Film's Chris Evangelista called "Clayface" a shapeless villain spin-off, many other critics were considerably more impressed with the film. Zaki Hasan of the San Francisco Chronicle zeroed in on what might be the most important of the film for Gunn and Peter Safran, writing, "As proof of concept for a DC Universe that can accommodate stories beyond the usual superhero spectacle, it largely works." From the very beginning, Gunn has made clear that the DC Universe would accommodate myriad tones rather than sticking to a house style à la the Marvel Cinematic Universe. "Clayface" is the first major test of that flexibility, and according to Hasan, Gunn and company have passed.

More than simply proving James Gunn and Peter Safran's DC Universe can accommodate differing filmmaking styles, "Clayface" managed to impress several critics on its own terms. The Independent's Clarisse Loughrey praised lead actor Tom Rhys Harries for doing "much of the heavy lifting" and for projecting an air of "wounded intensity" that wasn't even remotely dulled by the many layers of prosthetics involved. Robbie Collin of The Daily Telegraph, meanwhile, wrote about "how much fun it is to soak up a film like this on what feels like its creators' largely unhindered terms," suggesting he didn't get the same sense of "practiced pastiche" that Zaki Hasan identified in his review.

On the other side of things, many critics were not all that taken with "Clayface." USA Today's Brian Truitt criticized what he saw as "a severe lack of character development," while David Fear of Rolling Stone went slightly further in comparing "Clayface" to "eating a handful of Play-Doh," adding, "and twice as flavorless."

Clearly, then, "Clayface" isn't enjoying unanimous praise. But it is generally faring better than "Supergirl," which Adam Graham of the Detroit News called "a super disappointment" and Robbie Collin (who liked "Clayface) called "an endless orangey-grey rehash of scenes from 'Mad Max' and 'Star Wars.'" For now, however, "Superman" remains the DC Universe's most critically and commercially successful movie (though "Clayface" could be the box office hit DC needs after "Supergirl"), and doesn't look to be in any danger of being toppled in that regard.

#3160. Rule 30: one black cell

Start with a single black square. Apply one tiny rule, row after row. What grows is part orderly, part wild, and nobody can yet prove whether the column running straight down its middle ever repeats.

Picture a row of squares, each black or white. To make the next row, every square looks at three squares above it: the one directly above, and that one's left and right neighbours. Those three colours can come in eight combinations, and the rule simply says, for each combination, what colour the new square is.

Write the eight answers as a binary number and you get 00011110, which is 30. That is the whole of Rule 30. Below, the rule is applied one square at a time: the three boxed squares decide the new one, and the matching entry of the rule lights up.

In the early 1980s Stephen Wolfram set out to look systematically at the simplest possible programs of this kind. There are only 256 of them. Most do something dull: they die out, repeat, or make neat nested patterns. Rule 30 does not. Its left side settles into regular stripes, but its right side never settles at all, and its centre column passes the standard tests of randomness.

That observation, that a very simple rule can produce behaviour which looks random, became a cornerstone of Wolfram's A New Kind of Science (2002). For many years Rule 30 generated Mathematica's random integers. Its pattern clads the walls of Cambridge North railway station. And it was the subject of a challenge.

On that day Wolfram offered $30,000 in prizes, $10,000 for each of three questions about the centre column. Seven years on, all three are open. Read the column as a coin, black for heads and white for tails:

The coin is not random: every toss is fixed by the rule. So each answer is simply yes or no, and only a proof can settle it. Wolfram's team has computed a billion cells of the column. They look perfectly random, and that proves nothing.

Look again at the pyramid. Its left side is striped and its right side is wild, but neither is quite what it seems. Both edges carry order with an exact shape, and both shapes were found on 9 October 2026 by looking hard at the picture.

Read the pyramid in lines parallel to its left edge, its diagonals. The first few diagonals settle into short repeating patterns, and once a run of them repeats, it repeats for ever, because those diagonals depend only on one another. So at every row there is an exact count of diagonals that have reached their eternal stripes. Below, they are shaded, and the magenta line is the edge of the shaded band: the exact place where order ends.

It does not run down the side of the pyramid. It starts near the centre at the very top and leans out at about a quarter of the speed of the pyramid's own edge, 0.244 squares per row on average. That is close to the speed, about 0.246, at which a change made on the right spreads leftwards through a random row; whether the two are exactly equal is not known. The chart follows the edge to row 524,288: about that straight line it wanders like a random walk.

Gareth noticed this by eye: along the wild right edge, the white triangles that touch it arrive at perfectly even spacing, and only their sizes differ. It is exactly true. At every second row a white triangle starts one square inside the edge, and its width depends only on how many times 2 divides the row number, like the marks on a ruler. Rows 2, 6, 10, 14 and so on (twice an odd number) get the smallest triangle, rows 4, 12, 20 the next size up, rows 8, 24, 40 the next, and so on.

The reason is that every diagonal parallel to the right edge repeats with a period that is a power of 2. At a row that is a multiple of a diagonal's period, the diagonal is white, as it was at the start; the triangle ends at the first diagonal whose period does not divide the row. This is proved for all time.

Neither kind of order reaches the prize column. At row t the centre column sits on the t-th diagonal from either edge, ever deeper, where the periods have grown too long to see: the 54th diagonal from the right edge repeats only after 4,194,304 rows. Order at the edges says nothing yet about the middle, and that is why the problems are hard.

Now to the prize column itself. Problem 1 asks whether it can ever repeat. So assume it does, in the simplest way it could: white, black, white, black, for ever. Hold the centre to that beat, like a wall, and run Rule 30 on the squares to its right from a random start. The column right next to the wall does something remarkable. It falls into a fixed loop of 56 steps, a wheel, and stays on it until something from further right knocks it off, after which it settles back at a new position on the wheel.

At the top is the triangle Rule 30 draws from one cell, with the prize column down its middle and the off-centre region where the chaos lives. It is the top half of a square standing on its corner. Below is the wheel, which is really two clocks at once, of 8 and of 7 positions. Beside it runs the wall experiment that drives it. Rule 30 has a useful property: if you know the centre column and the column beside it, every square to the left is forced, one after another. So a repeating centre forces the whole left side. The prize asks about one black square; we ask the same of every finite start, a pattern with only finitely many black squares, which includes it. For any of those the forced left side would have to turn white for good beyond some depth. A counterexample to Problem 1 is exactly that: a white edge that never breaks.

The same property works the other way round. Choose anything for the centre to say, the primes, the Fibonacci numbers, the digits of π in binary, your own name, and build the start one square at a time, leftwards: the square t places left of the centre decides the centre at step t outright, without disturbing the steps before. So every sequence can be made. The catch is that the start never ends; each step needs one more square. Cut it to a finite start and the sequence breaks, just as the first square it lacks reaches the centre. Even the best of every start of 18 squares follows the primes for only 18 steps, and π for 19. A finite start buys about one step per square, whatever it is asked to say, unless the sequence is one Rule 30 makes by itself. Problem 1 asks whether any repeating pattern is one of those.

Whatever the pattern, after a short stretch (the dashed line) the left side freezes into a crystal: a wall built from one brick, repeating across as well as down. The brick is never blank, so the left side can never turn white. That is Jen's theorem of 1990. Across every pattern of even length up to 20, 1,398,100 of them, only 20 different bricks ever appear.

So a counterexample's second column can never repeat, and its left side has no crystal to settle into. The picture below shows how that left side is built. That makes the left side a pile of Sierpinski triangles. Start with one black square, and fill each row below it by a far simpler rule than Rule 30: a square is black when exactly one of the two squares above it, straight up and up-right, is black (this comparison is the Gray code, which chapter 7 comes back to). The single dot grows into a triangle made of three half-size copies of itself, each made of three smaller copies, and so on for ever: the Sierpinski triangle, which is also the pattern of the odd numbers in Pascal's triangle. Give the same rule several dots and each grows its own triangle, but where two triangles overlap they cancel, so a square ends up black exactly when an odd number of triangles cover it.

This is not special to the left side, nor to running sideways. Read the ordinary way, down the page, Rule 30 is the same kind of rule: do what Rule 60 does, black when the square up-left and the square above differ, then flip the square under every kick, a white square with a black one to its right. Rule 60 alone grows one black square into a Sierpinski triangle, and each kick starts another, so every square of the whole pyramid, the striped side and the wild side alike, is an odd overlap of Sierpinski triangles. About a quarter of all squares are kicks on both sides; what differs is only how they are arranged, in regular stripes on the left and scattered on the right. All the triangles lean the same way, down and to the right, because Rule 30 uses its left neighbour exactly and its right neighbour only through the kick, which is also why only the left side can be built sideways.

It drags a disturbance along with it, and that disturbance pulls on the squares that must stay white with a beat: odd, odd, even, odd, odd, even, the pattern of the Fibonacci numbers 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. Together it makes a counterexample implausible, but it is not a proof. Everything comes down to one statement nobody has a method for. Holding the centre to its beat has to cost real information: the number of starting patterns that keep the beat should fall off exponentially with time. We can measure that fall-off, and it is close to one bit per step. The part of the cost paid by the start's left side is proved. The part paid by its right side is the open problem.

One construction shows how close the edge of possibility is. A row of 84 squares, repeated end to end for ever, comes back to itself every six steps. Its centre ticks white, black, white, black for good, and the column beside it plays the short loop over and over. It is no counterexample, because it has infinitely many black squares, but it is a real Rule 30 pattern doing exactly what a counterexample would have to do.

Watched as it runs, the ring is simpler than it looks. Each step of Rule 30 turns the whole ring by 14 of its 84 squares, one sixth of a turn, which is why it comes back every six steps. Its whole infinite history is one brick, 14 squares wide and 6 rows tall with 43 black squares, laid like bricks in a wall, each column of bricks one row lower than the one before. It is one of the 20 crystals above.

Behind it is a general fact we proved. Rule 30 can be cut into eight jigsaw pieces, one for each case of the rule, and its histories are exactly the ways of fitting them together. A row that comes back moved further than a change could have travelled in the time, as this ring does, must repeat across as well, so its history is a wall of one brick. The same pieces make walls like this one, so Problem 1 is not a question about the pieces: it asks whether the one picture grown from a single black piece can ever repeat down its middle.

Take any whole number. If it is even, halve it; if it is odd, triple it, add one and halve it. Repeat. Lothar Collatz asked in 1937 whether every start eventually falls to 1. Computers have checked every start below 271, about 2.4 × 1021; nobody can prove it; and a prize of ¥120 million (Bakuage, 2021) waits for a proof or a counterexample.

It looks like another world, but in binary it is the same machine as Rule 30. A number written in binary is a row of black and white squares, and multiplying it by 3/2, the heart of Collatz's odd step (which is 3n/2 plus a half), works on that row much as Rule 30 does. Both start from one simple step, the Gray code, and add a correction.

The Gray code is a way of counting in binary in which each number differs from the one before in a single digit: 000, 001, 011, 010, 110, 111, 101, 100. It is named after Frank Gray of Bell Labs, and it is used wherever a reading must never jump, as in a rotary encoder. Its recipe is one comparison: write the number in binary and compare each digit with the one on its left, black where they differ. Repeat that step row after row from a single black square and the Sierpinski triangle of chapter 5 appears. Counting in Gray code changes digit number 0, 1, 0, 2, 0, 1, 0, 3, ... at steps 1, 2, 3, 4, ...: the digit is how many times 2 divides the step, which is the edge ruler of chapter 3 again, so the ruler's rhythm, and its sound, is the Gray code counting. Below, one number goes through three rules side by side.

So the two problems are two ways of getting the same simple rule slightly wrong, and both unroll into overlapping Sierpinski triangles, one hanging from each correction. Here both run from a single black square: Rule 30, and the powers of 3, which are what repeated multiplication by 3/2 does to a single 1 (the halving only shifts the row).

The likeness reaches the edges too. Every second row of the powers of 3 has a run of white squares just inside its black edge, and its length depends only on how many times 2 divides the row number: the same kind of ruler that Gareth spotted on Rule 30's edge. For the powers of 3 it is a classical theorem, the "lifting the exponent" lemma, and the ruler is perfectly regular, one square longer for each doubling. Rule 30's grows by about two and a half squares for each doubling, irregularly; that rate is measured, not proved. In both pictures every diagonal parallel to the right edge repeats with a period that is a power of 2, and in both the middle is where nobody can prove anything.

The questions are the same shape as well. In both, a finite start, a number's finitely many digits or a row's finitely many black squares, is fed to a rule that can be run backwards, and the question is whether what comes out beyond the part the start controls behaves like coin tosses. One exact statement holds word for word in both: a stretch of the output can repeat only if it is no longer than the current state is large, counted in digits or in squares (on the Collatz side this was already known). And the method that settles the easy cases stops at the same place in both, and in a third famous problem, Mahler's 3/2 problem of 1968. It shows that no counterexample exists when the constrained part can behave in only finitely many ways, and says nothing once it can behave in infinitely many, which is exactly where Rule 30's white-black case and Mahler's question sit.

So each side holds a tool the other lacks. Collatz has arithmetic: remainders modulo powers of 3, and linear forms in logarithms, which bound how close a power of 3 can come to a power of 2. Rule 30 has locality: its corrections are single squares, which is what made the exact rules about edge events possible. A proof on either side would show what a proof on the other has to replace. Neither side has one yet. The details, with every claim's source, are in the Collatz part of the record.

#3153. Tom Skerritt, Alien And Top Gun Actor, Dead At 93

Tom Skerritt, a dashingly handsome actor who smoothly moved between leading roles and supporting parts, and whose surprise exit in Ridley Scott's "Alien" still has moviegoers gasping, has died at the age of 93. His "heartbroken" family announced that the "M*A*S*H," "Top Gun," and "A River Runs Through It" actor died of natural causes.

In a statement released to the press, they said, "While the world knew Tom through his remarkable acting career, we knew him for his humor, curiosity, wisdom, generosity and the love he brought into our lives. He cared deeply about imagination, creativity, storytelling, service, veterans, the natural world and helping others find and share their own voices."

Born and raised in Detroit, Michigan, Skerritt served four years in the United States Air Force before finding his passion for theater at UCLA. He initially leaned toward directing, but demonstrated acting skills that eventually caught the attention of Robert Altman, who cast him as the troublemaking Duke Forrest in the New Hollywood classic "M*A*S*H." Duke was the third wheel to the caddish antics of Hawkeye Pierce (Donald Sutherland) and Trapper John McIntyre (Elliott Gould), and while Skerritt enjoyed playing fun-loving doofuses (he was wonderful as Strawberry in the Cheech and Chong vehicle "Up in Smoke"), he began to gravitate toward characters imbued with sincerity and gravitas. That's when the Skerritt that most moviegoers know emerged: calm, avuncular, and dead sexy.

"Alien" was a formative film for most Gen X-ers. Released two years after "Star Wars," there was a sense that the crew of the Nostromo would band together to eliminate the H.R. Geiger-designed xenomorph. Space was the place for heroism, not horror. So it was striking that Skerritt's Dallas is hesitant, leery and, before he's dispatched, defeated as he stalks the acid-blooded beast. He goes into the bowels of the Nostromo with some degree of expertise, but every step of the way you know he's overmatched.

Skerritt tried his hand at Charles Bronson vigilantism with "Fighting Back," but he was better suited to mentor roles in "SpaceCamp" and "Top Gun." One major exception was his portrayal of wealthy mark Darryl Cooper, who gets seduced by Drew Barrymore's title character in Katt Shea's sex-infused thriller "Poison Ivy." This coincided with his run as a Guess jeans model, so it wasn't bizarre to see Skerritt cavorting with a young screen partner. At least Shea made the coupling feel icky, and made it clear that Barrymore's character was in control.

Still, Skerritt's Everest was "A River Runs Through It," where he played a patient Presbyterian minister who lets his children become the men they were destined to be. It's lighthearted at times, then brutally tragic. I often think of Skerritt modestly walking off with his fly-fishing haul, and then his anguished sermon in the wake of his son's death. The final line of that film is "I am haunted by waters." I'm haunted by Tom Skerritt.
