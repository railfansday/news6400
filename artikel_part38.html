#4550. PancakeSwap Opens Vote To Cut CAKE Emissions By Around 69%

TL;DR: PancakeSwap’s ‘The Flip’ vote would boost liquidity providers‘ share of certain trading fees to 88% while cutting daily CAKE emissions to 7,000.

PancakeSwap holders are being asked to decide how much value should go to liquidity providers and how much new CAKE the protocol should continue issuing. Voting has opened on ‘The Flip’, a proposal first outlined to the community earlier in October.

Under the plan, liquidity providers on v3 and Infinity pools would keep 88% of swap fees, up from roughly two-thirds on many pools. The protocol’s remaining 12% fee share would go to its treasury. V2’s existing fee setup would not be changed by this part of the proposal.

The other major adjustment is on the emissions side: effective daily CAKE issuance would fall from 22,500 to 7,000, a reduction of approximately 69%. The remaining issuance is intended for PancakeSwap’s ecosystem growth fund, known as the Pantry.

Supporters will argue that paying liquidity more generously can improve trading depth and that cutting new token issuance may reduce dilution. The trade-off is how the treasury finances development and incentives over time. Nothing is final until the vote passes and code changes are rolled out. The proposed implementation would take place in phases from November, rather than changing every supported chain overnight.

#4545. Fhenix Brings Encrypted Smart Contract Computing To Ethereum And Arbitrum

TL;DR: Fhenix says CoFHE is available on Ethereum and Arbitrum mainnets, giving approved applications a way to perform computations involving encrypted data.

A major weakness of public blockchain applications is that transparency is often the default, even where participants would prefer to keep commercial details private. Fhenix is trying to address that with CoFHE, its fully homomorphic encryption-powered computation system.

The company’s public developer information now describes CoFHE as live on Ethereum and Arbitrum mainnet. Applications can use its coprocessor to work with encrypted inputs instead of revealing every sensitive value to the wider network. That is relevant to private trading, financial positions and other cases where full public disclosure can be a drawback.

The important limitation is access. Fhenix says its early mainnet deployment uses an allowlist for contracts submitting CoFHE workloads, while open testnets remain available for experimentation. Its technical materials also distinguish research throughput measurements from results achieved in live production.

The development doesn’t magically make all Ethereum transactions private. It provides an additional tool to developers prepared to adopt encrypted computation and work within the initial deployment restrictions. If it proves practical under real workloads, confidential finance could move closer to established smart contract ecosystems rather than demanding a wholly separate chain.

Monero (XMR) is built so that an outside observer cannot see who paid whom, or how much. Every transaction hides its sender, its receiver, and its amount by default, and there is no way to send a transparent Monero payment by mistake. That is why the question “Is Monero traceable?” usually gets a short answer: not from the chain alone.

The longer answer is changing. Monero’s developers are preparing a major privacy upgrade, known as FCMP++, which would replace the ring signatures that hide every spend today. It is not live yet, and it has no official fork date. Here is what it would change, what research says about Monero’s privacy now, and what users can do in the meantime.

HiddenSwap (hiddenswap.com) is a no-KYC, anonymous crypto exchange for crypto-to-crypto swaps: no account, no email, and no ID needed to swap. It follows the upgrade on a public FCMP++ status tracker, with sources for every entry.

Monero uses three tools, each covering one part of a payment.

Open a recent Monero transaction on a block explorer and you will see the 16 ring members of each input, one-time output addresses, and amounts shown as unknown. None of it names a sender or a receiver.

Monero’s privacy has been tested in public, and early studies found real weaknesses. A 2017 study by Kumar and colleagues could identify the real output being spent in 87% of the cases it looked at. A 2018 paper by Möser and colleagues found that many early inputs had no decoys at all, which let researchers rule out spent coins in other rings, and that the newest ring member was usually the real one.

Most of those problems were fixed by the protocol’s own changes. A minimum ring size ended spends without decoys, RingCT inputs turned out to be largely immune to the “chain reaction” the 2018 paper described, and wallets now pick decoys using the same spending-time curve that paper measured. The 2018 study’s warning was mainly about transactions made before February 2017.

Research on today’s rings of 16 continues. One known weak point is a pattern where you send to and receive from the same party again and again; if that party wants to follow your funds, it may be able to make a statistical guess. The fix on your side is simple: do not move coins back and forth through the same counterparty.

FCMP++ stands for Full-Chain Membership Proofs, combined with spend authorisation and linkability. Instead of hiding a spend among 16 ring members, a transaction would prove that it spends one of all the spendable outputs on the chain, which the developers put at more than 150 million.

A new addressing protocol called Carrot ships with FCMP++. Addresses from new Carrot wallets look the same as old ones, and Carrot adds protection against the Janus address-linking attack and the so-called burning bug.

The only public timeline is a draft plan by a Monero developer, who calls it “a non-binding working draft.” It pencilled in a first fork-enabled release for October 14 to 21, 2026, a fully featured release with hardware wallet and multisig support in early December, and activation around the first week of March 2027. However, at the time of writing on October 23, getmonero.org has not announced a fork-enabled release. Dates in that plan have moved before and can move again; Monero’s own blog said in May 2026 that a fork date was not set.
