An emotional Ousmane Dembele had barely settled back into his chair at Paris' historic Theatre du Chatelet last September, gold-dipped orb in hand, when campaigns for this year's men's Ballon d'Or prize began to whirr into action.

Entourages roused their candidates, media revelled in the hype. Who deserved the award in 2026? A World Cup year, no less, in which the narrative occasionally became not just which players could lead their country to glory, but how doing so would help them secure football's biggest individual accolade.

"The Ballon d'Or has been discussed every single day," said European football expert Guillem Balague on BBC Sport's Euro Leagues podcast.

"Quite clearly it follows the pattern of society, where it puts a lot of emphasis on the individual - it's harder to understand why a collective works, it becomes very easy to understand why one dribbles or doesn't dribble."

Clamour for the spotlight only increased as the red-carpet ceremony neared.

"You can't underestimate the PR machine that some clubs and some players are going to use," added French football journalist Julien Laurens.

Managers and sporting directors talked up their charges, and questioned why others on their squad did not make the 30-man shortlist.

Deco branded it a "huge scandal" that Barcelona pair Raphinha and Pedri missed out, but backed Lamine Yamal as overwhelming favourite to win it. Not, he says, because he is also a Barca player, but "because I like football".

Star names have appeared frequently in the media. Lamine Yamal himself insisted he will not "beg for anything" - after last year saying he dreamed of "winning many" - but suggested the prize should not merely go to the top goalscorer.

Indeed - since a short spell of national team captains and managers also voting ended in 2015 - the 30-player shortlist drawn up by the publications France Football and L'Equipe has been voted on by one journalist from each of the top 100 Fifa-ranked nations.

"You could argue we are in a more interesting time because for basically 15 years it was Messi v Ronaldo and it certainly feels more open. It is a different play now," adds Kirkham.

"The social noise is deafening, relentless, all-consuming - every fan has an insatiable appetite, every point is riffed on, reacted to, but you also have this domino effect in microcosms of the media on every platform."

Clips go viral - a great goal, magical piece of skill, a dazzling dribble - but so do opinions.

For someone like Three Lions captain Kane, hoping to become the first English winner in 25 years and only the fifth of all time, the prestige is huge - especially with the ceremony held in London to mark the 70th anniversary of Stanley Matthews winning the inaugural prize.

"It would be a massive achievement if it did happen," Kane said on Wednesday, having remained coy on his own chances.

It may be an individual award, but winning is huge for clubs, too.

"This means an incredible impact in terms of popularity, visibility and also financial returns for the club," explains Braida. "Today, with the rise of social media and followers - factors that shape commercial aspects - this is even more true."

Kirkham concludes: "It is a global point - that means more audience and more eyeballs, and that means more money around the world."

#348. Raids at far-right group's offices at European Parliament

Authorities searched the Patriots for Europe's European Parliament offices in a long-running funding inquiry. The group says the charges pertain to its predecessor and there is "no legal continuity" between the two.

The third-largest alliance of parties in the European Parliament, the right-wing nationalist Patriots for Europe (PfE) group, on Wednesday said searches had been carried out at "administrative offices" at the European Parliament buildings in Brussels and Strasbourg.

PfE said the searches appeared to be tied to the long-running probe into alleged misuse of funds by members of the now-disbanded Identity and Democracy (ID) Group.

PfE formed out of the ashes of ID, losing and gaining some members along the way, after the last European Parliament elections in 2024.

The European Public Prosecutor's Office — the independent body that investigates and prosecutes crimes against the EU budget, such as corruption or misuse of EU funds — said it searched premises in France and Belgium.

It said this was part of an ongoing investigation into the use of European Union funds by a former European Parliament group.

EPPO did not say which group was involved and said it would not publish further details for now for fear of compromising the investigation.

PfE, meanwhile, confirmed the activity around its offices but also tried to stress its distance from the case since its rebrand and reformation — even though its constituent political parties are largely the same with the notable exception of the Alternative for Germany (AfD) now being excluded.

"Since this morning, the European Public Prosecutor's Office has been carrying out searches in administrative offices at the European Parliament in Brussels and Strasbourg. It would appear that these searches are linked to the complaint lodged against the Identity and Democracy Group (ID) dissolved in 2024," it wrote in a statement.

"The Patriots for Europe Group emphasizes that there is no legal continuity with the ID Group and that it is not involved in the ongoing investigations," it said.

A French member of the European Parliament for the Rassemblement National or National Rally (RN) party, Jean-Paul Garraud, said that "the offices of staff members of the former Identity and Democracy group" were targeted.

European Parliament auditors concluded last year that ID members had misused at least €4.3 million (roughly $4.8 million) in EU funds during the previous legislative term from 2019 to 2024.

According to their report, the money flowed to companies close to the group and to associations supporting anti-abortion campaigners.

The case was reminiscent of, but separate from, the conviction against RN leader Marine Le Pen, herself a former MEP, that for a time looked set to stifle her bid for the French presidency.

The ID group existed for one term in the European Parliament, from 2019 to 2024. It included France's RN, Germany's AfD, Austria's Freedom Party (FPÖ) and Italy's Lega, among other members.

It fell apart after major internal friction during the 2024 European election campaign, not least because of dissatisfaction within the group with their lead candidate Maximilian Krah of the AfD over a number of issues — from controversial remarks about Nazi Germany through to allegations of his close allies being Russian and Chinese influence, with one of Krah's aides since convicted of spying for China.

RN, FPÖ and Lega are part of the new PfE group, which claims "no legal continuity" to the dissolved ID alliance, but the AfD was not invited to join the PfE.

PfE has 85 seats in the 719-seat European Parliament, making it the third-largest group in the chamber, but a rather distant third behind the center-right European People's Party (184) and the center-left Socialists and Democrats (135).

The European Parliament relies heavily on such groupings, which try to unite political parties from various member states with broadly comparable policy positions and political alignment.

The AfD set up a much smaller splinter far-right grouping called Europe of Sovereign Nations (ESN), with just 27 seats, 15 of them held by the AfD.

#337. Trump, Putin and Xi at one table, without Ukraine. What Moscow wants from the proposal and why Beijing might agree.

Moscow is pushing for a summit with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping to discuss ending the war in Ukraine, without Kyiv or the EU. What does Russia hope to gain, and why might China be willing to take part?

The administration of US President Donald Trump has stepped up diplomatic efforts this autumn to revive stalled negotiations aimed at ending Russia's war in Ukraine.

Washington has increased pressure on Kyiv and its European partners while seeking to draw Moscow back to the negotiating table through a series of concessions, including a deal involving Russian diesel exports to the US and the temporary easing of some sanctions.

Yet the Kremlin appears in no hurry to resume meaningful peace talks. Moscow is ready to hold negotiations with Ukraine at a technical level next month, the Kremlin said after the meeting between US special envoy Steve Witkoff and Russian President Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg on October 21.

Putin agreed to resume the peace talks mediated by Washington after November 10, his aide Yury Ushakov said after the meeting. He added that the next round of negotiations could take place in Abu Dhabi and would focus on "building the security system for Russia and Ukraine after the end of the war."

According to his aide, Putin also shared some thoughts with Witkoff on the possible trilateral summit involving US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping. Ushakov pointed out that Beijing is already preparing the meeting.

Moscow proposed to hold a three-way meeting on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, scheduled for November 18-19 in Shenzhen, China, The Wall Street Journal reported earlier this week. According to WSJ sources, Putin hopes to reach an agreement with Trump and Xi on ending the war in Ukraine on terms favorable to Russia.

Moscow's proposal for the three-way summit involving Russia, the United States and China — but excluding Ukraine and the European Union — offers a glimpse of the negotiating format the Kremlin would prefer. Russia insists that Ukraine must be prepared to make territorial concessions before negotiations can move forward. Putin has also repeatedly stressed that he is willing to negotiate only if Russian interests are taken into account.

According to the WSJ, Putin views the proposed gathering in Shenzhen as a potential new Yalta Conference, referring to the 1945 meeting of the leaders of the United States, Great Britain and the Soviet Union to discuss the defeat of Nazi Germany and the postwar order in Europe.

The newspaper reported that Chinese officials had indicated through diplomatic channels that Beijing would not object to the three-way format if Trump agreed to participate. Trump, whose relations with Kyiv have deteriorated significantly in recent weeks, has also expressed openness to such talks.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy pointed out during his visit in Oslo on October 20 that talks concerning the future of his country will be acceptable only if Ukraine gets a seat at the negotiating table.

Temur Umarov, a researcher at the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center in Berlin who specializes in China-Russia relations, is not surprised by the Kremlin's proposal. He told DW that Moscow wants to feel respected by Washington and treated as a major international power.

The previous US administration was unwilling to discuss questions affecting Ukraine's sovereignty with Russia without Kyiv's participation. Now, Umarov said, the Kremlin believes such negotiations may have become possible.

Moscow apparently hopes that any agreement reached at the proposed meeting would prompt the White House to pressure Ukraine and the EU into accepting its terms. But what role would China actually play?

Umarov believes Beijing would participate largely as a symbolic partner, rather than as an active mediator responsible for the outcome. "Beijing has no intention of taking responsibility for Ukraine's future," he said.

Simply attending the summit would not impose any additional obligations on China. "It is hardly surprising that the Kremlin insists on this format," Umarov explained. Umarov believes China has little direct interest in such negotiations. #321. AI Guardrails Aren’t Stopping Criminal Hackers

As AI adoption spreads among both malicious hackers and defenders, even relatively unsophisticated cybercriminals are incorporating agentic AI into their campaigns and getting around model safeguards, according to new details published on Wednesday about recent successful attacks by four different threat groups.

Researchers from the threat intelligence firm CrowdStrike looked at the AI infrastructure powering attacks perpetrated between May and August by four previously unreported groups—one that operates in Chinese, one in Spanish and English, one in Indonesian, and one in Russian. The former two exhibited sophisticated AI “harnesses,” the software that supports and guides a model to carry out tasks. The latter two showed less technical prowess, researchers found, but were still able to compromise multiple targets. All had left their AI infrastructure openly accessible on web servers, allowing the researchers to analyze their activity and which models they used—largely versions of Anthropic’s Claude as well as versions of the open-weight Deepseek models. All the actors devised ways to allow their campaigns to run autonomously over relatively long periods of time.

“You can leverage different models for different aspects in the operation. Model routing and selection is very important because it’s expensive and a lot of times would be overkill to use newer models,” says Adam Meyers, CrowdStrike's senior vice president of counter adversary work. The researchers observed the attackers using both open- and closed-weight models; in almost all cases, they were using older versions rather than working with frontier models. Meyers notes that doing as much as possible with older models is common in legitimate AI usage, too, as costs mount.

The attackers also used multiple models to take advantage of differences in the guardrails meant to prevent people from using AI to carry out digital attacks.

“The models don't all have equivalent guardrails and protections, even from one company,” Meyers says. He adds about the sophistication of the hacking campaigns overall that “these attacks are not using new exploits or novel vulnerabilities. They’re really just taking advantage of the speed and the volume that you can use AI for in conducting these operations targeting the bulk of security hygiene issues in a lot of these environments—things that were preventable but weren’t prevented.”

The researchers found that all four threat actors circumvented AI safeguards, often by convincing a model that the activity was all legitimate and authorized, often for penetration testing or other security evaluation. One method used by many of the groups involves adding files to an agent’s working directory to try to show that tasks are approved and authorized. The attackers also used traditional jailbreaking methods, crafting prompts and narratives framing their hacking as authorized and benign, and proactively addressing potential questions or concerns the models might raise. The more sophisticated actors were typically more strategic in their world-building, while the less sophisticated actors sometimes resorted to more crude and aggressive prompts that tried to bully the models into accepting that the activity was legitimate.

The Chinese-speaking actor also used what's known as a “run time patch,” in this case a tool known as ClawGod, to add functionality on top of Claude Code and attempt to remove the “CYBER_RISK_INSTRUCTION” from the Claude Code system prompt, an instruction directing Claude Code to only participate in authorized security testing, not abusive or destructive hacking.

The actor that worked in Spanish and English targeted Argentine health care entities and successfully compromised at least 15 organizations. CrowdStrike researchers see evidence that the actor stole credentials and medical records from at least one of these victims. The actor special built a penetration testing harness, the kind an organization would use to test its own infrastructure, to find and fix vulnerabilities. Using models including Claude Opus 4.5, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Deepseek V4 Pro, the group set up a full infrastructure for autonomous attacks involving a “Supervisor” Claude Code agent to direct the steps of an attack and an “Executor” agent to carry them out.

The attacker working in Chinese also built penetration testing harnesses to guide Claude Code in reconnaissance and then autonomously attack once a human had selected a course of action. The researchers found that the actor successfully compromised government targets in both Brazil and Colombia and also targeted private organizations in Brazil and the United States in a range of sectors that include biotech, healthcare, food and beverage, retail, technology, and travel.

According to the researchers, the Chinese-speaking actor primarily relied on Claude Opus 4.8, but also used other models including Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Deepseek Chat. The actor accessed Claude through an AI proxy service based in China but set up multiple fallbacks to other AI tools if Claude was not available through the proxy.

Anthropic did not provide comment ahead of publication, and Deepseek did not immediately respond to WIRED's requests for comment.

The researchers say that both the Russian-speaking actor, which seems to have been focused on financially motivated “e-crime,” and the Indonesian-speaking actor (the least sophisticated of the four) used more basic, publicly available skills and harnesses to carry out their attacks. But both were still successful at compromising targets. This included multiple Indonesian government domains, a Bangladeshi government domain, and some private-sector targets, in the case of the Indonesian-speaking actor.

“What we are seeing is humans are doing some of the initial probing in these situations and then they’re engaging the AI for the hacking at scale,” CrowdStrike’s Meyers says. “I find it interesting that even with agentic attacks with all of these capabilities, we could still see that there’s a human behind it and what they’re up to.”

“Over the course of 90 years, JPL’s first-of-a-kind missions have transformed our understanding of the universe and our place in it,” said Dave Gallagher, JPL’s director since 2025. Protecting humanity is also the goal of the asteroid-hunting NEO Surveyor (Near-Earth Object Surveyor) mission, which, managed by JPL, will hunt for potentially hazardous space rocks that other telescopes can’t find. Stone said that with both Voyagers exploring regions never before visited, “every day is a day of discovery.” The same could be said for JPL.

The judges found that hospitals, distributors and chemists enjoyed large margins on medicines. Analysis by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority revealed a trade mark-up on some cancer drugs averaging 170%, with some extreme examples stretching to more than 700%.

A 30% trade margin cap on the price of 42 drugs has been in place for some years but many others remained off this list. Now, prodded by the supreme court’s findings, the government has imposed the cap on 137 other drugs.

“The measure is expected to reduce medicine prices by up to 70% and help cancer patients save 2,500 crore rupees [£199m] annually, significantly reducing the amount they pay from their own pockets,” a statement from the Department of Pharmaceuticals said.

People on low incomes in India dread illness, as the cost of treatment can mean financial ruin, particularly for cancer patients.

Whenever the Indian Cancer Society holds a free diagnosis camp in Gurgaon where he lives, Krishan Kalra urges local drivers, maids, cooks and tailors to turn up because tests they cannot afford are offered free.

With the cost of cancer drugs ranging in price from 38,000 to 380,000 rupees, many families quietly walk out of the doctor’s consulting room upon hearing the estimated cost of treatment.

Studies by the Public Health Foundation of India show that spending on healthcare pushes about 55 million Indians a year below the poverty line.

Few Indians can afford the premiums for comprehensive health insurance. The government’s nationwide insurance scheme, Ayushman Bharat, covers treatment for some cancers but it provides only 500,000 rupees’ worth of annual cover for a family – the cost of just one dose of Kadcyla (ado-trastuzumab emtansine), a drug used to treat breast cancer. Patients may need up to 14 doses.

Dr Ravi Kannan is a surgical oncologist and director of Cachar cancer hospital and research centre in Silchar, Assam. He has worked in hospitals elsewhere in India and is familiar with doctors’ trauma of seeing patients walk out to go home and die.

“Some families start the treatment, hoping against hope to manage the cost. They start but they don’t come back for chemotherapy or radiation. They can’t sustain the cost,” he said. “The decision is a welcome step if the outcome is that more patients can access the drugs they need.”

India records about 1.56m new cancer cases annually – up from 1.39m cases in 2020 – making it a significant public health challenge.

Unfortunately, the court’s ruling came too late for Radhakrishnan, who died in September 2022 while her case was still pending in Kerala.

Six months after Bulgaria's parliamentary election, the country is gearing up to choose its next president.

Although the role of president in Bulgaria is largely ceremonial, the election on October 25 — with a potential runoff on November 1 — will serve as a test for the government of former President Rumen Radev, whose party won a landslide victory in April, securing an unprecedented majority — the first in Bulgaria in almost three decades.

Although there are 27 candidates on the ballot, the race has narrowed to a duel between the incumbent president, Iliana Yotova, who served two terms as Radev's vice president and is backed by the prime minister, and former caretaker Prime Minister Andrey Gyurov, who is supported by opposition parties.

According to the Bulgarian Constitution, the Bulgarian president embodies national unity. He or she represents the country at international level (with a few exceptions), is the commander-in-chief of its armed forces, appoints members of the Supreme Judicial Council and various regulatory bodies, including caretaker governments, and has the power to veto legislation.

Hristo Panchugov, a political scientist at the New Bulgarian University in Sofia, argued that although the president's powers are formally limited, the office is particularly significant in the current context.

"For the first time in Bulgaria's modern democratic history, we have an absolutely uncontested majority that is not held together by any coalition, but rather by a single political party that faces an opposition that is completely falling apart," he told DW.

Panchugov went on to say that it would have been better if the campaign had focused more on "whether the president could serve as a counterbalance."

Opposition candidate Andrey Gyurov reiterated this point in an interview with DW: "We are seeing a concentration of power that always bears the marks of a slide toward autocracy... A president should know how to say no."

Iliana Yotova declined DW's request for an interview.

Initially, the presidential race was characterized by small rallies, a lack of televised debates and numerous personal attacks on the two leading candidates.

In recent weeks, however, following drone attacks on ships in the Black Sea, the two frontrunners' diverging positions on support for Ukraine have come to the fore.

"There are many warmongers in our country; I am not one of them," she said at a campaign rally this month. "As long as I am president — and if you place your trust in me — not a single Bulgarian soldier will go to Ukraine."

Nevertheless, the recent drone attacks on commercial ships in the Black Sea have put new pressure on both the president and Rumen Radev's Progressive Bulgaria party.

"The drone strikes in the Black Sea brought the otherwise abstract geopolitical situation right into the daily lives of every Bulgarian," Boriana Dimitrova, managing partner of the Alpha Research market and social research agency, told DW.

Many in Europe, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, have laid the blame for the attacks squarely at Russia's door. The fact that Bulgaria's leaders did not clearly attribute blame for the attacks fueled frustration across the country.

A week later, after the second attack, Prime Minister Radev said, "We lack solid, clear evidence to level accusations against any specific party; however logic suggests the outcome works to the advantage of Russia." He then summoned the Russian ambassador for talks.

The stance of the government and president is having an impact on the race.

"We're seeing a slight decline in support for Iliana Yotova, which is not threatening her first-place position by any means in the first round, but there has been a decline given the ambiguity of some of the positions she has taken," said Boriana Dimitrova.

As the issue of security entered the public debate, some Bulgarians took to the streets in Sofia to protest against what they saw as the government's ambiguous foreign policy stance on this matter.

"I think the drone attacks in the Black Sea and the utterly spineless stance of the president, the prime minister, and everyone acting in unison were the last straw, and I believe that, on the eve of the presidential election, this should serve as a catalyst," Vlada Tzenova, one of the protesters, told DW.

All this is reflected in the preliminary results of the latest opinion poll conducted by Alpha Research. While expected voter turnout is up, the gap between the two frontrunners has narrowed: Yotova is still ahead, leading Gyurov by about 12 percentage points in the first round, but the margin is smaller than it was.

Nevertheless, these recent dynamics do not necessarily mean a shift in the balance of power.

Panchugov said that Gyurov's campaign has been hampered by his hesitancy to declare his candidacy, a lack of "passion" in his campaign, and the fact that he made few comments on current affairs before he joined the race.

Moreover, supporters of the candidate of the pro-Russian nationalist Vazrazhdane (Revival) party, which ranked third in the Alpha Research poll, would be most likely to support Yotova in a second round.

What could decide the outcome of the election is the fact that both GERB and DPS, two of the established parties that attracted the support of more than 660,000 voters in April, did not field a presidential candidate.

According to Alpha Research, half of GERB's supporters do not intend to vote in the first round. The party dominated Bulgaria's political landscape for 17 years but lost power in December following mass anti-corruption protests backed by opposition parties supporting Andrey Gyurov.

Most of the remaining GERB supporters are expected to back Gyurov, as the party has historically opposed Iliana Yotova's political home, the Bulgarian Socialist Party.

At the same time, both Gyurov's campaign and nationalist parties are seeing increased mobilization, with the latter drawing support from voters disappointed by the government's cautious response to the drone attacks.

"We can view these elections as the first test of confidence in the party that recently won the election," Boriana Dimitrova said. "In peaceful times, the center attracts the most attention; in turbulent times, those candidates who take a clearer stance — regardless of the direction — gain greater visibility."

#303. The political ad wars are still being fought over the airwaves even as digital gains share

While advertising dollars increasingly shift toward streaming and digital platforms, the political ad war is still being fought over the free airwaves.

Broadcast television companies are set to capture the greatest portion of political ad spend ahead of the midterm elections, according to advertising intelligence company AdImpact.

The 2025-26 election cycle is forecast to be the most expensive on record, with a predicted $11.6 billion in total spending. That's higher than the $11.2 billion spent on advertising during the 2024 presidential election cycle and the $8.9 billion spent during the 2022 midterms.

An estimated $5.6 billion of advertising spend ahead of the midterms, or 48%, is expected to go to broadcast companies, AdImpact found.

"It's still a local game," said StoneX Equity Research Analyst Daniel Kurnos in an interview. "Yes, there are national elections, but you have to win counties. And if you have to win counties, you have to reach voters in these counties."

Broadcast audiences — those tuning into NBC, ABC, CBS, Fox or their local affiliate stations — are generally more politically active, Kurnos said: "You cater to the demos that vote. You cater to the votes that matter."

That makes political advertising one of the last bastions of broadcast's dominance.

While broadcasters are expected to capture roughly half of political advertising spending this cycle, they account for just 7.7% of the more than $470 billion in general advertising spend forecast for this year in the United States, according to Magna data published by S&P Global in July.

Terry Nelson, a founding partner of Plus Communications and the national political director for former President George W. Bush's 2004 reelection campaign, told CNBC that broadcast spots allow political strategists to reach large numbers of voters "as efficiently as we can from a cost perspective."

Though cord-cutting has reshaped the TV landscape as customers drop cable, broadcast remains free and broadly accessible.

Broadcast, which transmits local news, sports, classic game shows like "Wheel of Fortune" and national networks, captured more hours of television use than cable in July, according to Nielsen's most recent monthly streaming report, The Gauge. The category accounted for 19.5% of television use, while cable captured 18.7%. Streaming led television use by far, at 49%.

Broadcast is still particularly good at delivering high viewership during live sports games, Nelson noted.

"If you think about an NFL audience, for example, you're still going to deliver big audiences amongst large segments of the population," he said. "So no matter how you dice that demographic, whether it's men, women, or old people, young people, or whatever the case might be, the NFL game is still going to deliver large segments of all of those audiences."

Brad Seitter, CEO of TVB, a trade organization representing the local broadcast industry, boiled down broadcast's strength during election cycles to "trust, reach and because it works."

Seitter told CNBC in a statement that television's perceived reliability is of utmost importance for political campaigns.

"Gaining the trust of a voter equals a vote for your candidate or issue," Seitter said.

He also said that he believes broadcast does a better job of promoting political candidates and issues than some digital channels.

"You may see a political ad on social media, but you don't trust the content of the ad because users are conditioned to not trust what they hear or see on social media," Seitter said. "Conversely, viewers trust their local broadcast TV news, so when they view a political ad, they are more likely to believe the content of the ad on a platform they know has long-standing rules, regulations and trust."

Seitter said that political campaigns continue to place advertisements on traditional TV because there is clear evidence that they are influential on voting behavior.

"Nothing moves the needle in polling like running an ad on broadcast TV," Seitter said.

According to a study conducted by TVB, 42% of Democrats and 49% of Republicans said broadcast or cable television was the most important form of advertising media in influencing how they cast their vote.

Erik Brydges, head of performance and political demand at sell-side ad company Magnite, told CNBC that broadcast's perceived influence is tied to the long history of the platform.

"There are multiple studies out there that show a correlation between the number of points you buy on TV in relation to moving poll numbers, because the medium's been around for like 70 years," he said.

And yet Brydges said he believes that broadcasting's strength is also due, in part, to muscle memory.

"A lot of campaigns and a lot of the consultants who work on the campaigns have just been really used to buying TV," Brydges said. "There's an old adage out there that no political consultant is ever going to get fired for suggesting buying more TV."

Broadcast companies are already feeling the windfall from elevated advertising spending.

Publicly traded broadcasters, including Nexstar Media Group, Gray Media, Sinclair and E.W. Scripps, each reported high political advertising revenue in their most recent fiscal quarters and forecast continued growth through the fall as the midterms inch closer.

StoneX's Kurnos told CNBC that broadcasting companies rely on the low-lift revenue from political advertising to help manage their debt.

"They have all the infrastructure in place for [standard advertising] already," Kurnos said. "And you don't need to work on the creative. You don't need to work on the selling. … Political is just, therefore, pure margin dollars."

Nexstar, which owns or operates at least 265 stations and a slew of websites and mobile apps, reported its highest quarterly revenue in the company's history. Net revenue hit $1.99 billion, and advertising revenue was $862 million, according to the August earnings report.

Overall revenue in the second fiscal quarter was largely driven by the incorporation of broadcast rival Tegna, which Nexstar officially acquired in March.

However, even discounting additional revenue from the merger, the company still reported fiscal second-quarter growth at legacy Nexstar that was primarily driven by $75 million of political advertising revenue, according to the company's most recent earnings call.

Gray Media, though operationally smaller than Nexstar, could also be a company to watch, according to Kurnos.

The company's stations operate in several markets with the most competitive races and thus the greatest ad spend: Gray has exposure in 12 competitive Senate races and all 11 competitive gubernatorial races, according to Kurnos.

"Gray has always been the most efficient political generator of the broadcast group," he said.

Sinclair and Scripps also reported political ad revenue as a bright spot in their earnings reports for the most recent fiscal quarter.

E.W. Scripps said that political ad revenue hit the highest second-quarter level ever, while Sinclair said that political ad revenue had grown 9% from the second quarter of 2022 during the last midterms season.

"No other medium delivers a political message as powerfully and reliably as broadcast television," E.W. Scripps CEO Adam Symson said on the company's second-quarter earnings call.

On the other end of the media spectrum, connected TV represents the fastest-growing category of political advertising spend, according to AdImpact. The category encompasses internet-connected devices like Apple TV or Roku.

It's predicted to capture 23%, or $2.7 billion, of this cycle's ad spending, AdImpact found, and is likely to continue to gain share.

Magnite's Brydges said the complicated and rapidly changing nature of connected TV, or CTV, and free, ad-supported streaming channels — also called FAST — has prevented some advertisers thus far from taking advantage of high viewership numbers.

"If you look at some of the FAST streaming services — some of your Plutos, your Tubis — a campaign or a consultant might not understand what those are," Brydges said. "Therefore, they might just be like, 'Well, if I don't understand what it is, then it's probably not worth investment.'"

However, he said he believes political consultants are becoming more attuned to the benefits that these platforms provide.

SKDK's Pia Carusone, who manages the firm's political advertising department, told CNBC in an interview that she sees value in streaming's specific audience targeting features.

"If you're buying NBC in New York City, for example, you're buying the whole market," she said of traditional advertising strategy. "Increasingly, now with the streaming and CTV platforms, they're able to really offer a lot more nuance in their targeting. So we're able to sort of deliver against those audiences more directly."

But ultimately, Carusone said, political advertising strategy relies on maintaining a diverse portfolio.

"You can't just do digital. You can't just do linear," she said. "Good buyers today are buying across the spectrum."

#301. A revolutionary new microscope could reveal the hidden machinery of life

Almost a century ago, a breakthrough in microscopy gave scientists an entirely new way to examine the inside of cells. Known as phase contrast, the technique made previously faint or nearly invisible cellular structures much easier to distinguish. Its importance was recognized with a Nobel Prize in 1953.

Now, physicists at the University of California, Berkeley, have brought that same principle to electron microscopy, a technology capable of magnifying objects roughly 10,000 times more than conventional light microscopes.

The researchers have developed a device called a laser phase plate that could significantly improve cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM). This powerful imaging method has transformed the study of protein structures and helped speed up drug discovery over the past decade. Even so, it has a major limitation: Most human proteins are so small that obtaining clear images of them remains extremely difficult.

The new laser technology could help overcome that obstacle, potentially allowing scientists to image proteins as small as one-third the size of those that challenge current microscopes.

An even greater impact could come from improvements to cryoelectron tomography (cryo-ET). This newer approach combines images captured from multiple angles to construct three-dimensional views of proteins and other molecules. Unlike techniques that study isolated molecules in a solution, cryo-ET can reveal how proteins are arranged and interact within their natural cellular surroundings.

“Cryo-EM has become the new, fastest-growing method for resolving the structure of biological macromolecules, and cryo-ET is expected to show how these molecules work together in their natural, cellular context,” said Holger Müller, a UC Berkeley professor of physics and faculty scientist at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory who led the development effort. “But because of signal-to-noise limitations, the majority of human and animal proteins are too small to be analyzed by these methods. The increase in signal-to-noise ratio provided by this laser phase plate is expected to overcome these important limitations.”

The breakthrough relies on what the researchers describe as the world's most intense focused continuous-wave laser. Rather than simply illuminating a specimen, the laser interacts directly with the microscope's electron beam, altering its phase to increase image contrast.

This adjustment makes it easier to distinguish small molecules, including hemoglobin, from their surroundings. It could also improve the visibility of structures inside cells, such as the nucleus and mitochondria.

“With cryo-ET, we're looking at small, very complicated cellular material that’s incredibly crowded inside the cell,” said Bridget Carragher, founding technical director of imaging at Biohub in Redwood City, California. “It’s like a forest of trees, and you’re trying to find one leaf on one tree in there. Cryo-ET needs a dramatic step forward in contrast, so we can start to see what's going on inside the cell. That's what the laser phase plate promises to give us.”

With financial support from Biohub, Müller acquired an advanced cryo-EM instrument and equipped it with the new laser phase plate. He named the resulting microscope Theia, after the ancient Greek Titaness associated with light and radiance.

Meanwhile, Carragher is directing the construction of a related microscope at Biohub's imaging facility in Redwood City. Her team's instrument uses two lasers rather than one, building on theoretical research conducted by Müller and his collaborators.

The two laser beams are arranged at right angles to one another and each operates at approximately half the power. This configuration helps prevent components from burning out while also reducing optical distortions that could interfere with image quality.

Both research teams are working with Thermo Fisher Scientific, the leading manufacturer of cryo-EM instruments.

“Theia is the Formula 1 microscope,” Müller said. “It has extra electron optics that give it better resolution than the standard cryo-EM, even without the laser. With the addition of the laser phase plate, we hope that it really becomes the world's best instrument overall.”

The Berkeley team's latest images and technical findings are presented in the journal Science. Details of Biohub's two-laser design are available in a recently posted preprint.

To understand why the new technology matters, it helps to look at a longstanding challenge in biological imaging.

Because plant and animal cells consist largely of water, they are relatively transparent when viewed under an ordinary light microscope. Although this transparency might seem beneficial, it creates a problem. Small cellular structures, including mitochondria and nuclei, scatter very little light and therefore appear only slightly darker than the surrounding material.

Scientists have traditionally addressed this problem by adding stains to create greater contrast. However, staining also kills the cells.

In 1930, Dutch physicist Frits Zernike realized that light passing through biological material changes in more ways than simply becoming brighter or dimmer. When light scatters inside a cell, it also experiences a tiny delay that changes its phase, or the timing of its wave peaks.

Although the human eye cannot directly detect this phase shift, Zernike discovered that it could be converted into a visible difference in brightness. By shifting the phase of the unscattered light by 90 degrees, he made subtle cellular features stand out more clearly against their background when the light reached the viewer's eye.

This innovation became the foundation of phase-contrast microscopy, earning Zernike the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physics.

By the early 1940s, scientists had already recognized how useful phase contrast was for studying cells. They began exploring whether the same idea might improve electron microscopes, which use electrons instead of light to examine structures as small as individual proteins.

Unfortunately, early attempts encountered serious technical difficulties. Devices designed to alter the phase of electron beams often weakened the beam too much, introduced image instability, or reduced resolution.

A possible solution emerged in 2010, when Müller and Robert Glaeser, now a UC Berkeley professor emeritus of molecular and cell biology, proposed using an intense laser to produce the necessary phase shift. Their approach offered a way to improve contrast without sacrificing electron beam intensity.

Glaeser has played an important role in developing cryo-EM, which offers a potentially simpler way to determine molecular structures than traditional X-ray crystallography.

X-ray crystallography requires researchers to grow crystals of the molecules they want to study and obtain access to an intense X-ray source. Cryo-EM avoids the need to crystallize molecules, but it presents difficulties of its own.

One of the biggest problems is that an electron beam can heat and eventually damage the specimen being examined. This limits how much information can be collected and how detailed the resulting images can become. Covering specimens with metal can reduce damage and improve contrast, but it also makes the images less sharp.

During the 1960s, researchers began investigating whether freezing biological samples could protect them from radiation damage.

Glaeser demonstrated that cooling specimens helped preserve them during imaging. He also proposed using a less intense electron beam to examine thousands of frozen molecules at once, further limiting the damage.

Because the molecules would be positioned at different angles, computers could analyze and combine their images to reconstruct a highly detailed molecular structure.

These advances helped establish the foundations of modern cryo-EM, whose developers received the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. During their acceptance remarks, the Nobel laureates acknowledged Glaeser's contributions.

The Nobel Committee praised the technology, explaining that cryo-EM “both simplifies and improves the imaging of biomolecules. This method has moved biochemistry into a new era.”

Turning the laser phase plate concept into working technology required years of engineering.

Following their 2010 proposal, Müller spent 15 years pursuing the idea, with part of the research supported by the National Institutes of Health.

The first major challenge was generating an extraordinarily intense laser beam concentrated into a tiny area. The researchers needed enough light intensity to change the phase of an electron beam by 90 degrees.

It took a decade to develop the necessary system.

Their solution was to confine a continuous laser beam inside a spherical chamber lined with mirrors. As the light repeatedly reflected through the cavity more than 10,000 times, its intensity increased dramatically while the beam became focused into an extremely small spot.

“It’s 75 kilowatts focused to a few microns,” Müller said. “That’s more powerful than what you use for welding. It's more power than a military laser. It builds up the brightest continuous laser focus ever.”

The team first demonstrated that the concept worked by installing a laser phase plate in one of Glaeser's older electron microscopes.

Additional funding from Biohub then made it possible to acquire a customized, state-of-the-art Thermo Fisher Krios cryo-EM microscope and modify it to accommodate the laser technology.

In their latest research, the scientists tested the upgraded microscope using six different samples that varied in size and preparation method. The laser consistently improved image resolution, with particularly important gains for specimens that are difficult to image using standard methods.

“For the most challenging cases — small particles, bad specimens — the laser produces a very considerable advantage,” Müller said.

To demonstrate the new microscope's capabilities, the researchers reconstructed images of two different proteins.

The first was aldolase, a protein found in muscle that modern cryo-EM systems can already image relatively easily. The second was hemoglobin, the protein responsible for carrying oxygen through the bloodstream.

Hemoglobin is much smaller and lies near the lower size limit of proteins that conventional cryo-EM instruments can reliably analyze.

Using the laser phase plate, the researchers improved the resolution of both protein structures. The improvement was especially pronounced for hemoglobin, highlighting the technology's potential to overcome the difficulties associated with smaller molecules.

“The bottom line is, if you have a large protein and a really good sample — a fresh one or one frozen without bubbles, for example — you may not need the phase plate to get a single, high-quality image. But for a small protein and a bad sample, laser-on is best,” Müller said. “This could fill an enormous gap in our knowledge of protein structures that can’t be crystallized or are too small for today’s cryo-EM. And it will be revolutionary for cryo-ET.”

Protein mass is typically expressed in daltons, a unit named after English chemist John Dalton. One dalton is defined as one-twelfth the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Conventional cryo-EM struggles to image proteins smaller than approximately 70 kilodaltons. That limitation is particularly significant because proteins below this threshold account for about 90% of the human proteome, the complete collection of proteins produced by the human body.

With the laser phase plate, researchers can now image proteins as small as 50 kilodaltons, although doing so remains challenging. Such proteins are even smaller than hemoglobin.

Müller believes the technology could eventually allow scientists to study proteins as small as 17 kilodaltons (the size of the protein myoglobin).

Reaching that goal may require another improvement: using a tightly focused electron beam rather than the defocused beam currently needed to produce sufficient contrast without the laser phase plate.

According to Müller, the laser system should make it possible to obtain contrast directly from phase changes in a focused electron beam.

If successful, this adjustment could double the contrast and signal-to-noise ratio again, adding to the improvement already achieved with the laser phase plate.

Such progress could make it possible to study a far larger proportion of the body's proteins and provide unprecedented insight into how they interact within the complex environment of cells.

“This technology is a step function change for biology,” said Stephani Otte, Biohub’s Vice President of Imaging Science. “We are going to be able to see how molecular machines operate inside the living cell, in context, for the first time. What was once invisible will become visible — and that changes everything about how we understand disease.”

The Berkeley research team also includes Glaeser; postdoctoral fellows and co-first authors Petar Petrov and Jessie Zhang; staff scientist Jonathan Remis; postdoctoral fellow Hang Cheng; and current and former physics graduate students Jeremy Axelrod, Eric Cooper, Ian Hicklin, Shahar Sandhaus, and Cooper Schnurr.
