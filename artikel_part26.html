#5147. Farm country has beef with Trump as midterms approach

Cattle feed at a ranch in Dublin, Texas, on September 11, 2026. RONALDO SCHEMIDT/AFP for Getty Images hide caption

Driving around his farm in a four-wheeler, Scott Birker warned that his cattle were in a bad mood.

"They're a little grumpy with me right now," he said.

When asked why, he said matter-of-factly: "We just got done weaning."

Birker should know his herd well, given that he's been raising beef cattle outside the Eastern Iowa town of Garrison since 2001. His operation has about 150 "mama cows" and sells feeder calves, as well as some cattle for meat. And Birker is also the northeast regional vice president for the Iowa Cattlemen's Association.

All of which is to say that since August, when Trump announced that his administration would raise the quota on beef imported under lower tariffs – temporarily suspending tariffs on up to 300,000 metric tons of imported beef – Birker knows it when he says: that hurt people.

"That surprise, with a very volatile market already, with a tight supply, hurts us really, really fast and really, really hard," Birker said.

It's not that raising beef cattle was easy before that new import policy. Margins are always tight, says Zach Raff, incoming livestock economist at Iowa State University. But there have also been other challenges – for example, drought in much of the western U.S. made hay much more expensive and shrank the nation's beef herd.

"The beef herd size is at an all time low – a 75-year-low," said Raff. "So you couple that with very strong demand. And that's going to make prices what you see at the store right now."

Ground beef currently costs around $6.80 a pound – a fact of which the Trump administration is keenly aware. But the administration's attempts to bring those prices down have backfired politically and possibly economically..

Multiple Trump administration policies have made life tougher for beef producers. Last year, amid the trade war between the two countries, China cut its US beef purchases. In February, Trump signed an executive order to subject some Argentinian beef to fewer tariffs.

And in August, he signed the executive order to suspend tariffs on 300,000 metric tons of beef imports – specifically, lean beef trimmings, with the goal of lowering the price of ground beef. The order added that the imported beef was to be sold at 25% below market prices.

That helped push U.S. cattle prices lower, but the far bigger challenge, according to Raff, was the amount of uncertainty it introduced.

"If I'm a producer, I think, well, look what's happening – perhaps next year they're going to import even more. The year after, there's going to be a different policy," said Raff. "And I'm going to say, 'Well, these prices for my cattle right now are at record highs and projected to be high. So I'm just going to send my heifers to be processed.' I'm not going to decide to replace the beef herd when I know that there's a lot of uncertainty."

This means the beef imports can have an added downside, disincentivizing producers from keeping breeding stock around that could grow the beef herd, which would lower beef prices in the long run.

From an economics 101 perspective, the Trump decision to import more beef does make sense as a way of bringing down consumer prices. But the 300,000 metric tons that could be exempted from tariffs is equivalent to around 2% of total US beef consumption.

That made for a lose-lose situation: the policy was enough to rattle beef producers, but not enough to help consumers. Indeed, the average ground beef price per pound in September was only about 13 cents lower than it was in August.

All of this looms over the 2026 midterm elections, especially in farm states such as Iowa. Iowa Republican Rep. Ashley Hinson, who's running for the state's open Senate seat, made news when she publicly broke with the Trump administration on its decision to lower tariffs on beef imports.

"We need to focus on affordability, but I think this isn't the way to do that," she said on Fox News. "I think we need to focus on helping our cattlemen grow their herd."

Her opponent, Democratic state legislator Josh Turek, has in turn been stressing that Hinson has supported some Trump policies that have hurt farmers writ large.

"She's been in favor of all this five times in favor of the tariffs," he said in an interview with NPR. "Farmers rely on markets, on free trade, more than any other group."

In the governor's race, similarly, Democratic state auditor Rob Sand has sharply criticized Trump's trade policies, while Republican businessman Zach Lahn has avoided that kind of criticism.

Iowa Republican strategist David Oman says that in either race, the beef policy could be a deciding factor.

"Both of them will be two- or three-, four-point races in my view," he said. "So any one element like this topic that cuts against you hurts."

Indeed, the administration has been playing defense on the beef policy from the beginning. Speaking in Iowa just days after the beef executive order, Secretary of Agriculture Brooke Rollins acknowledged that the August order lifting the beef tariff rate quotas, or TRQs, might not be popular.

"I believe everyone will look back on this time and say, okay, didn't love the three month lift of the TRQ, but we're so grateful to a president that is willing to listen, willing to make big, bold decisions, and willing to put it all out there for our American producers," she said at the Iowa Farm Progress Show.

Seth Watkins, who has his own cattle herd in southwestern Iowa, outside the town of Clarinda, says that these trade decisions make him upset not only with Trump, but with all of Washington.

"As much as what the president does frustrates me, I think I'm more frustrated with Congress, who should be exercising the checks and balances we learned about in high school civics," he said.

He's a Democrat and going to vote blue in this election, though he says he doesn't blame other farmers who have supported Trump.

"They honestly were told that tariffs were going to make markets better," he said. "They were told that we were going to enter this new golden era. I just filled up my pickup with $6.30 diesel fuel."

This is not just about Iowa. Several states with some of the tightest races this year – Texas, Nebraska, Montana, and Kansas – are also major beef-producing states, meaning lots of people weighing these particular trade policies as they vote.

Back on his farm, Birker knows how he'll vote, too: "I'll vote Republican."

Birker stresses that this is purely a personal decision, and that the Iowa Cattlemen are a non partisan group. But for his part, he generally votes Republican, and getting to talk personally to Hinson also reinforced his choice.

As for Trump, Birker approves of how he's doing as president.

"I do, I do," he said. "I just think it's a long putt. He's a businessman, and I think we've got to stick it out with him."

Trump will have the opportunity to convince more Iowans when he travels to Sioux City this week as part of his pre-midterm push.

#5145. The secret to handling stress better may start at breakfast

Feeling overwhelmed by stress or struggling to adjust when things go wrong? Everyday habits such as eating a nutritious breakfast, getting regular exercise, and sleeping enough may play an important role in how well your mind handles difficult situations.

New research from Binghamton University suggests that these healthy routines are connected to a mental skill called psychological flexibility, which helps people adapt to challenges and recover from stressful experiences.

Psychological flexibility refers to the ability to adjust your thoughts, emotions, and actions as circumstances change. Rather than becoming trapped in negative thinking or overwhelmed by frustration, people with greater psychological flexibility can recognize their emotions, consider their options, and respond in a more productive way.

"You might know someone who stays cool under pressure," said Lina Begdache, an associate professor of health and wellness studies at Binghamton University and lead author of the research. "The kind of person who misses a flight and, instead of panicking, calmly adapts to the situation. This person may still feel stressed, but they're better able to manage it through psychological flexibility."

"People may say that these are resilient people, but they also have what's called psychological flexibility. They're able to change the way they think about the situation and then use brain resources to handle the stress."

How Breakfast, Sleep, and Exercise Relate to Mental Resilience

To explore the relationship between lifestyle choices and emotional resilience, Begdache worked with Jason Cherry, a former Binghamton assistant professor of physical therapy, and former student Alexander J. Talkachov.

The researchers collected anonymous survey responses from approximately 400 college students, gathering information about their diets, sleep patterns, exercise habits, and other lifestyle factors.

Their findings revealed an important connection between healthy routines and the ability to cope with stress. Students who reported healthier habits, including regular physical activity and consistent breakfast consumption, also tended to demonstrate greater psychological flexibility and resilience.

The opposite pattern also emerged. Students who reported less healthy behaviors, including frequent fast food consumption and insufficient sleep, tended to show greater psychological rigidity, meaning they had more difficulty adjusting their thoughts and behaviors when circumstances changed.

Because the research relied on survey responses, the results demonstrate associations rather than proving that these habits directly improve psychological flexibility or resilience.

Why Psychological Flexibility Matters for Stress

According to Begdache, psychological flexibility gives people the mental space to recognize what they are experiencing instead of becoming completely absorbed by their stress. This ability to reassess a situation can make it easier to understand difficult emotions and determine how to respond.

She explained that improvements in diet and lifestyle may help support this kind of mental adaptability.

"When we're under stress, we feel like we fuse with the stress. We live the stress. But psychological flexibility is like stepping back and thinking, 'I feel this because of that. What can I do?' Identifying your emotions sometimes helps you find the solution for these emotions," said Begdache.

The findings also build on Begdache's previous research, which linked nutritious diets to greater resilience and poorer dietary habits to reduced resilience.

The latest study offers a possible explanation for that relationship. Rather than influencing resilience alone, diet and lifestyle may support psychological flexibility, which in turn helps people manage stress more effectively.

"The new finding here is that diet and lifestyle don't just make you resilient by themselves. They help you build the psychological flexibility, which, in turn, makes you a resilient person."

The study, "Dietary and lifestyle factors and resilience: the role of psychological flexibility as a mediator," was published in the Journal of American College Health.

#5144. Rio Tinto eyes a giant hot-brick battery to cut fossil fuel use

Rio Tinto wants to put Australia’s cheap midday electricity to work at its Yarwun alumina refinery – by storing it in hot bricks and using that heat battery to make steam after the sun sets.

The global mining giant and California-based Rondo Energy have started engineering and design work on a huge heat battery near Gladstone, Queensland. Rondo calls it Australia’s largest planned thermal energy storage project and says it would be a first for the alumina industry.

Alumina is a hard white powder made of aluminum and oxygen that’s used to make aluminum metal, sandpaper, and strong ceramics. Globally, aluminum sector refining accounts for roughly 175 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent annually.

It’s still at the study stage, but the potential payoff is substantial. Rio Tinto estimates the battery could supply around 5% of Yarwun’s steam needs and cut the refinery’s direct emissions by about 67,000 metric tons of carbon dioxide annually. It would replace some of the coal and natural gas used to generate steam.

Rondo’s heat battery uses electricity to heat refractory bricks, which can withstand high temperatures. The bricks hold that energy as heat, which is then used to produce steam for the refinery’s existing steam network.

That matters because industrial heat demand doesn’t stop when solar production drops. Rondo’s heat battery charges during hours when electricity is cheap and plentiful, then delivers heat continuously so factories can keep running.

The proposed Yarwun system would have 360 megawatt-hours (MWh) of thermal storage and deliver up to 30 megawatts MW of heat. The Australian Renewable Energy Agency (ARENA) lists its electrical charging capacity at 78 MW, allowing it to take in electricity faster than it releases the stored energy as heat.

For the refinery, another attraction is that the battery would feed into equipment it already uses. The study will assess whether that integration can deliver reliable high-pressure steam at a cost that makes sense.

“This project shows how Australia’s abundant energy can become a competitive advantage for industry,” said Dr. Kira Rundel, Rondo’s head of Australia. “Heat batteries allow manufacturers to use low-cost electricity to produce the reliable, round-the-clock heat their operations need.”

ARENA is providing an initial A$3.6 million for the engineering and design study. If the project moves ahead, the agency will provide up to A$38 million for deployment. There’s also a further A$33.3 million in contingency support.

Rio Tinto will match ARENA’s A$3.6 million study contribution. The company says its total investment over the project’s life is expected to exceed ARENA’s funding if deployment goes ahead.

“Reducing emissions from alumina refining is complex, particularly when it comes to generating the high-pressure steam needed for our operations,” said Rio Tinto Pacific Operations managing director Armando Torres.

“This study will help us determine whether thermal energy storage can provide a technically and economically viable alternative to fossil fuels for part of Yarwun’s steam requirements.”

The feasibility study is expected to take about 12 months.

#5140. Summer-peaking states could save big with heat pumps, report says

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

From 1980 to 2000, electricity demand rose while prices dropped. Heat pumps could help do that again, according to a report from RMI.

There is sufficient available capacity on the U.S. electric grid for 86 million additional home heat pumps to operate — and adding them could reduce residential electric rates by an average of 13% in summer-peaking states, according to new research from RMI.

There are about 23 million heat pumps operating in the United States today; expanding that almost five-fold with a mix of all-electric and hybrid heat pumps could save $15.6 billion per year — or $200 annually per customer, RMI said. A similar deployment using only all-electric heat pumps could reduce residential rates by an average of 6% across summer-peaking states, the analysis found.

“Strategic heat pump deployment can improve grid utilization and put meaningful downward pressure on residential electricity rates,” RMI’s report concluded. “Load growth doesn’t have to mean higher rates.”

The clean energy nonprofit was founded as Rocky Mountain Institute in 1982 following the energy crisis of the late 1970s, which prompted the federal government to set efficiency standards, among other things.

Its report points out that U.S. national average residential electricity rates are up more than 8% in two years, and that many are blaming new demand, particularly from large-load data centers. But rising demand doesn’t have to drive up costs, the report says.

“Thankfully, we’ve successfully dealt with this before: US electricity demand grew by over 70% between 1980 and 2000, while inflation-adjusted average retail rates fell by approximately 26%,” it said. “One major reason was improved utilization and efficiency of the electric grid. During this same time period, utilization of the bulk power system (i.e., average load relative to installed generating capacity) improved by roughly 18%, allowing utilities to spread fixed infrastructure costs over more kilowatt-hours sold and put downward pressure on rates.”

The report concludes that heat pumps could help do that again, especially in summer-peaking states where filling winter headroom increases sales without requiring new generation resources.

RMI generated its analysis utilizing seasonal generation capacity from the U.S. Energy Information Administration, and hourly load modeling from Evolved Energy Research and National Lab of the Rockies.

States in the Southwest, Upper Midwest and Northeast tend to have “significant” winter headroom, as their grid use peaks in the summer, the report said. Many states in the Southeast and Pacific Northwest have similar summer and winter headroom, “in part because of widespread electric resistance heating,” RMI noted.

It estimated that ​​​expanded heat pump deployment could mean $375 in annual savings per customer in Michigan; $390 in New Jersey; and $300 in Pennsylvania.

RMI cautioned, however, that its analysis should be interpreted as “directional estimates that identify where the greatest opportunities exist” rather than precise forecasts.

The biggest way for states to expand heat pump use, particularly following the elimination of federal incentives and weatherization support, is to set heat pump installation goals and redesign utility incentive programs, said Ryan Shea, a manager in RMI’s carbon-free buildings team.

The aim for states is “not necessarily putting more money to incentive programs, because that's a bit challenging right now in the political environment, but rather redirecting where the dollars are going,” Shea said in an interview.

“There's still a lot of incentives for central air conditioners, furnaces and boilers,” Shea said. “Our recommendation is to shift some of that funding away from the one-way air conditioner to a heat pump that does heating and cooling to really better utilize those ratepayer funds.”

Another key is to improve electric rate design so that it is “more cost reflective of the grid today,” Shea said. “Generally, in states where there's this opportunity for winter headroom, that means that electricity costs should be lower in the winter than the summer, and that would help spur adoption.”

Other RMI recommendations to expand heat pump uptake include updating local building codes and adopting standards for new construction to encourage higher efficiency systems.

#5139. Honda Is Building an Induction Charging Highway in Japan

Working in partnership with a road construction company, Honda hopes to charge civilian and commercial vehicles as they drive.

Honda's North American EV plans were torpedoed by tariffs and market instability, leading the company to cancel the rollout of three new EVs, including the 0-Series SUV and Saloon, earlier this year. However, in its home country of Japan, Honda is still looking for new routes for EV innovation.

One instance of this forward-looking challenge is its Mobile Power Pack, a 22-pound battery that can be swapped out of electric scooters at combination charging stations and vending machines. Considering that Honda's story began on two wheels, and knowing how the urban layout of Japan favors short-range scooters, this innovation was both fitting and quite practical, and has been around for a couple of years.

Four-wheeled EVs present a little bigger challenge, as swapping out a vehicle-sized battery pack is much more challenging. Instead, Honda is putting something that was previously science fiction into actual reality: induction charging for EVs while they travel along a road. Working in partnership with Taisei Corporation, Honda will be conducting real-world durability and feasibility testing on the Tateyama Expressway to the east of Tokyo, starting early next year.

The charging infrastructure will consist of a pattern of inverters embedded in the tarmac of the driving lane, fed by regular DC power supply stations on the side of the road. Vehicles equipped with receiving panels on the underside will be able to absorb charge as they travel along, at charging rates up to a test level of 150 kWh for larger commercial vehicles.

The primary innovation here is simplified wiring for the in-tarmac units, which combine inverter and coil into a single, high-durability pad. The plan is to run literally a million wheels over the surface, to demonstrate that the cost of embedding such infrastructure pays off with minimal long-term maintenance.

Could on-the-go charging be coming to a road near you soon? Probably not. Japan does have the odd poorly maintained rural canyon road, but its freeway and urban infrastructure is billiards-table-smooth. You really don't want to be dealing with potholes that can electrocute people, and roads in the United States are not maintained nearly as well as those in Japan.

However, durability testing is a good way to prove out the concept, and induction charging roads could be useful for commercial applications. Especially in cases where cargo shuttles never stop moving, as in a busy port, having a section of road that tops up a battery while driving could keep the machinery moving. In the meantime, Honda's electrification plan for North America looks like it is on pause.

#5131. Scientists discover how harmful “zombie cells” hide from the immune system

Researchers led by Cedars-Sinai Health Sciences University have discovered a protein that may allow harmful aging cells to escape detection by the immune system. By helping these cells remain in the body instead of being cleared away, the protein could contribute to their accumulation and the development of health problems associated with aging.

The discovery, published in Cell Metabolism, identifies PD-L2 as a potential target for therapies aimed at eliminating harmful aging cells. Scientists also believe the protein could serve as a blood marker, providing a way to detect and monitor these cells.

"Our findings suggest that PD-L2 may help aging cells stay in the body when they would normally be removed by the immune system," said Selim Chaib, PhD, research assistant professor of Medicine at Cedars-Sinai and first and co-corresponding author of the study.

How Harmful Aging Cells Escape the Immune System

As cells age or become damaged, some enter a state known as senescence. These cells stop dividing but remain alive, lingering in tissues rather than disappearing. Over time, they can accumulate and release substances that trigger inflammation and disrupt the normal activity of surrounding cells.

The buildup of these senescent cells has been associated with a range of health problems that become more common with age. Understanding why they persist, particularly when the immune system would normally remove them, could help scientists develop new ways to reduce their harmful effects.

The researchers found that PD-L2 may play an important role in this process by helping aging cells avoid immune clearance.

Blocking PD-L2 Reduces Aging Cells in Mice

To investigate the protein's role, scientists conducted experiments in laboratory mice in which PD-L2 was either removed or blocked. Both approaches reduced the accumulation of senescent cells and lowered levels of the inflammatory substances they release.

The benefits extended beyond changes at the cellular level. Mice with reduced PD-L2 activity also showed improvements in their ability to process sugar and demonstrated greater grip strength, suggesting that targeting the protein could influence physical functions affected by aging.

The team also examined human cells and tissues. They discovered that senescent human cells produced more PD-L2 than younger cells and that levels of the protein rose with age in certain human tissues. These findings suggest the mechanism observed in mice may also be relevant to human aging.

A Potential New Target for Age-Related Diseases

The results raise the possibility that blocking PD-L2 could help the immune system recognize and eliminate harmful aging cells, potentially reducing some of the health problems associated with their buildup.

"If we can find a way to block this protein, we may be able to help the immune system get rid of these cells and potentially improve health problems linked with aging," said James Kirkland, MD, PhD, director of the Center for Advanced Gerotherapeutics and senior author of the study.

Although the findings are promising, more research is needed before the approach can be considered for treating people. Future studies will investigate whether blocking PD-L2 is safe and can deliver meaningful health benefits in humans. Researchers also hope to clarify how the protein contributes to diseases associated with aging.

#5128. The capacity market crisis is a design problem, not a verdict on competition

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

PJM’s capacity price spikes are a symptom of a market that drifted too far toward spot-price dependence, not an indication that competition itself does not work, write Atlantic Council experts.

Frank Willey is an assistant director and Stephen Greene is a nonresident senior fellow in the Atlantic Council’s Global Energy Center.

Across the country, wholesale markets are signaling looming electricity shortages, and some observers have called for a return to the regulated, vertically integrated utility model. The PJM Interconnection's latest capacity market auction cleared at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s approved cap for the third consecutive time, more than eleven times the price four years ago, and fell short of PJM’s reliability requirement. The results are indicative of the scope of the underinvestment crisis.

However, advocating for competitive markets to be abolished and replaced with vertically integrated models conflates concerns about aspects of competitive wholesale market implementation with a fundamental failure of the market-based framework itself.

Competitive markets have brought innovation and efficiency to the deployment of power generation and have removed the poor incentives that in the past have plagued the vertically integrated model. Rather than discarding competitive markets altogether, along with the decades of improvements they have brought, states, FERC and regional transmission organizations should drive greater use of long-term power purchase agreements between generators and off-takers, including load-serving entities, or LSEs, that serve retail customers. These long-term agreements support the more durable, long-term investment signals encouraging the sustained buildout of infrastructure that RTO-managed spot and capacity markets cannot reliably provide.

As PJM's recent white paper lays out, these long-term agreements could be bilateral agreements between independent power producers and LSEs, driven by hedging requirements imposed by regulators, or directly between IPPs and RTOs, funded through a reliability charge to participants.

Introducing competition into electricity markets was a policy goal pursued over several decades. A series of legislative actions and FERC orders enabled competitive wholesale power markets.

In PJM, as in many restructured markets, retail competition never developed as robustly as regulators expected. Thirteen states and the District of Columbia offer residential electricity choice programs but include attractive default-provider provisions that substantially reduce the incentives for competition. In 2024, residential customers’ subscription rates to competitive suppliers hovered around 25% in those states, excluding Texas, according to EIA data.

Utility commissions pushed to make default service options very attractive, in part to show that restructuring would deliver lower prices quickly, oftentimes mandating rate reductions of 10% to 20% as the program was implemented. Many states also encouraged default providers to lean heavily on the spot market rather than locking in longer-term supply. Rather than making electricity markets and the underlying system more efficient, many academic papers have argued that electricity market restructuring created a structural subsidy under which the cost of default retail supply is subsidized by regulated transmission and distribution service.

Until recently, the danger of relying heavily on the spot market has not been evident, because low demand growth masked the importance of long-term contracts, including for capacity. Such “hedging” contracts would protect consumers from volatile wholesale prices and give generators predictable revenue streams, thereby facilitating more investment in additional generation capacity (at lower cost, which helps moderate prices). As demand growth has accelerated, this fault in market implementation has been exposed, and price spikes have been the result.

Introducing competition into electricity markets was never intended to yield total reliance on central RTO-administered spot markets. In fact, the original designers of markets envisioned sophisticated LSEs procuring power from sophisticated generation operators, while RTO markets would simply handle transmission constraints and “imbalances” close to or in real-time.

In most well-functioning commodity markets, such as agricultural commodities, steel or bulk chemicals, buyers and sellers rely on a mix of contracts and spot purchases to manage supply, demand and volatility. Long-term contracts provide stability for both consumers and producers, keeping supply and demand in rough balance, while some participation in short-term or spot markets allows both sides to make shorter-term adjustments. Electricity market restructuring had two goals: to bring competition to electric generation and retail sales and to reduce regulated utilities’ structural bias toward over-investing in capital assets to drive revenue growth. Restructuring has delivered on both of these goals and brought substantial benefits to consumers over the past two decades. Neither goal required abandoning contracts nor exposing consumers to volatile spot markets.

Managing demand growth requires providing predictable and durable incentives for generation investment. There is no business case for a developer to build generation on the back of short-term price spikes when supply costs are high, as they are now, because the higher investment needs to be recovered over the life of the project, while, under the current market structure, the prices available through the market are likely to return to lower, normal levels in just a few years.

An all-spot market is feasible in theory, but the extreme spot prices that would result would be disruptive and unacceptable. Long-term contracts work better for both suppliers and customers. Even in Texas, where shortages can drive spot energy prices to high levels, the retail market structure drives retail providers to put contracts in place to hedge against short-term price spikes.

Encouraging long-term agreements in competitive wholesale power markets would support reliability while maintaining the original goals of restructuring. If anything, the use of long-term contracts is consistent with how these markets were expected to work from the start: independent developers still compete to offer the best generation projects and still bear the risk of executing them. What changes is simply restoring the contracting layer that was abandoned in the move to a retail structure reliant on primarily short-term contracts. That restoration doesn't require reinventing wholesale markets, but rather using tools already available under the existing framework.

Prudent buyers would build a portfolio of overlapping agreements signed at different times, so contracts signed during a shortage at higher prices are balanced by contracts signed when new capacity is cheaper to build. Spot markets still play a role, reconciling the gap between what was contracted and what is actually needed at delivery, but they should not be relied on to carry the entire market, any more than they do in other commodity markets.

PJM's capacity price spikes are a symptom of a market that drifted too far toward spot-price dependence, not an indication that competition itself does not work. Reorienting competitive wholesale markets requires rebuilding the long-term contracting layer through hedging requirements driven by state regulators, RTO-brokered reliability charges, or both, rather than abolishing the competitive market structure and the benefits it has already demonstrated.

A new lithium battery energy storage system (BESS) manufacturer opened in the United States in September, and it plans to supply the commercial, industrial and utility-scale markets. NeoVolta Power, a joint-venture between lithium brands NeoVolta and PotisEdge, unveiled its 4-GWh manufacturing site in Pendergrass, Georgia, about one hour northeast of Atlanta. The site is designed to support FEOC-compliant and domestic content-eligible solutions for U.S. project developers.

Solar Power World talked with NeoVolta CEO Ardes Johnson after the factory opening to learn more about the future of BESS manufacturing in the United States.

Johnson: NeoVolta started in 2018 as a residential battery storage company, founded by Brent Willson in La Jolla, California, with the first system shipping in 2019 and a Nasdaq listing in 2022. For several years the business centered on selling home battery systems like the NV14 through certified solar installers.

That changed when the residential storage market tightened sharply following federal incentive changes. The pullback made clear that a broader footprint was needed — spanning residential, commercial, industrial and utility-scale, and extending from selling equipment to manufacturing it domestically.

Today NeoVolta is best described as a U.S. energy storage manufacturing platform. The path from a residential unit built in a garage to a gigawatt-hour-scale manufacturing line in Georgia has been the central story of the company’s growth.

Pendergrass is a roughly 210,000-ft2 facility on the I-85 corridor in northeast Georgia, operated through NeoVolta Power, the joint venture with PotisEdge, a LONGi Green Energy company. The first production line is a 4-GWh line at full run rate and, as orders warrant, building toward 8 GWh with a second line.

It’s worth being precise about what manufacturing means here, because it goes beyond final assembly. NeoVolta Power has its own battery management system (BMS), storage energy management system (EMS) and intelligent cell contact system (iCCS) — and that combination is the real differentiator, not just mechanical integration. The iCCS sits directly on the battery pack with a voltage and temperature sensor on every cell, catching a cell going bad minutes before it becomes a safety event, confirming a thermal event within about a minute to trigger suppression and trending degradation months in advance — a more automatable, lower-cost design than the wire-harness approach still common across the industry. The BMS draws on EV-grade battery management heritage and handles cell- and pack-level monitoring, active balancing and full protection. The EMS is the layer that lets these systems participate in grid services like frequency regulation and arbitrage, rather than just storing electrons passively.

So when cells arrive, the work done in Pendergrass is the engineering value-add: integrating those cells with proprietary BMS, iCCS and EMS hardware and software into a finished, grid-ready pack and container, built and tested on NeoVolta’s own equipment.

I’m not sure if difficult is exactly the right word, but there were many challenges that needed to be thought through and solved for. NeoVolta had no prior large-scale manufacturing experience — it was all residential storage — which is why the Pendergrass venture was structured as a partnership rather than a solo build; PotisEdge’s experience brought the equipment installation, commissioning and ramp-up expertise NeoVolta didn’t have in-house. While our plant is highly automated, we are currently staffing up more engineering, facilities, operations and all the supporting talent that will allow us to scale efficiently.

The policy environment around FEOC rules has also been genuinely complex, given that NeoVolta Power’s manufacturing partner has ties to a Chinese parent company. The joint venture had to be structured carefully — with NeoVolta holding the controlling interest, including 100% of the board — to stay compliant, and the rules themselves are still being finalized by the IRS, which complicates planning. Related to that, the domestic cell supply chain isn’t mature yet: U.S.-made cells aren’t abundantly available at the volume or price point needed, so Chinese-sourced cells have bridged the gap in the near term, even as non-FEOC content requirements rise each year toward 2030.

Finally, there’s the operational and financial lift of a first-of-its-kind ramp: commissioning a new line, hiring and training a production workforce, converting pipeline into signed, binding contracts and financing it through a mix of equity, debt and project-level financing — all while the legacy residential business was contracting at the same time.

The U.S. is in the early innings of a real onshoring shift, one likely to happen in stages rather than overnight. Most U.S. manufacturers today, NeoVolta included, still rely on imported cells — first from China, moving toward Southeast Asia and eventually toward domestic cell production as capacity comes online. The SK On agreement illustrates that path: 9 GWh of U.S.-manufactured LFP cells from 2027 to 2031, with a framework that could scale to 18 GWh.

But the cell is only one piece of the value chain, there are other components where differentiation also matters. A lot of manufacturers still treat the DC block like little more than a container of batteries — cells, a basic BMS and not much else. NeoVolta builds it differently: BMS, EMS and iCCS are engineered directly into the DC block, so products like NVAPEx carry real power electronics and controls — cell-level safety monitoring, active management and grid-facing intelligence — rather than a passive box of cells.

That expertise also points to where the product roadmap is headed. On the C&I side, NVGAIN already pairs that same BMS/EMS/iCCS foundation with an integrated PCS. The plan is to carry that power electronics expertise forward into a PCS-equipped version of the utility-scale platform as well, bringing the level of integration proven out in NVGAIN up to grid scale.

At the end of the day, for the next three-to-five years, policy will keep driving the pace, and it’s one of the few things that cuts across the aisle in Washington right now. NeoVolta continues to have conversations with policymakers on both the Democratic and Republican sides, and whatever else divides them, there’s no real constituency arguing for fewer U.S. manufacturing jobs or deeper dependence on Chinese battery supply chains. That’s part of why the non-FEOC content thresholds rising through 2030 have staying power regardless of which party holds the White House or Congress — they’re forcing the supply chain to localize on a set timeline, whether or not the economics are fully there yet. Demand isn’t the constraint either — grid modernization, utility-scale storage and increasingly data center power needs are pulling hard on this market. The companies that combine real power electronics engineering now with a credible path to domestic cells over the next three to four years are positioned to come out ahead.

#5116. Cornell scientists identify rare human genetic disorder in two unusually long-legged cats

Two brothers named Gary and Shaggy have helped Cornell University researchers make a rare genetic discovery. In a study published in Scientific Reports, scientists reported the first molecular characterization of Marfan syndrome in domestic cats, a condition primarily recognized in humans.

Gary and Shaggy first stood out as kittens because of their unusually long limbs. As they grew older, veterinarians also identified abnormalities in their eye structures and an enlarged aorta, the body's main artery. Together, these symptoms suggested Marfan syndrome, an inherited condition that weakens connective tissues throughout the body. The disorder affects approximately 1 in 4,000 people, but these brothers represented the first known documented feline cases.

"The findings provide a foundation for improved veterinary diagnostics," said senior author Dr. Jacquelyn Evans, assistant professor in the College of Veterinary Medicine and at the Baker Institute for Animal Health. "It can help veterinarians recognize similar cases in the future and may help develop genetic tests."

Scientists Uncover a Surprising Genetic Twist

To determine what caused the brothers' unusual symptoms, researchers from multiple disciplines conducted thorough medical examinations and analyzed their DNA. Their investigation identified changes in a gene called FBN1, which provides instructions for producing fibrillin-1. This protein is essential for healthy connective tissue, helping support structures throughout the body, including blood vessels, bones, ligaments, skin, and eyes.

Genetic testing revealed that Gary and Shaggy each had two altered copies of FBN1, having inherited one from each parent. This was especially notable because, in humans, a mutation in just one copy of the gene can be enough to cause Marfan syndrome. Having two altered copies is exceptionally uncommon and can severely disrupt the production of normal fibrillin-1.

Yet the brothers' genetic changes had an unexpected effect. Further analysis showed that their particular variant did not completely disable the gene. Instead, it interfered with how the gene's instructions were processed while still allowing some normal activity to continue. That remaining function helps explain why Gary and Shaggy were able to survive into adulthood, even though carrying two copies of such a mutation could otherwise have resulted in a much more severe form of the disorder.

#5101. Scientists reveal why some workout recovery tricks can backfire

Finishing a tough workout always feels good. That is, until a few hours later when those uncomfortable muscle aches start setting in. This discomfort can linger for days – making easy, everyday tasks a challenge.

In search of relief, some recreational gym-goers are adopting the kind of recovery techniques once reserved for elite athletes. Social media and professional sport have helped popularize everything from ice baths and compression garments to massage and recovery supplements.

While many of these products promise faster recovery, reduced soreness and improved performance, the science is often more nuanced.

Few recovery strategies have received as much attention as ice baths. This involves immersing oneself in water that is below 15°C to boost recovery. But the evidence for this benefit is mixed.

Ice baths help ease inflammation, which research suggests can reduce muscle soreness and help athletes recover muscle function more effectively in a 24-hour period.

But there’s a catch. While inflammation causes soreness, it also triggers adaptations that build muscle. Dampening this response with ice baths may limit some of the long-term benefits of training – particularly if they’re used regularly.

When it comes to ice baths, side-stepping short-term pain may sacrifice long-term gains. It’s best to only use them when rapid recovery is vital.

Compression garments are commonly worn after exercise to speed recovery. The idea is that the external pressure they apply supports circulation, enhances blood flow to the muscles, and reduces swelling.

But while compression garments might make you think they’re helping as they reduce soreness, evidence showing they actually improve muscle recovery is generally less convincing.

A study from 2022 put them to the test. One group was given compression garments; the other group was given a placebo tablet that they were told was equally as effective as compression.

The compression garments were shown to improve circulation and muscle blood flow, while the placebo had no effect on these measures. This suggests the effects of compression garments on recovery are physiological and aren’t simply due to expectation or perception.

However, whether these changes meaningfully improve performance is less clear. Although emerging evidence suggests compression garments genuinely affect some markers of recovery, this is unlikely to directly restore muscle function.

So, compression garments may mainly help because they make you feel better recovered.

Massage is one of the few recovery interventions with relatively consistent scientific support. It can reduce soreness and improve perceived recovery after exercise.

It’s not currently clear what mechanisms actually trigger the recovery benefits of massage. But it could be due to the fact that massage reduces muscle stiffness, improves circulation and boosts relaxation.

But although athletes feel less sore and perceptions of recovery are improved after a massage, objective measures of performance and muscle function are modest and inconsistent.

Massage might ease the discomfort of training, but don’t expect miraculous performance restoration.

The nutrition supplement market is a big business, with hundreds of brightly coloured recovery drinks and capsules promising reduced soreness and faster recovery. But not all supplements are created equal. Some appear to confer small benefits, while some come with hype that science doesn’t back up.

Whey protein, for instance, is a well-researched supplement. Rich in amino acids (the building blocks of protein), it stimulates muscle protein synthesis – the process which helps your muscles grow. Whey protein is shown to have small-to-moderate benefits for muscle recovery – but evidence that it can alleviate soreness is inconsistent.

Branch chain amino acids (BCAAs) are widely-used supplements that typically contain a mixture of the amino acids leucine, isoleucine and valine. BCAAs are popular because it’s believed the leucine they contain switches on protein synthesis.

Yet despite their popularity, BCAAs appear less effective than complete protein sources for muscle recovery. This is because muscle repair requires all essential amino acids – not just the ones found in BCAAs. Food sources such as chicken, beef, salmon and tofu contain all the essential amino acids.

Tart cherry juice is a relatively new supplement in the fitness world. It’s rich in polyphenols – plant compounds that are said to have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant (reducing or preventing cell damage) effects. It’s for these reasons that tart cherry juice is thought to help reduce muscle damage and soreness.

Although this is still an emerging area of research, evidence suggests these compounds have small-to-moderate effects on reducing soreness after workouts, and may also help restore muscular strength and power.

Omega-3 (fish oil) supplements are often championed as a way to reduce inflammation and accelerate recovery after exercise. While some research suggests they can modestly reduce muscle soreness and help preserve muscle function after strenuous exercise, results are far from consistent. As such, omega-3 supplements are unlikely to vastly improve recovery.

Importantly, all of these nutrients can be obtained from a healthy, balanced diet. Science also shows that it’s better to get these nutrients from whole foods over expensive supplements, as whole foods provide other important nutrients that may interact synergistically to support health.
