#1894. One lesson Senne Lammens learned after Tottenham’s late equaliser

Senne Lammens believes Manchester United must learn to finish games off after their late collapse against Tottenham Hotspur.

The result left the goalkeeper frustrated at Old Trafford despite leading the game and with a player more on the field.

Manchester United looked set for three points after Bryan Mbeumo put them ahead in the second half, but Dominic Solanke’s stoppage-time equaliser denied Michael Carrick’s side a much-needed victory.

Speaking to MUTV, Senne Lammens admitted the team had struggled early on but had done enough after the break to put themselves in a winning position.

“Football can be a bit cruel at times,” the Belgian goalkeeper said. “There are a lot of ups and downs, especially in a game like that.”

Senne Lammens felt United failed to take advantage of Tottenham’s red card, allowing the visitors too much space to find a late equaliser.

“We’ve won a lot of games against 10 men [in the past], but you also know there can be a tricky situation when you’re playing against 10 men, because they have nothing to lose and they know that.”

He added that United should have scored again to put the result beyond doubt or, at the very least, prevented Tottenham from creating further chances.

“I think that’s what we did wrong. We gave them a little bit too much breathing room to create those chances, and then one ball has to go right for them, one bounce has to go their way, and they can score like that.”

The goalkeeper also pointed to similarities with United’s earlier disappointment against Everton, where points slipped away late in the match.

“You feel like those games just slip away from you, and there are one or two situations where you don’t really have control,” he said.

“I think there’s a lot to learn from today. It wasn’t all negative, not at all, but especially the way it ended left a very bad feeling.”

“I think the easy answer is that we have to be able to kill the game faster and not put ourselves in that position to give them a chance.”

Despite the frustration, Lammens said there were positives to take from the performance. However, the late goal had left a bitter feeling, and United now need to turn those lessons into better results.

#1889. No wonder there is frantic hype around Carlos Baleba - desperate Man United know his £70m move HAS to work... and did Man City sell the wrong goalkeeper this summer? IAN LADYMAN on My Football Weekend

In football desperation can lead to exaggeration. Straws are clung on to and early conclusions drawn. That is why in and around Manchester United so much is being made of new midfielder Carlos Baleba.

The 22-year-old has played less than two hours of Premier League football since his move from Brighton in the summer.

He is clearly a very good player. He has a lovely first touch, can pass and seems to have the kind of awareness of time and space that no coach can really teach.

But United manager Michael Carrick got lost a little in exaggeration when describing his midfield as ‘terrific’ and ‘fantastic’ after the draw at Leeds. He is not alone.

Baleba has been hailed as some kind of shape-shifting saviour since leaving behind some early-season injury worries to come off the bench against Tottenham nine days ago.

But all the frantic hype is understandable because Baleba’s £70million switch from Brighton simply has to work. If it doesn’t, United are in even more trouble than they would appear after taking only seven points from their opening seven Premier League games.

This was the summer of the United midfield rebuild. There was £70m for Baleba, £50m for Andrey Santos from Chelsea and £35m for Youri Tielemans from Aston Villa. The club have spent upwards of £150m on a new central platform for their team and if it doesn’t work there will be no cavalry coming to sort it all out next year.

United are trying to rebuild a squad like people spruce up a house. Bathroom, kitchen, bedroom.

Carlos Baleba started his first Premier League game for Manchester United at Leeds

The intelligence from inside Old Trafford is that next summer the transfer budget will be spent on the defence.

So the midfield has been ticked off yet still we sat at Elland Road and watched Leeds take 61 per cent of the possession.

That’s a huge number when you are playing a supposed top-six team and it shows where United are.

Carrick’s players can’t control games. Not with the ball anyway. United haven’t been able to do for years and until they do they will continue to live or die on the strength of their counter-attack.

Carrick spoke of having witnessed a ‘fresh start’ at Elland Road yesterday and that was a huge stretch.

For Baleba, the weight on a young man’s shoulders already looks rather heavy.

Carrick also spoke yesterday of having metaphorical headphones on as he looks to find a way round his current problems. The insinuation was that the noise about United’s predicament doesn’t bother him.

I wonder how he really feels, though, about the presence in the background of an Amazon film crew and also a journalist still trying to fulfil a Jim Ratcliffe brief to write a book chronicling United’s return to eminence.

United are said to be getting in the region of £20m for allowing access to Amazon. That’s about a quarter of what a club gets for qualifying for the Champions League.

Carrick will hope that one doesn’t get in the way of the other.

At Leeds Daniel Farke is threatening to be the club’s most successful manager since David O’Leary 25 years ago.

Outside Elland Road there is a mural tribute to a real Leeds hero, Marcelo Bielsa – who brought Leeds up in 2020 but couldn’t sustain it.

Maybe Farke will get one of those, too, but what the German deserves more than anything is a new contract.

Daniel Farke's Leeds sit seventh in the table having lost just one of their first seven games

Farke had it all on to prove himself a Premier League boss after a yo-yo spell with Norwich but that journey of validation now seems complete.

The 49-year-old’s current deal with the club expires at the end of this season. It’s said a new one has been tentatively agreed.

Leeds fans will feel better when they hear news of a signature.

The FA coaching network have been convinced for two or three years that James Trafford would get past Dean Henderson to become Jordan Pickford’s successor as England No1.

For a while that looked fanciful but national coach Thomas Tuchel agrees and with maybe good reason.

The last time we saw the Leeds goalkeeper playing regularly in the Premier League was for Burnley two seasons ago and he looked like a boy in a man’s outfit, bullied at set-pieces and unable to command his penalty area.

Here we are in 2026 – less than two years out from a home European Championship – and the Cumbrian, signed from Manchester City for £40m in the summer, looks like a different goalkeeper.

The more I watch him play this season and the more I watch Gianluigi Donnarumma at City, the more I wonder if the Manchester club sold the wrong one.

City’s start to the season has been overshadowed by numbers – 115 of them to be precise – but there has been no disguising the leaps forward they have made on the field under Enzo Maresca.

The Premier League leaders have now racked up exactly 100 shots in seven games – a figure skewed a little by the fact they only managed six when playing against United with ten men before the international break.

One high-profile presenter has been accused of being a member of the ‘f*****g red cartel’.

They weren't – as far as we are aware – a member of the commission that passed down the guilty verdicts.

Better then to tell one of the great football tales from when Arsenal won at the City Ground by the same scoreline back in January 1999.

One of football’s most ridiculous laws – and the bar here is terrifyingly high – states that a penalty or foul cannot be given at corners and set-pieces until the ball is actually in flight.

It’s because of this that Brentford’s Yehor Yarmolyuk can be sent off for his headbutt on Liverpool’s Jeremy Jacquet on Saturday but the visiting team cannot be awarded a penalty.

Has it not crossed anybody’s mind that if players knew they could be properly penalised for all the tedious argy bargy we regularly witness as we await the delivery of a corner that they may think twice before grabbing hold of their opponent in the first place?

Newcastle say they wished ‘to preserve the song’s legacy while strengthening its impact inside the stadium’.

Having listened to that song on endless occasions since first setting foot inside St James’ Park in 1998, I am not sure it needed to be any stronger.
