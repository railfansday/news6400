While working on his land in a village in northern Egypt, a man made an astonishing discovery: the remains of two massive statues of Ramesses II.

The man, Khaled el-Shahat, immediately notified the Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities, which sent a team to the village of Behbeit El Hagar to excavate and study the statues, the ministry said in a translated statement.

Ramesses II had one of the longest reigns in Egypt's history, ruling from around 1279 to 1213 B.C. During his 66 years in power, Egypt controlled an empire that stretched as far northeast as modern-day Syria. He initiated many building projects throughout the empire, and numerous Ramesses II statues have been found throughout Egypt.

Archaeologists had previously found architectural fragments bearing the name of Ramesses II at Behbeit El Hagar, which is known for its temple dedicated to Isis, a goddess associated with healing and magic, said Mahmoud Emam, an Egyptologist at Cairo University who has worked on remains from the site but was not involved with the recent excavation. He noted that the newly found statues were unearthed on the western side of the temple complex.

Both of the newfound statues were once larger than life, with the bigger one likely standing nearly 28 feet (8.5 meters) tall when it was originally built. However, most of what remains is its head, which is 5.2 feet (1.6 m) tall and depicts the pharaoh wearing the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt.

Part of this large statue's back pillar has a hieroglyphic inscription that gives the pharaoh's "Horus" name: Hor-Ka-Nakht-Mery-Maat. This name means "Strong Bull, Beloved of Right, Truth," Joyce Tyldesley, a professor emeritus of Egyptology at the University of Manchester in the U.K., wrote in her book "Ramesses: Egypt's Greatest Pharaoh" (Penguin Books, 2001). In Egypt, it was common for pharaohs to have several alternate names with a Horus name, given in honor of the sky god Horus.

The second statue, which stands about 7.9 feet (2.4 m), also likely depicts Ramesses II, the statement said. The king is shown wearing the "Nemes" headdress, a type of striped headcloth that pharaohs wore, the Egypt Exploration Society notes on its website.

Near this statue is a block of pink granite with two hieroglyphic inscriptions that name Ramesses II and describe him as "King of Upper and Lower Egypt" and the "Son of Ra," an ancient Egyptian sun god, the statement said.

Hieroglyphs have been found on the statue remains. Some of them describe Ramesses II as the "son of Ra," a sun god in ancient Egypt.

The new finds add more evidence that Ramesses II was heavily invested in the site.

Previous discoveries include two architectural fragments bearing the name of Ramesses II near the Isis temple at Behbeit El Hagar, Emam and his colleagues reported in a 2017 paper. One is believed to be from the upper part of a statue, and another seems to originate from the upper part of a wall.

"We concluded that Ramesses II had undertaken major building works at the temple and recommended further archaeological investigations to shed more light on the early history of the site," Emam said. He noted that the site is threatened by urban growth and that excavation work should be carried out at the temple.

#5946. A Torchy’s Tacos Customer Says She Found A Cow Tooth In Her Taco

There are some things you expect to find in a taco: shredded lettuce, a zesty salsa, fresh veggies. A cow tooth is definitely not one of them.

A Texas woman is suing Torchy’s Tacos after she says she found what turned out to be a cow tooth in a takeout order from the chain’s South Congress location in Austin. According to her attorney, Scott Vasquez, she ordered a Democrat taco—which includes beef barbacoa, avocado, cotija cheese, cilantro, and diced onions—when she made the discovery, he told News Channel 10.

Vasquez said his client’s first reaction was basically what you’d expect: “Her reaction was disgust. ‘What is this?’ Looking at it, she thought it was a tooth.”

Torchy’s later confirmed that the item was, in fact, a cow tooth.

According to the lawsuit filed Tuesday, the plaintiff alleges that Torchy’s knew or should have known something like this could end up in its food and that it was negligent in its safety standards. Vasquez argued that someone should have caught the tooth somewhere along the supply or preparation process before the food reached the customer.

Torchy’s Tacos said in a statement that it was aware of the complaint and takes food safety concerns seriously. The company also explained that the barbacoa used in the taco is made with natural beef cheek sourced from a supplier. After reviewing the complaint, Torchy’s said it identified the item as a cow tooth and that this can occasionally happen. The company said it has since worked with the supplier to reinforce procedures to prevent it from happening again.

The woman’s attorney also raised a safety concern, saying an object like that could potentially be a choking hazard. The lawsuit seeks $250,000 or less, with a jury to determine the final amount.

It is worth noting that the negligence claims are allegations in the lawsuit, not established findings. Torchy’s has acknowledged the incident itself and confirmed that the object was a cow tooth. Still, this may be one of those stories that makes you inspect your next taco just a little more closely.

#5943. The Unexplored World of Fashion in Silent Film

“Goddesses in the Machine: Fashion in American Silent Film” is the first major exhibition dedicated to the topic

Some 20 years ago when Michelle Finamore was thumbing through archival footage at the British Film Institute, she hit the fashion-history jackpot. In the middle of a collection of early 20th-century newsreels (weekly compilations of current events screened in theaters before the feature film) was the legendary French couturier Madeleine Vionnet explaining how she puts her thumbprint on her garments’ labels to prove their authenticity. “It was astonishing,” Finamore, a Salem, Massachusetts-based curator and author, recalls to Harper’s Bazaar. “The footage hadn’t been catalogued under ‘fashion’, and no one in my field had ever seen it before.”

This was one of countless research revelations Finamore encountered during her PhD program at Bard Graduate Center (BGC), in which she focused on fashion in early cinema. This fall her scholarship comes full circle with “Goddesses in the Machine: Fashion in American Silent Film”, the first major exhibition dedicated to the topic (on view through January 3, 2027). Co-curated with fellow BGC alum Emma Cormack, BGC’s Associate Curator for Exhibitions and Study Collection Curator, the fascinating show presents rare surviving objects—think posters, ephemera, and utterly glamorous costumes—from 1895 to the introduction of “talkies” during the late 1920s. Many of the glittering garments are also seen in motion via beautifully restored film clips.

Over three floors (plus a fourth dedicated to current PhD candidates’ reconstructions of ensembles in the exhibition), visitors will discover how film costuming went from the actors’ responsibility to the studio-driven enterprise we know today. Amid the wide array of objects on view, perhaps what is most impressive is the sheer volume of extant garments and accessories in remarkably good condition, despite being more than a century old (and as several film clips attest, worn during feverish dance numbers). Finamore and Cormack note that both the films and costumes required conservation feats due to their fragility (according to the Library of Congress, only approximately 20% of films from the silent era have been preserved; many in compromised states as the cellulose-nitrate film stock has faded, degraded, and combusted).

Also fascinating are the mix of institutions (from the Museum of the City of New York to La Cinémathèque française) and private collectors who loaned these objects. They paint a picture of how this exhibition could only be done by a true insider, like Finamore. Nevertheless, while collecting categories within the silent-film community run the gamut (from lobby cards to footwear), Finamore underscores how much all collectors care about providing access. For example, “Goddesses in the Machine” features several glass slides (ads that were projected onscreen before a film) from the Starts Thursday! Cinema Slide Archive, in which more than 10,000 slides have been digitized and made available online.

Cormack adds it’s also common for private collectors to concentrate on a single starlet, whether it’s Clara Bow or Gloria Swanson: “People have their favorite actresses, which illustrates how mesmerizing these women were on the screen at the time and how they still have that magnetism for many today.” The exhibition charts the rise of fan culture, as well as the commercial industries that tapped actors’ and actresses’ marketing potential. The show includes samples of the dozens of weekly and monthly film magazines that gained a mass following, as well as scrapbooks featuring movie memorabilia and clippings, crafted by devoted fans. Additionally discussed is the close relationship between production companies and department stores to sell celebrity-endorsed or celebrity-emulating products.

The exhibition is sure to forge new fans of silent film, with its bevy of sumptuously rhinestoned, sequined, and feathered frocks, many representing couture-level craftsmanship. Cormack says “texture and reflectivity were extremely important, as designers were clearly designing for a body in motion” and maximum visual impact. She adds that early costume designs reflect a marked degree of “experimentation and curiosity” because they were a crucial tool for conveying character in the absence of audio.

“One thing I always love about these early films is the incredible clarity and crispness of the nitrate film. When you see one that has been restored, there’s a gorgeous contrast and depth to the black and white,” says Finamore on the distinctive visual experience of early cinema, which allows the costumes to literally and figuratively shine. “In our era of AI, it would be nice to draw people into the beauty of these truly handcrafted garments.”

Beyond their undeniable glamour and allure, the earliest garments in the exhibition signal a unique moment when New York was the center of not only the American fashion industry (in part, due to Parisian couture houses shuttering during World War I) but also its film industry. The first showing of a projected film in the U.S. took place in New York City in 1896, and by 1908 there were more than 200 movie theaters across the city.

Before the 1920s when film costume design became professionalized, actresses were often required by contract to supply their own onscreen fashions. While some sewed their garments, others spent their own earnings or were given allowances to work with their preferred designers (the show includes a note from Irene Castle to Madame Frances asking for a discount). A handful of New York City’s top fashion designers who also designed for the stage, such as Henri Bendel and Lucile (Lady Duff Gordon), became go-tos for the burgeoning silver screen (Goddesses in the Machine standouts include two looks Bendel designed for Lillian Gish in the 1920 film Way Down East; this is their first time being publicly exhibited). It was at New York ateliers helmed by Lucile, as well as Finamore’s favorite, the lesser-known designer Madame Frances, that several Hollywood costume designers, including Travis Banton and Howard Greer, would find their footing.

It was paramount for Finamore to showcase key players who hadn’t yet had “their moment in the sun.” “I kept coming across Madame Frances as being so actively involved in the theater industry and later the film industry, but then she just disappears,” says the curator, who loves “a fashion mystery.” Also featured is Odessa Warren Grey, a Harlem-based milliner, fashion designer, and actress, whose film-design career became known after the 2014 rediscovery of a fragment of Lime Kiln Club Field Day (1913), one of the earliest-known productions featuring a Black cast. “Many of these designers were female entrepreneurs, running their own boutiques that were very successful,” Finamore adds.

With the promise of warmer weather, a diverse landscape, and lower production costs, California began to overtake New York’s film dominance during the mid-1910s. In addition to Clare West (the first studio designer on the West Coast), Goddesses in the Machine spotlights several designers who rose to prominence during this era, including Natacha Rambova and Salvatore Ferragamo, as well as lesser-known talents, such as shoe designer George Brusinski. Part of the reason it’s so challenging to track costume designers’ contributions prior to this time is that they only began to be credited onscreen after 1917 (it was not until 1949 that the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences presented the first Best Costume Design award).

Film studios started maintaining their own costume collections, particularly for historical dramas and Westerns. As filming became more varied, so did its costumes. Dancing, horseback riding, driving, and swimming on screen required active attire, which consequently paved the way for more liberating garments offscreen. While bifurcated bottoms gradually became normalized, traditionally masculine formalwear became a way for actresses to convey courage and defiance.

It’s easy to imagine a West Coast iteration of Goddesses in the Machine, though Cormack believes “it would also be exciting to take the exhibition’s framework and tell stories about different film centers around the country, like in Chicago or Ithaca.” She notes, however, that the garments on view would need time to rest, or new objects could be chosen. While Finamore spent years tracking certain garments down for her dream exhibition, others are already coming out of the woodwork. “We have a picture of Aileen Pringle in a dramatic embellished jacket, and during installation, a curator of a museum that loaned several garments saw it and said he knew who had the garment,” says Finamore, hoping more pieces will reveal themselves.

Meanwhile another one of Finamore’s ambitions for the exhibition is already coming true: “I was really hoping that one of the outcomes of this exhibit would be to preserve a film that hasn’t been preserved or restored.” After realizing David Stenn, a film and TV writer and film preservationist, was “as obsessed with Madame Frances as [she] is,” Finamore asked about the possibility of restoring an early talkie about the designer’s life called On Your Back, which is in the UCLA Film & Television Archive. Thanks to Stenn, who is on the organization’s board, the film will eventually be able to be viewed again.

“We’re hoping this exhibition will spur further interest and research about fashion in early cinema,” Cormack adds. “If our visitors can walk away with a better understanding of the period and a greater sense of curiosity about what came before, then we will have been successful.”

Physicists have now suspended a single positron — the antimatter equivalent of an electron — in mid-air and cooled it to its lowest possible energy state.

The set-up will enable researchers to study magnetic properties of the positron with unprecedented precision. Physicists hope to spot subtle differences between matter and antimatter that could shed light on one of physics’ greatest mysteries.

No one knows why the world is made overwhelmingly of matter. Today’s best theory of particles and forces, the standard model, suggests that just as much antimatter should have been created during the Big Bang.

But when they come into contact, matter and antimatter — which have the same mass but are opposite in properties such as charge — disappear into a burst of energy. If they had been created in equal amounts, “all the matter and antimatter would annihilate and there’d be no Universe left”, says Gerald Gabrielse, a physicist at Northwestern University in Evanston, Illinois, who led the work.

Finding a mismatch in measurements between matter and antimatter “would be revolutionary in physics”, says Gabrielse. The standard model has proved maddeningly accurate at predicting experimental results despite being known to be incomplete. It cannot, for example, explain the apparent existence of dark matter, a substance thought to interact mainly through gravity.

The experiment, detailed in Physical Review Letters on 21 October1, is a “remarkable achievement” that will provide “a powerful probe for physics beyond the standard model”, says Barbara Maria Latacz, an antimatter physicist at CERN near Geneva, Switzerland. “I cannot wait to see their first precision measurements,” says Stefan Ulmer, a physicist at Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf (HHU) in Germany.

Since the 1960s, physicists have studied antimatter in ever-finer detail, hoping to find discrepancies with matter. They have observed a slight preference for matter in the behaviours of a handful of particles, but nowhere near enough to account for the world’s composition.

So far, exquisitely detailed comparisons of protons and antiprotons haven’t uncovered any such differences. Now, Gabrielse’s group hopes to compare a property that can be measured with 10,000 times greater precision in electrons and positrons: their ‘magnetic moments’, which determine how particles wobble on their axis in a magnetic field.

To do their experiments, the team first faced a significant challenge: capturing a positron. They started with fast-moving positrons emerging from radioactive decay and slowed them down by scattering them off atoms in a crystal of tungsten. Although many were annihilated, the team skimmed off the slowest of the survivors and fed them into a trap.

Stellar cores called white dwarfs come in an astonishing array of colors, including what scientists say is the first known green star and the reddest star ever found, new research reveals.

A white dwarf star is the dense, cooling core that remains after a low- to medium-mass star runs out of fuel. The new study, published Thursday (Oct. 15) in the journal RAS Techniques and Instruments, shows that white dwarfs come in far more colors than their name suggests.

White dwarf stars got their moniker because the first known examples glowed in either white or pale blue. The new study, however, suggests that "white" dwarfs also emit visible-light colors like pink, green, cyan, purple and yellow.

"Most stars, including most white dwarfs, behave like 'perfect radiators,' so their colour is set almost entirely by temperature. But nobody had really looked into what else might be possible for white dwarfs," Mark Hollands, an astronomer at the University of Warwick in the U.K. and first author of the study, said in a statement. "It turns out these incredibly dense stars can produce colours nobody expected, and some of them genuinely surprised me."

The variety of colors is possible due to white dwarfs' densities compared with main-sequence stars, the study suggests. Cooler white dwarfs have a density of about 1 gram per cubic centimeter, much like flowing water on Earth. The density of these white dwarfs is also millions of times denser than the sun's visible surface, or photosphere, leading to unusual conditions.

"Any trace elements that may be present, such as carbon, calcium and sodium, interact with the surrounding dense helium plasma and absorb light across a wider range of colours than they would at lower densities," the researchers said in a statement. "This shifts a star's colour away from the orange, white and blue [typical] of a perfect radiator."

White dwarfs are far away and small, making it difficult to verify their color. So, in the new study, astronomers inferred the colors using spectra, the distinctive chemical fingerprints produced by stars' light. The team analyzed the spectra within a model of color vision that the human eye can see, making the color pop out.

Scientists found that carbon-rich white dwarfs tend to be purple or pink, while those that contain debris from asteroids or comets are usually yellower. Cyan or pale-blue white dwarfs occur when hydrogen and helium molecules strike each other in very dense atmospheres.

The team also discovered WD J1330+6435 — a green star that contains rocky material. The green comes from the additional elements of calcium, lithium and sodium in the star's atmosphere, creating a unique "chartreuse green appearance" that would be visible to the human eye, if we could see it up close.

"The traces of sodium in the atmosphere of this white dwarf absorb the orange part of the spectrum, leaving behind a ghostly green glow," Hollands explained, adding that the one-of-a-kind green star "feels like something out of science fiction." The team also discovered what is likely the reddest star ever found. This white dwarf, called WD J1011+2845, is located roughly 48 light-years from our planet. It has carbon closer to its surface — because the element is being lifted from the interior, due to strong convection currents — that is bending the star's light. This process is making a "deep salmon-pink glow" because the carbon molecules are obstructing the star's other wavelengths.

Overall, in the sample of stars studied — 151 white dwarfs within 65 light-years of Earth — about 1 in 30 white dwarfs glowed in a color other than white. This could mean there are far more colorful stellar remnants littering the universe than anyone anticipated.

A fossil that sat in a museum collection for over a century is a previously unknown species of predatory dinosaur, researchers have found. The discovery could help scientists solve a longstanding mystery about how some of the Cretaceous period's most formidable hunters spread around the globe.

The dinosaur lived on what is now the Isle of Wight in southern England about 120 million years ago. It belonged to a group of two-legged carnivores called megaraptors, which are related to tyrannosaurs. But unlike tyrannousars, megaraptors are thought to have relied on their powerful arms and large curved claws to attack prey and defend themselves.

The newly identified species was described in an Oct. 14 study in the journal Royal Society Open Science. It is the first megaraptor identified in Europe, filling an important gap in the fossil record of a group whose evolutionary history remains poorly understood.

"It is a real mystery that this fossil was overlooked for so long, sitting in a drawer for nearly 100 years," study first author Cassius Morrison, a researcher in the Department of Earth Sciences at University College London (UCL) said in a statement. "Lots of bones are found on the Isle of Wight — who knows what else is waiting to be discovered."

The discovery of the megaraptor began in 1924, when an amateur fossil collector discovered a dinosaur skull fragment on the Isle of Wight. The specimen eventually entered the collections of London's Natural History Museum, but it was largely forgotten.

Recently, researchers revisited the fossil as part of a study on dinosaur braincases. To identify the animal, the researchers used micro-CT scanning to examine the fossil's internal structure and build detailed 3D digital models of the space that once held the dinosaur's brain.

The researchers then compared the animal's anatomy with that of other meat-eating dinosaurs, including tyrannosaurs, spinosaurs and other theropods (the group of two-legged, mostly meat-eating dinosaurs). Several features differentiated the unknown specimen, including an almost oval opening where the spinal cord entered the skull and an unusual, hourglass-shaped ridge at the back of the braincase.

Computer-based evolutionary analyses placed the fossils within the Megaraptora family tree, supporting the idea that the skull represented a never-before-seen species. The researchers named the dinosaur Invictibellatrix amberina, combining the Latin words "unconquered" and "female warrior" for the genus name, while amberina refers to amber found in the rocks associated with the fossil.

Megaraptors lived between roughly 125 million and 66 million years ago and varied considerably in size. While early members were relatively small, some later species became enormous predators. Maip macrothorax, discovered in Argentina, may have reached up to 33 feet (10 meters) long and weighed as much as an elephant. Based on comparisons with related dinosaurs, I. amberina was much smaller, likely around 10 to 13 feet (3 to 4 m) long and standing roughly 5 feet (1.5 m) tall. The researchers estimated it weighed around 440 to 660 pounds (200 to 300 kilograms), making it one of the smallest known megaraptors.

"It was not the apex predator of its time, but for humans this dinosaur would have been more dangerous than a T. rex," Morrison said. "We would have been perfectly sized prey, about a third of their body weight. They definitely would have chased us."

Although the newly described fossil doesn't preserve the animal's arms or claws, its closest relative suggests that its claws may have measured around 4 to 8 inches (10 to 20 centimeters) long. The researchers also suggested I. amberina may have been feathered, though that feature can't be determined directly from the specimen.

Scientists have long suspected that megaraptors originated in Asia before spreading to other parts of the world, eventually becoming the dominant predators across the southern supercontinent Gondwana, which included present-day Africa, South America and Antarctica. But a lack of European fossils left an important gap in verifying this migration route.

The new study's evolutionary models suggest that I. amberina descended from Asian megaraptors that dispersed into Europe around 130 million years ago. From there, members of the group may have moved through what is now Africa and further into Gondwana via temporary landbridges and temporary land connections across the shallow Tethys Ocean.

"This new species is the first proof we have that megaraptorans were also present in Europe and that they reached Gondwana this way rather than island-hopping further south," study co-author Charlie Scherer, a doctoral student at UCL Earth Sciences, said in the statement.

However, the specific route these animals took over millions of years is still unknown. The researchers also cannot yet explain why megaraptors disappeared from northern areas, but changes in climate or competition with other predatory dinosaurs may have contributed. More research on other megaraptor fossils like I. amberina could help uncover more clues.

"Work like this shows the exceptional value of working with historical museum collections where there are still many discoveries to be made," study co-author Paul Barrett, a researcher of dinosaur evolution at the Natural History Museum in London, said in the statement.

#5908. Make-Ahead Mashed Potatoes

Thanksgiving dinner can feel like a game of Tetris, but this make-ahead mashed potato recipe gives you one less thing to juggle. It lets you prepare the potatoes up to two days in advance, then reheat them just before serving without sacrificing their rich flavor or velvety texture.

The secret is in the technique: Simmer whole, skin-on Yukon Gold potatoes to keep them from absorbing excess water, then pass them through a potato ricer with plenty of butter. Adding the butter before the warm milk and cream coats the potato starches in fat, helping the mash stay smooth and creamy even after refrigeration. A little sour cream adds a welcome tang.

For extra flavor, try adding roasted garlic, finely grated Parmesan, or infusing the milk and cream, as we do in BA’s Best Mashed Potatoes, with bay leaves, thyme, or rosemary.

How far in advance can you make mashed potatoes?

Up to two days. Let the finished mashed potatoes cool slightly, then cover and refrigerate promptly. Need a longer head start? Learn how to freeze mashed potatoes.

How do you reheat mashed potatoes without making them gummy?

Gently warm them in a saucepan over medium-low heat with the additional milk and cream to restore the proper consistency. Stir often but gently to prevent scorching without overworking the potatoes, which can turn them gluey.

Place 4 lb. medium Yukon Gold potatoes, scrubbed, in a large pot and pour in cold water to cover by 1". Add a large handful of kosher salt (water should taste briny, like the ocean) and bring to a boil. Reduce heat and simmer until potatoes are very tender but not crumbly, 30–40 minutes. Drain and return potatoes to warm pot to dry (off heat).

Meanwhile, heat ⅔ cup heavy cream and ⅔ cup whole milk in a small saucepan over medium until just about to simmer. Reduce heat to low and keep warm until ready to add to potatoes.

Pass hot potatoes and 1¼ cups (2½ sticks) unsalted butter, cut into pieces, through ricer into a large bowl (this will help combine them quickly; most of the skins should stay behind, but pick out any small pieces that get through if you want); season generously with salt. Gradually add half of the warm cream mixture to mashed potatoes, folding with a spatula until combined, followed by ½ cup sour cream. Gradually add remaining cream mixture until you reach your desired consistency (the potatoes will thicken slightly as they sit). Taste and season with salt and freshly ground black pepper as desired.

Transfer mashed potatoes to a baking dish or other shallow container, let them cool until the potatoes stop steaming, then cover and chill up to 2 days.

To serve, transfer refrigerated potatoes to a small pot and add ⅓ cup heavy cream and ⅓ cup whole milk; reheat over medium-low, stirring often (but gently) to prevent scorching.

But apparently, being handcrafted specifically for someone isn't enough anymore—you also have to wear their merch.

Skarsgård—who has been working with Harry Lambert, the longtime stylist of Harry Styles—was wearing a custom look by the London-based brand Chopova Lowena. Printed on the beaded jersey was a portrait of Colman as the fisherwoman, with little spiky embellishments framing her face. It was quite sweet, actually. The sorta thing your mom might have on the mantelpiece, only that in this case, it was stretched across a six-foot-something Swedish man.

He wore it with the brand's signature taffeta midi skirt in green and pink tartan, complete with pleats and silver carabiners. Plus, black socks. Black shoes. An Oscar-winning actress on his torso. And to think that getting dressed for a red carpet used to involve calling someone about a tux.

Alexander Skarsgard, in custom Chopova Lowena, at the Wicker premiere in Los Angeles.

And look, this does make the rest of us seem a bit lazy. There are guys out there who won't even put their girlfriend on their main grid. Skarsgård has put his on-screen wife right there on his chest—with beading! Your anniversary Instagram story with the tiny heart in the corner is looking a little half-assed now, isn't it?

Imagine going for dinner with someone wearing this. You're sitting opposite them, trying to work out what you want, and there you are again, just below the bread basket. They take their jacket off. More you. The waiter comes over and immediately knows who's paying. (There would also be the question of which photograph was chosen, because “I had a top made with your face on it” is lovely, in theory. “I had a top made with that picture of you that you've asked me to delete six times” is a very different evening.)

For Skarsgård, though, it helps that Colman was right there beside him, looking very pleased with the whole thing. Which is probably the response you'd want after going to all that trouble. Especially if the top's custom. You're not getting a refund on Olivia.
