#4050. Michael Strahan Shares the Words That Helped His Daughter Through Brain Cancer Treatment

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

For years, Michael Strahan appeared in commercials for St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital, delivering a message he never imagined would become so personal: “No parent should have to hear that their child has cancer.”

But in 2023, the beloved TV host and former NFL player faced that reality when his daughter, Isabella, was diagnosed with brain cancer at 19 years old.

“[That] line that I used to deliver, usually at the end of the commercial…here I am actually filling that line in my life in real time,” Strahan told Healthline.

Strahan believes his longtime connection with St. Jude was more than a coincidence. After years of advocating for families affected by childhood cancer, he found himself on the other side of that experience.

“When I needed that family…they were there for me,” he said. “I’m just appreciative, and I think things come back to you; give out good, good comes back.”

In October 2023, Isabella began experiencing vomiting, headaches, and balance issues during her freshman year at the University of Southern California (USC). Tests revealed that she had medulloblastoma, a fast-growing cancerous brain tumor located in the cerebellum. She underwent emergency brain surgery, radiation therapy, and four rounds of chemotherapy.

While medulloblastoma typically occurs in children between the ages of 3 and 9, it can also affect young adults like Isabella.

“[The] biology in adolescents and young adults can differ meaningfully from pediatric cases, requiring specialized expertise at centers of excellence for this disease,” Arnab Chakravarti, MD, director of the Brain Tumor Program at The Ohio State University, told Healthline.

Medulloblastoma is sometimes confused with glioblastoma, the most common type of malignant primary brain tumor in adults. Glioblastoma is particularly aggressive, with a median overall survival of just 15 months and a 5-year survival rate of less than 10%.

However, Chakravarti explained that medulloblastoma has a significantly higher cure rate than glioblastoma.

“[This is] a point of genuine hope that deserves to be communicated clearly,” he said.

Radiation therapy is a cornerstone of medulloblastoma treatment. Isabella received proton therapy, which uses precisely targeted radiation to treat tumors while limiting exposure to surrounding healthy tissue.

“For medulloblastoma, this physical precision is transformative,” Chakravarti said. “Proton craniospinal irradiation dramatically reduces [collateral] exposure — preserving cochlear function and hearing, reducing cardiovascular risk, protecting the thyroid, and most critically, sparing developing brain tissue in ways that translate into measurably better neurocognitive outcomes years after treatment.”

He added that multiple prospective studies have shown that children and young adults treated with proton craniospinal irradiation experience less cognitive decline and better quality of life compared to those treated with conventional radiation.

Today, Isabella is back at USC, embracing the everyday routines she once had to put on hold.

“I am back at USC, so…it’s been great, in the midst of exams, so it’s a little stressful, but I actually [am] inviting the stress in because at one point I didn’t have exams, so I’m happy to be back,” Isabella told Healthline.

Along with attending classes, Isabella is enjoying activities such as working out and cooking. She continues to undergo MRI scans of her head and spine every three months, but said that after another year, the scans will become less frequent, occurring every six months.

“But really…I don’t have a lot of doctor’s appointments,” she said.

“It feels like a brand-new life, but it’s a good one. And that’s wonderful,” Isabella said.

Isabella said her family’s support played an important role in helping her through cancer treatment. Spending so much time together in the hospital brought them closer as they watched movies and played cards and board games to pass the time.

“[It] really, really bonds you to people, and just having my dad and my whole family there was something that [you] can’t beat,” she told Healthline.

Michael said that despite the difficult circumstances, he cherishes the time he spent with his daughter. Before her diagnosis, Isabella was a typical teenager who spent much of her time at school or with friends.

“They say you spend the most time with your kid for the first few years of their life, and then once school starts, you don’t get a chance to spend as much time as you would like with them,” he said.

While Michael said he and Isabella already had a close relationship, her diagnosis brought the family even closer and helped them reflect on what mattered most.

“[What] was really important was family, and relationships, and the love that you have for each other, and the support you have to give each other,” he said. “I think that our relationship now is better than ever.”

Today, Michael makes an effort to visit Isabella at USC whenever he’s in Los Angeles during the NFL season.

“Some weekends she [has time for me]. Some she doesn’t,” he said. “But just to have that relationship with your child, where they want to hang out with you, is a good feeling, and that’s the relationship that we have.”

Michael and Isabella are sharing their story to raise awareness about brain cancer and offer hope to other families facing similar challenges.

They recently participated in a fireside chat in New York City hosted by Real Chemistry and SMAC, where they discussed their family’s experience navigating Isabella’s diagnosis, mental health, caregiving, and the impact of sharing their story.

Throughout her treatment, Isabella also documented her experience through a series of YouTube vlogs and the ABC primetime documentary, “Life Interrupted: Isabella Strahan’s Fight to Beat Cancer.”

For Michael, helping others is a value he learned from his parents. He recalled how his father used his transportation business in Germany to support people in need after retiring from the military.

“[He] would take donations, and use his money and his trucks, and drive stuff to Yugoslavia and Czechoslovakia, and drop it off there at local churches and the shelters for people, who he had no connection with,” said Michael. “So for me, I think I do what I feel is on my heart and what’s important to me.”

For parents caring for children with cancer, Michael emphasized that it’s OK to feel overwhelmed or emotional. He encouraged them to show their children love and support while helping them maintain hope for the future.

“I think positivity is the main thing because it was so tough on her at the time when she was [saying], ‘I can’t do it. I can’t do this. I can’t do that,’” said Michael.

Michael said he made a conscious effort to replace words like “can’t” and “if” with “when” while encouraging Isabella during treatment.

“Anytime she got down on herself, I’d say, ‘when you go back to college, when you do this, when you do that,’” said Michael. “And I just think the positive attitude and the love that you can give and the confidence that you can display towards your loved one [or] your child who’s going through something like this is the only thing you can do because you’re at the mercy of medicine.”

Now in her senior year at USC, Isabella is looking ahead to life after graduation.

“[There’s] a lot of interesting opportunities and things that I want to pursue,” she said.

Michael has no doubt she will find her way.

“She’s so talented, and this experience, in my opinion, has opened up my eyes to more talent that she has that I didn’t realize…the talent to connect people, and be in front of a crowd. I always thought she’d be nervous because I get nervous, but she’s better than me,” he said.

#4049. The remarkable bond between a man and a deer

Published Oct 18, 2026 3:00 PM EDT

Excerpted from PENELOPE by Paul Bochner. Copyright © 2026 by Paul Bochner. Published in the United States by Crown, an imprint of the Crown Publishing Group, a division of Penguin Random House LLC.

On the evening of March 18, as it was growing dark, I went to the window, not knowing what was drawing me. Next to the house stood a shadow, ghost-like, shrunken, at one with the dusk, almost translucent, and I recognized The Girl with the Broken Foot.

I walked out into the falling wet snow, slowly approaching her, and she allowed me to come close. Nine months old, she was the size of a fawn half that age. Starkly emaciated, her body seemed to be collapsing inward, her head held low. Literal hunger and a deeper starvation were palpable. She bore the haunted look of terminal solitude seen in souls driven to the margins of society, among the liv-ing, yet apart. She seemed so frail that I couldn’t imagine she would live more than another day or two.

Going inside and returning with a bowl of apples, I cut them into pieces and placed a few on the ground between us. She was raven-ously hungry and I gave her a few more apples, and still more, until she had eaten a total of eight. After she had finished eating, she stayed with me. We stood about a foot apart. She showed no fear of me, a fact which I would later come to recognize as meaningful. The moment had the compelling quality which has been present at the onset of many enduring connections in my life, of being un-bound by linear time.

“I am watching over you,” I said softly. “If you come here, I will take care of you. You are not alone.”

It was fully dark when we parted, having been together for an hour. I expected that she would recede into the woods, yet the next morning she was sleeping against the house, directly beneath the window closest to where I sit all day to work. Save for the wall separating us, she could not have gotten any closer to me.

When I went around to the front of the house, she struggled to her feet and approached. In the morning light, I was better able to assess the state of her health. Months of solitary survival had taken a severe toll. Her body seemed hollowed, her ribs and pelvis sharply visible beneath her skin, and it felt a miracle that she was alive. On her right flank was a hemispherical mass the size of a cantaloupe, which I took to be a tumor, yet it had an open, running wound at its center, which she periodically craned her neck to lick. Her eyes were running and had congealed masses at their inner corners.

I bent to get a look at her injured foot. From not being walked on, the backward-facing cloven hoof had grown to an abnormal length and was curling upward. The wrist, which unnaturally bore her weight, had a flattened forward surface, an oval about two inches across, worn raw, without hair or skin, only pale pink–grey flesh, which, at its center, appeared to have been further abraded to the bone. Her right front foot, forced to compensate, was splayed and rockered, her right scapula protruded, and her front quarters were substantially lower than her hips.

I again placed apple pieces on the ground between us. I spoke very gently to her, saying, “You are the best one of all the deer. You are the most beautiful. You are good.” I continued to talk to her while she ate her apples, and when she had taken all she could, she returned to her resting spot under the window. I went inside to work, keeping periodic watch, pleased that she seemed to have claimed a place. But in a few minutes, from around the corner of the house came the twins, followed by their mother who immediately and ag-gressively charged at her. The Girl with the Broken Foot lurched upright and hobbled down the embankment, across the road and into the woods.

She was once again alone, but a step had been taken. Since the previous evening, things had changed. Even before her second feed-ing she had recognized me on sight. She had approached and had again allowed me near.

Although I had seen her fewer than half a dozen times in the preceding months, from the night of our first connection, she began to come to the house every day. She slowly gained strength and I would watch for her and thrill to the sight of her slow approach. It was our private time. We stood together, and I placed her food on the ground between us. After she ate, she would stare into my eyes for long periods, seemingly entranced. I told her stories. I sang softly to her. I told her she was my beauty.

So began our journey, and one of the greatest loves I have ever known.

#4048. No, I am not evil for wanting to turn space whale city-builder Beyond These Stars into a Moby Dick simulator

I am simply tormented with an everlasting itch for things remote

I think I'm a pretty decent Space Whale Mayor, all told. A caring and responsible fellow traveler for Kewa, the astral cetacean on whose craggy, cloud-hung back every city in Beyond These Stars is founded. For each hexagon of whaledirt I transform into a mine or a smelter, I give back to the creature in some way – adding saplings to the forests that emit my city's bubble of breathable air, or spreading compost across the cracked stone of Kewa's neglected extremities. I have unblocked the river that runs along their spine, irrigating the badlands and rousing long-dormant seeds from the dust.

OK, so my progress hasn't been tidy. I did manage to poison half the river, after getting a bit extravagant with wood-fired power plants, but I'm swiftly repairing the damage, having built three automated tile cleaners on each bank, while pausing nearby steel production to avoid adding any more slag and soot to the water. Meanwhile, my hedge gardens gleam like jewels socketed in amongst the dull bronze wires of my roads. My foresters labour zealously to push back the grey. My people are full of good cheer, despite living on an exclusive diet of tea and potatoes. They don't much like the stinking, iridescent river, but they do like all the playgrounds and fountains I've set up near the major workplaces.

Some day, we will paint this whole whale green. Some day, we will unlock the secret of communicating with Kewa and doubtless, bask in their praise and gratitude, while gaining some insight on their movements through the cosmos - including how long we have to dispatch foraging expeditions to any planets in a given solar system. I am staying with the trouble, living the principle of mutual thriving. I am nothing like those assholes who play Factorio. Except… except sometimes I get Bad Thoughts. Sometimes, I wonder what it might be like to live on a really screwed-up Space Whale. I imagine letting the whole river turn purple. I imagine running the furnaces all night. I imagine desolating the orchards, shrinking the atmosphere to a reeking dome of industry, while my Peeps sicken and die or go on strike. Yes, hahaha, yes!

I'm not being entirely contrarian. I've played a few 'sustainably minded' builders or anti-builders – favourites include The Wandering Village, Generation Exile, and Terra Nil - and while I enjoy the antidote they offer to the dead-eyed capitalism of SimCity and so on, eco-friendly projects such as these are at permanent risk of becoming Cosy. Even Wholesome! You need some nastiness, if you want the Whole Earth vibe to feel meaningful rather than ornamental. As the saying goes: to enjoy bodily warmth, some small part of you must be cold. I'm not sure Beyond These Stars has that dog in it, though I've enjoyed the three hours I've played.

Space Whale element aside, Beyond These Stars is a sturdy metropolis-herder with a few rough edges. The bugbears may be familiar, if you've played many games like this. The interface is a little indigestible: while there are plenty of highlighted overlays, production graphs, and pop-up map icons, it can be a chore working out that, say, your foundry is refusing to output steel because the smelter supplying it with iron has no power, because the power plant has no wood shavings to burn… because you forgot you put all your lumberjacks on furlough 20 minutes ago, in order to free up workers for the potato patches.

The effort of finding bottlenecks feels like it will fade with practice, but I suspect I'm always going be slightly at odds with the construction interface, which sees you dragging out roads and plonking down sites alongside them. Buildings tend to fill 1-3 hexagons. I find these configurations hard to picture, partly because my spatial reasoning has been ravaged by 20 years of videogame journalism, and partly because the architecture itself doesn't always correspond visually to the hexagonal template. It can be a faff preserving gaps, so as not to block roads off, especially before you unlock power line technology - initially, buildings that require electricity must be stuffed together in a line. Fortunately, you can relocate buildings with minimal penalty.

More positively, Beyond These Stars does a fair job of making research feel organic by tethering unlocks to in-game accomplishments, such as knocking out five copper bars, rather than having some designated Science Huts cough up Science Points. It weaves the tech tree into the city's development in a pleasing way. Annoying templates aside, I also like the look and feel of the architecture. It's both rustic and futuristic, as though somebody had grafted warp nacelles to a chicken coop. And I'm enjoying the push-and-pull of meeting the needs of your Peeps and expanding toward resources, without ruining the humpback geography.

The game's ethic of looking after the 'living, breathing' (but literally!!!) world beneath manifests in lots of little ways. There's persistent pressure to keep noxious byproduct in check by turning buildings off, or setting them to only operate on demand. You have to decide where to hold off and spread the trees, however much you might need that land for farming. You have to keep hunting for pockets of ground you can rewild, such as cleared-out resource deposits, to counter the fallout from new factories on the perimeter.

How well you tend to the biosphere affects not just the air and soil quality but Kewa's health and mood. Sickly, morose Space Whales sprout thorny patches that make your citizens sad, while robust, life-loving leviathans are covered in flowers that do the opposite. There are larger impacts to explore in the course of the game's singleplayer campaign: apparently, Kewa is an amnesiac, recovering their past over the course of the story. The memories in question aren't necessarily great news for Kewa's human barnacle infestation.

If I play more, it'll be in vicious hope of things getting really bad. Campaign aside, Beyond These Stars has a sandbox mode and a set of themed scenarios. The scenarios include runs where you only get to build on planets, as in the developer's previous (and moderately well-received) Before We Leave. The sandbox lets you adjust resource availability and other factors so as to make life really punishing for yourself and Kewa. Perhaps given a sufficiently crappy sandbox, and just the right amount of neglect, I can drive Kewa to despair. What a treat that'd be, roaming the galaxy aboard a crazed and depressed Space Whale, at least until Kewa finds a sufficiently huge asteroid and manages to scrape me off. Circan’s November 2025 video game sales report has easily been one of the most anticipated. For one, November is always one of the most lucrative for the games industry, but this year in particular has seen several major hits - including new entries in two rival series.

We’re talking, of course, about Battlefield 6, and Black Ops 7 the latest releases in their respective multiplayer shooter series. The decades-old fight to claim the top spot has long been won by Call of Duty, but the latest Battlefield has achieved what many thought was impossible.

Cricana’s report covers spending on video game software, hardware, and accessories in the U.S. November 2025 saw spending fall by 4% compared to November 2024, bringing in a total of $5.9 billion.

Separately from the best-selling game title fight, November has actually been one of the worst for the industry in terms of hardware sales. The report notes that the three major platform holders - Sony, Microsoft, and Nintendo have all seen their hardware sales decline.

This all doesn’t bode especially well for the industry. November has always been a key holiday shopping month, but it’s clear the constantly rising console prices - and already high game prices - have had a big impact.

#4046. Expert ‘bat wranglers’ put GPS trackers on California’s bats

Published Oct 19, 2026 9:00 AM EDT

October is officially Bat Appreciation Month. In the spirit of the season and conservation, a team of biologists in California recently spent time finding bats at Muir Woods National Monument and Samuel P. Taylor State Park. And while Halloween is fast approaching, the activity had nothing to do with spooky festivities and everything to do with tracking hoary (Lasiurus cinereus) and western red bats (Lasiurus blossevillii).

The goal of their survey was to put GPS trackers on bats from the two migratory species. Hoary bats have grey-white fur and are the most widespread bat species in North, Central, and South America. Western red bats, also known as southern red bats, sport a reddish fur and are found in parts of South America. In redwood forests, bats like nesting within holes inside large trees.

“When they migrate, those little guys can cover a LOT of ground!” the Golden Gate National Recreation Area writes, referencing both species. “By learning about these migratory patterns, we can better understand entire ecosystems like Muir Woods.”

Bats are important members of such ecosystems. They are pollinators, they spread seeds, and they help manage insect populations. To catch these helpful agents, scientists set up a sheer, delicate net called a “mist net” over a creek at dusk. All bats are nocturnal, and when they went searching for their next meal—bugs—they flew into the net.

It was a bit of a trick for the winged mammals, but a treat for the human team. “Expert bat wranglers” carefully took them out of the nets, noted their weight and size, presumably attached the GPS tracker, and then returned them to the wild.

The Golden Gate National Recreation Area’s shared pictures of tiny, disgruntled looking bats carefully pinched between gloved fingers. In one image, an expert holds a bat wing up to the light of a colleague’s headlamp. In fact, illuminated wing membrane patterns literally shed light on the bat’s age and developmental stage.

While this survey focused on two bat species, researchers at the Muir Woods National Monument have previously spotted 10 different species.

#4043. Hormone Therapy May Help Improve Menopause Brain Fog, Mood Changes

  • Hormone therapy may affect changes in the brain that occur during menopause. 
  • A recent study has shown that hormone therapy may help improve “brain fog” symptoms during menopause.
  • Another study found that hormone therapy may improve mood changes that are often associated with menopause. 
  • Other research has alleviated long-standing concerns over a possible link between menopause hormone therapy and dementia. 

Menopause hormone therapy (MHT), also known as hormone replacement therapy (HRT), helps manage various menopause symptoms. 

Currently, MHT is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to help relieve hot flashes, night sweats, vaginal dryness, and dyspareunia (pain during sex). 

However, recent research has found that MHT may also help improve mental symptoms like “brain fog” and mood changes. 

Other research also found that MHT does not affect dementia risk. This research followed the FDA’s removal of the black box warning on these products, which suggested they may increase the risk.  

Together, these studies show that MHT may help people experiencing menopause in ways not previously realized and that it is generally safe to use. 

Hormone therapy may ease  menopause ‘brain fog’ 

Research published in Scientific Reports on September 1 suggests that an estrogen hormone treatment that combines estriol and progesterone may help ease the symptoms of brain fog during menopause. 

Brain fog may involve memory lapses, difficulty concentrating, and slower thinking. 

Just about two-thirds of women experience cognitive difficulties, like brain fog, during the menopausal transition. However, there is currently no Food and Drug Administration (FDA)- approved treatment for these symptoms. 

This study was an observational case series that followed 20 menopausal women with an average age of 53. Each participant was given a baseline assessment for cognitive symptoms prior to treatment. 

They were then treated for 12 months with a customized regimen of estriol and progesterone.

Estriol is a natural form of estrogen that occurs during pregnancy and has been used in Europe and Asia to treat other symptoms of menopause. 

After 12 months, the participants reported significant reductions in: 

  • brain fog
  • concentration 
  • working memory 
  • verbal memory
  • processing speed
  • problem solving

“Estrogen plays a well-documented role in protecting the brain, yet there are still no approved type and dose of treatment that specifically targets the cognitive symptoms so many women experience during menopause,” Rhonda Voskuhl, MD, neurologist, member of the Comprehensive Menopause Center at UCLA Health, and senior study author, said in a press release. 

“Women are often told to simply live with brain fog, when in fact there is a neuroscience basis for it, and potentially a way to address it. That gap in care is what motivated us to look more closely at estriol,” Voskuhl said. 

This was a small, preliminary case series without a placebo group or randomization. More research is needed to confirm the benefits for cognitive symptoms. 

Key points

  • Brain fog is a common symptom of menopause with no approved treatment. 
  • A recent study found that hormone therapy with estriol and progesterone may help improve cognitive symptoms during menopause. 
  • More research is needed to confirm the effects of hormone therapy on cognitive symptoms. 

Hormone therapy may help with menopause mood changes

A study published September 9 in Menopause, the journal of the Menopause Society, showed that hormone therapy may help improve mood changes in women in midlife. 

The researchers noted that mood disturbances, such as depression, irritability, anxiety, and mental exhaustion, affect up to 68% of women during menopause. 

“The erratic changes and loss of estrogen associated with this hormonal transition can lower serotonin and dopamine levels, which [are] associated with depression and cognitive changes, including poor concentration, brain fog, and memory loss,” said Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB/GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study. 

The study included 260 individuals with an average age of 52 years. Just over half of the participants had a history of anxiety or depression, and 24% were using an antidepressant medication at the start of the study. 

Each participant completed the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) both before and after initiating hormone therapy for FDA-approved indications, such as hot flashes and night sweats. 

The average follow-up during the study was 4.5 months. Between the baseline and the follow-up, there was a significant reduction in the MRS psychological scores. The percentage of participants with severe mood symptoms decreased from 62.3% to 24.6%. 

Treatment response didn’t appear to differ by age, menopausal stage, psychiatric history, or antidepressant use. These findings suggest that hormone therapy may help improve mood symptoms in midlife, especially for those with higher severity of symptoms before treatment. 

“This study supports the evidence that starting hormone therapy for the typical disruptive symptoms of menopause may bring collateral benefits, including improvement in mood,” Ross said. “Communicating with your healthcare provider is always best to manage your symptoms and prepare yourself properly for this next stage of life.” 

Key points

  • A recent study showed that hormone therapy may help improve mood changes associated with menopause. 
  • The biggest improvement was seen in those with more severe symptoms before beginning treatment. 
  • Improvement with hormone therapy was seen despite age, menopause stage, and psychiatric history. 

Menopause hormone therapy not linked to dementia

For decades, women have been warned about the serious potential risks associated with menopause hormone therapy (MHT). This included an FDA “black box” warning citing complications like cardiovascular disease, cancer, and dementia. 

However, a recent meta-analysis commissioned by the World Health Organization (WHO) that included data from more than a million women, and was published in The Lancet Healthy Longevity, found that MHT neither increased nor decreased dementia risk. 

“The World Health Organisation currently provides no guidance on MHT and dementia risk, leaving a critical gap for women, clinicians, and policymakers. Our work will help inform upcoming WHO guidelines on reducing the risk of cognitive decline and dementia,” Melissa Melville, a PhD candidate and aging researcher in the Department of Clinical Health Psychology at the University College London, and first author of the study, told Healthline in a previous interview. 

In the same interview, Melville stressed that the importance of this work is twofold: for clinicians, there is an urgent need for higher-quality research, and for patients, decisions about MHT should be guided by symptoms and well-established benefits and risks.

Key points

  • A new meta-analysis has helped to ease long-standing fears regarding the association between menopause hormone therapy and dementia. 
  • The analysis reviewed data from more than a million women and found that MHT neither raises nor decreases the risk of dementia. 
  • The researchers noted that decisions about MHT should be guided by symptoms and well-established benefits and risks.

The old timey version of asking for a cup of sugar

Going Medieval may have been in early access for almost five years, but even after it arrived in 1.0 back in March developer Foxy Voxel have continued to build upon it. The sim's latest update has arrived today, bringing with it a whole bunch of diplomacy. Relations? With other people? Perish the thought! But I suppose sometimes there are some benefits to such a thing.

#4039. This snake cartwheels to escape danger

Published Oct 19, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

Snakes will bite, rattle their tails, and even spit venom to escape danger. But of all the world’s species, southeast Asia’s dwarf reed snake (Pseudorabdion longiceps) relies on an especially fascinating escape method. When threatened, the diminutive serpent rolls away like a speeding wheel.

An adult dwarf reed snake typically measures less than 12 inches long, but its unique locomotive maneuvers allow it to retreat as fast one foot per second—about four times faster than it travels by simply slithering on the ground. It’s not a flailing getaway, either. The snake also easily rolls along a straight path while somehow keeping its head both stable and facing forward.

Now, researchers finally have a better understanding of how the dwarf reed snake achieves such dizzying acrobatics. In a study published today in the journal Current Biology, a team at Japan’s Toho University detailed the specific physiology that enables its locomotion after filming the snakes with high-speed cameras. Essentially, the snake doesn’t rotate its entire body in a singular, forward motion—it whips one side of its body forward towards the ground while an arched portion swings down by its head in what the study’s authors call a “stroke.” The snake then alternates between its left and right side coming into contact with the ground for each successive stroke. Essentially, it’s like twirling a rope in a lateral circle while holding one end in your hand—except that end is the snake’s head.

The cumulative efforts bypass the traditional idea of a wheel requiring something like a central axle. Instead, this ingenious, highly controlled movement allows the animal to keep its head and sensory organs upright and facing ahead while traveling at such high speed. This is compounded by the dwarf reed snake directing regions of its body to engage in large, alternating rotations while stabilizing its head.

By gaining better insight into these biological mechanisms, researchers also highlight the difficulty in trying to predict an animal’s capabilities based solely on its physiology. A snake may have primarily evolved to slither—but that doesn’t mean it can’t get creative when necessary.

#4035. Why You Are Drawn to Partners Who Need 'Fixing'

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Updated October 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

As I’ve sat across from many people who are empathetic, giving, and caring, I’ve heard them ask the same question over and over again: "Why, why do I keep falling for people I have to save?!”

Their dating history looks less like courtship and more like scavenging: the addicted, the financially unstable, or the emotionally unavailable. And they always carry the same belief: With enough love and patience, my loved one will finally become the partner I deserve.

It is what has been informally known in psychology as savior complex.

Most people would assume it’s maybe bad luck or poor judgement about partners. But the pattern is much older than any of your relationships. To understand why you seek out the broken, we need to look at what happened when you were small.

In a healthy family, a child is a subject—a person with valid feelings, desires, and boundaries. In a narcissistic family, the child is something else, namely an extension, a function, or a commodity meant to meet the parent's needs.

Dan Shaw describes this traumatic narcissism as a relational system in which the parent seeks control over the child's inner life to regulate their own fragile self-esteem.

As a child, you were drafted into roles you were never meant to hold. You were cast as the friend-therapist, listening to your mother's marital problems. You were the emotional regulator, walking on eggshells to prevent your father's rage. So, you learned early that your value was tied to your usefulness, and that to be loved means to be useful.

Over time, this became a belief: I exist to help. I am loved for what I do, not for who I am. And the better I save someone, or endeavor to fix their life, the more they value my presence.

If you grew up believing your job was to fix your parent's inner world, then a healthy, stable partner can feel very strange and even boring.You seek out the broken because they feel familiar, something you know from home.

The savior complex is self-sabotaging because without a crisis to manage, you no longer know how to relate. Even when the fixer-upper starts to get better—e.g., they find a job or overcome an addiction—Savior, who devoted everything to this moment, may feel empty, or even irritated. Automatically, you stay stuck in what Shaw calls a "relational system of subjugation," in which your partner’s autonomy might feel like betrayal because your autonomy was deemed betrayal by your parents.

Better dating strategies don’t really reach the root of the savior complex. Consider the following steps instead.

Step 1: Legalize the Anger. Many adult children of narcissists feel guilty for being angry at their parents. But anger is a normal response to having your boundaries violated. You need to grieve the parent you never had and the childhood you sacrificed. And grieving includes expressing and dealing with your anger about the dear thing you've either lost or never had.

Step 2: Rewrite the Introjected Voice. You need to identify the parental voice inside your head that tells you that you are selfish for having needs. This means entering a dialogue with this voice and setting boundaries, both within yourself and with others. It will include learning that no is pretty much a complete sentence.

Step 3: Lear to Tolerate Calm. If chaos has been your baseline, peace will feel wrong at first. You may interpret a partner's steadiness as indifference, or you may find yourself provoking conflict just to feel something familiar. This phase is for retraining your nervous system to accept safety without sabotaging it.

Step 4: Establish the Non-Narcissistic Relationship. Therapy often becomes your first experience of a relationship where you are not being used. In this space, the therapist provides what we call affective mirroring—seeing you for who you are, not for what you can do.

If you find yourself in the savior complex trap, remember that your desire to help is a good part of you. You don’t need to divest yourself of it and become selfish or egotistical. But it was forged in a fire you were never meant to endure, one that engendered the habit of fixing and saving others, at the expense of your own well-being. And this habit is worth “fixing.”

I practiced on an actual trail only once through the whole cycle, and those Ohio hills were a far cry from the Alps. But I also remembered something Troyer had told me about a week before the race: “In marathoning it's all about PRs, and then in trail running, it's completion. You finish the race, that's awesome,” he said. “I think it's kind of liberating—now it's kind of the purest form of racing for me. It’s people against people, and time doesn't matter because the course changes.”

I truly let go of my temptation to chase numbers on that ascent. And as I began to eye my competitors, I couldn’t help but think about the old adage that you can’t run someone else’s race. I realized as I was fighting to make my way up those first hills that on the trail, it goes a step further: You can only run the race the trail gives you. If you try to push beyond, there are too many factors that will humble you, quick. You can come prepared and equipped as best you can, but once the gun goes off, you’re going against the terrain, the weather, your stomach—the list goes on.

“There's so much more problem solving,” Woods said. “There are so many other factors that come into play.”

Once the asphalt roads turned to wooded trails, I found my rhythm. I was climbing slowly, putting the deliberate training on my stairwells into practice. I kept putting one foot in front of the other, using my climbing poles for extra purchase, and eventually, I just found a rhythm. I didn’t feel like I had dismissed my mind’s compulsion to check my speed by flipping a switch; instead, I had quieted the impulse and embraced the process of moving through my environment, taking careful steps to avoid slipping and managing my energy for all the steps to come.

Woods had talked about this. “You're not turning your brain off—we're not like, meditating out in nature,” she said. “Your brain is still on, but in a different capacity. You're focusing on different things.”

Aside from staying upright and managing my energy, what I was most focused on was how lucky I was to be there at that moment. I ran through heavily wooded paths, jumped from rock to rock on scrambles, and felt like one of the Fellowship in The Lord of the Rings out in the wide open, surrounded my mountains on every side. Time in nature has been shown to improve mental health—and the sense of wonder from being in the Alps felt like another source of fuel to keep me moving.

That’s around the time that I finally checked my watch to do more than just keep up with my fueling. I wasn’t as far along the course as I expected I might be, but I wasn’t concerned. It was hot and I was barely moving at a clip that would be considered running. I didn’t care. I was running with the trail, not against my watch. That was the key.

After almost eight hours on my feet, I finally entered Chamonix, where the town’s streets were staged for an epic last stretch to the finish line. Crowds of people ringed the road and cheered for runners, a stark shift from the quiet wooded paths I had just left. After the hills, woods, and wide open valleys, I was back on pavement. But even as I steeled myself to go faster, running free of the obstructions I had mastered behind me, checking my pace wasn’t a concern. My sense of satisfaction and accomplishment beat any feeling I’ve had beating a benchmark. The trail gave me far more. When I stopped my watch after finishing, the numbers didn’t mean as much as the experience as a whole.

The space around Earth is turning into a junkyard. Researchers think a refundable deposit scheme could force satellite operators to clean up their act before a tipping point is reached.

For years, space sustainability experts have warned that the growing number of satellites in orbit and the quantity of dead metal left behind threatens to trigger a dangerous cascade of collisions known as the Kessler Syndrome. Predicted in the 1970s by NASA physicist Donald Kessler, this chain of orbital smashups could make the space around Earth unusable and unsafe.

The dangerous tipping might be nearer than some think. Humankind's utilization of space is at an inflection point, as companies such as SpaceX, Blue Origin and Amazon plan to launch constellations of hundreds of thousands of telecommunication satellites and orbiting data centers in the coming years.

"Low Earth orbit looks like lots of other resources that we have trashed as economic actors," Daniel Kaffine, a professor of environmental and resource economics at University of Colorado Boulder, told Space.com.

"Waiting until it gets really, really bad to start implementing any of these types of policy interventions has historically been what we have done," Kaffine added. "But maybe we can think about things we can do right now."

In a paper published last month in the journal Nature Communications, Kaffine and his collaborators propose a scheme that would require satellite operators to post a bond of $200,000 before launch, refundable with interest upon the completion of a successful deorbit. Companies that fail to remove their junk would lose the money, which could go toward a shared pool funding active debris-removal missions, the researchers said.

"You could possibly raise billions of dollars by that," said Kaffine. "We think it's a very neat policy that doesn't require international coordination."

International bodies, such as the Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee, ask satellite operators to remove their spacecraft from orbit within 25 years after the mission's end. The American Federal Communications Commission (FCC) imposes an even stricter standard — a five-year requirement for satellites in low Earth orbit. Still, a staggering 60% of satellites remain in place when their propellant runs out, the researchers say, as no consequences ensue if they fail to deorbit.

Solving the problem on the international level would be difficult, as international bodies have no powers to enforce compliance. Moreover, the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS), which sets down rules for the peaceful exploration of outer space, requires the unanimous support of every member state to pass new regulations, which is nearly impossible to achieve.

"The policy we propose could be implemented on a national level, which is why we think it's interesting," Indigo Brownhall, a space sustainability researcher at University College London and the paper's lead author, told Space.com. "Any country in Europe can request satellites registered with them to follow this bond policy."

To put it simply, no satellite registered in a given country gets to space before putting the money into the kitty. The researchers acknowledge that it might be hard to persuade major players historically averse to regulations, such as the U.S., to roll out such a scheme. But they say a more environmentally conscious block, like the European Union (EU), could enforce compliance by limiting market access to those who observe their rules.

"The consumer market in Europe is massive, like 300 to 400 million people," Brownhall said. "And the EU can request anyone who wants to sell space data or any products in the EU to follow their rules. They did this with GDPR [General Data Protection Regulation]. And you can imagine that somebody like Starlink [SpaceX's satellite-internet subsidiary] surely wants to sell Starlink terminals to Europeans."

The authors estimate that, even if the scheme only covered 20% of global satellite launches, it would reduce the number of derelict satellites careening around the planet by 18%.

Currently, more than 57,000 pieces of space debris larger than 4 inches (10 centimeters) hurtle around Earth completely out of control, according to estimates by the European Space Agency. That's double the figure from less than 10 years ago. Most of these fragments were generated by explosions and orbital collisions that shattered satellites and used rocket stages into clouds of dangerous debris.

"Leaving big satellites that no longer work up there is like a ticking time bomb," Brownhall said. "Active satellites can move out of the way, but when two derelict satellites are going to hit each other, there is nothing you can do but watch it."

Kaffine said the policy could be modelled on U.S. oil operators' bond scheme, in place since the 1960s, which ensures that oil giants plug used oil wells after ceasing extraction.

"If the oil operators clean up after completing the oil production job, they get the money back," said Kaffine. "If they don't, the money goes into a fund, which the government then uses to clean up orphan wells. It's quite an analogous situation to what we see in space."

In the meantime, evidence of the worsening orbital situation is coming thick and fast. Space situational awareness companies such as U.S.-based LeoLabs, which use networks of ground-based radars and telescopes to monitor traffic in space, report increasing numbers of disintegration events — incidents involving either an explosion or a collision between old satellites, fragments and rocket bodies. In the past six months, LeoLabs has reported at least four fragmentation events in space, which together created hundreds of new pieces of space debris.

#4028. Why Resilience Is Not a Solo Act

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

What do you think of when you hear the word resilience?

The number one answer is “bouncing back!” We often think of the person who keeps on going even when times are hard, or the person who gets knocked down and gets back up. They are the ones who adapt, find their way forward, and always keep it moving.

These days, resilience has become central in most conversations about mental health and well-being. We are all struggling with uncertainty, stress, change, and an overwhelming constant stream of information about what’s happening in the world. Science says that coping with difficult circumstances is an important part of taking care of ourselves.

The trouble is, sometimes we define resilience too narrowly. We focus more on whether someone can keep going and forget to ask, “How are you really doing?”

Research about resilience has raised an important question: Is adapting to difficult circumstances the same as being well?

Given all this is happening in the world, we have become pretty good at functioning under stress. We are able to go to work, take care of our families, meet most of our responsibilities, and continue to show up for those around us. The trouble is, just because we’re still functioning, we assume that we’re doing fine.

The issue is that functioning is not the same as doing well. To better understand why simply functioning is not enough, consider the concept of weathering, which describes how prolonged and ongoing exposure to stress often accumulates over time and negatively impacts our bodies. Weathering research demonstrates the difference between repeatedly responding to difficult circumstances and flourishing in life.

It explains why someone can be productive and dependable while also being exhausted and overwhelmed. We are generally able to adapt to unhealthy situations even when those situations are not healthy for us.

As a certified resiliency advocate, I am always cautious when resilience is presented as something people need to develop within themselves. While there are many skills that we can learn to help us manage stress, it’s important to recognize that our relationships, workplaces, families, and communities significantly influence our mental health and well-being.

My point is that the question cannot only be “How can I handle this difficult situation better?” It must also be “What is happening around me that is making life so difficult, and what support will help me get through it?”

Let’s be clear. I’m not saying resilience means we should create a life of ease. Without discomfort, we would not grow. It’s an important aspect of life to have that difficult conversation, try something new, or make that challenging decision.

Having said that, recent work on resilience suggests that we “microdose” discomfort into our lives regularly. So, rather than avoiding challenges altogether, we should practice experiencing small amounts of discomfort designed to help us build confidence to get through challenges. The goal is to put ourselves in minimally stressful situations, such as putting our phone away, speaking up in crowds, or trying something new.

The distinction is that there is a difference between a little healthy discomfort and ongoing chronic stressful situations. For example, learning to have a difficult conversation is very different from learning to tolerate a toxic relationship. Becoming comfortable with change is not the same as staying in a harmful situation. One builds your muscle while the other tears you down.

So, how can we get through challenging times? One important protective factor is having access to social connections. Connections help us get through challenging times and provide the support that we need.

Connection is not simply access to people. Real connection requires relationships where we feel seen, known, supported, and like we belong. Examples include a community gathering, sharing a meal with someone, or just meeting a friend for coffee.

When we talk about resilience as though it is built inside of a person, we are forgetting about how protective factors support us during difficult times. For many of us, our relationships are part of how we get through the tough stuff. When we are feeling overwhelmed, having someone to call changes our experience. When we need to feel grounded, a community gives us a sense of stability. Just knowing that people will notice our struggles makes it easier to ask for help.

While resilience is very personal, we rarely practice it alone.

Resilience means a lot of different things to different people. For some it means getting through; for others it means asking for help. The key is to know what your skills are and when you need to focus on building new ones.

We don’t want to focus on “How resilient am I?” but rather, “What is helping me stay healthy?” or “Who can I turn to?”

In the end, resilience is not about how much we can handle. It’s really about what we’re doing to take care of ourselves, how we connect with others, and whether we are in situations that allow us to thrive.

Tattoos are pretty permanent decisions, and laser removal is often described as more painful than actually getting them in the first place. But what about a design that can only be seen when you want to see it? Engineers at the University of Colorado Boulder are working on a new type of tattoo ink to answer that question. Their dye is specifically concocted to physically alter depending on exposure to visible or ultraviolet (UV) light.

The team described their invention in a study published earlier this month in the journal Matter & Light, and they didn’t only demonstrate the capabilities in the lab on silicone and pig skin. One of its creators, Carson Bruns, has already tattooed himself with the ink.

“[It’s] almost a tradition,” the study co-author recently told Nature.

Bruns, a nanoscientist at UC Boulder, previously developed a now-commercialized tattoo product called “Magic Ink” that turns magenta depending on its exposure to UV light. To highlight its efficacy, Bruns gave himself “solar freckles” as a method to track his exposure to sunlight. In similar fashion, he recently completed a similar session on his leg using the disappearing ink.

The formula currently comes in three colors—yellow, blue, and magenta—and essentially relies on the same principles behind transition lenses and some textiles. Each dye is composed of tiny spheres of acrylic glass. These beads contain photochromes, which are chemical compounds engineered to absorb specific light wavelength photons. In this case, the photochomic dye’s spheres begin in their original color, but then flatten into transparency after absorbing corresponding light photons exposed to UV rays. The dye remains this way until it absorbs visible light photons, which destroys the chemical bond and allows the spheres to return to their original shape.

Aside from the lab tests, Bruns tattooed his own leg over about 12 months using various dye combinations, all of which responded as intended to UV and visible light exposure. The ink also remained effective for over two years—but the team’s intention is go beyond a nifty parlor trick. Future ink iterations may be particularly useful in medical procedures, such as temporary markings to help maintain accuracy during direct radiotherapy sessions. For now, however, the photochromic tattoo dyes remain for laboratory (and Bruns’) use only until further safety assessments are completed.

Trying different gels and drink mixes, he pushed his carbs up to 120, 130 and 140 grams per hour. And in December of 2023, he entered the McDowell Mountain Frenzy 50 Miler and won. By the summer of 2024, he was ready for the Silver Rush 50 mile in Leadville.

He won by 25 minutes, and earned a slot in the Leadville 100 mile.

ROCHE KEPT TRAINING, both his legs and his stomach, taking in carbs at levels many regard as controversial. But even as he did, getting to the start line of Leadville was itself a kind of victory for other reasons.

In training, Roche only runs five days a week (and at lower mileage than most elite ultrarunners, with a focus on speed). Some days he rides his bike, which is what he was doing in April of 2024 when a car turned into him and sent him flying into a fence. He woke up with a concussion and a broken wrist. But as soon as he was recovered enough, he kept training (running with his arm in a sling) and keeping up the carbs.

“I trained like a competitive eater,” Roche says, learning to take in the amount of fluid and gels he’d need. “People in the competitive eating world actually practice with fluids, because they don't want to eat hot dogs every day. So I would use fluids to practice the bloat and teach my body not to reject that amount, because I knew I'd have to take in up to 56 ounces per hour and not reject it.”

By August, Roche was ready in his body. In his head, slightly less so.

“I still had so much doubt about whether it was possible or even a worthwhile goal to chase, with kids and work all the stuff going on,” he says now.

But Megan did want him to race, and so at 4 a.m. in Leadville, when the starting gun went off, he pushed those doubts aside and focused on the numbers. Not long after the start, he checked his heart rate.

“I knew how many calories-ish I burned at certain heart rates,” Roche says. “And when I looked at my heart rate, it was saying: ‘Look, you can go faster and still replace your calories.’ So a few miles into the race I was like, ‘All right, I guess it's time to race now.”

Roche made a rule for himself that he had to run every step of the way, with no walking. He describes the race as “incredibly fun,” which is not something people usually say about 100-milers.

“We saw him at Twin Lakes (mile 40) the first time and he looked great,” says his pacer Teddy Bross. “When came back into Twin Lakes (mile 60), in all honesty, he looked about the same.”

Bross ran the last 40 miles with Roche, and says there was one touch-and-go moment on the “power lines” climb where Roche started getting nauseous. But he was able to slow down, breathe and get it under control. Then the two runners sped on.

“On flats and downhills, I think we were somewhere around seven minutes a mile,” says Bross. “We were cooking,”

As they approached the finish line, Roche’s gap over second place widened. By the time he crossed it, he’d beaten his own prediction by one minute.

His time of 15:26:34 beat the Leadville record by more than 16 minutes.

THERE ARE MANY opinions on the so-called “high carb revolution,” or “carb mania,” as Alex Hutchinson, author of Endure: Mind, Body, and the Curiously Elastic Limits of Human Performance, calls it.

“The high-carb stuff is very much an open question scientifically,” Hutchinson says, “There's some logic, some anecdotes, and a tiny bit of data, but there are also lots of reasons to be skeptical. The thing with ultrarunning is that it's pretty much impossible to run randomized trials where people do all-out hundred-milers over and over. Everyone is making some guesses and doing some self-experimentation, but some people are more willing to try longshots.”

Andy Jones-Wilkins, a coach who has been running ultras since the 1990s, has seen a lot of trends come and go. He calls the push for high-carb fueling an "incredible marketing opportunity” which companies are taking advantage of, but cautions that it might not be the best strategy for mid- and back-of-the-pack runners.

“For athletes who are maybe a little older, in their 40s or above, somewhere in the range of 60 to 70 grams of carbs is per hour is plenty, especially if you're going to be going for more than six, seven eight hours.” The main issue, he says, is gastrointestinal distress, since slower runners aren’t working at the same intensity as elites, and just don’t need as many carbs.

“We know that ultras give people enough GI tract problems,” says Jones-Wilkins, “And to add to that by encouraging people to pump in over 100 grams of carbs, I would just urge people to tread carefully and experiment, because there's all kinds of stuff that can happen when you're eating a gel version of Thanksgiving dinner, then trying to run.”

Jason Koop, author of Training Essentials for Ultrarunning, says that while high-carb fueling seems new, it’s not. “The pro peloton [in cycling] has been doing this for years,” Koops says. “So anybody who thinks they're doing something new is either fooling themselves or hasn’t been paying attention. The origin of it is in very high-performance cycling, of which extremely few humans on the planet actually have a need to—or even can—ingest that amount of carbohydrate.”

Tyler Fox is a coach and a runner (coached by Roche) who’s had some success at the “lower end of high carb,” And while he does recommend higher carb intake for his athletes, it’s with a lot of caveats, including an athlete’s fitness and ability to maintain intensity.

“I think the narrative should be ‘fueling the work,’” Fox says. “Because if you're not able to sustain things from a mechanical perspective you're limited later by your ability to put out power.” According to Fox, in a 10-hour race, if you can run at a high intensity, you're going to be able to use more carbs, “but if you run at a conservative, less taxing output, your burn rate is going to be lower. And that's where you could run into high carb backfiring.”

In other words, if the carbs aren't being burned, they’ll find a way out though other channels, and you're risking a blowout on the side of the trail. Still, Fox sees the upside to higher carb intake with nuance. “I'm not suggesting that it's exclusively for higher level athletes” Fox says. “It does allow you to do more work, if you're prepared for it. But your training has to support it.”

Anna Gibson, a track and sub-ultra trail runner also coached by Roche, is sold on the benefits of high-carb fueling, and definitely has the training to support it. Last year the former NCAA athlete ran a 1500 meter PR, and competed in the Olympic trials. “In my last race I was putting down 110 grams of carbs per hour,” Gibson says. “It definitely took a lot of gut training to get to that point. But I credit that with my increase in performance.”

That race was the Gorge Waterfalls 30k, in which she won, broke the course record and finished second outright.

Despite all the unknowns, there’s no doubt that Roche’s output is enough to burn whatever he can cram in. “I think David has a clear understanding of what the science shows,” says Hutchinson, “and, just as importantly, what it doesn't show. Some people might look at that evidence and say, 'Nah, not quite enough to convince me to try it.' But David's an optimist: he weighs the odds and says, 'So you're telling me there's a chance!'

“I used to always think that I just sucked at the end of long races,” he says, “I can remember so many 50ks where I was like, 'Well, if I'm dying at mile 26, why would I go farther than this? Clearly I'm fighting my physiology.’

“Then I essentially went from normal fueling to high fueling and all of a sudden I become one of the best. So at the end of the day, it's really just: You give me 75 gels and I can give you the world.”

BACK HOME AFTER the Leadville 50, Roche’s recovery was fast. He says that after a day of feeling terrible, he improved quickly. In a couple weeks he was back to running form, which he attributes to his high-carb approach.

“I think the magic of carbs isn't race day performance necessarily. It's how much it helps you adapt and recover from training styles that will level up your fitness over time. So I'm going to be able to respond better to this workout, then come back stronger from it and stack up training in a way that makes me 36-years-old going on 22. “

Yet even as he recovered, Roche started to wonder if he got lucky in Leadville. “I thought, ‘What if it was all a fluke? What if I didn't actually learn anything about high-carb fueling or training or anything? What if I just had a good day at the right time and basically it was the stars aligning?”

To banish his doubts, Roche signed up for the Javelina Jundred in Arizona, just two months after Leadville. The race would be hot—nearly 100 degrees—so he started heat training, and practicing getting more fluids in.

At Javelina, Roche flew through the course, winning in 12:45:04 and missing the course record by less than two minutes. “The difference between Leadville and Javelina,” Roche says, “is that at Leadville, even in the moment, it felt like something magical was happening. It felt like something I don't know if I'm ever going to be able to repeat. But at Javelina I felt like I could do this any Saturday.”

The victory also earned him a “golden ticket” to the prestigious Western States 100 Mile.

Roche continued training and racing through the spring of 2025, with some of the usual ultrarunning ups and dowsn. In March he sprained an ankle in the Big Alta 50k, and had to drop out. But in May, he raced the Quad Rock, 50 Miler, winning by over 40 minutes and setting a new course record. The win gave him a “huge boost in confidence” for Western States in June.

WHETHER HIGH-CARB fueling will work for most runners remains to be seen. While there is some indication it might be different from previous fads like barefoot running, fat-adapted training and so on, runners should still proceed with care.

“Your average ultrarunner,” says Koop, “and even your good ultrarunner, needs to be cautious about titrating this up just because they saw somebody on Instagram do it. In many cases it's not necessary based on the energetic demands, and in most cases it’s not even pragmatic, because they can't take in that much carbohydrate.”

Jones-Wilkins agrees. “My prediction is that we’ll see kind of a settling into the fact that high carb works for the elites, but it doesn't work for the vast majority of people. They'll settle somewhere in the middle. But I urge athletes to tread carefully and experiment with what works for them. Because what works for me might not work for you and might not work for the next guy.”

ON JUNE 28, Roche lined up with some of the best in the world including legendary mountain runner Kilian Jornet, who won the race in 2011. This time, he wasn’t making any bold predictions.

“I’m a big-time underdog,” Roche said before the race, “even an underdog for top five. And that's the way I like it. I know I can run fast there relative to historic times. It's just a question of how fast everybody else goes.”

They went out very fast, with Roche in the lead up initial four-mile climb. Then as they moved through the mountains, a pack of at least twelve elite men pushed a sub-record pace. They hung together for the first 30 miles, all running within a minute or two of each other.

Roche was in the mix, hovering around fifth place, running shoulder-to-shoulder with Jornet and the others. The race was going according to plan. By mile 40, in the heat of the day, things started to spread out. At mile 40, Roche came in right behind race leaders Chris Myers and (eventual winner) Caleb Olson. At mile 56, he was solidly in fifth place.

“I felt great almost the whole time, even when I came into Michigan Bluff right behind Jeff. I was in a great spot. In retrospect, I was in the ideal spot.”

But soon after leaving the Michigan Bluff aid station, on the downhill, Roche’s race also started going downhill. He suspects he took in too many electrolytes given his heat training. But for whatever reason, his vision got blurry and he had some minor GI distress. So he dialed down the carbs, which he now thinks was a mistake.

“I went lower when I should have gone higher,” he says. “The gels were going down fine. But even after that I was able to recover once my sodium levels got better. I moved up and felt great. Then the lack of gels hit me, and that lead to some problems on the mental end.

Roche started fixating on his health, catastrophizing, and was suddenly filled with anxiety about seeing his kids again. He hesitates to call it a panic attack, but when his pacer, Teddy Bross (who is a physician) gave him some breathing exercises, they helped. Looking back he feels like he prepared his body, but not his mind.

“I think because of life being so white knuckled with two young kids and everything,” he says, “I didn't do the basic stuff I always ask athletes to do as a coach—like make it okay to get to that place where it feels kind of scary. Basically, I didn't nail down the mental side as much as I needed to going into a 100 mile.” He ended up dropping at 62 miles.

But as every ultrarunner knows, DNFs are just as important as getting on the podium, if not moreso. “The magic of ultrarunning,” Roche says, “is that you get stripped down to your core and from that you can grow back stronger. So I'm excited to get back out there and do another 100. And if I do qualify [for Western States] again, you’d better believe I'm coming back.”

#4013. The mysterious dancing plague that killed people in 1518

Published Oct 20, 2026 8:59 AM EDT

In the summer of 1518, people in Strasbourg couldn’t seem to stop dancing—some even danced themselves to death.

It all started with one woman, who stepped into the street and began moving uncontrollably. Soon, dozens of other residents followed suit, overcome with the inexplicable urge to hop, jerk, and sway. Within weeks, the outbreak had spread throughout the city, leaving officials, physicians, and religious leaders scrambling to figure out what was happening—and, more importantly, how to make it stop.

Five centuries later, the dancing plague of 1518 remains one of history’s strangest medical mysteries. Historical records confirm that a bizarre episode turned a medieval city into a very unwilling dance floor that summer, but exactly why it happened remains a matter of debate.

The dancing plague began on July 14, 1518, when a woman named Frau Troffea started dancing in Strasbourg, then part of the Holy Roman Empire and now a city in northeastern France. Though her husband begged her to stop, Troffea kept moving as day faded to night, while a crowd of onlookers gathered around to watch. Eventually, she collapsed and fell asleep, but when she awoke the next morning, she resumed her solitary jig.

Troffea’s uncontrollable dancing, known as choreomania, seemed to be contagious. Dozens of other people were soon gyrating in the streets of Strasbourg and, by late August, as many as 400 individuals had succumbed.

At the time, the leading theory was that St. Vitus, a fourth-century Sicilian martyr believed to punish sinners by making them dance, had cursed the afflicted. Then, once St. Vitus decided his victims had suffered enough, he would relieve them of the curse.

City leaders determined that the best course of action was to keep the dancers dancing, so they cleared the outdoor grain market, built a stage, and hired musicians. They didn’t explain their rationale in any known, surviving documents, so historians don’t know exactly why they did this. But John Waller, associate professor of the history of medicine at Michigan State University and the author of The Dancing Plague: The Strange, True Story of an Extraordinary Illness, suspects they had two reasons: They believed dancing could help people purge physical impurities from their bodies, and they viewed it as a form of penance.

“If people were believed to have been cursed by God or a saint for some kind of immorality, well, dancing was associated with hedonism and that was fundamentally immoral,” he tells Popular Science. “A way of basically punishing oneself would be to dance to the point of exhaustion and pain and potentially even death. It would make sense with that penitential framework.” In other words, from the leaders’ perspective, if dancing was their punishment, let them be punished.

Regardless of their motives, the authorities’ decision had “very, very clear consequences,” he says. It was a hot summer, and the dancers were likely under the full glare of the sun. They weren’t eating or drinking much, and they weren’t resting.

“This is certainly going to be extremely dangerous for a large number of people, particularly anybody with any kind of susceptibility to heart attack or stroke,” says Waller.

The historical records are murky on exactly how many people died—one writer claimed as many as 15 per day, at least for a time—but it was enough to make city leaders completely change course.

“They essentially banned dancing, except for some people in the privacy of their own homes, and then instituted a program of penance and prayer,” says Waller. “They clearly realized that what they’d been doing before was causing harm and I suspect they got the impression that it was making things worse.”

Eventually, the authorities transported the remaining dancers to a shrine dedicated to St. Vitus about 30 miles away, where religious leaders went to work praying and leaving offerings. As days and weeks passed, the dancers overcame their choreomania and returned to their normal lives—traumatized and tired, but otherwise fine.

The dancing plague of 1518 wasn’t the only such incident of its kind. Numerous other outbreaks of uncontrollable dancing occurred in western Europe between the late 1300s and mid-1500s—and Waller thinks it’s no coincidence that many were clustered along the Rhine River and its tributaries.

“Rivers are very good for, obviously, transporting trade goods, but they’re also very good for spreading ideas,” he says. “From the headwaters in Switzerland all the way down to where the Rhine flows into the North Sea, you’ve got this belief in a saint who curses with a dance.”

Now that we have the benefit of hindsight, what might explain the dancing plague of 1518 and others like it? One theory is that the dancers were sickened by ergot, a fungus that can contaminate grain. Another holds venomous spiders bit the afflicted. Demonic possession, epilepsy, typhus, and encephalitis—brain inflammation caused by an infection or autoimmune response—have also been put forward as possible explanations.

But after considering all the evidence, Waller thinks there’s only one plausible theory that truly makes sense. He suspects the dancers of Strasbourg were all victims of mass hysteria, also known as mass psychogenic illness, brought on by intense psychological distress.

The years leading up to 1518 were rough, particularly for the lower classes, with failed crops, bad weather, economic issues, social unrest, religious tensions, and illnesses like smallpox, leprosy, and syphilis taking a toll on peoples’ psyches. They were hungry, anxious, sad, scared, and worried that God had abandoned them.

Waller suspects the people living in Strasbourg simply reached their breaking point, then entered into a trance-like state that rendered them largely insensitive to their extreme fatigue and sore feet. They danced, Waller argues, rather than weeping, fighting, praying, or engaging in some other behavior because of their belief in St. Vitus and his dancing curse.

In 1976, a college paper blamed the Salem witch trials on moldy bread. It wasn’t true.

Given the period-specific cultural and religious underpinnings, could something like the dancing plague of 1518 happen today?

Waller shares several modern examples of mass psychogenic illness that, although they don’t involve dancing, he finds to be similar in nature. More than 300 students in Blackburn, England, suffered from a mysterious fainting fit in 1956, for example, and hundreds of people succumbed to alternating fits of laughing and sobbing in East Africa in 1962. In 2007, students at a Catholic boarding school near Mexico City came down with a mysterious sickness that made walking difficult. A year later, 20 female students in Tanzania suffered a mass fainting episode during their final-year national examinations.

And although the dancing plague of 1518 took place more than 500 years ago, Waller says we can still learn plenty from it. For him, the most important takeaway is “the way in which our expression of severe distress can be culturally conditioned and channeled.”

When Waller initially started researching the dancing plague of 1518, it was such a “wild phenomenon” that his inclination was to think “as strange as the past can be, this was simply too strange to have actually happened,” he says. But as he dug deeper—and, more importantly, began to understand the context within which it arose—it started to make more and more sense.

In other words, he says, it reminds us that psychological distress is not expressed in a vacuum. The culture, beliefs, and circumstances around us can shape how our inner turmoil manifests—and, in some cases, how it spreads from one person to another.

Matthew Perry gave us the beloved character "Chandler Bing." For more than a decade, his sarcasm and quick wit made us laugh, cry, and eventually wonder what happens when the applause ends. Friends is an iconic and nostalgic show that, to many, represents the comforting reality of the "found family"—that beautiful chapter of emerging adulthood when your friends are your primary source of stability, love, and support. The sadness we feel when watching Chandler Bing now stems from a bittersweet mix of his character's tragic upbringing, his defense mechanisms, and the real-world tragedy of the actor who portrayed him, Matthew Perry.

In his memoir Friends, Lovers, and the Big Terrible Thing, the late Matthew Perry, the actor known for playing the sarcastic but lovable Chandler Bing in the hit TV show Friends, discussed the impacts of his parents’ acrimonious divorce on his emotional well-being as a child. He explains how loyalty binds, conflict, and a sense of being alone created a deep-seated sense of shame and self-blame for him as a child. Perry discussed a childhood of feeling like an outsider looking in on his family, with evasive parents preoccupied with their own personal and professional aspirations. Perry, by his own admission, felt abandoned and unworthy. He never understood why his father chose to leave the family to pursue his dream of becoming an actor in Hollywood and felt that his mother was preoccupied with her “new life” and “new family.” Perry explained that being torn between two parents in two different countries was heartbreaking; he often found himself flying as an unaccompanied minor from the age of 5. On those long, lonely flights, he would try to tell himself that he was embarking on an exciting adventure but on the inside, he felt terrified and forgotten. In his own words, Perry explains:

There was now a family growing up around me, a family I didn’t really feel a part of. It was around this time that I made the conscious choice to say, Fuck it—it’s every man for himself. That’s when the bad behavior started—I got shitty grades, I started smoking, I beat up Pierre’s son (an eventual prime minister himself) Justin Trudeau . . . I made the choice to live in my head and not in my heart. It was safer in my head—you couldn’t be broken there, not yet anyway . . . I was a bad kid.

No one is born believing themselves unworthy of love, respect, or comfort; unworthiness is learned through painful life experiences. This death by a thousand cuts is the subtle yet deeply painful experience of complex trauma. Perry identifies as a “bad” or “broken” person many times in his memoir. Research has found that adult survivors of clustered adverse childhood experience go on to develop high-risk maladaptive behaviors, difficulty in establishing and maintaining healthy relationships, mental distress, and poor physical health. Adverse childhood experiences can include growing up in a home in which substance abuse, divorce, mental illness, suicidal ideation, violence, sexual abuse, or criminality are present.

Perry, like so many other children of divorce, explains in his memoir that he felt that he was placed in what is called a “loyalty bind.” Though alliances and conflicts naturally emerge in all family systems within a dysfunctional family system, loyalty binds are especially threatening to a child’s well-being. A child may be forced to take sides in conflicts between parents or between parents and siblings. This can often result in the child feeling forced to choose as a demonstration of loyalty but ultimately feeling deeply shameful. The Adverse Childhood Experiences Study explored the relationship between childhood trauma and long-term health outcomes; in the study, researchers discovered that kids facing multiple challenges in their life are at a higher risk of encountering difficulties such as struggling with emotions, performance in school, relationships, and impulsive behavior as they grow older.

When a person experiences childhood trauma, they may seek comfort in repetitive behaviors that can potentially lead to issues like addiction. Moreover, enduring significant trauma during childhood can contribute to feelings of sadness, increased risk of suicidality, weakened physical health, autoimmune diseases, and the impulse to engage in self-destructive and harmful behaviors. Perry developed several core negative beliefs; in his own words, he viewed himself as “broken, bent, unlovable.” Perry’s childhood marked by loneliness, isolation, and despair reportedly led him to seek solace through substance use in a ritualistic and compulsive manner, which in turn gave rise to a lasting battle with addiction. #4006. How to Train for a Trail Race in a City

Our product picks are editor-tested, expert-approved. We may earn a commission through links on our site. Why Trust Us?

Welcome to the Wild World of Trail Running! We're diving deep on the races, training tips, and gear you need to know to get outside off paved roads to run a whole new way. Click here for more stories about the trail explosion.

I SIGNED UP for a desert trail race in Arizona last year, my first ever. Right as my training cycle started, I moved from Salt Lake City to Brooklyn, trading easy access to Utah's parks and mountains for the paved city streets of New York. My new home was about as far from the cactus-lined single-track of the Javelina 31K I was preparing to run at as I could imagine.

I'm not the kind of person with a wall full of medals from road races (I've never run one), but I do love the outdoors. Spending time outside was a big part of my life throughout a decade of living in the American West, and I’ve been a casual trail runner for years, mostly because it lets me move through nature faster than hiking. Once I was on the East Coast, trading dirt for pavement was my only option. I trained however I could: over city bridges, through Brooklyn's Prospect Park, and even on a treadmill. And somehow? It worked. A few months after my first trail race, I leveled up to run another, a 55K in Moab—and I trained in the city for that, too.

More people than ever are dipping their toes into trail races and ultramarathons. In 2024 alone, over 40,000 people completed an ultra for the first time, according to UltraRunning Magazine. But while road marathons are (relatively) predictable—the consistent asphalt surface, unobstructed routes, and regular aid stations—trail races demand a different kind of training. You’ll need to prepare for long climbs, technical terrain, and hours on your feet. Some athletes get caught up in making sure their training environment is as close to race day as possible, so urban-dwelling trail runners might be worried that they could never properly prepare for their long, off-road runs close to home. But it’s totally doable. Here’s how to get ready for a trail race when getting out into nature isn't so easy.

ULTRAMARATHONS COME IN a range of distances—from 50K and 50 miles to 100K, 100 miles, and even races over 200 miles (maybe don’t start there.) The first step is to pick your distance and your race.

“With a road marathon, 95 percent of them are basically the same elevation profile,” says Ron Hammett, running coach for Sundog Running who I worked with for both races. But trail races can vary dramatically, and your race’s location will dictate your approach. For example, if you pick a race with 6,000 feet of elevation gain and loss, you’ll need to prepare your body for that vertical challenge. Similarly, races on particularly rugged terrain require extra joint and stability work.

For city-based beginners, you'll be best-suited to choose a race that’s less technical. The Javelina 31K had minimal elevation and smooth trails—and even though the Moab Red Hot 55K race featured technical terrain, much of it was on firm slickrock, which is almost as hard as pavement, so my body was ready for the impact after road training.

RUNNING ON ROADS often means repeating the same motion over and over: The same pace, with the same foot strike, using the same muscle groups. But with trail running, you’ll be leaping over rocks, side-shuffling around roots, and landing at odd angles. Add in steep climbs and pounding descents, and suddenly your usual run training doesn’t cut it.

Hammett recommends incorporating lower-body dominant compound movements—squats, Romanian deadlifts, and step-ups—into your routine. Core strength is also crucial for balance and stability, and calf raises can help to keep your legs from feeling like jello on mile 15, while also making your ankles more resilient.

“Another method of simulating those technical trails is plyometrics,” says Hammett. Warm-up by jumping rope or hopping back and forth on one foot to get the tissue ready to handle the load of running.

Then, get more specific with your exercises based on your personal needs. For me, this meant stretching and strengthening my calves to prevent shin splints, and strengthening my hip flexors and outer glutes for hip stability.

CITY TRAINING CAN rarely match the natural elevation of trails, but it’s important to work with what you have nearby. Dave Hashim, an NYC-based ultrarunner who takes on huge chunks of city miles and has completed races like Black Canyon 100K and the Escarpment Trail Run (along with city running feats like traversing the perimeter of Manhattan), integrates the Williamsburg Bridge and Queensboro bridge into nearly every run to get some elevation gain into his training plan.

If you can’t access any hills, you might have to embrace the treadmill for hill sprints—or even for hiking. By putting on a weighted vest or your race-day pack and setting a steady incline, you can simulate a significant portion of your course's vert. For my training, Hammett programmed weighted treadmill hikes with 1,200 feet of climbing at a five to 15 percent incline. As race day neared, I was logging 2,000 to 3,000 feet of elevation gain per session on the treadmill.

But the treadmill can get boring very quickly, so it helps to find ways to keep it interesting, like intervals. For one long run, I downloaded the Moab Red Hot course map onto my Suunto app and adjusted the incline on the treadmill to follow the elevation profile for the first 17 miles of the run. Brutal, but worth it.

TRAIL RACES OFTEN require a level of self-sufficiency: you might go miles without an aid station, and you’ll need to carry food, water, and extra layers of clothing. “In the city, there are actually a lot of different ways to train for that—you just have to be open to being uncomfortable,” says Hashim.

Instead of repeating the same loop every weekend, he maps out new routes across different neighborhoods. This novelty helps simulate the unpredictability of race day—new terrain, surprising turns, or mental fatigue from being somewhere unfamiliar. “It gets you more comfortable when you are doing an ultra, where you’re running in places you’ve never been,” he says.

You’ll also have to mix up your workouts. If you’ve trained for road races, you know the program can be very formulaic: Easy runs, a track day, a tempo run, a weekend long run. The goal is to hit your stride and maintain the same pace throughout the entire race. But this approach doesn’t work as well for trail ultras, where you’ll need to adjust your pace based on your terrain.

Hashim’s workouts might include two-mile hill repeats over six hours or downhill sprints followed by a three-mile tempo run—workouts that don’t make sense for road racing but perfectly mimic the unpredictable nature of trail events.

JUST REMEMBER THAT the trail can—and will—be an unpredictable environment. “If you're running on carbon plated shoes on the road, and you're hitting a seven flat pace expecting to do this time, you’re in for a world of pain,” says Hashim.

Trail pace is slower. A lot slower. Mileage that takes you two hours on pavement could easily take four hours on dirt. And when the terrain changes every mile, pace just doesn’t matter the same way as it does for road races. That’s why Hashim never sets a strict time goal. Instead, he plans a general timeline to help plan nutrition and hydration. “I think you have to be okay with throwing what you learned about road racing out the window,” he says. “A lot of people focus on stats and heart rate. But for me, how I feel during a race is paramount.”

That mindset starts in training. Try using rate of perceived exertion (RPE)—a scale of one to ten that reflects how hard a workout feels—instead of chasing numbers from your watch. It’ll help you get used to running by feel, which matters a lot more when the trail throws a climb at you.

And even though it’s called a trail run—you will be hiking parts of it. “What's the most efficient way of getting from start to finish? For these longer races, it's definitely not running the whole way,” says Hammett. During your training runs, incorporate run-walk intervals (like 8:2 or 4:1) to help your body adjust to the constant stop/start rhythm. The hiking sections are a great time to hydrate and fuel—two things you definitely don’t want to slack on during an ultra.

Trail running isn’t just physically demanding—it requires a mindset shift from the conditions lots of runners are accustomed to for training and racing. You have to know that things will go sideways—and you have to keep moving anyway. The end results will prove to be worth the extra effort.

Hashim recalls a race in Marin County, California that tested his limits: “It just felt like waves crashing, and I just wasn't able to swim out of it,” he said.

But then he looked up, and saw exactly where his fight against the trail had taken him. “I was living in a screensaver. It was just rolling green hills for miles, blue sky. I thought, how can I be angry and frustrated when this is what I get to look at?”

#4001. New comet accidentally discovered by space telescope

Published Oct 20, 2026 11:15 AM EDT

Launched in 2023, the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Euclid space telescope observes some of the most distant regions of the visible universe for signs of dark matter and dark energy. But even while focused on the “big picture,” Euclid’s onboard equipment is surprising astronomers by capturing tiny photobombers like errant comets.

Its recent portraits of one specific dusty space rock are especially impressive, however. Euclid spotted a never-before-seen comet sailing through space more than 807.7 million miles from Earth. The telescope isn’t designed to automatically flag an image interloper. Instead, the bright blemish seen in multiple images was spotted by a moving object detection program designed by Mikko Pöntinen, an astronomer at the University of Helsinki in Finland.

“I was excited to see my software flag a ‘streak’ smeared across several images—the result of a fast-moving object,” said Pöntinen. “Further inspection revealed that it had a fuzzy cloud around it, indicative of an active object like a comet that is shedding dust and gas.”

Pöntinen quickly relayed his findings to the International Astronomical Union’s Minor Planet Center (MPC), which then passed the news along to researchers around the world. On October 8, the MPC confirmed the newly discovered comet, which is now classified C/2026 S1 (Euclid).

C/2026 S1 was seen from five different fields of view over multiple days in the Euclid Deep Field North region of the telescope’s scans. Astronomers now know the comet’s trajectory isn’t located on the same horizontal plane as the planets. Instead, its path is technically taking it over the solar system along a multi-thousand-year orbit.

Euclid’s discovery is particularly striking because the telescope’s six-year mission is specifically mapped to avoid areas of the sky that are laden with comets and asteroids.

“Very few comets are expected to be discovered by Euclid, so it was a happy coincidence,” added Euclid archive scientist Bruno Altieri.
