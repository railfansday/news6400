#6186. OpenAI Intros Dots, AI Agents that Continue Working Between User Conversations

OpenAI has introduced Dots, GPT-6 Astra-powered agents that run on their own cloud computers, using connected applications to carry on with assignments between conversations and incorporating users' feedback as they go.

OpenAI is also previewing specialist Dots for organizations, with separate identities and credentials for defined responsibilities. Those enterprise deployments are beginning with focused pilots. The company is working with Microsoft on integration with Agent 365's governance and security controls.

The broader ambition seems to be to make delegation last beyond a chat session. That change could reduce the need to repeatedly explain a project. It also makes supervision a continuing obligation: An instruction that was appropriate yesterday may no longer fit today's circumstances.

One important distinction appears in OpenAI's privacy and safety FAQ. Disconnecting an application stops new access through that connection, but does not delete information already incorporated into a Dot's context.

Users currently cannot inspect or directly edit individual Dot memories. Deleting a Dot deletes its own context, while files and conversations it created remain separately stored. Plugin permissions are shared across Dots, ChatGPT, ChatGPT Work, and Codex.

For an organization, that creates two related governance questions: what an agent can retrieve now, and what it already knows. Revoking a connection addresses the first without necessarily resolving the second.

OpenAI's safety account distinguishes authorized tasks that continue in the background from unsolicited "proactive research." The latter uses tools restricted to reading permitted sources. Those research tasks cannot directly send messages, change connected application content, or control a browser or desktop. Any subsequent action must pass the usual rules and checks.

A separate system, Auto-review, checks certain planned actions against instructions and safety requirements. OpenAI says the agent cannot modify the controls enforcing those checks.

"Dots can still make mistakes," the company cautions.

OpenAI's Dots system-card appendix acknowledges that persistence and multi-agent systems are not new. Its evaluations examine how continuing work affects authorization boundaries.

In one test involving successive related tasks, moderate scope violations increased from 8.6% to 19.7% when the number of intervening tasks doubled from five to 10. Examples included carrying information between unrelated tasks or editing a shared document beyond the intended scope. The company reported no severe breaches or exfiltration in that evaluation.

These are controlled test results, not measured failure rates in customer deployments. They nevertheless identify a problem that a successful demonstration cannot settle: whether an agent keeps the right boundaries as its workload accumulates.

According to OpenAI's DevDay recap, Dots are available to Pro and Business Premium users in eligible markets. Enterprise, Edu, and Healthcare access is an administrator-enabled beta, off by default.

The operational question is how organizations will keep delegated authority current. A continuing assignment needs a human owner who can revise its scope and decide when to stop it. Otherwise, the convenience of leaving work with an agent can outlast the reason it was authorized.

#6185. This Artist Uses a Six Foot Tall Hand Loom to Create Textiles That Are So Luxurious People “Won't Let Them Go”

Weaving is painstaking work. To begin one of her textiles, Marina Contro must first set up her hand loom: an antique standing six feet tall. “I thread every string through these shafts,” explains the San Francisco-based artist. “For that, I have to sit inside the loom on the cloth beam, but when I’m tying up the pedals I’ll be under the warp on the floor.” On a recent afternoon, she had loaded the machinery with ochre and brown cashmere, which she would then manipulate in silence, counting out eight picks, or stitches, for each square of a gingham pattern.

Contro at her loom, which she sourced from a purveyor in Vermont that rescues the old devices

The resulting blanket appears in a joint exhibition with woodworker Noah Cohen, on view October 24 to January 2 at Blunk Space—the destination gallery in Point Reyes Station, California, near the late sculptor JB Blunk’s former home. “Her weavings are deceptively luxurious,” says Blunk’s daughter, Mariah Nielson, who tapped Roman Alonso and Steven Johanknecht of the AD100 firm Commune Design to curate the show. “Once I put one of her blankets in a client’s hands they won’t let it go,” reports Alonso.

Contro, who grew up in Chicago and Colorado, enrolled in the textiles master’s program at Cranbrook Academy of Art after developing a fascination with liturgical vestments as an undergraduate religious studies major. In Michigan, she asked to use the historic looms in the school’s Kingswood Weaving Studio, set up by Loja Saarinen in 1931 and now used by Cranbrook high school students. “You’re so immersed in history when you’re in that space,” she explains. After graduating in 2017, she sought out equally inspirational settings, such as Andrea Zittel’s residency program near Joshua Tree and the Blunk House, where she lived and worked earlier this year.

Operating within the confines of her old-school loom, Contro tends toward plain weaves and twills, preferring the look, feel, and drape of those simple structures. The maximum width she can achieve is 48 inches, so she often joins panels for a finished piece. Seams in turn become the focal points, achieving the stop-and-look-closer effect of an Agnes Martin painting. Whether intentional or not, she says, “I like rewarding the viewer for spending a little bit of time with it.”

#6176. How to Style Fall 2026’s Top Denim Trends

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

From inky indigo to skinny jeans and slouchy fits, there’s something for everyone

The changing season ignites a healthy urge to find newness in our wardrobes again. For many, this means refreshing the essentials: stocking up on your favorite T-shirts, perhaps trading in a few sweaters for a fresher batch, or replenishing your denim with styles that feel more current. Luckily, the fall/winter 2026 runways provided plenty of denim trends to play with this year.

Whether it’s dark inky indigo, slim silhouettes, or the seemingly ever-present slouchy jean, there’s something for every taste. And though designers have given us quite the feast of fresh and exciting styles, the preeminent question remains: How do you actually wear them?

Below, you'll find seven of the season’s trending denim styles and how to style each of them.

For fall, one of the more novel choices made by designers was opting for dark, inky indigo-hued jeans that will surely add some variety to your current denim rotation. To lean into the polished nature of this darker wash, go for a sleek denim-on-denim look. Add subtle Western flair with low-heeled booties and hammered gold-tone earrings, and finish with a sophisticated oversize cashmere peacoat.

There’s something quite summery about a denim dress, but the beauty of early fall is that you can rework your summer clothes for the cooler months, something designers are showcasing more and more, as evidenced by some of the heavy outerwear we saw for spring/summer 2027. To bring your denim dress into the current season, throw on a long-line knit cardigan in a neutral tone. Further, instead of going for a heel or a high-shaft boot, take a cue from the Zimmermann show and opt for a loafer, which grounds the look and takes away any sense of preciousness from the dress. Finish things off with a sizable bag that can hold all your necessities for the day and more.

For so many seasons, the mandate was bigger, baggier, and barrel-legged, and it seems like we are finally seeing the pendulum swing the other way, with brands like Khaite and Valentino advocating for a slimmer silhouette. For an outfit that leans more relaxed but still shows you put in effort, style your skinny jeans with an equally svelte short-sleeve knit. Pair it with calf-hair mules and a croc-embossed bag for added texture, and introduce a little color and interest with a brightly colored enamel bracelet.

To give your favorite utilitarian jeans a bit of cosmopolitan edge, smarten things up with a cropped leather jacket. For a more casual yet sporty approach, throw on a trendy quarter-zip and a pair of scrunch tennis shoes in optic white, which play nicely when paired with a a white tee peaking through underneath your sweater.

If you’re not a skinny-jean fan, don’t worry; there were just as many slouchy, baggy options on the runway. However, this season, it seems designers were interested in giving the slouchy silhouette a sense of occasion by dressing it up a bit more. To keep that relaxed shape while still feeling elevated, pair it with a cognac suede wrap coat that feels inherently dressier and more special. To keep that momentum going, add a pop of red with a pair of textured flats. Add a striped T-shirt as a step up from your run-of-the-mill plain white tee.

The message for fall/winter 2026, and in many ways spring/summer 2027 (thanks in large part to Prada), is the skirt. However, these are not the ultrashort miniskirts from the post-lockdown era. These newer iterations hit at the knee and, at times, extend all the way to the ankle. A denim skirt lets you choose something a bit more dressed up, but in a fabric tailored for everyday wear. Give the denim skirt the preppy treatment with a tweed blazer layered over a lived-in sweatshirt and plaid button-up shirt. Finish everything off with a second-skin suede boot.

Thanks in large part to Dario Vitale’s single runway collection for Versace, colorful denim feels excitingly fresh and desirable again. When it comes to styling it, keep the outfit to a handful of colors to avoid looking muddled. A gray henley offers a step up from a plain white T-shirt, with the muted tone allowing the red denim to stand out even more. Meanwhile, a darker, more subdued purple belt lets you dabble in the ever-popular combination of red and purple while still looking polished. To top it off, a denim jacket lets you play with double denim without necessarily looking like you are.

#6174. Can Wicker Make You Better Boyfriend Material?

The married filmmakers behind the provocative fable explain why the perfect guy bends but never breaks.

The man Alexander Skarsgård plays in Wicker may be woven together with sticks and straw, but he’s the kind of guy you want to be. Never mind that he’s a literal basket case.

Mystical forces bring him to life, but he proves himself to be more grounded than most of the ordinary people in his village. He’s a devoted partner, a helpful neighbor, a tireless worker, and, on top of it all, an impressive lover. Those who don’t adore him completely loathe him, usually for the exact same reasons.

The bawdy storybook comedy, which hits theaters on Friday, is written and directed by the husband-and-wife filmmaking team Alex Huston Fischer and Eleanor Wilson, who adapted it from the short story “The Wicker Husband” by Ursula Wills. “A lot of our own relationship was examined when we were writing the film,” Wilson says.

“A lot of the Wicker Husband is me, right?” Fischer says.

“Sure,” his wife says slowly. Too slowly. “For sure.”

“Yeah,” Fischer repeats with a satisfied smile: “Just based on me.”

In this grown-up fairy tale, the Wicker Husband is assembled by a basket weaver (played by Peter Dinklage) who can handcraft living, feeling beings. He is asked to make this ideal companion by Olivia Colman’s lonesome, middle-aged outcast, known as The Fisherwoman. (Everyone in the story is known only by their work.) Her Wicker Husband adores her, but their fellow townsfolk grow curious, then jealous, and finally turn against both of them, which stress-tests their happily-ever-after.

“There’s a lot of fun to be had with all the villagers and making fun of how they react and their own shortcomings within their relationships,” Wilson says.

Wicker also gets surprisingly provocative. This is not a bedtime story for children, but more like a bedtime story for adults. The oddball couple’s loud nighttime passions both unsettle and intrigue their neighbors. Some men in the village feel he makes them look bad, and they’re mostly right—many of the local ladies only wish they had partners who were so capable and caring. A few of the couples have even more provocative ideas in mind, as if adding a third made of furniture material is as easy as adding another chair to their table.

Even though the story seems to be set long ago, it has modern trends on its mind. “So many people in the world are talking about different ways that we can do relationships. We wanted to bring a lot of that into the script,” Wilson says. “They’re very lusty and it sort of opens up this possibility of a different type of sex life.”

For a few men, the Wicker Husband becomes aspirational. A young fellow known as the Bottle Washer starts to see Skarsgård’s character as a mentor, after too long being misled by the folktale version of toxic influencers. “He sees a different way of being a man because everyone else in the village just models this sort of masculine bravado and complains about their wives and jokes about all the nagging that they do or whatever,” Fischer says. “The Bottle Washer sees in the Wicker Husband that maybe there’s a gentler way to behave.”

Moviegoers might see the same. Along with last year’s Train Dreams, Wicker models a kind of honorable masculinity. The Wicker Husband is strong but thoughtful, protective but sensitive, and he’s deeply, madly devoted to his Fisherwoman. Still, their best-laid plans start to unravel, testing what it takes to hold their relationship all together.

The filmmaking couple doesn’t pretend to have magical answers. Wicker simply emerged from trying to ask the right questions as their own relationship deepened. “We actually got married a couple months before we wrote the first draft of Wicker,” Wilson says.

“It was a very bold choice to write a satire about marriage,” her husband adds.

“As if we know everything,” his wife says.

They tied the knot in 2021 and finished the screenplay later that year, but like many modern couples they had been together for a long time before. As they start unspooling their own history, Fischer says: “I always think of When Harry Met Sally, and all those little vignettes, the old couples who tell their stories.”

“We are coming up on eleven years this month,” Wilson says. Their first movie, the 2020 comedy Save Yourselves!, about a couple who are terminally online, came together after four years of dating. But even before that, their work lives and romantic lives were always entwined.

“When we met, we started working together right away on gig jobs because we were in the freelance life,” her husband explains. “Eleanor hired me to shoot a documentary in Iceland. I hired Eleanor to produce these little short docs around the U.S., and we traveled together. We found that we were always sort of compatible and had a good time creatively. Making movies together was unexpected.”

“We met on Tinder,” Wilson says. “It is pretty crazy, because I did not think when I met Alex that I would find my life partner and my work partner in the same day. I was actively not looking for that.”

Fischer wasn’t either. “It’s like, ‘I hope she’s a lawyer. I hope I get to meet a doctor or someone with a real job,’ ” he says.

“Massive fail on both our parts,” his wife jokes.

Finding support in each other during tough times is one of the prevailing themes in Wicker. All too often in real life, however, hardship fractures the relationship rather than forging it into something strong. “[Life] sneaks up on you, which is what the movie’s about. It catches you off guard and it’s difficult,” Fischer says.

“Then you have to rise to the occasion,” Wilson says.

“When you’re in a relationship with somebody, there’s nowhere to hide,” her husband says.

That may be The Wicker Husband’s true magic power: He’s brand new, so he’s not capable of pretending. He doesn’t attempt to act tougher than he is, and isn’t afraid to say what is actually in his head rather than expecting her to mind-read. “He’s very literal and most people aren’t very literal,” Fischer says. “Most people don’t say what they think at all.”

“They’re just nervous to say their actual feelings, and so they dance around it,” Wilson adds.

“He only says what he feels,” her husband adds.

The Fisherwoman has been around longer, is more battered by life, and is therefore more circumspect. “The Fisherwoman isn’t a perfect partner,” Wilson says. “She’s never really opened herself up in this way before. When you do, it reveals parts of yourself that are not always pretty. She enters into this [relationship] as a bit of a joke to mock the village, and then everything that happens takes her by surprise.”

When the women of the village begin to fawn over her woven man, her insecurities overwhelm her. “Her whole thing is that she has to learn to love herself,” Wilson says. “You can’t really let love into your life until you accept that you are worthy of love.” She looks to her husband, sitting beside her on the Zoom chat. “And I don’t know … I felt like that’s what I went through in past relationships.”

“Same,” Fischer says. “I also identified with the Fisherwoman more than the Wicker Husband, for that reason.”

“What attracted us to the short story was that allegory,” Wilson says. “It’s really fun for audiences to be able to see themselves in these roles, or see what they would or wouldn’t do or how they would do it differently, and then have discussions about it. We did test screenings, and it was really fun when we saw people sticking up for different characters. That’s the best thing.”

In wicker making, as well as romantic disagreements, the same lesson applies: It is better to bend than to break.

#6173. Ina Garten’s Biggest Cookbook Lesson Started With A Roast Chicken Nobody Wanted

Before Ina Garten became the person I trust to make everything from a fall salad to a very large Cosmopolitan look effortless, she was figuring out the food business one roast chicken at a time. And apparently, even Ina had to serve a few flops before figuring out what people actually wanted.

On a recent episode of The Mel Robbins Podcast, Garten looked back on the earliest days of Barefoot Contessa, the tiny Westhampton specialty food store she bought at 30 despite having zero experience running one. She’d come from working on nuclear energy policy in Washington, D.C., but Garten doesn’t actually see the career pivot as quite as random as it sounds.

“If you’re cooking, you’re testing things,” she told Robbins. “But instead of ending up with, like, gibberellic acid, you end up with roast chicken. So which would you rather have?”

The learning curve was immediate. Garten said she didn’t know how to cash out a register, slice smoked salmon, or scale the 12 brownies she could make for friends into 1,000 for customers. After her first week, she even told Jeffrey, “This is the stupidest thing I’ve ever done in my life.”

Her first day as owner didn’t exactly scream future food empire, either. The register had just $87 in it, prompting Jeffrey to do the math and tell her, “I don’t think you’re gonna make it here.” By the end of the very next day, a busy Friday before Memorial Day weekend, the store was completely cleaned out. Garten and the previous owner stayed up through the night making chocolate cake and chicken salad so they could do it all again. But one of the most important lessons came from food that wasn’t selling.

Garten initially assumed a Hamptons specialty shop called for fancy food, so she made dishes like veal with morels and displayed roast chickens on a huge white platter surrounded by fresh herbs. Her takeaway? “People have elegant taste, but they don’t want fancy food at home.” Garten said the realization “totally informed all of my cookbooks.”

That philosophy feels pretty familiar if you’ve ever made her Honey White Bread or Crispy Chicken With Lemon Orzo. When Garten eventually did start writing cookbooks, she said her formula was simple: dependable recipes, beautiful photography, and ingredients you could actually find at the grocery store.

And, fittingly, when Robbins asked Garten for her favorite recipe at the end of the conversation, she didn’t name anything particularly elaborate. She picked a French apple tart with a thin buttery crust, sliced apples, butter, and sugar.

“It’s a very simple recipe,” Garten said. “I just think it’s just gorgeous.”

#6172. The Frankfurt Kitchen Changed Design History Forever—Inside the Race to Save the Last Ones Standing

The call arrives with a deadline. An apartment in one of Frankfurt’s 1920s housing estates is being renovated, and its original fitted kitchen is scheduled for removal. Volunteers grab their slotted screwdrivers and spattles, chisels and hammers; they may have only a few days to save the cook space before it’s gone for good.

The rescuers come from the Ernst-May-Gesellschaft—or the Ernst May Society, in English—a 23-year-old nonprofit dedicated to preserving surviving examples of the Frankfurt Kitchen, arguably the single most important development in modern kitchen design. “Every contemporary kitchen is based on the DNA of the Frankfurt Kitchen,” says Laura Gerlach, whose photographic archive of extant interiors, The Library of Frankfurt Kitchens, has been exhibited at the society’s show house. “The Frankfurt Kitchen marked a turning point in architecture and in the concept of living and radically scrutinized and rethought the idea of housework. It’s what we mean when we say ‘kitchen.’”

Each remaining cook space is located in one of the city’s Neues Frankfurt (New Frankfurt) estates, a collection of apartment complexes that were built after World War I to address the city’s housing shortage. Though historians estimate that at least 10,000 Frankfurt Kitchens were installed beginning in 1926, most have been discarded or remodeled out of existence. A century after they were first designed, reexamining their relevance in history has never been more important, while protecting their future has never been more dire.

In 1925, the mayor of Frankfurt appointed architect Ernst May as the city’s building councilor and tasked him with designing a comprehensive program to address the housing shortage. This was the beginning of Neues Frankfurt. He assembled a team of city planners, engineers, and designers to execute the project, including a trailblazing young architect, Margarete Schütte-Lihotzky. As one of Austria’s first practicing female architects, she was passionate about a human-centric approach to design. In Vienna earlier that decade, she had begun thinking systematically about how architecture might lessen women’s domestic workload.

The Frankfurt Kitchen was designed by Margarete Schütte-Lihotzky in 1926 for Neues Frankfurt housing estates.

Knowing this, May charged her with devising a space-efficient kitchen that could be prefabricated and would rationalize domestic labor, saving women time and energy. “The idea was to minimize the number of steps they took, say between peeling the potato and the oven, ostensibly freeing up their time for other pursuits outside the house,” explains Staci Steinberger, a curator at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art (LACMA), which recently acquired a Frankfurt Kitchen that is now on view as part of the exhibit, “The Kitchen and Its Discontents.”

What looks familiar today was wholly radical at the time. Before Schütte-Lihotzky’s “modern laboratory,” working-class kitchens consisted of a jumble of mismatched wooden tables and cupboards in the corner of a shared living space. The cooking stove often doubled as the dwelling’s heat source. Apartments could come with little more than a sink, leaving tenants to supply everything from shelves to food safes. Schütte-Lihotzky made what was then the women’s domain the architect’s concern, bringing women’s work to the “forefront in an extraordinary way,” says Christine Zwingl, a Viennese architect and the leading expert on Schütte-Lihotzky. “It is a design by a woman, a contribution to women’s emancipation, and a piece of women’s history.”

At just 6.2 by 11.3 feet, every inch in a Frankfurt Kitchen had a purpose. Dishes drained on shelves above the sink; cooking scraps were swept directly into a removable ventilated drawer for disposal; an ironing board folded down beneath an adjustable ceiling lamp. Perhaps most memorable were the labeled aluminum storage bins for flour, sugar, and other pantry staples, each with a handle and pouring spout and slotted into a discreet wall-mounted cubby. Most transformative, however, was the electric stove, which replaced cumbersome, soot-stained, wood-burning ranges.

Prior to the Frankfurt Kitchen, most cook spaces were a hodgepodge of furniture, storage, and a stove that all were typically purchased by the tenants.

Influenced by railway dining cars and Taylorism—a system of measuring and streamlining labor to maximize efficiency on factory floors—Schütte-Lihotzky arranged continuous counters, built-in cabinets, stoves, and sinks in assembly-line fashion. By designing the fittings alongside the apartment itself, she reduced tenants’ upfront furnishing costs, while linoleum flooring, tiled backsplashes, and aluminum drawers simplified cleaning. Even the kitchen’s original color, blue, had a practical rationale: The hue was thought to repel flies. “It made cooking and cleaning easier,” Steinberger says, though she notes that the new design “didn’t change who was doing the work.”

A close view of the Frankfurt Kitchen’s built-in containers, which were intended for dry goods.

The Frankfurt Kitchen soon reached an audience beyond its birthplace. In 1927, photographs of the kitchen appeared in the Swiss architecture journal Das Werk. Three years later, Schütte-Lihotzky joined May’s team in the Soviet Union, where they were invited to apply the wider lessons of Neues Frankfurt’s program to new industrial towns. These exchanges were part of an international effort to rethink domestic labor: From housing schemes in Prague and Brussels to American manufacturers’ test kitchens, designers were exploring related ideas about efficiency.

After World War II, expanding appliance production and consumer markets brought those ambitions to scale. Even as new equipment and materials transformed the room, the principle of planning storage, appliances, and work surfaces as a continuous, complementary whole endured. A century later, the Frankfurt Kitchen remains the genesis of today’s contemporary cooking space.

Today, parts from approximately 50 kitchens, all saved by the Ernst-May-Gesellschaft—and their predecessor Gesellschaft für Kunst und Denkmalpflege (Society for Art and Monument Preservation)—fill a 2,150-square-foot warehouse in Frankfurt-Fechenheim. Towering metal shelves hold everything from opal-glass mushroom lampshades to massive plastic tubs of hardware. Door frames stacked like cards lean against a far wall beside pallets of crumbling Solnhofen limestone floor tiles. “It’s an amazing place,” says conservator Kerstin Frost, who works closely with the nonprofit on restoration projects, including the deep-blue Frankfurt Kitchen that LACMA acquired.

The Frankfurt Kitchen on display as part of LACMA’s The Kitchen and Its Discontents exhibit.

For years, the Ernst-May-Gesellschaft has served as the official intermediary between the kitchen’s original apartment units and the museum and cultural institutions they often end up in. Because the kitchens are in public buildings, the housing administration would neither sell them nor allow residents to claim and keep them when an apartment was scheduled for renovation. Unless they were immediately transferred to a museum or recognized collection, they were destroyed, says Frost.

When Peter Schepp, a volunteer with the society, approached the housing administration about the soon-to-be-discarded kitchens, the response was straightforward: “We throw it away. Do you want it?” He arranged for the society to be notified whenever one was slated for removal. Over the years, volunteers have salvaged everything from nearly intact kitchens (though this is increasingly rare) to individual cabinets and fittings, adding them to the collection. If renovations by successive generations of tenants have left little of the original kitchen intact, “we rescue just the few original remaining parts, like the screws that fix the furnishings to the wall,” Schepp said.

A reconstructed Frankfurt Kitchen on display at the V&A East Storehouse in London.

Before the Ernst-May-Gesellschaft volunteers begin dismantling a kitchen, a photographer documents the interior with a fisheye lens while the team measures and sketches the room. Then they begin carefully disassembling its surviving original elements and transporting them to the warehouse. The goal is to preserve and restore as much as possible, eventually combining components like handles and aluminum bins from different kitchens to reconstruct a complete model for museums or similar academic and cultural institutions.

At the warehouse, the restoration process begins with “cleaning, cleaning, cleaning,” Frost says. Decades of dust, grime, and grease form a layer over the workspaces. Restorers address common signs of wear and tear, such as drawer runners worn down by nearly two centimeters and drawer backs reduced to half their original thickness by decades of knives repeatedly striking the wood. Conservators also remove inconspicuous (often inauthentic) additions. Particleboard, for example, signals a contemporary renovation since the original kitchens were composed primarily of solid softwood and wood-core board.

For Schepp and Frost, the Frankfurt Kitchen’s value is wrapped up in its endurance. Even during our current era of innovation and technological transformation, today’s kitchen looks remarkably similar to the one Schütte-Lihotzky designed 100 years earlier. And if high-quality, socially responsive design could be imagined and integrated then, it might still be possible now.

Gerlach, the photographer, began documenting the kitchens in 360-degree panoramas after discovering that existing photographs rarely captured the relationship between their individual components. Her aim was to preserve the Frankfurt Kitchen as a “concept rather than merely a piece of furniture,” allowing future generations to understand how the space functioned as a whole.

Because of the housing administration’s policies, removal was historically the only route to preservation for most Frankfurt kitchens. But over the past two years, that has begun to shift. Several Neues Frankfurt estates and their original interiors have been placed under Denkmalschutz, or landmark protection, and the city has hired Frost to restore the kitchens for new tenants. Working with architect Kristian Kaffenberger, she preserves the 1920s cabinetry while incorporating space for refrigerators and dishwashers, adding storage, and raising the counters from 31 to 35 inches to accommodate both the shift from seated to standing work and a century of increasing average heights for men and women. Frost likens the distinction between conserving a kitchen in place and reconstructing one in a museum to the difference between a musical instrument still being played and one only on display. “Both are necessary,” she says. “Museums have been very, very important in ensuring their survival.”

While museum displays of the Frankfurt Kitchen such as this one at the V&A East Storehouse are important, so is preserving them in their natural environment.

This program has slowed the flow of parts into the Fechenheim warehouse. Schepp recalls receiving as many as 10 calls in a single year, compared with roughly two or three annually today. Yet he considers the in situ initiative an extension of the society’s years-long efforts. “It cuts our source,” he says. “But we don’t mind. We consider it a success.”

#6170. Bringing a Date to a Wedding Is Basically a Relationship Test

There are a few big firsts to any new relationship: the first time you spend the night, the first time you meet their friends, and of course the first time you bring them as your “plus one” to a wedding.

For some people, bringing a date to a wedding is a big deal—you’re bringing a partner around your family and friends and they’ll probably be in someone’s wedding photos. For other people, it’s just another date—and the perfect opportunity to assess the relationship.

According to a Hily dating app survey, 51 percent of Gen Z daters and 46 percent of Millennials say the most appealing part of bringing someone to a wedding is getting to see how they behave around friends and family.

For the plus-one, a wedding is basically a social obstacle course. You’re surrounded by strangers, meeting someone's friends and family, making small talk, navigating a crowded dance floor, potentially drinking, and trying to figure out how much time you’re supposed to spend attached to your date versus mingling on your own. This is exactly why it can reveal things about a relationship that your usual date-night routine might not.

A wedding gives you a chance to see how your partner interacts with people they’ve known for years, people they’ve just met, and people they may not particularly like—all in one night.

“If you were invited and have been dating for, let's say, six months to a year, yes, I do think it can be a nice test,” matchmaker and relationship expert Susan Trombetti tells Cosmopolitan. “They may be able to meet extended friends and family. Plus, you can see how they handle big events like this.”

So what, exactly, should you be watching for?

First: Do they make an effort to participate?

That doesn’t mean your partner needs to be the life of the party or spend six straight hours on the dance floor (unless that’s what you’re looking for). But if they’re meeting your friends and family for the first time, you probably want to see some willingness to spark up a conversation.

“Are they on the dance floor? Are they engaging with family and friends? Or are they sitting in the corner alone and quiet?” Trombetti says. “Did they drink too much? All of these are very telling of how a person is.”

The point isn’t to judge someone for being introverted or nervous. A person can be shy at first and still make an effort to participate. What you’re really looking for is how they handle being outside their comfort zone—and whether they’re willing to meet you there.

A partner who spends the entire night sulking because they don’t know anyone, refuses to talk to your friends, or gets sloppy drunk might be giving you some pretty useful information. So might the person who happily strikes up a conversation with your college roommate, dances with your aunt, and somehow ends up helping your dad find his missing jacket.

There’s another important part of the wedding test: how your partner handles the fact that you probably won’t be able to give them your undivided attention.

If you’re in the wedding party, you may be running around taking photos, giving a speech, helping the couple, or getting pulled into conversations with relatives you haven’t seen in years. Your date could easily end up feeling abandoned, especially if they don’t know anyone. That’s why the responsibility isn’t entirely on the plus-one.

“If your newer partner is attending with you and does not know anyone, try to introduce them prior to another guest that you think they might vibe with,” Trombetti says. “Communicate with your partner that you will be busy and you want them to have fun!”

Then, actually make good on that promise.

“Check in with them even if you are busy,” she says. “And when the night calms down—pictures are done, toasts, etc.—sit with them, bring them on the dance floor, and introduce them to your friends and family so they can join the conversations.”

Basically: Don’t drop your new partner into a room full of your loved ones and expect them to entertain themselves for five hours.

Also, a little wedding insecurity is understandable. You might be talking to an ex you haven’t seen in years. Your college friends might be swapping embarrassing stories about you. Your partner could suddenly find themselves surrounded by people who have known you much longer than they have.

Trombetti says jealousy or feeling overwhelmed can become particularly telling when you’ve already made a genuine effort to help your partner feel comfortable.

On the flip side, a partner who can roll with the chaos, meet your people, and have a good time—even when you’re occasionally pulled away—is a pretty green flag.

Of course, not everyone sees a wedding invitation as a major relationship milestone. And increasingly, people are even treating weddings as a dating opportunity from the start. More than half of Millennials say they’d likely accept a wedding plus-one invitation from a dating-app match they’d never met in person. The same survey found that 57 percent of Gen Z daters and 50 percent of Millennials say strong chemistry alone would make them comfortable bringing a new date to a wedding.

For some people, “Want to come to my friend’s wedding with me?” is less meet my family and more “want to get dressed up and have a weirdly elaborate first date?” (just with free food).

But whether you’ve known them for a few months or a few minutes, the wedding shouldn’t necessarily be a test for if you found “The One.” It’s more an experiment to see whether the person you’re dating can handle real life with you.

Can they navigate a room full of people they don’t know? Can they give you space when you’re busy and still make an effort to connect with the people who matter to you? Can they have fun without getting wildly drunk? Can they manage their jealousy? Can the two of you survive an evening that doesn’t revolve entirely around each other? Can you have fun together?

Eventually, relationships are going to involve more than dinner reservations and cozy nights in. There will be family functions, work parties, birthdays, holidays, awkward social obligations and approximately a million situations where you’re both going to have to figure out how to be a team in a room full of other people. The wedding guest relationship test can give you a little glimpse into what life with that other person might be like.

And hopefully it goes so well that one day, you can have a wedding of your own that your friends can use a test for their own relationships.

#6168. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

If you still appreciate elements of the floral look, you can certainly embrace it.

#6165. The 5 Shoes Every Man Should Own

From chilling in the park to a black-tie gala, these picks will cover any situation life can throw at you.

Every product was carefully curated by an Esquire editor. We may earn a commission from these links. Here’s how we test products and why you should trust us.

Nothing wrecks the vibe of a good outfit more effectively than a bad pair of shoes. This is empirically verifiable. You just need to look at a politician wearing a perfectly nice suit with horrible hybrid shoes—you know the ones; sneaker soles but dressy up top—to see the phenomenon for yourself. It ain’t great.

So what’s a guy to do? Get the right shoes, of course. You can’t screw up a carefully considered suit with a terrible pair of kicks if you’ve jettisoned all the flawed footwear from your rotation and replaced it with a lineup of lace-ups and slip-ons that always look great. Which is exactly why we’ve pulled together this list of the five shoe styles every guy should know and, ideally, own.

You might balk at the declaration that you need five different shoes just to lay the right foundation for your wardrobe, and that’s a reasonable reaction. As you read through what’s below, you’ll see that we’ve provided some broader context and a few tips for those who can’t—or won’t—get everything at once. The idea here is to give you the necessary guidance for building, over whatever timeline works for you, a collection that can handle pretty much everything life could throw your way style-wise. (So we’re not including exercise gear, which should be considered from a “function-first” perspective.)

Whether you’re busting out the tux for a black-tie gala, trying to impress the in-laws over dinner, or simply popping out for a cup of coffee, these are the five shoe styles that’ll serve you well—and, if you take care of them, for many years to come. Let’s get into it.

A couple years ago, in the Fall/Winter 2024 edition of the Big Black Book, we posited that derbies are the most versatile shoes you can own right now. This still holds true. Rooted in classic menswear but designed for casual use, they’ll pair perfectly with everything from a tweed or corduroy suit to beat-up jeans and a white tee. We know guys love their sneakers, but if you could have just one shoe, we’d heartily recommend the derby.

The problem is that a lot of brands out there call their shoes by all the wrong kinds of names. So we need to get a little bit technical for a moment. You can identify a derby by its “open” lacing system, in which the facings (the parts that have the eyelets) are sewn on top of the vamp (the front part of the shoe). That’s what differentiates it from its more formal cousin, the oxford, which has a “closed” lacing system in which the facings are sewn into the vamp, creating a dressier and more streamlined look. You can find all sorts of derbies out there—with broguing, with split or moccasin-style toes, with wing tips or cap toes. But our favorite, when it comes to sheer adaptability, is a simple, rounded, plain toe.

You can get derbies from just about every brand that does proper shoes, which means that the list of labels we’re about to recommend here applies to the rest of the footwear styles we talk about in this article, with the exception of sneakers. (We’ll toss in other notable names where applicable.) Meermin, Beckett Simonon, and Thursday Boot Co. offer solid value near the lower end of the price spectrum, and they all specialize in shoes as opposed to a full “lifestyle” offering, which is a good sign when you’re buying footwear. If we’re talking mall brands, J. Crew is a good spot to look, as is Dr. Martens if you want something less expensive with a little more attitude. Moving up, you’ve got players like Grant Stone, Morjas, Grenson’s G2 line, Vinny’s, and Allen Edmonds (skip the hybrid BS and go for the classics). Stepping over from there you find yourself in the classic English shoemaker space, which covers everything from Cheaney, Loake, and Crockett & Jones to Edward Green, George Cleverley, Tricker’s, Grenson’s G1 line, and John Lobb. If you prefer something Spanish, you can’t go wrong with Carmina. French? Paraboot. And for the Americans? Let us never forget good old Alden. We could keep going, but for the sake of all our sanity, let’s consider that incomplete list a solid starting point.

You’re going to need a formal shoe at some point, because you’re going to need to wear a business suit—something in dark, slightly sheeny worsted wool, as opposed to a casual suit in cotton or tweed—at some point. The right shoe for a dressier suit is the oxford. As we said before, that’s thanks to the closed lacing system, which makes the silhouette sleeker and sharper. Start with black, because it goes well with navy, gray, and (duh) black. Keep it polished, and remember to use shoe trees after each wear to absorb excess moisture and retain the shape of the shoe. Some folks like a plain toe for an oxford. Some like a little broguing. Some like a wing tip and a lot of broguing. If you ask us, you can’t do better than a cap toe, sans the perforation.

Wear your oxfords to your next big business meeting or fancy date night if things are going great. Wear them to a funeral or to court if things are going ... not so great. This might offend purists who believe a tux can be worn only with a patent-leather pump, but we think that with a spit polish, you can even wear your oxfords with a tux. Just don’t tell the trads.

Think of loafers as a chilled-out take on the derby. Because they’re slip-ons, they’re just a touch more casual. That means they work very nicely with chinos—crisp or worn in, but we prefer the latter—and oxford-cloth button-downs. Or try yours with fatigue trousers and a chore jacket. And come summer, they’re the best hard-bottom shoe on this list to wear with shorts. But because they have long been a staple of preppy American style, they can—however incongruous some may find this—work very well with a navy suit, preferably from a Waspy institution like J. Press or Brooks Brothers.

As for what type of loafer? Well, the default is a penny loafer. We like the kind with a “beef-roll” detail on each side of the strap. (You can see it on the pair from J. Crew below.) But you can also opt for a more minimalist flat-strap penny loafer. There are also kiltie loafers, tassel loafers, venetian loafers with a totally unadorned vamp, and, of course, the famous horsebit loafer. It’s all a matter of preference, and, really, you can’t go wrong.

Maybe it’s rainy. Maybe you’re walking through the woods. Maybe you revealed that you can’t change a flat tire in front of your partner and you want to look a little more rugged the next time you get dressed. There are many reasons to pull on a pair of boots, and there is no version of life in which at least one of them doesn’t present itself to you in the nearish future.

The question, then, is which pair of boots you need. The answer: not something dressy. Not yet, at least. You’ve already got shoes for any occasion that requires formality. So the boots should be casual. You could opt for bulkier Chelseas from Blundstone, which wear nicely with jeans, feel great underfoot, and have the added benefit of decent weather resistance even though they aren’t billed as rain boots. You could get something like Thursday Boot Co.’s Captain lace-up, which draws from old service boots and looks about as classic as can be. (If you’re looking to invest, Wolverine’s 1000 Mile boot is a nice upgrade. And if you’re looking to splurge, Viberg also does a service boot that is just *chef’s kiss*.) Or you could go the workwear route with something from Red Wing Heritage or Nicks Boots. As with loafers, there’s not necessarily an incorrect choice in the general sense—it’s all about picking what feels right for you.

Please, sneakerheads, remember: We’re talking about a pared-down, only-the-necessities list here. If you’re in the culture, a true collector, feel free to hunt down as many limited-edition kicks as you please and let your freak flag fly when it comes to colorways. For the rest of us, a simple pair of sneakers in an easygoing white will do the trick 99.99 percent of the time. Converse makes a great pair. So do Vans, Sperry, Common Projects, Koio, Nike, Adidas, Reebok, New Balance, and just about any other brand you could name. Wear them on a long walk with a friend. Wear them to the park on a sunny day. Wear them for your early-morning coffee runs. Wear them to the office, if your office is cool with it (and most of them are these days).

You don’t need us to tell you what to wear them with. White sneakers go with everything. Well ... almost everything. Unless you want to look as if you’re trapped in 2014—or a European CEO who can’t let go of his youth—don’t wear them with a suit. There may come a day when that look feels fresh again. That day has not yet arrived.

You don’t have to stop at five if you don’t want to. Though the styles above will serve you well in pretty much all aspects of your life, there are plenty of other shoe styles that—while not technically essential—will be welcome additions to your footwear rotation. Some of these styles are more timely, like the ultra-low-profile sneakers that are still showing up on the feet of downtown cool guys who must have very strong arches. Others are perennial, like the much-beloved (if a little weird) Wallabee from our friends across the pond at British brand Clarks. And then there are things like roper boots, engineer boots, and cowboy boots. Clogs and slipper-like slip-ons. Even Mary Janes! Take your pick, man. Just make sure you’ve got enough room to store them all.

Anthropic's new Claude release, Sonnet 5.5, brings cybersecurity safeguards that can change which model handles a request during a Claude conversation. Higher-risk tasks can fall back to Sonnet 5.

According to Anthropic's explanation of model switching, automatic fallback is enabled by default in Claude. Users receive a notice, and the answer is labeled with the model that provided it. The chat then stays on Sonnet 5 unless the user changes the selection.

The company identifies penetration testing and exploit generation among activities that may trigger cybersecurity fallbacks. Source-code vulnerability scanning remains supported. Biology and reasoning-extraction safeguards can block requests outright, rather than route them elsewhere.

The distinction matters operationally. A block stops the request; a fallback allows an attempt to continue with a different model. Neither outcome should be treated as interchangeable with completion by the model the user originally chose.

Anthropic says Sonnet 5.5 generates output more than 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and can reduce cost per task by up to 30%, based on its testing. Token prices remain unchanged.

Those claims describe performance under the company's test conditions. An organization evaluating the service also needs to examine what happens when its own assignments encounter safeguards.

An internal pilot could record how often work is interrupted, whether fallback answers are usable, and when users need help. Such measurements would give buyers a more relevant basis for deployment than assuming every task receives the advertised model's capabilities.

Anthropic has announced plans to expand its Cyber Verification Program with tiered access for Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5, and Mythos models. Its current program guidance explicitly excludes the two new 5.5 models and says the expansion is forthcoming.

Organizations should distinguish those planned arrangements from access available today.

The broader enterprise issue is accountability. When an AI service changes models during an assignment, someone still needs to determine whether the resulting work is suitable. A visible handoff helps, but organizations must decide how employees should respond to it.

Astrocytes perform a diverse range of tasks that facilitate brain physiology, raising the possibility that these diverse astrocytic roles are enabled by functionally distinct subtypes1,2. Recent studies identified molecular diversity among astrocytes3; however, whether this molecular heterogeneity reflects specialized astrocyte subpopulations with distinct functions remains unclear. Here, on the basis of previous molecular and functional studies4, we generated a CD51-FLP mouse line that enables direct comparison of the functional properties between CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte subpopulations in neural circuits in vivo. We show that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes have unique morphological, physiological and developmental properties in both the cortex and hippocampus. Functional studies demonstrate that CD51+ astrocytes selectively promote synapse formation in the developing cortex and circuit plasticity in the hippocampus. Mechanistically, we found that CD51+ astrocytes function through glypican 6 to promote synapse formation in the cortex, while CD51 itself drives circuit plasticity in the hippocampus. Applying these findings to brain injury, we found that CD51+ astrocytes promote functional recovery after stroke through enhanced synaptic remodelling. Taken together, our studies identify specialized astrocyte subpopulations that exhibit distinct functional properties in the developing, mature and injured brain.

Cellular diversity is an operational necessity of all organ systems, in which diverse cell types participate in a division of labour that orchestrates essential physiological activities. Astrocytes are the most abundant type of glial cell in the central nervous system (CNS) and have vital roles in all facets of brain physiology, including neurotransmission, synaptogenesis, circuit function, metabolic support and maintenance of the blood–brain barrier, along with responses to injury and degeneration1,5,6,7. Advances in single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies have facilitated the identification of several prospective astrocyte subpopulations, suggesting that these diverse roles may be conducted by subsets of distinct astrocytes8. However, whether specialized astrocytes are capable of executing a subset of these diverse functions remains very poorly defined, as experimentally examining the extent to which molecular heterogeneity reflects functionally distinct subtypes of astrocytes in active neural circuits remains a major challenge. While several previous studies identified astrocyte populations that express unique markers and demonstrated roles for these specific genes in astrocytes in the context of neural circuits, whether these populations have unique functional properties remains unclear9,10. Unambiguous demonstration of functionally distinct astrocyte subpopulations at the cellular level requires a direct comparison of the functional properties between two populations in vivo. Critically, tools that enable selective labelling and manipulation of defined subsets of astrocytes, which would facilitate the direct comparison between different subpopulations of astrocytes, do not exist. Thus, whether functionally distinct subtypes of astrocytes exist in neural circuits has remained unresolved. Here we identify CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes as subpopulations with distinct properties and diverse functions in regulating neural circuits across postnatal cortical development, learning and memory, and brain repair.

Using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-based approaches in the adult mouse brain, our previous studies identified CD51, the αV integrin subunit encoded by Itgav, as a cell surface marker capable of subdividing subsets of astrocytes with unique molecular properties4. Whether CD51-expressing astrocytes are endowed with unique properties in vivo in functioning neural circuits remains unclear. We therefore generated a mouse line that enables the selective targeting of CD51+ astrocytes in the mouse brain. To achieve this, we engineered a CD51-FLP BAC (bacterial artificial chromosome) mouse line and intercrossed the CD51-FLP founder line with Rosa-FSF-tdTomato mice (where FSF denotes FRT-stop-FRT), such that the reporter is expressed in the presence of FLP activity (CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato) (Fig. 1a). Analysis of this mouse line revealed expression of CD51 protein in tdTomato-expressing cells, indicating successful targeting of this population (Extended Data Fig. 1c–e). tdTomato expression was also evident in a subset of endothelial cells, but rarely in other cells types (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Importantly, tdTomato expression in astrocytes was highly concordant with immunoreactivity to an anti-CD51 antibody (Extended Data Figs. 1c–e and 2c,d). To assess the extent to which the CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato line targets astrocytes in the brain of 8-week-old mice, we co-stained for the pan-astrocyte marker SOX9 and evaluated the constituency of CD51+ (tdTomato+SOX9+) and CD51− (tdTomato−SOX9+) astrocytes across a host of brain regions (Fig. 1b,c and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). This analysis revealed the presence of CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes in varying proportions throughout the brain, with CD51+ astrocytes representing approximately 19% of hippocampal astrocytes, 13% of olfactory bulb astrocytes and 38% of cortical astrocytes (Fig. 1c). Notably, our finding that 19% of astrocytes in the hippocampus are CD51+ is consistent with publicly available scRNA-seq data from the adult mouse hippocampus11 (Extended Data Fig. 1f). These data indicate that the CD51-FLP mouse can distinguish CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes in the adult brain.

We next sought to assess the cellular properties of CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte populations in the cortex and hippocampus, focusing on cell morphology and Ca2+ activity. The CD51-FLP mouse line allows us to use the FLP-FRT system to conditionally express reporters using intersectional viral approaches in CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes12,13. Combining these FLP-based viral tools with the CD51-FLP mouse, we can label CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte subpopulations with tdTomato (Fig. 1d). Accordingly, we injected the cortex or hippocampus of 4-week-old CD51-FLP mice, collected mouse tissue at 8 weeks of age and assessed expression of the conditional tdTomato reporter. Using the tdTomato reporter to assess morphology, we found that CD51+ astrocytes in the cortex exhibit enhanced morphological complexity compared with CD51− astrocytes; by contrast, CD51+ astrocytes in the hippocampus exhibited comparatively decreased complexity (Fig. 1e,f). Using similar approaches, we generated conditional adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) that enabled us to selectively target CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes with GCaMP6, an optical sensor that reliably reports Ca2+ activity14 (Fig. 1g). Using the same workflow as described above, we used two-photon imaging to measure Ca2+ activity in ex vivo slices from the cortex or hippocampus and found that CD51+ astrocytes in the cortex exhibit enhanced spontaneous Ca2+ activity compared with CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 1h,i). By contrast, in the hippocampus, CD51− astrocytes exhibited elevated spontaneous Ca2+ activity compared with CD51+ astrocytes (Fig. 1j,k). Together, these studies indicate that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes are endowed with unique morphological and physiological properties within and across the cortex and hippocampus.

The identification of unique properties for CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes in the adult brain led us to next examine whether they also exhibit unique developmental characteristics, such as proliferation and timelines of expansion. Using postnatal cortical development as a model, in conjunction with the CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato mouse line, we evaluated the constituency of CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes during the postnatal day 1 (P1) to P28 developmental interval when astrocytogenesis occurs in the cortex15,16,17. This analysis revealed a steady increase in the proportion of CD51+ astrocytes between P1 and P21, with the relative number of tdTomato+SOX9+ astrocytes peaking at about 40% by P21 (Fig. 1l–n). This finding was conserved across individual cortical layers (Extended Data Fig. 2e). These observations suggest that CD51+ cells undergo a proliferative expansion during this interval. Given that around 50% of cortical astrocytes are generated by local proliferation outside the germinal zones and in the brain parenchyma between P1 and P21 (refs. 15,16,18), we next assessed the relative proliferation rates of these populations. Using EdU-labelling approaches (Fig. 1l), we found that CD51− astrocytes are highly proliferative at P1, which is followed by enhanced proliferation of CD51+ astrocytes at P7–21 (Fig. 1m–o). Immunostaining for the cell cycle markers Ki-67 and phosphorylated histone H3 yielded the same pattern of results (Extended Data Fig. 2f–k). These data are consistent with our observation of increased numbers of CD51+ astrocytes during this interval, indicating that CD51+ astrocytes proliferate and expand in the brain parenchyma during the P1–21 interval. By contrast, CD51− astrocytes appear to be predominantly generated earlier in development and exhibit less proliferative capacity during the later stages of the P1–21 interval.

We next sought to understand the origin of CD51+ astrocytes. Analysis of developing astrocyte RNA-seq data19 revealed that expression of Itgav emerged after birth in astrocyte lineage cells (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Concordantly, using CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato mice, we found that SOX2+ precursors within the ventricular/subventricular zone did not express tdTomato (CD51) at the onset of cortical gliogenesis at embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5), but a subset was tdTomato positive at P1 (Extended Data Fig. 3c–e). These data suggest that CD51+ astrocytes are specified after the onset of gliogenesis. Notably, we identified binding motifs for the glial development transcription factor nuclear factor I-A (NFIA) in upstream regulatory regions of Itgav (Extended Data Fig. 3g), suggesting that NFIA could be involved in regulating CD51 expression and forming CD51+ astrocytes. To test this, we generated Aldh1l1-creER;NFIA-flox;Rosa-LSL-tdTomato mice, which enabled tamoxifen-inducible deletion of Nfia and expression of tdTomato in astrocytes (hereafter, Nfia conditional knockout (cKO); Extended Data Fig. 3h). Aldh1l1-creER;Rosa-LSL-tdTomato mice were used as controls. We injected pups with tamoxifen within 24 h of birth, when relatively few CD51+ astrocytes are present in the cortex (Fig. 1n). At P28, we found that Nfia-cKO mice had a significantly smaller proportion of CD51+ astrocytes in the cortex compared with the control mice (Extended Data Fig. 3h–l), indicating that NFIA regulates the development of CD51+ astrocytes.

Postnatal astrocyte development is regulated in part by extrinsic cues through neuronal-activity-dependent mechanisms20,21. To test whether neuronal activity affected the proportions of CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes, we used Gq-DREADD (designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs) stimulation of cortical excitatory neurons from P7–21 adapted from our previous study21 (Extended Data Fig. 3m–r). However, we found that this manipulation did not affect the proportions of astrocytes that were CD51+ or CD51−. Thus, the origin of the diverse CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte populations that we have identified appears to be predominantly dependent on cell-intrinsic genetic programs rather than neuronal activity.

Having found that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes exhibit unique properties, we next examined whether these populations demonstrate distinct molecular profiles. To achieve this, we intercrossed the CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato line with the Aldh1l1-GFP mouse line (Fig. 2a). This approach enabled us to FACS-isolate CD51+ (GFP+tdTomato+) and CD51− astrocytes (GFP+tdTomato−) from the cortex and hippocampus of 8-week-old mice (Extended Data Fig. 1c) and perform transcriptomic analysis using RNA-seq. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that the isolated CD51+GFP+ and CD51−GFP+ populations exhibit molecular signatures of astrocytes and demonstrate transcriptomic profiles that are distinct from one another (Extended Data Fig. 4a–d,f and Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, comparison with FACS-isolated astrocyte populations from our previous studies4 revealed a strong correlation between CD51+GFP+ astrocytes and population C, which also expresses CD51 (Extended Data Fig. 4e). Next, we assessed Gene Ontology (GO) terms enriched in CD51+ astrocytes from the cortex, finding genes associated with synaptic function, synapse assembly and interactions with neurons enriched in this population (Fig. 2b,c and Supplementary Table 2). Given that astrocytes have essential roles in synapse formation22,23, we next assessed the expression of known synaptogenic genes in CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes, finding enriched expression of glypican family members in CD51+ astrocytes (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 5). In summary, these data indicate that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes have unique molecular features.

The foregoing transcriptomic analyses indicate that CD51+ astrocytes are endowed with unique synaptogenic properties. Moreover, our previous in vitro studies suggest that CD51+ populations engender enhanced synapse formation on cultured neurons4. Together, these observations led us to examine the capacity of CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes to promote synapse formation in vivo in the developing postnatal cortex. Previous studies have shown that chemogenetic, DREADD-based (hM3Dq) stimulation of astrocytes promotes neuronal circuit activity24,25. These collective observations led us to examine whether DREADD-based stimulation of CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes can promote synapse formation during postnatal cortical development. To test this, we combined conditional AAV tools with FLP-on or FLP-off elements regulating hM3Dq expression with the CD51-FLP mouse lines, such that, in one condition, hM3Dq is expressed exclusively in CD51+ astrocytes and, in the other condition, it is expressed exclusively in CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 2e–h). Following our established protocol for chemogenetic stimulation in the developing cortex21, we injected AAVs at P0–2, treated the mice with 5 mg per kg of clozapine N-oxide (CNO) (or saline as a control) from P7–21 to stimulate astrocytes and collected mouse tissue at P21 (Fig. 2e). We next used immunostaining to assess synapse numbers in layers II/III of the cortex, finding that DREADD-based stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes promoted a robust increase in the formation of excitatory synapses (Fig. 2i,j), as well as inhibitory synapses (Extended Data Fig. 6a–d). Notably, stimulation of CD51− astrocytes did not significantly enhance excitatory or inhibitory synapse formation (Fig. 2i,j and Extended Data Fig. 6a–d). In parallel, we performed electrophysiological recordings from layer II/III at P21 and found that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes led to a concordant increase in synaptic activity, while stimulation of CD51− astrocytes did not (Fig. 2k,l). Notably, after CD51+ astrocyte stimulation, we observed an increase in synaptic current frequency, but not amplitude, which is consistent with an increase in the number of synapses (Fig. 2k,l). Critically, our astrocyte stimulation paradigm did not alter neuronal numbers or layering, indicating the observed phenomenon is not due to alterations in the constituency or organization of cortical neurons (Extended Data Fig. 7). hM3D+ astrocytes robustly expressed FOS 90 min after the last CNO injection, validating that our repeated stimulation paradigm enabled persistent astrocyte activation (Extended Data Fig. 8a–c). Moreover, repeated CNO administration in the absence of hM3D did not affect synapse numbers (Extended Data Fig. 8d–f). These data indicate that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes selectively promotes synapse formation in the developing cortex.

These observations led us to investigate the mechanisms by which CD51+ astrocytes promote synapse formation in the developing cortex. Our transcriptomic analysis identified members of the glypican family as demonstrating enriched expression in cortical CD51+ astrocytes26,27 (Fig. 2d). Using viral approaches in CD51-FLP mice to label CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes, we found that glypican 6 (GPC6) protein expression is enriched in CD51+ astrocytes (Extended Data Fig. 5c). This led us to next examine whether Gpc6 expression in CD51+ astrocytes is necessary for the enhanced synaptogenic effect we observed (Fig. 2i–l). To achieve this, we intercrossed the CD51-FLP line with the Rosa-LSL-Cas9 line generating the CD51-FLP;Rosa-LSL-Cas9-eGFP mouse line (where LSL denotes loxP-stop-loxP, for Cre-dependent Cas9-eGFP expression). Using a similar paradigm as described above, mice were injected with an AAV containing single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting Gpc6 (omitted in the controls), an AAV containing an FLP-inducible astrocyte-specific Cre and the abovementioned AAV driving FLP-dependent astrocyte-specific hM3Dq expression (Fig. 2m). Together, this system enabled us to delete Gpc6 from and subsequently stimulate CD51+ astrocytes. Mice were injected at P1, treated with CNO beginning at P7 and collected at P21 (Fig. 2m); subsequent analysis of GPC6 expression revealed robust and selective KO in CD51+ astrocytes expressing Cre (Extended Data Fig. 6e–g). We next assessed synapse formation through immunostaining and found that KO of Gpc6 eliminated hM3Dq-induced increases in excitatory synapse formation (Fig. 2n,o). Furthermore, overexpression of Gpc6 with AAV-GFAP-Gpc6 injected intracortically between P1 was sufficient to increase synapse numbers measured at P21 (Extended Data Fig. 8g–k). RNA-seq analysis of hM3D–mCherry+CD51+ astrocytes following our CNO or saline injection paradigm (Extended Data Fig. 9) revealed an upregulation of pathways related to protein trafficking to distal processes, suggesting that Gq stimulation may promote delivery of GPC6 to astrocyte leaflets where it drives synapse formation. Collectively, these data indicate that CD51+ astrocytes selectively promote synapse formation and use GPC6 as part of this mechanism.

The preceding finding established that CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte populations have distinct functional properties towards synaptogenesis in the developing cortex. We next sought to expand our functional comparison between CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes to other brain regions, associated circuits and stages of life, focusing on the adult hippocampus. Transcriptomic analysis of hippocampal CD51+ astrocytes indicates that they are also enriched in synaptogenic and synaptic transmission-related genes when compared with CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 2c,d and Extended Data Fig. 4a–c). These observations, coupled with the synaptogenic function of CD51+ astrocytes in the developing cortex, led us to hypothesize that they may also selectively influence hippocampal circuits and associated memory behaviours. To test this, we combined the CD51-FLP mouse with the conditional AAV tools that enable selective expression of hM3Dq in CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes (Figs. 2f and 3a), injecting the hippocampus of 8-week-old mice. Key controls included using AAVs containing hM3Dq that did not selectively target CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes, as previous studies have shown that pan-astrocytic stimulation of hippocampal astrocytes can enhance hippocampal circuit function24,25. As a frontline experiment, we collected mouse tissue 4 weeks after injection, generated hippocampal slices and evaluated long-term potentiation (LTP). Using subthreshold stimulation (40 Hz, 10 pulses) of the Schaffer collateral, we found that stimulation of pan-astrocytes, CD51+ astrocytes and CD51− astrocytes were all capable of inducing LTP (Extended Data Fig. 10c,d). In parallel, we generated another set of these experimental groups and performed a series of memory behaviours, finding that DREADD-based stimulation led to enhanced memory performance in each group (Extended Data Fig. 10e,f).

In the hippocampus, around 80–85% of astrocytes are CD51−, while about 15–20% of astrocytes are CD51+ (Fig. 1c). Our observation that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes yielded the same enhancement of LTP and memory performance as CD51− astrocytes, despite four to sixfold less representation in the hippocampus, suggests that CD51+ astrocytes are significantly more effective at enhancing hippocampal circuit plasticity than CD51− astrocytes. To test this, we repeated these experiments using a dilution of the GFAP-FRT-hM3Dq-mCherry-FRT AAV that targets CD51− astrocytes. We found that a 1:50 dilution restricts expression in CD51− astrocytes to around 15–20% of all astrocytes, matching the proportion of CD51+ astrocytes in the hippocampus (Extended Data Fig. 10a,b). Similar to the above studies, we injected CD51-FLP mice with undiluted versions of pan-astrocytic and CD51+ targeting virus, as well as the 1:50 dilution of the CD51− targeting virus at 8 weeks and collected mouse tissue 4 weeks later for LTP studies, while also conducting memory behaviour analysis on a separate cohort at the same timepoint. Notably, these studies revealed that stimulation of around 20% of CD51− astrocytes was incapable of inducing LTP at subthreshold levels or enhancing memory performance, while stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes induced LTP and enhanced memory performance (Fig. 3d–g). Consistent with these findings, analysis of basal synaptic activity of CA1 neurons revealed that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes promotes synaptic activity, while targeting around 20% of CD51− astrocytes did not (Fig. 3b,c). Additional studies revealed no difference in paired-pulse ratio, indicating that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes enhances synaptic input and connectivity (Extended Data Fig. 11). Furthermore, the levels of LTP, synaptic activity and memory performance after stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes was comparable to pan-astrocyte stimulation (Fig. 3c–g and Extended Data Fig. 10c–f). Collectively, these data indicate that CD51+ astrocytes are more effective than CD51− astrocytes at promoting hippocampal circuit plasticity and associated memory behaviours.

Our studies in the developing cortex and adult hippocampus indicate that CD51+ astrocytes have specialized functions that facilitate neural circuit activity. To further understand the mechanisms by which this population engenders circuit function, we focused on CD51 itself, which is encoded by the gene integrin alpha V28. We used the adult hippocampus as a model system in which to assess how manipulation of CD51 affects circuit function. To delete Itgav in hippocampal astrocytes, we used similar approaches used in the GPC6 experiment (Fig. 2m), except in these studies we injected Rosa-LSL-Cas9 mice with AAVs to express (1) a gRNA targeting Itgav; and (2) cre under control of the Gfap promoter (Fig. 4a). Littermates not carrying the Rosa-LSL-Cas9 allele were used as controls. When put together, this system enabled us to selectively express Cas9 and the Itgav-targeting gRNA in hippocampal astrocytes. Accordingly, we injected the hippocampus of 8-week-old LSL-Cas9 mice (and wild-type littermates) and collected mouse tissue 1 month later, confirming selective deletion of Itgav in hippocampal astrocytes (Fig. 4b). Using a similar workflow to that described above, we generated mice in which Itgav has been knocked out in hippocampal astrocytes, collected mouse tissue 4 weeks after injection and assessed LTP and basal synaptic activity. Using theta-burst stimulation to evoke LTP, we found that deletion of astrocytic Itgav led to a loss of potentiation (Fig. 4c,d), coupled with a significant reduction in the basal levels of synaptic activity in CA1 neurons (Fig. 4e–g). Using a parallel cohort of mice generated using the same workflow, we conducted a series of memory behavioural assays, finding that mice with Itgav-KO exhibited significant defects in memory performance (Fig. 4h,i). To test this system, we injected the hippocampus of Aldh1l1-GFP or wild-type mice at 8 weeks of age, collected mouse tissue 1 month later and confirmed robust overexpression of HA–CD51 specifically in astrocytes (Fig. 4l and Extended Data Fig. 12). We next repeated this experiment on wild-type mice, using LTP and basal synaptic properties as our frontline experiments on mouse tissue collected 1 month after AAV injection. For the LTP studies, we used subthreshold stimulation (40 Hz, 10 pulse) of the Schaffer collateral and found that overexpression of CD51 in hippocampal astrocytes elicited LTP (Fig. 4m,n). Parallel studies on memory behaviours revealed a congruent increase in both spatial- and fear-memory behaviours after CD51 overexpression (Fig. 4q,r). Taken together, these studies indicate that astrocytic CD51 protein has a direct role in regulating hippocampal circuit activity and contributes to the specialized synaptic functions of CD51+ astrocytes.

To further understand how CD51 influences hippocampal circuit function, we overexpressed HA–CD51 in both hippocampal and cortical astrocytes, and performed immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry (IP–MS) to identify prospective interacting partners specific to each region. Analysis of the protein interactome for CD51 revealed region-specific interactions (Extended Data Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 3). Focusing on CD51-interacting proteins that are specific to the hippocampus, we found that CD51 interacted with NMDA receptors (GRIN1), GABA transporters (SLC32A1), Na+/K+ channels (HCN1) and SLC16A7 (also known as MCT2). Interactions with these proteins is consistent with a role for CD51 in modulating hippocampal circuit function.

Astrocytes have central roles in every form of neurological pathology29. As CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes exhibit unique functional properties during cortical development and in functioning neural circuits, we next examined whether these populations are endowed with distinct characteristics under pathological conditions. We focused these studies on cortical stroke injury because astrocytes are known to contribute to the repair process, of which synaptic remodelling is a key component30,31. We first evaluated core features of CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte populations in a cortical photothrombotic model of ischaemic stroke32,33. Using our FLP-dependent AAVs in conjunction with the CD51-FLP mouse (Fig. 1d), we assessed the morphological properties of peri-infarct CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes after stroke. These studies revealed that peri-infarct CD51+ astrocytes displayed an enhanced morphological complexity compared with CD51− astrocytes at 7 and 21 days after stroke (Fig. 5a,b). Moreover, we observed an accumulation of CD51+ astrocytes at the lesion border, suggesting that CD51+ astrocytes preferentially form the astrocyte border (Extended Data Fig. 14a). This finding was further supported by spatial transcriptomics after stroke in which elevated expression of Itgav is observed at the lesion border34 (Extended Data Fig. 14b). These observations, coupled with our findings that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes exhibit different proliferative capacities during postnatal cortical development (Fig. 1n,o) led us to assess their proliferative response after stroke, which is part of the astrocyte response to injury32. We induced strokes in CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato mice, labelled proliferating cells with EdU from days 3–7 after stroke and assessed proliferative responses at 21 days after injury (Fig. 5c). We found that CD51+ astrocytes (SOX9+tdTomato+) were more proliferative (EdU+) at the infarct border compared with CD51− astrocytes (SOX9+tdTomato−) (Fig. 5d,e). We observed similar results when assessing proliferation by staining for the cell cycle markers Ki-67 and phosphorylated histone H3 7 days after stroke (Extended Data Fig. 14c). Furthermore, we again observed an accumulation of CD51+ astrocytes at the lesion border (Fig. 5d). Together, these data indicate that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes exhibit unique cellular responses to cortical stroke, which parallel their distinct properties in the developing cortex.

Given the distinct properties of CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes responding to stroke, we next examined whether these two populations differentially interacted with synapses in peri-infarct cortex. To accomplish this, we used the Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based neuron-astrocyte proximity assay (NAPA), which enables measurement of astrocyte–synapse interactions at synaptic distance scales35. NAPA involves expressing extracellular membrane-targeted GFP (FRET donor) in astrocytes and presynapse-targeted mCherry (FRET acceptor) in a defined neuronal afferent projection. In this assay, FRET signals are reflective of synaptic-scale proximity between GFP-expressing astrocytes and mCherry-expressing synapses. We injected the cortex, targeting regions that would later surround the infarct, of two groups of CD51-FLP mice with AAVs encoding the astrocytic component (NAPA-a) controlled by Flp-dependent elements to specify expression in either CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 5f,g). In both groups, we labelled thalamocortical projections with the neuronal component (NAPA-n). We subjected mice to strokes 3 weeks after AAV injections and prepared tissue for FRET imaging in peri-infarct cortex 7 days later (Fig. 5f). Peri-infarct CD51+ astrocytes had significantly greater FRET signal than CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 5g,h). These data suggest that CD51+ astrocytes exhibit closer interactions with synapses in remodelling peri-infarct regions after stroke.

The preceding studies indicate that DREADD-based stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes selectively promotes synapse formation in the developing cortex (Fig. 2), leading us to examine whether such a manipulation would similarly affect synaptic remodelling after stroke, which is linked with functional recovery31. Using the CD51-FLP mouse line and FLP-dependent AAVs previously described (Fig. 2e,f), we expressed hM3Dq (or lck-smV5 as an inert control) in CD51+ or CD51− cortical astrocytes in regions that would later surround the infarct, and induced strokes 4 weeks later (Fig. 5i,j). Beginning 7 days after stroke, we treated mice with CNO or saline twice daily until 21 days after stroke (Fig. 5i,j). During this timeline, we assessed motor function using the grid walk assay at three key timepoints: (1) before stroke; (2) 1 week after stroke before commencing CNO or saline treatment; and (3) 3 weeks after stroke (after 2 weeks of CNO or saline treatment) (Fig. 5i). We found that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes resulted in enhanced recovery of motor function in comparison to the controls and stimulation of CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 5k). Importantly, there was no difference in the lesion volume between groups (Fig. 5l and Extended Data Fig. 14d). We next assessed synapse numbers in the peri-infarct cortex after stimulation, finding that CD51+ astrocyte stimulation increased numbers of excitatory and inhibitory synapses, while there were no observable differences in synapse formation after CD51− astrocyte stimulation (Fig. 5m–o and Extended Data Fig. 14e–g). We observed no differences in per-infarct vessel density, neuron numbers, GFAP+ immunoreactivity or IBA1+ immunoreactivity (Extended Data Fig. 15). These observations suggest that the functional recovery observed after CD51 stimulation is the result of increased synapse remodelling leading to enhanced restoration of circuit function. Collectively, these data indicate that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes selectively enhances functional recovery after stroke and that the unique synaptogenic properties of CD51+ astrocytes are conserved across postnatal development and repair after brain injury.

The nature of astrocyte diversity in the CNS has remained enigmatic owing to the paucity of tools that enable targeting of specific subpopulations, coupled with a lack of robust and clearly defined functional benchmarks1,2. In our study, we generated a mouse line and associated viral tools that enable selective targeting and manipulation of CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte subpopulations in the developing, mature and injured brain. We found that CD51+ astrocytes are functionally distinct from CD51− astrocytes and have specialized functions that support synaptogenesis in the developing and injured cortex, along with memory circuit function in the hippocampus. Critically, comparing the functional properties between subpopulation of cells is essential for operationally defining distinct subpopulations and our newly developed tools enable such direct comparisons between CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes. Inferenced heterogeneity from single-cell transcriptomic or developmental patterning data does not incorporate functional evaluation, as mRNA profiles cannot predict physiological outputs for astrocytes1,2,3. Parallel studies identified genes that exhibit restricted expression in subsets of astrocytes and demonstrated roles for these genes in core astrocyte functions; however, they do not directly compare the functional properties between prospective astrocyte subpopulations9,10. In contrast to neuronal diversity, which has clearly defined physiological, molecular and morphological criteria, defining astrocyte diversity is not as clear cut. Our findings indicate that astrocyte subpopulations exhibit graded functional properties, with CD51+ astrocytes being more efficient at promoting interactions with functioning neural circuits under normal and pathological conditions. These observations suggest that the specialized properties of CD51+ astrocytes are driven by interactions with neurons, insinuating that additional, functionally distinct astrocyte subpopulations may arise from interactions with vascular or immune populations.

Our finding that CD51+ astrocytes engender synapse formation in the postnatal cortex, in part through elevated expression of GPC6, and exhibit unique proliferative properties suggests that these functional properties are specified early in development. Indeed, we found CD51+ populations initially appear in the ventricular/subventricular zone around P1 and their genesis is tied to intrinsic transcription factor programs, further suggesting that they represent a specified astrocyte subtype with distinct functional properties. These findings are consistent with observations in the spinal cord indicating that developmental patterning mechanisms generate distinct subtypes of astrocytes36. By contrast, clonal analysis of cortical astrocytes suggests that morphological diversity of astrocytes is linked to environmental cues, occurring after migration from the germinal zones37. The extent to which the functional properties of CD51+ astrocytes are shaped by environmental cues or are pre-ordained is an important future direction, where clonal analysis of this population may provide additional insight. While neuronal activity did not affect the generation of CD51+ astrocytes in the cortex, it remains possible that their functional properties exhibit plasticity and are tied to specific neural circuits in other brain regions. In the mature brain, CD51+ astrocytes and Itgav itself robustly support hippocampal circuit function, while also executing parallel roles in repairing synapses and circuit function after cortical stroke injury. Roles for astrocytes in hippocampal circuits and memory are well known38,39,40; however, our findings suggest that a select subset of astrocytes are sufficient to promote memory. These results parallel recent findings demonstrating that ensembles of learning-associated astrocytes function within memory engrams and are sufficient to promote memory recall13. Collectively, these observations raise the possibility that CD51+ astrocytes are preferentially recruited to memory engrams in the hippocampus and, more broadly, that CD51+ astrocytes may specifically participate in engrams associated with other forms of learning41.

The fear-conditioning chamber was a 25 cm × 35 cm arena with a metal grid floor. The arenas had different patterns on some walls as visual cues. #6151. Jordan Roth Recreates His West Village Apartment in a Parisian Hôtel Particulier

In partnership with Design Miami, the award-winning producer debuts a new art installation, “To Think of the Tranquil Life of Things.”

Jordan Roth has seven Tony Awards, an enviable couture collection, and, most recently, an acting credit on Ryan Murphy’s new series The Shards. This week, the Broadway producer is adding his own Parisian art installation to the list.

In partnership with Design Miami and Salon 94 Design, Roth is taking over the Hôtel de Guise, filling the 18th century hôtel particulier with a collection of pieces he created. In this new installation, called “To Think of the Tranquil Life of Things,” Roth transposes his West Village apartment, featured in ELLE Decor in 2016, into this elaborate, Parisian space. Inside an exhibition designed by ELLE Decor A-List firm Charlap Hyman & Herrero, visitors will find 16 images of Roth, each printed on a swath of fabric and draped over an individual piece of furniture.

Roth came up with the idea when he and his husband, Richie Jackson, were moving out of the New York apartment where Roth had spent the past 15 years and where, Roth says, “so much life happened.” He tells ELLE Decor, “I wanted to record this life. It’s about the way in which the objects we live with carry stories.”

When it came time to leave, Roth thought of a novel way to preserve those memories. “The movers were coming to take the furniture away,” when he had an idea: to shoot himself in 16 different looks he had worn while living in the home, he says. The images were photographed all with Roth sitting on various pieces of furniture, printed onto fabric, and draped over the Parisian furnishings. He also recreated the rooms in the exact layout of the New York home, but now in this Paris mansion. The inspiration stemmed from slipcovers but, he says, instead of concealing, “we are revealing something.”

The exhibit’s big moment comes in the form of the “chintz room,” a space where the furniture is covered in a fabric printed with dahlias, the flowers Roth grows and loves. While the installation is personal, Roth hopes his visitors also take with them an opportunity for self-reflection.

“I would be really happy if somebody came to experience this work and then went home and sat on their own couch,” he says, “and started to see their own history and aesthetic evolution and the narrative it holds.”
