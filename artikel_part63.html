Onimusha: Way of the Sword has received universal praise for its combat animations, which somehow manage to look natural and fluid no matter what angle or direction hero Miyamoto Musashi has to swing his sword to parry an incoming blow. In a post-launch interview, I asked Capcom how—beyond doing a ton of work across Onimusha's lengthy development—they managed to raise the bar for this sort of action game.

"Starting the project six years ago, we felt it was important to have that detail to sell the power and the excitement of the action, so it's something we really focused on," said director Satoru Nihei.

"Even at that starting point, we knew it was something that Capcom, as a company, have as a great strength: We have a lot of assets and tech available to us that allow us to create these varieties of actions. That's something we really felt we could leverage to make the game really stand out that a lot of other places can't really copy. On top of producing a lot of animations and motion capture, we have to give a lot of credit to the programmers for taking that data and putting it in a form that we can have them make these very smooth transitions, with these physics-based calculations, to go from one animation to another. It was a big team effort in getting the animations to look how they do."

PC Gamer actually got a behind-the-scenes look at that process when we traveled to Osaka this summer to see Onimusha: Way of the Sword wrapping up development.

The effort put into Onimusha's animations is even more impressive within the scope of the final game and its roster of intricately choreographed boss fights. Despite being the third of Capcom's three big releases this year, following Resident Evil: Requiem and Pragmata, Onimusha features some fancy samurai dismemberment tech new to the publisher's in-house game engine. Rather than factoring into the more scripted boss fights, it comes into play when Musashi chops weaker enemies into pieces with dramatic finishers like the lightning fast Issen strike.

"One particular piece of technology that was added specifically for this title that we couldn't have done before is what we call the dynamic dismemberment system," Nihei said. "If you slash enemies with certain attacks they get cut in the exact path that a sword passes through. While that's something that has been done in the past in other games, one of the challenges and things we built on that was brand new with Onimusha was having that happen along with enemies still being animated as they're dying and dropping.

"In lots of other games you'd cut them, they'd kind of freeze when you do the cut and then fall apart. We had to have the programmers add to RE Engine to be able to enable that to really sell the impact of moves like the Issen. That was something brand new added to the engine."

#3298. Romain Gavras Is Bummed About Those AI Versions of “Storm”

Anyone who caught Yung Lean’s MTV VMAs performance of “Storm,” his short film with director Romain Gavras’ audiovisual project Gener8ion, is no stranger to the complex choreography that went into the project. Unfortunately, that hasn’t stopped a bevy of copycats from “recreating” the film with AI, subbing in Yung Lean and the video’s crew of young male dancers for dogs, babies, grandmothers, Elon Musk, Jeffrey Epstein, and even Hitler.

The trajectory appears to have left no one more disappointed than Gavras himself, who told Vulture that the AI copies “made me sad for all the people who put in so much work on our video.” Per Gavras, the budget on the original film was “very, very, very, very low,” and he worked for free. Dancers were selected through a two-month casting search, and rehearsed with choreographer Damien Jalet for three days; it took Gavras about 15 tries to nail the unbroken take, captured on 16-mm film.

“A trend like this can make even good work look lame,” Gavras added. “Part of me wanted to quit filmmaking, move back to Greece, and become an olive farmer.” Although Gavras emphasized that he never intended “Storm” as a commentary on AI, he felt like AI companies took the video’s elaborate practical effects as a sort of dare. “The tech people were like, ‘Oh yeah? AI could never?’ Then they started replicating it."

Gavras also noted he’s exploring legal avenues to stop the slop, but the logistics of suing the multitudes of anonymous users behind the recreations are hairy to say the least. He told Vulture a few AI companies reached out about licensing the film, but he declined: “There’s an economic model coming that we didn’t want to be a part of."

In fact, all the copies were part of what galvanized him to present the project live at the MTV VMAs, where “Storm” was nominated for four awards. Gavras saw an opportunity to show the world that AI still can’t replicate what’s so cool about “Storm.”

“They’re missing the swagger of Yung Lean,” Gavras said. “He looks like a dangerous baby. There’s something in the imperfection of humanity, and the interior life of someone who dances or even someone who just smokes a cigarette, that’s not quite there yet. But who the fuck knows what will happen in a year.”

#3293. How to Prepare Now so the End of Daylight Saving Time Is Less Jarring

Sadly, we did not manage to abolish Daylight Saving time changes during that brief, hopeful moment a few years back, so in just a few weeks we will all be going ahead with our usual ritual: Wake up early, forgetting that the time changed during the night, then get confused when the time on our phone doesn’t match the one on the microwave. Here’s how to be a little more prepared.

Daylight saving time is what we call the time in summer, and yes, it’s daylight “saving” time, singular. This time actually gets its own time zone abbreviation: EDT (Eastern Daylight Time) instead of EST (Eastern Standard Time) on the east coast, with corresponding CDT, MDT, and PDT across the country. So in November we change from daylight time to standard time.

Specifically, daylight saving time ends at 2 a.m. on Sunday, Nov. 1, 2026. That’s when the clocks “fall back.” (In the spring, they "spring forward"—get it?) If you’re changing your clocks manually, 2 a.m. becomes 1 a.m. This means that if you’re used to waking up at 7 a.m., you’ll open your eyes after sleeping the usual amount of time to find that it is now 6 a.m.

The fall time change is a great moment for me, a night owl. When I was younger I would be thrilled to stay up an extra hour and still not feel excessively tired in the morning. These days, I’m more likely to use the time change as an assist to my morning routine. I’ll be up at 6:30 (for example) instead of 7:30, and I can get in an early workout or do some journaling.

If you’re more of a morning person, this may be a bit less pleasant. You’re going to fall asleep watching TV in the evening—earlier than you usually do, anyway. Maybe you should try to stay up a bit, to get a head start on shifting your routine an hour later.

For the creatures in your house who are blissfully ignorant of the concept of standardized time, routines are about to get a little weird. Most importantly: They’ll get hungry (and everything else) an hour earlier than usual.

If you think ahead, you can start moving breakfast time later, before the time change arrives. Instead of feeding your dog at 7, start feeding him at 7:15 now, and you’ll be less likely to get a cold nose in the face at 6 a.m. (Since we have about two weeks from this writing, you can shift the time to 7:30 after a few days, and then 7:45. You get the idea.)

Same goes for kids: Expect them to wake up an hour before the clock says they should, and to get tired (and overtired) an hour earlier than they normally would. A gradual shifting of routines will help here as well, and you may want to explain the situation ahead of time to kids who are old enough to understand. And if you expect your own tummy to be grumbling on cue? Consider a gradual change in mealtimes for yourself, as well, or at least have a snack on hand in case you need it.

When daylight saving time ends, we stop “saving” that extra hour of sunlight in the evening. Your evening workout or your evening commute will be darker and colder than before. On the bright side, we’ll see light earlier in the morning, which can be a good excuse to take a morning walk you might not otherwise take. (Morning light and morning exercise both help to set your body clock and may put you in a better mood for the day to come.)

It may be easier to wake up after the time change, but the flip side is that it may be harder to go to bed. This is a good time to take a look at your bedtime routine, or even to create a bedtime routine if you haven’t really had one.

I have a guide to good sleep hygiene here, but one of the most important tips is to give yourself a wind-down time in the evening—30 minutes minimum—where the lights are dim and you aren’t staring at a screen. It’s a good time to take a bath, read a book, or do something else you find relaxing. Soon you’ll be locked in to your new schedule—at least until our next time change in the spring.

#3291. Should You Be Sleeping With Socks On? Doctors Have a Surprising Answer

You know that moment when you kick one foot out from under the covers at 2 a.m.? Turns out your body is doing something clever. Temperature regulation is one of the most underrated parts of falling and staying asleep, and your feet are doing a surprising amount of the work. Sleep doctors say the debate over socks at bedtime is more interesting than it sounds, and the answer depends on more than just whether your feet run cold.

For a lot of people, yes. Warming your feet helps your body release heat and drop its core temperature, which is basically your brain’s cue that it’s time to shut down.

Dr. Craig Zebuda, chief medical officer at Yunara Life, told Real Simple that “although wearing socks to bed may seem like a small thing, it can actually help your body prepare for sleep.” Dr. Monika Mathur, a neurologist and sleep medicine specialist at Kaiser Permanente in Southern California, told the publication that “some small studies have found that warming the feet may shorten the time it takes to fall asleep and improve certain measures of sleep quality.” She called it “a low-risk trick rather than a proven cure,” and said it probably works best for people whose feet actually run cold.

Your feet are basically your body’s radiators. As bedtime nears, your core temperature dips as part of your circadian rhythm, according to Cleveland Clinic. And a lot of that heat escapes through your hands and feet as you fall asleep, Dr. Yoel Green, a sleep medicine physician and clinical assistant professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at Stanford Healthcare, told Everyday Health.

Dr. Leah Kaylor, a licensed clinical psychologist specializing in trauma and sleep, broke down the mechanism to Newsweek. “The bottoms of your feet contain specialized blood vessels that regulate heat. These vessels can quickly release heat by increasing blood flow to the skin,” she said. And yes, that explains the foot thing. “Ever wondered why you instinctively kick a foot out from under the covers? It’s your body’s clever way of maintaining an optimal sleep temperature,” Kaylor told Newsweek.

Honestly? Modest. A small study suggests toasty feet can help some people drift off faster, but don’t expect miracles.

Green told Everyday Health that “there isn’t strong evidence that wearing socks to bed meaningfully improves sleep. The available evidence suggests only modest effects, and it’s not something we typically focus on in sleep care.” That said, Green noted socks might help some people feel cozier at bedtime, and feeling cozy makes falling asleep easier.

For most healthy adults, totally fine, Zebuda told Real Simple. The two things worth watching are socks that squeeze your ankles and feet that get sweaty.

Tight bands can leave marks and bug your circulation, and trapped moisture can trigger skin irritation or infection. People with diabetes, peripheral neuropathy or circulation issues should be extra careful with tight or scratchy socks, since small skin problems can get missed.

Loose, breathable and moisture wicking (think bamboo) is the move, Mathur told Real Simple. Ankle or crew length is a solid starting point.

“It is best to keep them loose at the top with no tight elastic bands, so the circulation is not cut off,” Mathur said. Zebuda had one last ground rule. “Most importantly, choose a clean, dry pair that’s comfortable enough that you barely notice you’re wearing them,” he told Real Simple. Swap in a fresh pair every night so bacteria doesn’t throw a party.

Strava has built its reputation on runs, rides, and swims. Strength training, however, has always felt like an afterthought in the app. Strava is trying to close that gap with its new guided strength workouts, and I spent some time with the beginner session to see whether it delivers.

The update brings guided workouts directly in the app. Instead of just logging that you lifted, you're walked through each exercise with animations, and you track sets and reps live as you go. Tap into any exercise, and you'll find form guidance, tips, and the specific muscles it targets.

When you finish, everything (every exercise, set, and rep) is saved automatically to your activity through the Workout Log. Your muscle map then updates to show where the effort went, which is a neat way to see at a glance whether you've been neglecting certain areas.

I started with the beginner workout, and I want to be clear about who it's for: true beginners. There's no equipment required, so you don't need dumbbells, bands, or a gym membership. If you've been lifting for years, this isn't going to challenge you—but that's clearly not the point. For this first workout, it's designed to lower the barrier to entry.

The interface is clean and easy to use. I especially liked the graphics, which make it simple to follow along without having to pause and decipher what a movement is supposed to look like. Everything worked smoothly throughout, with no glitches, lag, or lost progress.

Exercise instructions sometimes cut off. A few of the written descriptions were truncated with no way to see the full text, so I couldn't always read the full guidance on form.

Switching sides isn't always clear. For movements that work one side at a time, it wasn't obvious when I was supposed to switch. A simple cue or prompt would go a long way, especially for beginners who are already juggling a lot of new information.

Screenshot uploads: Snap a screenshot of text-based sets and reps from a training plan or notes app and import it instantly.

Build-your-own workouts: Create custom sessions instead of relying only on Strava's.

More partner integrations: So strength data can flow in automatically from wherever you already train.

These additions are what could turn this from a nice beginner tool into something I'd actually use. The real test will be whether Strava delivers the variety, progression, and range that lifters need to stick with it over time. #3274. Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 review – It’s good to see you again

Call of Duty. I know you well after 20 years of this franchise, but you can still surprise me.

My career as a journalist began with CoD all the way back in college. My senior thesis in journalism school was a 10,000-word magazine piece about the franchise in the early 2010s, because it was my obsession at the time. Since then, I’ve played and experienced all there is to offer with varying levels of enjoyment and satisfaction, so it’s been hard not to feel a general level of fatigue with the yearly releases.

But still, every once in a while, the franchise is capable of greatness despite the wear and tear. Modern Warfare 4 is the latest and best example of this, offering three distinct modes of play that are all deep and well-made, and worth the price of admission this year.

The biggest takeaway from this year’s CoD is that, given the time needed to create a full-fledged game with multiple dedicated modes without having to rush another game out the door, Infinity Ward (and its multiple partnered CoD studios under Activision) is still more than capable of using three-plus years of dev time to create a good and varied experience.

While CoD may have built its empire on multiplayer greatness, MW4 shows that a good campaign goes a long way. And this is far and away the franchise’s best in recent years, and one of the greatest in franchise history.

MW4 closes out the story of the MW reboot trilogy in epic, dramatic, and emotional fashion. Mission variety and high stakes are complemented by excellent visuals and sound design, and a narrative that feels worthy of the characters and story that people have come to love.

Captain Price is out for revenge. North Korea attacks South Korea and throws a squad of new and interesting soldiers into the mix. Ghost and Gaz are tasked with a difficult mission, leading to some epic fan-service. It’s really well-done throughout, especially when it comes to the standards of CoD campaigns in recent years, carried further by excellent performances from its cast.

I wrote at length about MW4’s campaign last week, but to put it succinctly, it’s a great experience, albeit a short one. And it’s been awesome to see most players agree, experiencing the campaign in early access before full launch and enjoying all it has to offer.

One controversial mission in the game opens it up to an open-world scenario, similar to previous campaigns, but I felt it was done very well this time. Playing as a South Korean soldier behind enemy lines in North Korea, you’re forced to scavenge, survive, and complete objectives to save your squad that’s out of commission. I found it as a nice change of pace from the rest of the campaign’s standard corridor set pieces, and given that most of it can be skipped/sped through if you wish to do so, I don’t mind it at all.

On the heels of the popularity of games like ARC Raiders and Escape From Tarkov, CoD returns with its own variant on the extraction shooter with DMZ, and it basically functions as a fully fleshed-out extraction shooter within MW4 on top of its other offerings.

DMZ’s Beta in Modern Warfare II four years ago was more of a sandbox and proof of concept. This time around, it’s got its own dedicated progression, multiple methods of play, story missions, an explorable home base, and it feels designed to keep players invested in the long haul.

Visiting Infinity Ward HQ and playing DMZ within the studio brought a lot of insight into the mode, and it’s very positive. I mentioned in my impressions of DMZ earlier this week that I think it has massive potential, but in the end I think it will come down to how PvE, PvP, and PvEvP are balanced. This feels especially important as MW4 is the first CoD in many years without a third mode dedicated entirely to PvE (like Zombies or Spec Ops), and some players are already calling for PvE-exclusive experiences in DMZ.

At launch, DMZ feels like a mostly PvE-centric experience as players learn the ropes, with PvP encounters taking a backseat thus far. Some players may love it, while the sweaty Warzone gamers who want to treat it like battle royale may be scared off if they can’t actively find other squads to wipe throughout each 30-minute match. Trying to keep many subsets of players happy is one of the biggest struggles of a CoD dev, and now it’s set to take place in an extraction shooter where losing a gunfight might mean losing dozens of hours of progress or more. I wish them luck with that.

DMZ will continue to take shape over the course of the next few months, with Infinity Ward (along with its partners Beenox, Digital Legends, Activision Shanghai, Raven Software, Treyarch, Sledgehammer Games, and High Moon Studios who all worked on different aspects of MW4) keeping a close eye on how things are going down within the exclusion zone in the fictional location of Hajin. Season one adds a new endgame-style map, and season two unlocks the northern part of the map that includes Russia. Couple that with new weapons and gear every month or so, and there should be plenty to keep players busy if the mode scratches an extraction itch.

And then we have the multiplayer component. Love it or hate it (I’ve experienced both feelings throughout the series), CoD’s multiplayer is often what keeps people coming back throughout seasonal releases for new maps, new modes, new weapons, unlockable camos, and more. Bucking the trend of the past two games, MW4’s multiplayer brings it back to a more boots-on-the-ground realism style of movement, ditching the omnimovement and wall-jumping found in Black Ops 6 and 7. It’s a welcome change, and should even the playing field a bit for players who dislike the wildness of “movement demons,” as they are not-so-lovingly called online.

The base experience of MW4’s multiplayer is solid. The gunplay feels fun, and the movement is still CoD-like but with less ridiculousness than the futuristic games offer, as players can still do things like mantle quickly over obstacles, slide and dive, and jump-shot their way to victory in gunfights.

Traditionally, map design defines the CoD multiplayer experience, and those available at launch are hit-or-miss so far (plus there’s only 12 of them), so I’m hoping to see some variety among seasonal releases, with some new additions likely joining remade classics such as Shipment and more.

There’s also a metric ton of challenges and unlocks to keep players busy for months, and several new modes are quite fun. While playing at Infinity Ward, I especially enjoyed the new Counter-Strike/VALORANT-like mode Collateral, and Gunfight on the evolving map called Kill Block. But the main question with CoD multiplayer over the past decade or so is if you can retain your sanity long enough to keep up the joy.

Once again, the CoD multiplayer discussion is set to be dominated by skill-based matchmaking. This is unfortunate, because the gameplay itself is pretty strong, so I’m mainly plagued with worries and questions about how everything will shake out over seasonal content drops and balancing updates.

Treyarch opened Pandora’s box last year by changing up the formula and testing matchmaking playlists of different varieties where skill is less of a consideration, such as the Open matchmaking style. It felt great for a while, but it’s still an evolving situation, as ever. Worries and my own personal frustrations as an aging gamer aside, the base multiplayer experience feels fun, but could easily be an issue for those who feel filtered out or punished by SBMM.

Over the next year, updates could make or break how MW4 is remembered as a CoD game by its most fervent players. But as a day one experience, all three of its modes feel representative of the time and care that went into them, marking this as one of the better entries in the franchise in recent memory.

#3255. Smash Bros. director Masahiro Sakurai thinks gen-AI will cause a surge of unoriginal games, but says human creativity can't be replicated

Masahiro Sakurai has spoken about the pitfalls of generative AI use in game development, saying that while he believes the technology will result in a surge of unoriginal video games, human creativity - such as that shown by designers like Ico's Fumito Ueda - can't be replicated.

In an interview with Japanese news outlet Denfaminicogamer, Sakurai was asked about his time judging for the Game Designers Awards (a role he recently retired from) and his thoughts on the massive increase of game releases since the awards began. In 2010, 275 games were published on Steam, whereas more than 19,000 released this year.

"19,000 is amazing, but I think that number will increase even more from now on," Sakurai said (as translated from Japanese using Google Translate). "I'm sure of it. That's because AI will increase the number. However, I think most of them will become 'unprofitable titles'. There are too many to keep track of, and it's impossible to understand them all.

"When it comes to things created with AI... or things mass-produced with poor quality, I think most of them will simply be reproductions of systems seen in various other games. I think there are already a lot of those now."

Sakurai then spoke about a recent (and lovely) release called Librarian: Tidy Up the Arcane Library!, which challenges players to organise books on shelves, and pointed to other puzzle games with similarly simple premises, such as finding a needle in a haystack or organising a supermarket. These kinds of games, he suggested, will be very easy for AI to replicate.

We've already seen this happen, of course. Needle in a Haystack Simulator, for example - which asks players to find a needle buried among 5 million pieces of hay - went viral thanks to its absurd yet compelling premise. Following its surge in popularity, copies were quickly created, prompting Needle in a Haystack developer Studio Bitdot to plop a bright red "original" tag on its Steam artwork.

But Sakurai continued, saying generative AI may become powerful enough to replicate more complicated games, like acclaimed designer Fumito Ueda's Ico or Shadow of the Colossus. However, he emphasised that Ueda's future games will be "something that AI simply cannot replicate ... What kind of artistic style will his future works possess? That's something only he can create. I think that's where human creativity lies."

"Basically, AI refers to things that have been created so far, but it can't refer to things that haven't been created before," Sakurai elaborated. "I think this is true not only for AI, but for various kinds of creative works as well.

"I wouldn't go so far as to call them 'pioneers', but rather people who forge their own path... people who understand and can create their own unique flavour... will become increasingly important from now on. I think this is especially true in an era of mass production and low-quality goods."

Quickly made AI games have become an increasing presence in the games industry of late. Valve's current Steam Next Fest features more than double the number of demos compared to last year, due in part to the influx of 'AI slop'. Some indie developers have even pulled planned demos and playtests out of fear their games might be copied by AI before they're properly released.

#3253. How Elizabeth Holmes Really Feels About Nathan Fielder's You Can See Everything

Elizabeth Holmes is a near-mythic figure in the business world and for true crime obsessives. As the founder of Theranos and a convicted fraudster currently serving time in federal prison (one whose life is still a mystery in many ways), there's much to explore. That's what the new documentary "You Can See Everything" exposes. But how does the film's subject feel about it?

The movie is less about what happened at Holmes' company Theranos and more about, well, her. It takes place 34 days before Holmes is sent to prison as she invites a skeptical film crew to document her every move. But what begins as an intimate portrait of a famed felon becomes a three-year journey into the abyss.

Directed by Nathan Fielder ("The Rehearsal") and Lance Oppenheim ("Primetime"), A24's "You Can See Everything" defied expectations at the box office on its opening weekend. It's also a major Oscar front runner. So, now that it's out in the world, Holmes is very much back in the conversation, and one imagines she would have certain feelings about that.

In an interview with Wired, Fielder revealed that he recently spoke to Holmes after completing work on the movie. During a phone call from prison, Holmes shared a fairly surprising reaction to the doc and the conversation surrounding it:

"I answered the phone, and I said, 'Hey, how's it going?' She asked how I was doing. I asked her if she had seen any stuff about the film coming out and how she felt about it all. She told me that she had seen some stuff, but most things she just isn't seeing, so she's heard things, but she hasn't really read anything. And then, at the end, she said to me: 'I trust you.' ... I said, 'OK, thanks.'"

Speaking further in the interview, Nathan Fielder explained that he and Elizabeth Holmes kept talking about "other stuff." That includes her kids and then some updates that he declined to share. But after that, just for emphasis, at the end of the call, Holmes once again said, "I trust you." To which, Fielder admitted, "I don't know what she meant by that."

There's a lot to unpack here. Anyone who has watched "You Can See Everything" can tell you that Holmes has, shall we say, an interesting way of processing the world around her. For whatever it may be worth, the documentary has been met with near-universal praise up to this point. /Film's Ethan Anderton called "You Can See Everything" a "mystifying masterwork of mind-melting manipulation" in his review, which was a perfect 10/10.

But Holmes and her life partner Billy Evans clearly wanted to use this documentary to help with Holmes' public image. To whatever degree that was or was not accomplished is really up to the viewer to decide. "You Can See Everything" raises more questions than it answers (for better or worse), and most of those questions surround Holmes.

Whatever this documentary does for Holmes and her public image, good or bad, she evidently trusts Fielder and Lance Oppenheim to tell this story as they see fit. So, even though she almost certainly has seen the movie in its entirety, her feeling is, it seems, that it was Fielder's story to tell, despite it being about her life.

"You Can See Everything" is in theaters now.
