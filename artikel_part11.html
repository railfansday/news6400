"Necklaces" of ultracold atoms have provided new evidence to support a nearly 100-year-old quantum theory.

In 1931, physicists were trying to use quantum mechanics to explain why some materials are magnetic. Then, a young physicist named Hans Bethe looked at a single row of electrons. Each electron's spin can point up or down. Bethe worked out the math for how such a row behaves and found that if a few spins in the row flip, they can stick together and travel down the row as a single unit.

Decades later, physicists found that Bethe's method also works for larger particles moving along a line, such as atoms. If those particles attract each other, the math predicts they will bind into clusters called Bethe strings.

Now, physicists have found evidence of these clusters in a line of ultracold atoms. The findings were published Aug. 29 in the journal Nature Communications.

The discovery may not create new gadgets, but it addresses a fundamental mystery in physics. Everything around us, from magnets to phone chips, consists of countless interacting particles. This makes their behavior difficult to predict. Ultracold atoms serve as a clear and adjustable way to study these complex systems.

Researchers have spotted small Bethe strings before, in magnetic crystals and superconducting quantum circuits ‪— but those experiments captured strings one at a time.

Theory predicts that crowds of strings, in many sizes, can change how an entire system behaves, and studying that idea requires making many strings at once. That has been hard to do with atoms. Atoms that attract each other usually collapse into a clump. But this time, the team appears to have made many strings of different sizes at once, including some with more than six atoms.

To make the "necklace" the researchers had to create a Bose-Einstein condensate, a special phase of matter.

It's tricky to get atoms to attract each other. In three dimensions, a gas made of attracting atoms tends to collapse as the atoms clump together and form molecules. That process releases energy that kicks atoms out of the experiment, so the gas quickly collapses and disappears. But when they're in a single line, the atoms behave differently.

"They are really a bit like pearls on a string, right? Like on a necklace, they cannot go past," study co-author Hanns-Christoph Nägerl, an experimental physicist at the University of Innsbruck, told Live Science. "In 3D, everything would collapse. But in 1D, it does not collapse."

To build their "necklaces," the team started with tens of thousands of cesium atoms cooled into a Bose-Einstein condensate ‪— a state of matter in which atoms chilled to near absolute zero share a single quantum state. Laser beams then divided the condensate into thousands of thin tubes, each of which held up to a few dozen atoms in a line.

At first, the atoms repelled each other. The researchers used a magnetic field to gradually turn that repulsion down to zero, and then turned it into attraction.

The team couldn't see the strings directly. Instead, they let the atoms go and measured how fast the cloud spread. When the atoms could expand only along their tubes, any strings stayed intact. When the atoms were freed in all three dimensions, the strings broke apart and released their binding energy as motion. The researchers found that the 3D clouds spread with about three times as much energy as the one-dimensional ones did.

From that difference, the team estimated how tightly the atoms had been bound. The answer pointed to strings of at least six atoms. The measurements also closely matched predictions from a theory called generalized hydrodynamics, which describes a gas like this one as a fluid made of many kinds of particles, including strings of different sizes. It also tracks how each kind flows over time rather than following every atom.

Nägerl was careful not to call this proof. "That some theory fits to your data is not a proof that something exists," he said; it's just more evidence to support it.

The strings likely don't survive long. Up close, the atoms still feel the three-dimensional world, and the strings eventually decay into ordinary cesium molecules. In the coldest runs, the team lost about 40% of the atoms.

"Transiently you create these Bethe string states," Nägerl said. "But I don't think they are long-lived."

He has an idea for future experiments. Physicists recently learned to shield ultracold molecules with microwaves, which keeps the molecules from colliding at close range. Strings made from shielded molecules might last long enough to be studied directly. But Nägerl stressed that the idea is untested. "I've had nobody do a theory check on my claims now, but I think intuitively this should work," he said.

Another option is quantum gas microscopy, which images atoms one at a time and could offer more direct evidence of individual strings.

For Nägerl, Bethe strings are one step in understanding how quantum systems with many particles behave and settle down over time. His long-term goal is an experiment in which physicists control both how every particle moves and how its spin flips, letting them assemble model materials piece by piece, "like a LEGO type," he said. His team has begun the next step: using lasers to control the spins of the atoms in its one-dimensional gas. He described Bethe strings as "a little diversion." But because the strings form whenever the atoms attract, they can't be ignored.

#5896. How Often Should You Exfoliate?

A good skincare routine doesn’t need to be long or complicated. But if you want to take your skin to the next level, it pays to widen your arsenal beyond a cleanser, moisturizer, and SPF alone. Exfoliating, for instance, is one of the more worthwhile upgrades to your routine. By removing dead skin cells from the surface, exfoliation can yield a smoother texture, fewer clogged pores, and a brighter, more even skin tone. “For men, this also translates to fewer ingrown hairs and razor bumps, a closer shave, and healthier skin under the beard,” adds Corey L. Hartman, MD, FAAD, board-certified dermatologist and founder of Skin Wellness Dermatology in Birmingham, Alabama.

Done right, exfoliating can clarify your complexion and visibly bring lackluster skin to life. Done wrong or too often, however, and you risk irritating the very skin you’re trying to improve. The trick is knowing how much exfoliation your skin can handle, and which formula deserves a spot on your shelf.

For many men, two to three times a week is a reasonable target, says Brendan Camp, MD, double board-certified dermatologist at MDCS Dermatology in New York. But the right cadence also depends on your skin type. “Those with more sensitive skin may not be able to tolerate that frequency, while those with more oily or thick skin may notice they can exfoliate more often,” Camp says. And if you’re new to exfoliation, it’s best to start once weekly and work your way up only if your skin tolerates it.

Frequency also depends on the formula. For instance, some exfoliating cleansers are designed to use daily, while stronger leave-on acids or abrasive scrubs are often reserved for less occasional use. Heed the product instructions, but also gauge how your skin responds.

If you shave regularly, your razor is already doing some exfoliating of its own. Shaving removes dead skin cells, too, which is worth factoring in before adding another exfoliant. Hartman advises saving exfoliators for non-shave days (or nights if you typically shave in the morning).

And in case you’re dealing with a flare from conditions like eczema or rosacea, Camp advises skipping this step until your skin recovers.

First things first: Choose between a physical and chemical exfoliator. According to Camp, both options improve skin texture and reveal a brighter complexion. Yet physical exfoliators—which often use tiny particles like beads, sugar, or salt to polish the skin—tend to offer more immediate results. The catch? It’s easy to mistake more scrubbing for a higher payoff. Go too hard, and you can wind up with irritated, angry skin. If the instant gratification of a manual polish appeals to you (and you’re not dealing with acne or irritation), opt for gentle formulas and don’t take them to your face like a sandblaster.

Then there’s the wonderful world of chemical exfoliators, which casts a wider net of benefits. “Glycolic acid is more hydrating, mandelic acid is great for pigment concerns, salicylic acid works well for acne-prone skin, and lactic acid is gentle,” says Hartman. Plenty of products pair several exfoliating acids together, conveniently tackling several concerns in a single step. (This is a safer bet than layering different exfoliating products on top of each other.)

It’s possible to overdo things with any type of exfoliator—whether that’s by using it too often or applying a product that’s too intense for your skin. “Over-exfoliating the skin can damage the skin barrier, predisposing the skin to dryness, irritation, and infection,” says Camp. Err on the side of caution by taking a low and slow approach. All the while, you’ll want to avoid layering too many actives on top of each other. For instance, it’s best not to pair exfoliators with the likes of vitamin C and retinol.

All the while, don’t forget to include supporting players in your regimen. When you exfoliate, Camp advises applying a hydrating serum and/or moisturizer right after. Star ingredients to look for include hyaluronic acid and glycerin. “This restores the integrity of the skin barrier and prevents dryness,” he says. Lastly, he adds that exfoliating can make your skin more sun-sensitive, so don’t skip your morning SPF.

No matter if you need supporting clearing breakouts or improving tone or texture, it’s well worth adding this step into your lineup. Just remember that a little exfoliation can go a long way.

Archaeologists have discovered an ancient base camp in Alaska that has evidence of the oldest dwelling and possibly the earliest domesticated dogs in the Americas.

Archaeologists digging at a 13,800-year-old site in interior Alaska have unearthed thousands of artifacts and fossils, including what may be the bones of the earliest domesticated dogs in the Americas, the earliest needles and dwellings in the Western Hemisphere, and stone tools that reveal cultural connections across the Bering Land Bridge.

At a site called Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ (which means "They Were There" in the Alaska Native Athabascan language), about 34 miles (55 kilometers) southeast of Fairbanks, archaeologists have excavated an encampment that some of the first people to settle in North America used off and on for a millennium.

Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ is located in eastern Beringia, the geographical region that connected northeast Asia and northwest North America during the last ice age. The most recent archaeological and genetic studies suggest that the ancestors of today's Indigenous Americans entered the continent around 21,000 years ago. But since Beringian sites are often the remains of small seasonal hunting camps and are scattered across a large geographic area, much is still unknown about the earliest people who called the Western Hemisphere home.

"I expected we'd find hunting tools and knapping [stone tool making] debris surrounding a hearth or two, but I had no idea that we'd stumble into a base camp with tens of thousands of artifacts," study first author Kelly Graf, an archaeologist at the University of Kansas, told Live Science in an email. "It was a total surprise!"

In a study published Wednesday (Oct. 14) in the journal Science Advances, Graf and co-authors detailed their discoveries at Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ, focusing on the earliest occupation, which occurred around 13,800 years ago, as evidence of people "settling in" to their new territory.

The researchers found a hearth, a small pit, and a dirt stain where a wooden post had once stood, likely the remains of a lean-to or early tent house. Additionally, the archaeologists unearthed stone tools, bone needles and a pendant made from an antler. Nearby, they found an earth oven, which was full of charcoal and animal bones and was likely used to slow-cook meat for a large family group.

"The complex feature at Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ represents the earliest potential case of a habitation structure in North America," the researchers wrote in the study. "Early Alaskans knew their surroundings well."

The archaeologists were also surprised by the stone tools recovered at Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ because they are distinctively shaped and unique to central Alaska. Known as diamond-shaped hafted bifaces, the tools were crafted from rhyolite and basalt and are similar to those typically found much farther west in Beringia, in modern-day northern Siberia. Additionally, obsidian artifacts discovered at Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ reveal that people brought the volcanic rock to the site from a geological source hundreds of miles to the northwest.

Bones from more than a dozen wolf-like animals suggest a further connection to Asia, according to the researchers. An analysis of the jaw of one of the animals showed that it was not from a dire wolf (Aenocyon dirus) or from a Pleistocene Beringian or gray wolf (Canis lupus sp.) but rather a population of wolf-like animals unique to central Alaska. Current genetic evidence suggests that early domesticated dogs in North America came from Siberian wolves. The timing and geographical location of Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ provides circumstantial evidence that the people may have been dog owners.

"Like humans, potentially, the canids of Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ and other early Alaskan sites were recent immigrants from Asian Beringia," the researchers wrote.

Although Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ reveals a relationship with sites in Asia, there is also evidence of connections with early American sites south of the Canadian ice sheets, according to the study.

One clue to these connections is the presence of bone artifacts in a sewing kit. The archaeologists found eight bone needle fragments at Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ. Previously, the only recovered eyed needles had come from Paleoindian sites in temperate North America. Therefore, the needles from Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ are the oldest needles ever discovered in the Western Hemisphere, and they reveal connections with sites farther south, according to the study.

Another clue is the pendant, which was crafted from antler. The pendant had a hole drilled at one end and was fashioned into a point at the other end. This piece of ancient jewelry is similar to personal adornment items found farther south in North America.

"The co-occurrence of these cultural features at the single site of Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ is truly notable," the researchers wrote, and they "highlight the strong cultural connection between early Beringians and Americans."

It is still unclear whether the people at Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ were traveling to make these connections. "It would be hard to pin down whether or not they were moving back and forth during a lifetime, but at least we can say there is a connection," Graf said.

Archaeologists recovered bison bone and a hearth, among other things, at the Beringian site.

Future excavations in Beringia could help archaeologists learn more about the earliest people in the Americas, according to Graf. "Alaska is a big place and so remote, so there are really just a few small areas that have been intensively investigated," she said.

Spencer Pelton, an archaeologist at the University of Wyoming who was not involved in the research, wrote in an accompanying perspective published in the journal Science Advances that early Indigenous sites in Beringia are key to understanding the peopling of the Americas.

"Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ is remarkable because it is the oldest site in the Americas containing a large and rich assemblage of features, artifacts, and faunal refuse indicative of prolonged residential occupation, providing an unprecedented glimpse into the lives of North America's first residents," Pelton wrote.

Graf is already planning future archaeological excavations in the area. "We're hoping to bring some younger Alaska Native folks out with us in the coming summers," she said, as incorporating local knowledge is important to the project. "Two of our co-authors are elders from Alaska Native corporations nearest to the site," she said. Elders named the Shégˊ Xdaltthˊíˊ site and have visited it to discuss where to excavate and what the archaeologists might find.

There is much left to learn about what was happening in Beringia 14,000 years ago, the researchers wrote, particularly since there are currently only two archaeological sites — including the 24,000-year-old Bluefish Caves site in the Yukon — that suggest humans were settling in Beringia considerably earlier.

Sometimes, it takes a great struggle to build towering peaks. And what more thunderous event is there than the clashing of continental plates?

Geological pileups have birthed some of the largest and tallest mountains on the planet, including Mount Everest and its Himalayan neighbors. But has there ever been a peak taller than Mount Everest, which stands 29,032 feet (8,849 meters) above sea level?

The answer is still a mystery, but there are some geological clues about where the tallest peaks would have been in the ancient past, experts told Live Science.

Geologists don't have the level of detail required to pinpoint an exact highest peak hundreds of millions of years ago. "We're never going to get that granularity, unfortunately," Alan Collins, a professor of geology at Adelaide University in Australia, told Live Science.

However, clues in residual sediments lodged inside Earth's crust hint at the lost grandeur of primordial snowcaps and summits. Scientists study these crusts to understand how deep they went, which can reveal how tall ancient mountains might have been.

Mountains are really just parts of the continental crust that have been thrust upward as tectonic plates slowly crash together. This crust is very heavy, and there is much more to it than what you see on the surface; even the most spectacularly tall peaks, like Everest, are anchored in deep crust. Just as crust gets thrust up to form mountain peaks, it also gets pushed deep under the surface. Both Collins and Ziyi Zhu, a geochemist at Curtin University in Australia, compared mountains to icebergs, in the sense that only a bit pokes above the surface.

"We can argue or speculate that [the biggest mountain] was in a place where there were deep roots in the crust," Zhu told Live Science.

Geologists estimate that the Himalayas, for example, have a crustal column that goes down as far as 50 miles (80 kilometers). Compare that to Everest's elevation of about 29,000 feet (9,000 m), and the world's tallest mountain at most represents only 14% of the rock slab it's part of. That means there's a limit to how tall mountains can get before they collapse under their own weight.

The Himalayas began forming between 50 million and 40 million years ago from the collision between the Indian Eurasian continental plates. As far as mountains go, they are relatively young. And that's partly why they are still so tall. Tall, jagged mountains erode quickly over time, though the Himalayas are still growing as the two plates continue to collide.

Such continental collisions have happened countless times before, though. For example, the Transgondwanan Supermountain, an ancient range in what is now East Africa, began emerging some 650 million years ago when Africa and South America crashed into Arabia, India, Australia and Antarctica to form the supercontinent Gondwana.

Gondwana split up again, but evidence of these mountains in places like southern India, Sri Lanka, Tanzania and Kenya point to them being three times the length of the Himalayas. "If you walk around there now, you can see rocks that were at the bottom of this mountain belt," Collins told Live Science. On average, the crust is deeper than the crust of the Himalayas. "We think this is at least the scale of the Himalayas if not more," Collins said.

The crusts found in these areas also hint at how tall the mountains were, with sections that descend up to 18 miles (30 km) deep, despite half a billion years of erosion. "At some point, they were likely halfway down a crustal column that was 60 kilometers [37 miles deep]," Collins said. This means that the mountains above this base could have been as tall as Everest.

In the Himalayas, the tallest mountains sit around the rim of the Tibetan Plateau, which has the highest elevation on the planet, at about 3.7 miles (6 km) above sea level, on average. The Transgondwanan range may have had a similar plateau around southern Madagascar, meaning its rim would have been in eastern Madagascar, southern India and eastern Antarctica, Collins said.

Another candidate for the tallest mountains was the Grenville orogeny, which popped up with the formation of the supercontinent Rodinia starting about 1.3 billion years ago. Its remains can be seen around southeastern Ontario and northwestern Vermont. But less is known about this extinct range, Collins said, with some scholars arguing that it wouldn't have been as tall as the Transgondwanan range or the Himalayas.

Further back, some 2 billion years ago, the formation of the supercontinent Nuna (also called Columbia) also left evidence of truly giant mountains. Zhu's paper found evidence of this supermountain by examining the composition of zircon crystals left in the crust underneath this vanished range.

Look any further back, however, and the picture gets murkier. When Earth was hotter, mountains likely couldn't get as high because warmer crusts can support less weight, Collins said. Stable continental plates and rocks didn't start to form until about 4 billion years ago, and it took hundreds of millions of additional years for large continents to form.

For Zhu, the Nuna, Transgondwana and Himalaya ranges are currently the best candidates for holding the tallest mountains that ever existed. But it's likely impossible to pinpoint the highest specific peak, at least with current scientific methods. "It's an open question," Zhu said.

#5872. This Is What Halloween Candy Looked Like in the 1960s

We've lost some excellent Halloween candy since the '60s, yet somehow, we've kept the raisins.

Halloween in the 1960s was a little less elaborate than the theatrical holiday we know today. There was no popping into Target for a complete costume in a plastic bag. Costumes were often assembled from whatever was already in the house, and the treats waiting at the neighbors’ doors weren’t necessarily wrapped in plastic either. My mom was a teenager in the ’60s, and when I asked her what she remembered, she told me about going as a cowgirl one Halloween, likely outfitted with my grandpa’s bandana and her older brother’s hat and boots. A little ingenuity could get you through the evening, preferably with a good haul of candy to show for it.

The contents of kids’ Halloween bags at the time offer a fascinating glimpse of the era. Familiar chocolate bars shared space with forgotten confections, homemade sweets and the occasional box of raisins that probably disappointed children then just as much as it would now.

A chocolate bar called Chicken Dinner sounds like a joke, but Milwaukee’s Sperry Candy Company was entirely serious when it introduced the candy in 1923. Despite the name, it contained no poultry, just a chocolate-covered nut roll. The name borrowed from the old promise of a chicken in every pot, and Sperry took the bit well beyond the packaging.

The wrappers featured roasted chickens, and the company even sent enormous chicken-shaped promotional trucks onto the streets to advertise the candy. I can’t decide if seeing one drive past would have made me more or less inclined to try a Chicken Dinner bar, but I’d certainly have wanted a closer look.

Sadly, the chickens came home to roost in 1962, when Pearson’s acquired Sperry and discontinued the bar.

Butterfinger turned 40 years old in 1963, and its crisp, flaky peanut butter center was as distinctive then as it is now. The bar was among those advertised for Halloween that decade, and its appeal was easy to understand. Unlike the smooth fillings and chewy centers of most other chocolate bars, Butterfinger offered a delicate crunch that shattered into little peanut buttery flakes beneath its chocolate coating.

It’s a texture that still sets Butterfinger apart from nearly everything else in the candy aisle.

A Snickers bar in your Halloween bag in the ’60s was a particularly good find, especially in 1968, when the Fun Size version first appeared. Now, we almost take the handful of minis we grab from the bowl for granted!

The bar itself was familiar territory: milk chocolate, peanuts, caramel and nougat, just as it had been since its introduction in 1930. And while smaller candy bars certainly existed, finding a full-sized one would have been a welcome improvement over a handful of pennies or a stale piece of hard candy.

The Seven Up Bar is one of those discontinued candy bars that makes me question the judgment of everyone involved in its disappearance. The bar had nothing to do with the lemon-lime soda, although the two companies did eventually find themselves negotiating over the Seven Up name. Made by Pearson’s, the candy bar contained seven individually filled sections beneath a chocolate coating. Depending on the period, the fillings included flavors like caramel, coconut, fudge, buttercream, cherry cream, orange jelly and Brazil nut.

It was essentially an assorted box of chocolates in bar form, with none of the uncertainty of biting into a mystery piece. I can’t help thinking it was an excellent idea abandoned too soon.

If you found a 100 Grand in your Halloween bag in the ’60s, you’d have known it by a different name. Nestlé introduced the $100,000 Bar in 1964, filling it with chewy caramel and crisped rice beneath a milk chocolate coating. It was a rather ambitious name for a candy bar that originally cost a dime.

The original wrapper promised “A Fortune in Flavor,” and the candy quickly caught on. By the end of the decade, miniature versions had appeared too. The name eventually changed to 100 Grand, but the combination of gooey caramel and crunchy rice has stuck around. Apparently, it’s the one fortune that hasn’t suffered much from inflation.

Tootsie Pops were available, too, although the famous television commercial featuring the owl wouldn’t arrive until 1970.

The candy itself has changed remarkably little. It still occupies that curious territory between chocolate and caramel, requiring more chewing than its modest size would suggest. Whatever your feelings about candy corn, its unusually waxy texture and honeyed flavor have proved remarkably resistant to change. Set a handful of candy corn from the ’60s next to today’s version, and you’d have a difficult time telling them apart.

The chalky white sugar sticks came in miniature cigarette packs, sometimes designed to resemble actual tobacco brands. Kids could even pretend to smoke them, because nothing says childhood innocence quite like a pack-a-day habit.

The 1964 Surgeon General’s report would formally establish smoking as a major health hazard, helping change public attitudes toward cigarettes. But in the early ’60s, handing a child a tiny pack of candy smokes was still considered an adorable treat. It’s hard to imagine a better illustration of how much Halloween—and the world around it—has changed.

Bazooka offered something the chocolate bars couldn’t: reading material. Each piece of bright pink bubble gum came wrapped with a Bazooka Joe comic, a feature introduced in 1953 that gave kids something to enjoy long after the gum had lost its flavor.

The gum itself was famously firm, particularly when first unwrapped, but once you managed to soften it, there was the prospect of blowing an enormous pink bubble.

The caramel-coated popcorn and peanuts were only half the attraction; every box also contained a surprise. By the early 1960s, the company was promoting Cracker Jack for Halloween, and I suspect the possibility of finding a miniature toy inside the box was enough to make it a welcome addition to the evening’s haul.

The prizes weren’t particularly valuable, but that was hardly the point. Half the fun was digging through the popcorn to discover what you’d gotten. A candy apple was a rather extravagant addition to a Halloween haul in the 1960s. Instead of a few bites of factory-made chocolate, you got an entire apple encased in a glossy red shell of hardened sugar, often with a wooden stick for a handle. It was probably inconvenient to carry around while trick-or-treating, but I doubt many children objected to receiving something that looked as though it had come straight from a carnival.

Pixie Stix made their debut in 1959, just in time to give the children of the 1960s a remarkably efficient way to eat sugar. The colorful paper straws were filled with sweet-and-sour powdered candy, which you poured directly into your mouth. No chocolate, no caramel, no pretense of nutritional value. Just sugar with an easy delivery system.

The candy actually began as a powdered drink mix until someone noticed that children were skipping the water and eating the powder instead. Rather than discourage them, the company leaned into the idea. The kids had spoken, and they wanted their sugar straight.

Before individually wrapped candy became the default Halloween handout, plenty of neighbors made their own treats, and popcorn balls were a classic. Making them was a somewhat sticky undertaking: Freshly popped corn was coated in hot sugar syrup, then shaped into balls while the mixture was still pliable. Wrapped in waxed paper or cellophane, they were ready for the trick-or-treaters who came knocking.

And then there were raisins. In October 1962, the Chicago Daily Tribune recommended miniature boxes of Sun-Maid raisins for Halloween. The writer seemed confident that trick-or-treaters would be delighted, which suggests a rather optimistic understanding of what children hope to find in their Halloween bags.

Of course, the miniature Sun-Maid boxes are still with us, and so, I suspect, is the disappointment of discovering one at the bottom of a bag full of chocolate. Some traditions survive without the benefit of popular demand.

#5870. Why You Should Always Read the Label on Your Cleaning Spray

Until I started reviewing kitchen gear five years ago, I rarely read product manuals or user guides. I’m sure that’s due in large part to my ADHD, as my brain tends to move more quickly than step-by-step instructions allow. Plus, I like to figure things out on my own, thank you very much!

While user error can make for a funny punchline, it can also get expensive. For example, it might seem silly to read the manual for a nonstick frying pan or enameled cast-iron Dutch oven, but that’s where you’d learn that putting it in the dishwasher or using metal utensils can destroy or degrade the cooking surface.

In the case of cleaning products, the stakes can be even higher—especially in this era of, er, less restrictive food safety regulations.

Before you get to the instructions on the back of a cleaning spray bottle, you need to pay close attention to what’s on the front, because not all kitchen cleaning sprays (no, not even all all-purpose cleaning sprays) are created equally.

Though they might have some very minimal, naturally occurring disinfecting properties from ingredients like vinegar or essential oils, basic cleaning sprays like Mrs. Meyer’s and Aunt Fannie’s have one job—and that is to clean the surfaces in your kitchen. And by “clean,” I mean they contain surfactants, which help to dislodge grease and gunk so you can more easily wipe away a mess.

Mrs. Meyer’s Clean Day All-Purpose Cleaner Spray

Aunt Fannie’s All-Purpose Cleaning Vinegar Spray

But simply removing discernible debris won’t leave you with a germ-free countertop, which is why you also need a disinfectant to kill bacteria and viruses (not to be confused with a sanitizer, which only kills bacteria). And if you want to ensure your counters are so clean you could eat off of them, I recommend using both. If you spray a disinfecting solution on top of a spill, it’s not disinfecting the counter underneath the spill—it’s sanitizing the surface of whatever is stuck to your counter.

Here’s the real kicker, and the reason it’s important to read the instructions on the back of your disinfecting (or sanitizing) spray: In order for the product to kill the number of germs the label claims, it needs to sit on the surface in question for anywhere from about two to 10 minutes. This period is called the “dwell time” or “contact time,” and the product needs to remain visibly wet for however long it takes to be truly effective.

For example, Lysol's All-Purpose Cleaner (which is truly all-purpose when used properly), is a sanitizer at 10 seconds, but it only "kills 99.9% of viruses and bacteria" as the front of the label claims, if it’s left to sit for 10 minutes. The little asterisks after that claim lead you to the back label, which details the dwell time along with the common bacteria and viruses it kills.

“Use as directed” shows up four times in the online product description for Clorox’s Multi-Surface Disinfecting Mist, which also claims to kill 99.9% of viruses and bacteria. It even has a specific instruction for kitchens (to rinse off of any food-prep surfaces after a 10-minute dwell time).

The moral of the story? Use your cleaning products as directed, which unfortunately does require reading the fine print on the label. While you’re at it, you might want to consider replacing your microfiber cleaning cloths after a 2010 study found that microfiber becomes less effective at removing contaminants from surfaces the more it gets used.

Artist depiction of the heavy set facial features of a Denisovan.Credit: Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology/Chinese Academy of Sciences

A low, sloping forehead, a massive brow ridge that curves backwards, rectangle-like eye sockets, flat cheeks and a moderately elevated bridge of the nose. Taken together, these features are the hallmarks of the ancient humans called the Denisovans, say researchers who studied a trove of fossilized skull fragments, teeth and jaws found in northern China.

“We are finally starting to put a face to the Denisovan,” says Sally Wasef, a palaeogeneticist at the Queensland University of Technology in Brisbane, Australia.

Denisovans were first described in 2010, after researchers sequenced genetic material from a mysterious finger bone discovered in a cave in Siberia and found that it did not resemble that of Homo sapiens or Neanderthals (Homo neanderthalensis). Ancient proteins have since been used to identify remains from other parts of Asia as Denisovan, including the first near-complete Denisovan skull found near Harbin, which helped to put a face on the ancient humans.

Researchers report in Nature1 today that they have an even better idea of what Denisovans looked like after analysing the physical features of remains that belong to a collection of 20 fossils found in Xujiayao.

The Xujiayao remains are at least 130,000 years old and were found in the 1970s, alongside animal remains and tools. At the time, it was unclear what type of human these fossils belonged to, and they were classified as a species called Homo juluensis. By analysing ancient proteins from some of the fossils, Qiaomei Fu, a geneticist at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing, and her team identified the remains as Denisovan.

Wasef says that ancient-protein analysis is a valuable tool to complement DNA studies, particularly because proteins can survive longer than can DNA without fully degrading. Although DNA provides more-detailed information about an individual, protein analysis can still yield useful information about who an individual is related to.

Fabrice Demeter, a palaeoanthropologist at the University of Copenhagen, says that the findings add to growing evidence that Denisovans were a widespread and potentially diverse group of humans. “The study represents a major step forward” in our understanding of what these humans could have looked like and where they lived.

Fu’s team analysed small fragments of proteins, called peptides, from two Xujiayao bones and compared them with peptides found in humans and Neanderthals. They found a single amino-acid change that was present in multiple peptides from the Xujiayao bones and other East Asian fossils and is a known genetic marker for identifying Denisovans. The amino-acid change was not seen in human or Neanderthal samples.

You may not be able to control where your digital data ends up. But you know exactly how you can securely dispose of your paper records. The trick is deciding which shredder will best satisfy your serious or casual shredding needs, which usually comes down to a handful of features.

Once you start your shredding chore, you’d rather not be interrupted by problems with your shredder. Runtime tells you how long you can keep feeding paper into a shredder before its motor overheats and shuts down, forcing you to wait 30 to 45 minutes, depending on the model, for the machine to catch its mechanical breath.

A small personal shredder may run for only five or 10 minutes, which is plenty if you dispatch only a few documents at a time. If you’re regularly confronted by larger piles to pulverize, look for a runtime of 30 minutes or more.

Once you choose a shredder with longer runtimes, don’t obsess over the advertised cooldown time. You may fill the bin before the shredder overheats and, by the time you’ve disposed of all that confetti, the motor already has had a chance to cool before you return with an empty bin.

A shredder is still largely a mechanical device, a motor driving a set of cutters, augmented in many models by sensors and electronic controls. And as with any mechanical object, failure is often less a question of if than when. Long-term owner shredder reviews are especially useful for spotting recurring problems with motors, sensors, switches, and other parts that may not misbehave during short-term testing.

Unfortunately, getting help directly from some shredder brands can be harder than clearing a paper jam. During our ongoing shredder research, we’ve encountered multiple manufacturers failing to respond to repeated emails and phone calls. Some brands don’t even provide a U.S. customer-service number.

That doesn’t automatically make shredders from these contact-resistant brands a bad buy. Purchasing from an established store or online retailer with a clearly stated return or replacement policy gives you a more reliable resource to turn to if your new shredder turns out to be a lemon. Before buying—particularly from an unfamiliar brand—check both the manufacturer’s warranty and especially the retailer’s return/replace policy.

Unlike the shredders you may remember from old movies, most modern machines no longer turn documents into long linguini-like strips. Cross-cut shredders slice paper both lengthwise and crosswise into small rectangular scraps. Micro-cut models slice and dice those scraps into even tinier bits, leaving hundreds or even thousands of petite particles.

For most home and home-office use, either cutting method provides plenty of protection. Most shredders these days offer micro-cut machines with either P-4 security, which makes reconstructing a document the most puzzling jigsaw puzzle ever, or P-5 security, whose even more diminutive document destruction methods are favored by MDs, CPAs, JDs, or by other professionals with initials in their titles.

The larger and more powerful your shredder, the more sheets it can generally chew through at one time. Just don’t treat the number printed on the box as a dare.

Manufacturers’ rated sheet capacities are best-case numbers, usually based on neatly aligned standard-weight bond paper. Thicker stock, folds, staples, uneven piles, and stuffed envelopes can quickly turn a “12-sheet” machine into something more comfortable with eight or nine. Feeding a few sheets below the rated maximum is one of the easiest ways to avoid jams and unnecessary strain.

Manual shredders require you to stand there feeding paper in batches. Auto-feed machines let you load a stack, walk away, and get something else done while the shredder eats.

Here again, don’t take the rated auto-feed sheet capacity too literally. An “80-sheet” or “200-sheet” auto feeder may accommodate that many pristine sheets under ideal conditions, but real paperwork is rarely pristine. Wrinkles, folds, different paper weights, staples, and uneven stacks can reduce how much you can reliably leave it to swallow unattended. Plus, the bin on a high-capacity auto-feed model may fill before the maximum number of sheets are fed into it.

Auto-feed mechanisms also add more rollers, sensors, and jam-clearing compartments. And more moving mechanical parts create more places for something to eventually go wrong.

However, if you routinely accumulate large piles, the time a highly rated auto-feed shredder saves can easily outweigh the added complexity and potential mechanical issues.

A bigger bin means fewer trips to empty it, but gallon capacity tells only part of the story. Pull-out drawers are lighter and easier to clean and empty. Bins that require lifting the often heavy shredder mechanism off the top of a wastepaper basket can potentially scatter loose particles still in the cutter mechanism.

Also pay attention to what happens when you pull that drawer out. Paper confetti has an impressive ability to cling to plastic, pile up behind a bin, and otherwise escape the container designed to hold it. An easy-to-remove bin that keeps most of its shredded paper actually in the bin will save you emptying and cleanup aggravation, especially if you don’t wait until the bin is filled to its brim.

Shredders may look sturdy and impervious, but most are actually quite finicky eaters. Nearly every shredder will eventually jam if you feed it too much at one time or feed it the wrong type, shape, or mix of paper. More advanced models with more advanced anti-jam systems sense an overload before one occurs, or either automatically reverse or allow you to manually reverse to relieve the jam before you need to perform paper-removal surgery from the cutters with tweezers or needle-nose pliers.

The cheapest jam protection, however, is restraint. If a machine claims 12 sheets, regularly feeding it eight or nine instead will lower your odds of spending quality time violently tugging mangled paper back out of its teeth.

Most current shredders can swallow staples and paper clips, and many can also chew up plastic credit cards. CDs and other thicker media such as metal cards are less universally welcome.

Check the manufacturer’s instructions before you buy for what non-paper materials a shredder can successfully chew and before feeding anything other than paper into its cutters. A shredder capable of mincing a stack of tax records can object like a microwave forced to cook metal—something it wasn’t designed to eat.

Probably less than you think. For most people who dispatch a few bills, receipts, financial statements, or junk-mail offers every few days, well under $100 can buy a perfectly capable shredder.

Spending more generally buys stamina, convenience, and durability: longer runtimes, larger bins, higher sheet capacities, auto-feed, better jam protection, sometimes finer-cut security, and years if not decades of service. Those features become worth paying for as the amount and frequency of your shredding increase.

As with nearly all things, don’t be penny wise and pound foolish. You will find you will shred more than you anticipated—it’s just human nature to use a new device more than you though you would. Overall, shredders costing several hundreds of dollars make the most sense for people who regularly devour large stacks or have a specialized need. Don’t decide what to spend simply by asking how private your paperwork is; ask how much of it you need to turn into confetti and how often both now and in the future.

The most important shredder “spec” may be how little time it forces you to spend with it. Sheet capacity, runtime, security level, and bin capacity are the headline numbers I’d look at first. Runtime is particularly useful because it tells you something about how much work the machine can comfortably handle before demanding a rest.

But some of the things that most determine whether you’ll love or hate a shredder aren’t specs at all. Nearly every decent modern shredder can reduce ordinary documents beyond casual recognition. The bigger differences are how often it jams, how easily those jams clear, how cleanly the bin empties, and how much confetti winds up everywhere except inside it.

Check owner reviews for recurring complaints about jams, overheating, finicky sensors, messy bins, and other annoyances. AI agents on online sites can summarize customer reviews to answer many of these before-you-buy questions.

With shredders, old doesn’t necessarily mean obsolete. Sometimes it means proven.Unlike most consumer electronics, shredders don’t become outdated because they lack this year’s faster processor, new operating system, wireless standard, or other bleeding-edge technology. An older model that’s still attracting buyers and good reviews may actually offer something a brand-new machine can’t: years of accumulated evidence about how well it holds up.

Before buying an older model, check whether the manufacturer has introduced a newer or revised version. When shopping online, search the manufacturer’s current lineup or, if the retailer provides an AI or shopping assistant, ask whether there’s a newer version of the model you’re considering. Then compare old to new rather than automatically assuming new means better.

The best shredder maintenance is self-restraint. Don’t continually stuff the cutters to their rated maximum, allow the bin overflow, or feed the machine materials it wasn’t designed to digest.

Lubrication requirements vary. Some manufacturers recommend shredder oil applied at regular intervals, others suggest pre-lubricated oil sheets, and some models claim to require little or no lubrication at all. Follow the instructions for your particular machine rather than assuming every shredder requires the same type of mechanical TLC.

Beyond that, maintenance should be pretty painless: empty the bin before the paper snow reaches the cutters, occasionally clean dusty sensors if the manufacturer recommends it, and clear jams rather than repeatedly hammering the reverse button and hoping for divine intervention.

Despite the name, most modern “shredders” no longer shred paper into long strips. While cross cut and micro cut are clues for how small the ultimate shred pieces will be, P-ratings, part of the DIN 66399 security standard, put an actual number on “small.”

P-ratings run from P-1 through P-7; the higher the number, the smaller the pieces produced. For ordinary household paperwork, P-3 is already plenty secure. A cross-cut P-3 machine reduces a document to hundreds of pieces, which is more than enough discouragement for anyone contemplating reconstructing your bills, junk mail, receipts, or other everyday paperwork. After all, a document pieced back together like a taped mosaic isn’t likely to fool many people.

P-4 is the sweet spot for buyers who want their paperwork diced considerably finer without spending much more, and P-4 is common among consumer micro-cut shredders. P-5 turns documents into even tinier bits and makes sense for especially sensitive financial, legal, medical, or professional paperwork. P-6 and P-7 go further still, into security levels intended for documents whose contents matter considerably more than last month’s Visa bill.

For most of us, then, P-3 is plenty, P-4 buys extra peace of mind, and P-5 is for paperwork you’d really rather nobody put back together.

A new 4,300-mile (7,000-kilometer) undersea fiber cable will shuttle data between the U.S. and France at breakneck speeds of up to 1 million gigabits per second (Gbps) — enough to move around 125 terabytes of data every second, or roughly 25,000 HD movies, each 5 gigabytes large. The cable, called Petal, will be funded and operated by Facebook and Instagram parent company Meta.

When it comes online in 2029, the ocean-spanning cable will be the first to deliver petabit capacity — double the capacity of existing subsea cables of this length — across transoceanic distances, Meta representatives said in a statement. That's enough to allow 75% of the world's population to stream music at once.

Petal will also be the first commercial subsea cable system to use multi-core fiber technology, according to the statement. Whereas conventional fiber strands typically contain a single core, each of Petal's fiber strands will contain two cores that carry optical signals in opposite directions, allowing a single strand to provide the capacity that would conventionally require a pair of single-core fibers.

Each fiber strand is roughly as thick as a human hair, measuring just 125 micrometers in diameter. Petal will contain 24 pairs of dual-core fibers, giving it 96 individual fiber cores in total and enabling it to carry as much data as 48 conventional fiber pairs, Meta engineers said.

Japanese technology company NEC will be responsible for the overall design and construction of Petal, while Sumitomo Electric will provide the fiber itself.

"Achieving petabit-per-second capacity across a transoceanic submarine system represents a major technological milestone in the history of global telecommunications," Eduardo Mateo, chief strategy officer of the Submarine Networks Division at NEC, said in the statement.

Subsea cables are among the most critical layers of internet infrastructure, carrying around 99% of the world's internet traffic, according to the International Telecommunication Union, the United Nations ​agency for digital technologies.

Installing thousands of miles of fragile glass cable on the ocean floor is an engineering feat in itself. Equally challenging is ensuring that these cables can carry the tiny pulses of infrared light that transport fiber-optic data across such huge distances without impacting performance.

This is typically achieved through repeaters — amplifiers installed at regular intervals along the cable that use lasers to boost the optical signals.

A 4,300-mile subsea cable typically needs about 100 of these repeaters to amplify the signals along its length, Meta representatives said.

Petal will incorporate something called "fan-in/fan-out" technology. As each two-core fiber strand enters a repeater, an optical interface routes the signal from each core into a separate single-core fiber strand so that each can be amplified independently. The amplified signals are then routed back into the two cores of the dual-core fiber before continuing along the cable.

Combined with its dual-core fiber, this feat of signal engineering will enable Petal to double its data-carrying capacity without requiring a proportional increase in power, Meta representatives said.

Petal is one of many subsea cable investments by Meta in recent years, including Marea in 2018, Amitie in 2023 and Anjana in 2025. Marea, the company's first transatlantic fiber instalment, supports 192 Tbps, meaning Petal will offer more than five times that capacity.

#5863. Is Pumpkin Good for You?

Pumpkin is one of our favorite fall flavors, but its nutritional benefits are worth celebrating year-round. Packed with fiber and antioxidants, this colorful squash deserves a place on your plate beyond pie.

Pumpkin isn’t just for pie! Sure, its subtly sweet flavor makes it a natural fit for pumpkin desserts, but the winter squash can be used for so much more. Whether I’m stirring it into a jar of pumpkin chia pudding or blending it into a PSL-inspired smoothie, pumpkin is an easy, tasty way to work more nutrients into my day.

As a dietitian, I reach for this fall favorite for its flavor, beautiful color and impressive health benefits. This low-calorie squash is packed with beta-carotene, which your body converts to vitamin A to support vision and your immune system. It also provides fiber to help make meals feel more satisfying and keep digestion moving, plus antioxidants that help protect your cells from damage.

I incorporate it into everything from warming soups and oatmeal to muffins and smoothies. And thanks to the convenience of canned pumpkin, it’s a pantry staple I can keep around long after Halloween to work more veggies into everyday recipes.

Pumpkin is loaded with vitamin A, with 1 cup of canned pumpkin providing about 200% of an adult’s daily need. This essential nutrient helps you see clearly, especially in low light, and keeps the surface of your eyes healthy. Much of pumpkin’s vitamin A comes from beta-carotene, the pigment that creates that bright orange color. Pumpkin also contains lutein and zeaxanthin, two antioxidants that help protect the cells in your eyes from damage.

Cooking with pumpkin can help get more fiber into your day. High-fiber foods feed the beneficial bacteria in your gut, keep your digestive system moving and support regular bowel movements. Fiber also helps you feel fuller for longer, which can make it easier to manage hunger between meals—whether you’re working toward a weight goal or simply trying to eat more satisfying meals and snacks.

The vitamin A in pumpkin does more than keep your vision sharp! It also helps keep your skin and other tissues healthy, making it harder for germs to get into your body. Pumpkin also contains vitamin C and vitamin E, which play important roles in keeping your immune system working properly. Eating more pumpkin won’t keep every sniffle away, but it’s a tasty way to get more of these important nutrients.

Pumpkin is rich in carotenoids like beta-carotene. These antioxidants help protect your cells from the kind of damage that can contribute to inflammation and chronic disease over time. That’s one of the many reasons it’s worth eating a rainbow of fruits and veggies, as each color brings its own mix of protective plant compounds to the table.

Pumpkin has a few things going for it when it comes to heart health. It provides potassium, a mineral that helps keep blood pressure in a healthy range, along with fiber that can help support healthy cholesterol levels. Pumpkin is also naturally low in saturated fat, making it an easy choice for heart-healthy meals.

When it comes to pumpkin’s health benefits, it doesn’t matter if you eat fresh or canned pumpkin. It really comes down to time and convenience. I lean on fresh pumpkin when I’m making recipes like salad, roasted veggies or soup, where it contributes a vibrant color that really pops. It might be tempting to use one of the large orange pumpkins used for carving, but a sugar pumpkin or pie pumpkin is actually the best pumpkin for soups. These varieties have sweeter, smoother flesh and less water than carving pumpkins.

Canned pumpkin is already cooked and pureed, so it’s a convenient shortcut when you’re short on time or want to enjoy pumpkin outside of fall. It’s especially handy when a recipe calls for just a little puree, since you can measure out what you need instead of cooking a whole pumpkin. Just be sure to grab 100% canned pumpkin, not pumpkin pie filling, which has added sugar and spices.

If you’re starting with a whole pumpkin, tackle it the same way you’d cook any other winter squash. Cut the pumpkin in half, scoop out the seeds and stringy bits with a spoon, then roast the halves cut-side down at 400°F for about 45 minutes, or until the flesh is fork-tender. Once it’s cool enough to handle, scoop out the flesh and cube, mash or puree it.

Once you have your pumpkin puree, there are plenty of ways to put it to work! Use it to make pumpkin overnight oats, stir it into cozy pumpkin soups or turn it into something a little unexpected like pumpkin hummus. #5861. 11 Types of Vinegar: A Guide to Cooking With Them and More

There are an awful lot of vinegars in the world—which ones you cook with depends entirely on your favorite flavors and what you’re making. Maybe you favor red wine vinegar for the punchy tang it brings to salad dressing. Or you can’t imagine a bowl of white rice without the gentle acidity of seasoned rice vinegar running through it. Perhaps you fall asleep at night dreaming of bowls of vanilla ice cream covered in ripe summer strawberries dripping with balsamic vinegar.

Whether you’re the kind of cook who has a favorite vinegar for every occasion or simply grabs whichever bottle is closest to the stove, it pays to know what you’re working with. You can often substitute one vinegar for another, but it's worth being strategic. “Each vinegar contributes its own flavor and character rather than just adding acidity,” says Sathish Kumar, the head chef of Indienne in New York and Chicago.

So which bottles deserve a spot in your pantry? We’re breaking down 11 types of vinegar, what to use them for, and substitutions that work in a pinch.

Vinegar starts with alcohol, which bacteria convert into acetic acid. The starting ingredient—wine, apples, rice, malted barley, or sugarcane, for example—helps determine the flavor, as do the fermentation and aging processes. That’s how you get everything from the crisp, fruity tang of apple cider vinegar to the rich, savory complexity of Chinese black vinegar.

There are countless varieties of vinegar; these are just the ones we reach for most often.

Apple cider vinegar starts with—you guessed it—apples. The fruit is fermented first into hard cider and then into vinegar. The result is bright, fruity, and pleasantly tart with enough character to stand up to bold ingredients. It’s also the subject of some debatable health claims.

We like it in salad dressings, quick-pickled onions, and anything that could use a little fruitiness. It’s a natural partner for pork and chicken, particularly in apple cider braises, and its acidity cuts through the richness of cheeseburger fried rice or braised collard greens. And not for nothing, it makes a heck of a pie.

White wine vinegar is the closest everyday substitute, though Champagne vinegar or unseasoned rice vinegar can also work when you want a lighter touch.

Clear, inexpensive, and unapologetically sharp, distilled white vinegar is the pantry's most straightforward source of acidity. Made by fermenting distilled alcohol, often derived from grains, it has a relatively neutral flavor that lets other ingredients shine.

Tom Carlin, the chef of Burl in Evanston, Illinois, likes adding a dash to braises at the end of cooking. It brightens them “without changing the flavor at all,” he says. That same clean tang makes distilled white vinegar a natural choice for classic dill pickles. It’s also a great base for homemade flavored vinegars, especially those infused with chiles, herbs, or spices.

In many cooked dishes and dressings, you can substitute rice, apple cider, or white wine vinegar. Each will bring its own flavor to the party, so taste as you go.

Red wine vinegar is bold, fruity, and assertive. You can’t dress a classic Greek salad or splash oysters with mignonette without it. Like white wine vinegar, it begins with wine that undergoes a second fermentation, but the red wine base gives it a more robust fruit flavor.

Red wine vinegar brings welcome acidity to BA’s Best Gazpacho, coq au vin, a pot of hearty lentils, and rich meat marinades. If you’re making a vinaigrette for a salad loaded with tomatoes, olives, or cheese, this is a good bottle to reach for.

You can use white wine or apple cider vinegar in its place, but Chicago-based Sifr culinary director Sahil Sethi favors sherry vinegar. He says its nutty, aged character gives it a depth that mirrors red wine vinegar’s body and acidity.

Fermented from white wine, white wine vinegar has a lighter, more delicate flavor than red wine vinegar. Its gently fruity acidity makes it particularly useful when you want to brighten a dish without overpowering it.

Reach for it to use in salad dressings, seafood marinades, braised chicken, brothy steamed mussels, or a buttery pan sauce like beurre blanc. Conor Dalton, head chef at Smyth in Chicago, calls white wine vinegar “the most versatile” option and likes infusing it with herbs, such as lemon verbena, to sharpen creamy sauces for shrimp and balance richer preparations.

Champagne vinegar is an especially good substitute. Sherry, apple cider, and unseasoned rice vinegars can also stand in, depending on the dish.

Light, fruity, and delicately tangy, Champagne vinegar is typically made from wines produced using the same grape varieties as Champagne, such as Chardonnay and Pinot Noir. That doesn’t mean it’s necessarily made from Champagne wine, nor does it enjoy the same strict regional protections. Its subtle fruit and floral notes give it a gentler flavor than many white wine vinegars, though it isn’t necessarily less acidic. Some producers age Champagne vinegar in barrels to develop a rounder, more complex flavor. It’s a particularly good choice when a more assertive vinegar might steal the show.

Sethi calls Champagne vinegar his favorite for salad dressing thanks to its subtle fruitiness. Try it in a vinaigrette for tender greens, drizzled over seafood, or in a dressing for spring vegetables. It can bring a little lift to richer dishes, too, without contributing much competing flavor.

White wine vinegar, though slightly more assertive, is the most straightforward substitute. Unseasoned rice vinegar also works when a mild flavor is the primary goal.

If red wine vinegar is bright and brash, sherry vinegar is its smoother, more nuanced cousin. Made from sherry wine and aged in wood barrels, this amber-hued vinegar develops a rounded acidity with nutty, sometimes caramel-like notes.

That complexity makes sherry vinegar especially good at finishing sauces, dressing roasted vegetables, and brightening stews. Try it in a classic leeks vinaigrette, braised chicken escabèche, or a glaze for rib eye. Dalton also likes using it to deglaze a pan or brighten a sauce at the end of cooking.

Vinagre de Jerez comes from the area around Jerez, Spain, and must age in wood barrels for at least six months. It’s worth keeping a bottle around if you like a vinegar with a little more depth, but red or white wine vinegar works in many of the same applications. Malt vinegar has a more robust flavor, but it can contribute some of the same woody, caramel undertones.

Dark, rich, and sweet-tart, balsamic vinegar can be a finishing touch or an essential ingredient, depending on the bottle. Traditional balsamic vinegar, made in Modena or Reggio Emilia, Italy, starts with cooked grape must (freshly crushed grape juice) and ages in wood barrels for at least 12 years. The result is intensely concentrated, syrupy, and complex.

The balsamic vinegar you’re more likely to find at the supermarket is different. Commonly made by combining grape must with wine vinegar, it’s generally thinner, tangier, and far less expensive than traditional balsamic. That’s not a bad thing: It’s the kind we reach for when making balsamic dressing, roasting brussels sprouts with honey and garlic, or adding sweet-tart depth to Balsamic Mushroom and Sausage Pasta.

For a thicker finishing sauce, you can also simmer everyday balsamic into a balsamic reduction, concentrating its sweetness and acidity. Save especially good aged balsamic for drizzling over Parmesan, ripe tomatoes, or even strawberries and vanilla ice cream.

Made with grape must processed to minimize browning, white balsamic is paler, lighter, and fruitier than traditional dark balsamic. Use it to brighten a melon salad, dress roasted peppers, bring a little sweetness to a pan sauce without turning everything brown, or to make a sweet-and-tangy custard for a fruit tart.

Both varieties of balsamic are tricky to substitute because their sweetness is as important as their acidity. For dressings or cooked sauces, try sherry or apple cider vinegar with a little honey or brown sugar. It won’t have the same depth, but it’ll get you closer to the sweet-tart balance.

Mild, gently sweet, and endlessly versatile, rice vinegar is a staple in Japanese, Korean, Chinese, and Vietnamese cooking among other cuisines. It’s made by fermenting rice into alcohol and then vinegar, though its flavor and production vary by region.

At the grocery store, you’ll usually encounter two kinds: seasoned and unseasoned.

“Unseasoned rice vinegar has a clean, mild acidity with a subtle sweetness,” says Jae Seong Ha, head chef at Jeong Yuk Jeom restaurant in New York. That makes it a natural fit for Korean dressings, marinades, and pickled vegetables. We also like it in this Crispy Rice Salad with Spicy Tahini Dressing, tomato-basil salad, Salt and Pepper Fish, and miso-caramel glazed salmon.

Seasoned rice vinegar contains added sugar and salt, making it especially convenient for sushi rice and certain dressings.

Wondering about rice wine vinegar? “The terms rice vinegar and rice wine vinegar are used interchangeably,” Ha says. “When shopping, I pay more attention to the ingredient list and whether the vinegar is seasoned or unseasoned.”

To substitute unseasoned rice vinegar, try Champagne or white wine vinegar. If you need seasoned rice vinegar, start with unseasoned and add a little sugar and salt to taste. You can do the same with another mild vinegar in a pinch.

Dark, earthy, and deeply savory, black vinegar brings considerably more than acidity to a dish. There are several styles, including Chinkiang vinegar from China, but they’re generally made from fermented grains, such as rice, wheat, or sorghum.

“It has a little sweetness, a malty character, and more fermentation flavor” than milder rice vinegar, says Cheng Lin, chef-owner of Shota Omakase in Brooklyn. He likes it with dumplings, noodles, “and other dishes where you want the vinegar to have a stronger presence.”

Try black vinegar in dipping sauces for scallion pancakes, splashed into a cabbage stir-fry or Black Vinegar Chicken With Radishes, or whisked into a punchy dressing. A little goes a long way.

There’s no substitute for black vinegar’s complex, distinctive flavor. If you’re in a pinch, Lin suggests combining balsamic and unseasoned rice vinegar. “It won’t be exactly the same, but it gives you some of that darker, sweeter, aged character.”

If you’ve eaten fish and chips, you’ve probably tasted malt vinegar. Made from malted barley, it can range from amber to dark brown, and it has a sweet, nutty tang with a toasted flavor.

It’s hard to beat on french fries, but we’d happily splash it over roasted or steamed potatoes, or stir it into mayonnaise for dipping sweet potato wedges too. Malt vinegar can also lend depth to Sweet-and-Sour Tomato Chutney and other sauces. Reduce it with chiles and molasses for a sticky glaze for roast chicken or use it in a tangy lamb chop marinade.

For fried foods, a squeeze of lemon will provide a similar bright finish, though not malt vinegar’s toasty flavor. Apple cider or sherry vinegar is a reasonable stand-in for sauces and glazes.

Made from fermented sugarcane juice, cane vinegar is a staple in many Filipino kitchens—and important in many other cuisines, too, but the category is broad. Sukang maasim is an all-purpose white (clear) cane vinegar, while sukang Iloko is a darker variety hailing from the country’s north, often made from clay-aged sugarcane wine (basi).

Its flavor varies by producer and region, but it generally offers a clean, sometimes subtly sweet acidity. It’s a key ingredient in Philippine adobo, often balanced with soy sauce and aromatics like black pepper and bay leaf. Kumar also points to cane vinegar’s importance in Northern Indian pickling traditions, including sirka pyaaz, tangy onions commonly served alongside kebabs. It also works well in dipping sauces or infused with chiles for a spicy, tangy condiment.

If you can’t find cane vinegar, try unseasoned rice vinegar or apple cider vinegar, depending on the dish. Neither is an exact match, but both can provide the acidity you need. Coconut vinegar is another popular option in regions that favor cane vinegar, and can also work well in similar applications.

The world of vinegars is vast. Other varieties include honey vinegar, made from mead and vaguely similar to white balsamic; akazu, a beautifully layered red vinegar that comes from aged sake lees; and roasty-toasty beer vinegar, a good swap for malt vinegar.

Coconut vinegar, made from fermented coconut sap, is another versatile option, especially in Filipino and South Asian cooking. Kumar says its flavor is “distinctive and slightly funky” and that it works well in dipping sauces, marinades, and rich, savory dishes.

Ume-su (Japanese plum vinegar) isn’t technically vinegar at all. This intensely tart, salty seasoning is the tangy brine left over from making umeboshi (salt-preserved plums). It’s terrific in salad dressings or splashed over cooked vegetables.

Unseasoned rice vinegar and Champagne vinegar are good choices when you want acidity without an overpowering flavor. White wine vinegar is another versatile option. Distilled white vinegar has a neutral flavor, but a sharpness that can make it aggressive in some dishes.

Can you substitute one type of vinegar for another?

Usually, yes. Red and white wine vinegars can often stand in for each other, though red wine vinegar has a bolder flavor. Apple cider vinegar works well in place of white wine vinegar, while unseasoned rice vinegar is a good choice when you want something milder. Balsamic and black vinegars have more distinctive flavors and are harder to replace. For home canning and shelf-stable pickling, follow a tested recipe and use vinegar with the specified acidity rather than making substitutions based on taste alone.

Which vinegar is best for salad dressing?

You know that old saying: The limit does not exist. And a simple vinaigrette is a great way to taste the nuances of a new-to-you vinegar.

That said, red wine, white wine, Champagne, and sherry vinegars are all commonly used in Western-style salad dressings. Red wine vinegar works particularly well with bold ingredients like tomatoes and olives, while Champagne and white wine vinegars complement delicate greens. Reach for sherry vinegar when you want something a little richer and nuttier.

Most commercially produced vinegars don’t need refrigeration. “Vinegar is actually very easy to take care of,” Lin says. He recommends storing bottles tightly closed in a cool, dark cabinet away from sunlight and heat. Some vinegars may become cloudy or develop harmless sediment over time, but that doesn’t necessarily mean they’ve spoiled. Homemade infused vinegars may require refrigeration.

The answer depends on what you cook. One way to cover your bases is with something mild and versatile, like unseasoned rice vinegar; something fruity, like red wine or apple cider vinegar; and something distinctive, like sherry or balsamic.

But if chicken adobo is in heavy rotation, you’re going to want a steady supply of cane vinegar. Love fish and chips? Don’t skip the malt. Whatever your pleasure, there’s no wrong way to take an acid trip.

Recent research challenges a long-standing idea about how the brain develops: Rather than arising from a single source, the brain may emerge from two distinct populations of cells, the study finds.

"The brain appears as one organ," said study co-author Kyle Loh, an associate professor of developmental biology at Stanford University. "However, we discovered that the front part of the brain and the back part of the brain come from different sources, which are pushed together to create the appearance of a single organ," Loh told Live Science in an email.

The findings, published in September in the journal Nature Neuroscience, not only challenge established models of how the brain forms but may also explain why researchers have struggled to grow certain types of brain cells in the laboratory. The scientists say the work could offer new ways to investigate diseases that affect the brainstem, the "stalk" that connects the brain to the spinal cord.

The human brain is divided into three main regions — called the forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain — that perform different but interconnected functions.

The forebrain, which includes the cerebral hemispheres, is involved in higher-level cognitive processes, such as language, planning and abstract thinking. The midbrain, the uppermost part of the brainstem, connects the forebrain and the hindbrain and acts as a key relay center for sensory information and movement control. The hindbrain — which includes the bottom of the brainstem, along with other structures — also helps to coordinate movement, as well as regulate essential functions such as breathing, sleep and heart rate.

Historically, the hindbrain has been difficult to study. One reason is that scientists have faced persistent challenges in generating functional hindbrain neurons in laboratory conditions.

To help understand why, Loh and his team looked at the early stages of embryonic development in lab mice. They focused on gastrulation, the point when cells begin to organize into structures that will later form different organs. The researchers identified two distinct populations of immature cells that would go on to give rise to nervous system tissue, including the brain.

One cell population expressed a gene called Otx2, which helps to direct the early development of the forebrain and midbrain. The second group of cells expressed the gene Gbx2, which is involved in the development of the midbrain and hindbrain.

The functions of these genes were already known, but their distinct expression in these two groups of cells was revealed by the new research. The cell populations remained distinct as they developed into full-fledged nervous tissue, rather than overlapping.

The team also examined chromatin, the molecular structure that packages DNA tightly inside the nucleus and helps regulate which genes a cell can use. The two cell populations show different chromatin configurations, the team found, which further supports the idea that they follow separate developmental pathways.

That may help to explain why scientists' attempts to grow hindbrain neurons have been largely unsuccessful.

"Previous attempts to make hindbrain neurons likely tried to coax forebrain and midbrain progenitors into hindbrain cells, which our study shows is not possible," study co-author Rayyan Jokhai, a graduate student in Loh's lab, said in a statement.

Using the information uncovered in their experiments, the researchers successfully guided human stem cells to transform into functional hindbrain motor neurons, which are involved in controlling movement. Specifically, these cells produced proteins characteristic of the hard-to-grow hindbrain regions that help control muscles of the face and those involved in swallowing.

In several neurological diseases, including spinal muscular atrophy and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, motor neurons deteriorate and affected patients can lose their ability to swallow, speak and breathe independently.

"We cannot sample living motor neurons from a patient's hindbrain to watch how these diseases attack these neurons," Loh said. "Growing these hindbrain motor neurons in a dish gives us a way to investigate these diseases." Looking forward, the team aims to use their findings to study these diseases and pinpoint new treatments.

The new findings could also reveal something fundamental about the evolution of vertebrate animals.

The study authors investigated whether the two developmental pathways were unique to humans or reflected a much older evolutionary arrangement. They turned to previously published data on other species — including macaques (Macaca), zebrafish (Danio rerio) and chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus) — and again found two distinct cell populations expressing the two key genes.

And even in acorn worms — a marine invertebrate whose evolution diverged from vertebrates about 550 million years ago — they again found these two distinct groups of cells.

"The Stanford discovery establishes that the deep ancestry of the vertebrate brain is fundamentally modular," said Anna Di Cosmo, a professor of biology at the University of Naples Federico II in Italy who wasn't involved with the study. "The developmental separation between the anterior lineage (forebrain/midbrain) and the posterior lineage (hindbrain) is traceably ancient," Cosmo told Live Science in an email.

Loh's team speculates that these developmental trajectories seen in the womb may suggest that the two cell populations once belonged to two separate nervous systems. "Later in evolution, these two nervous systems may have been pushed together to give rise to the appearance of one integrated brain," Loh said.

However, the study doesn't reveal exactly when the two pathways arose or establish how they integrated. "It is important to emphasize that this is only an idea, and it might change as scientists learn more," Loh said.

The tuxedo in its earliest form was introduced in the mid 1800s by Edward VIII, then the Prince of Wales, who was looking for a casual alternative to the tailcoat. As it happens, our dear friend Ed had the benefit of palling around with the master tailors at Savile Row institution Henry Poole & Co.—and when he tasked them with dreaming up a newfangled “dinner jacket” to help him unwind in style they knew exactly what he needed.

The tuxedo as we know it today was only popularized stateside decades later, thanks to the efforts of, as menswear apocrypha has it, the well-heeled fellas of Tuxedo Park, NY. At that point, tuxedos were already distinguished by two crucial factors: a jacket with satin lapels, typically peak or shawl, and trousers with a matching satin stripe running down the exterior side. Today, those details remain the biggest difference between a tux and a regular ol’ suit, and if you see either on a two-piece rig it usually means it's intended for formal wear.
