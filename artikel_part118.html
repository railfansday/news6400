#547. Scientists finally uncover why statins can cause muscle pain

Statins are among the most important medications for preventing cardiovascular disease. By lowering cholesterol, they have helped save millions of lives and reduce the likelihood of heart attacks and strokes. However, these benefits can come with unwanted effects. Some patients develop muscle pain or weakness, while a small number experience serious muscle breakdown that can potentially cause kidney failure.

Scientists at the University of British Columbia (UBC), working with colleagues at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, have identified a molecular mechanism that helps explain these complications. Their findings, published in Nature Communications, could guide the development of safer statins that maintain their cholesterol-lowering benefits while minimizing muscle damage.

How Statins Trigger a Harmful Calcium Leak

To investigate what happens inside muscle cells, the researchers used cryo-electron microscopy, an advanced imaging method that allows scientists to examine protein structures in remarkable detail. Their investigation centered on the ryanodine receptor (RyR1), a protein that regulates the movement of calcium within muscle cells.

Under normal conditions, this protein functions as a controlled gateway, releasing calcium when muscles need to contract. The researchers found that statins can interfere with this process by holding the channel open. As a result, calcium escapes continuously into areas where it should be carefully regulated, creating conditions that can injure muscle tissue.

"We were able to see, almost atom by atom, how statins latch onto this channel," said lead author Dr. Steven Molinarolo, a postdoctoral researcher in UBC's department of biochemistry and molecular biology. "That leak of calcium explains why some patients experience muscle pain or, in extreme cases, life-threatening complications."

Three Statin Molecules Reveal the Mechanism Behind Muscle Damage

The researchers concentrated on atorvastatin, a commonly prescribed medication for lowering cholesterol. Although this particular drug was the main focus, their results suggest that similar interactions could occur with other medications in the statin family.

One of the most striking discoveries was the unusual way statin molecules attach to the calcium channel. Rather than acting individually, three molecules occupy a specific pocket within the protein. The first binds while the channel is closed, making it more likely to open. Two additional molecules then enter the pocket and hold the channel open, allowing calcium to continue leaking.

"This is the first time we've had a clear picture of how statins activate this channel," said Dr. Filip Van Petegem, senior author and professor at UBC's Life Sciences Institute. "It's a big step forward because it gives us a roadmap for designing statins that don't interact with muscle tissue."

This detailed understanding could give drug developers a way to address the problem at its source. By modifying the specific features of statin molecules that interfere with muscle proteins, scientists may be able to reduce unwanted effects without compromising the drugs' ability to control cholesterol.

A Potential Path Toward Safer Cholesterol Drugs

More than 200 million people worldwide use statins. Although severe muscle injury occurs in only a small percentage of patients, less serious problems such as soreness, weakness, and fatigue are considerably more widespread. These symptoms can be disruptive enough that some people discontinue their medication, potentially losing important protection against cardiovascular disease.

The discovery could eventually help researchers create treatments that are easier to tolerate, allowing more patients to continue taking medications that reduce their risk of heart attacks and strokes.

Achieving this level of understanding required exceptionally detailed imaging. Working with the high-resolution macromolecular cryo-electron microscopy facility at UBC's faculty of medicine, the team was able to observe precisely how statin molecules interact with the muscle protein. These structural insights connect a longstanding drug safety concern to a specific molecular process, providing valuable information for future medication development.

"Statins have been a cornerstone of cardiovascular care for decades," Dr. Van Petegem said. "Our goal is to make them even safer, so patients can benefit without fear of serious side effects."

Although additional research and drug development will be needed before these findings translate into new treatments, they offer a promising direction. For millions of people who depend on statins to protect their hearts, future medications with fewer muscle-related complications could make treatment more comfortable and improve everyday quality of life.

#546. Airbnb hosts can’t make guests do chores under new rules

Nothing says "relaxing vacation" like a list of chores to finish before checkout. It’s a common issue for travelers staying in Airbnbs to find themselves forced to strip beds, wash dishes, and take out the trash before catching a flight — all while still paying a cleaning fee.

But now, Airbnb is putting limits on that routine. For reservations booked on or after Nov. 20, hosts generally won’t be allowed to ask guests to do the housework any longer.

The update, announced by Airbnb on Oct. 20, draws a distinction between light tidying and cleaning the rental for its next occupants.

Dishes: Hosts can ask you to leave used dishes in the sink, but shouldn't ask you to wash and put them away.

Laundry: Gathering used towels is allowed. Stripping beds or starting a load of laundry generally isn't.

Trash: Hosts can ask you to separate garbage and recycling into designated indoor bins, but shouldn't ask you to haul it off-site.

Air conditioning: Hosts shouldn't tell you to avoid an amenity advertised in the listing, though they can ask you to turn it off when you leave.

Airbnb may make exceptions based on the property, the stay, and local conditions. Some examples include putting trash in an animal-proof container in bear country and limiting shower time during local drought restrictions.

There is an interesting financial wrinkle: Airbnb takes a cut of cleaning fees. Its single service fee, which is 15.5 percent for most hosts, applies to the nightly price and additional host charges, including cleaning. At that rate, $15.50 of a $100 cleaning fee goes to Airbnb. That means, if hosts raise those charges to cover more work, Airbnb could collect more, too.

The changes also address another familiar frustration: finding out about the rules only after the reservation is already paid for. Under the new policy, all house rules and requests must appear in the designated listing section when you book. That means no additional requirements slipped into a message, posted on the fridge, or delivered in person after arrival.

And hosts generally can’t attach their own fines or penalties, either. Hosts who persistently violate the policy could face a warning on their listing, reduced visibility in search, or other action from Airbnb.

To make those expectations harder to miss, Airbnb is displaying rules more prominently on listings, in guests' Trips tabs, and in reservation reminders. Listings with unresolved cleaning requests may also carry an alert reading "includes cleaning requests" — useful information before committing to a weekend away.

If this all sounds familiar, Airbnb has been working on the checkout chore problem for years. Back in Nov. 2022, the company said guests shouldn’t have to strip beds, do laundry, or vacuum before leaving. Its 2023 summer update made checkout instructions visible before booking and let guests flag excessive requests in reviews. Airbnb also said it would remove listings that repeatedly received low ratings over unreasonable chores.

The announcement has prompted plenty of travelers to celebrate and share their own horror stories.

One user recalled a stay that required stripping beds, washing every dish, cleaning the kitchen, and hauling trash to the road — despite a $100 cleaning fee.

Another said a host in Florida had tried to charge an additional $250 after their stay because they hadn’t washed and replaced the bedsheets and towels, taken out the trash, or swept the floor. According to the post, Airbnb customer service intervened and told the host they couldn’t charge extra for those tasks.

On Reddit, where a discussion in r/technology has drawn roughly 18,000 upvotes and 1,400 comments in 4 hours, users commiserate over similar experiences.

"We once had a host call us screaming because we piled the dirty towels in the bathtub instead of washing and drying them," one commenter wrote. Another described being asked to take garbage to an off-site dump that charged for disposal and closed on Sundays. The host told them, "You have to take it out on Saturday then bring your Sunday trash home."

"If you have to put your existing paying guests to work preparing your property for your future paying guests, while also charging everyone a cleaning fee, maybe hosting just isn't for you?" said one X user.

But the conversation also includes other frustrations with Airbnb. One X user argued that the combination of prices rivaling luxury hotels and an "extensive chore chart" had eroded the service's appeal. "There’s almost zero reason not to stay in a hotel at this point," he wrote. "Too late. So many of us have moved on back to hotels. Less hassle and cheaper," a Reddit user wrote in response to Airbnb's latest update.

Not all guests could agree on where basic courtesy ends and an unreasonable chore list begins, though. "I never minded dishes, but laundry was asking a bit much," one Reddit user wrote. Another said washing dishes and taking out trash seemed reasonable, particularly when food waste could start to smell.

Still, the online frustration hasn’t translated into an overall decline in Airbnb's business quite yet. In its second-quarter 2026 results, the company reported revenue of $3.6 billion, up 17 percent from a year earlier. Its combined "Nights and Seats Booked" metric, covering stays and experiences, rose 10 percent. So though some travelers may have sworn off the platform, bookings are still growing.

Nevertheless, Airbnb has drawn its line in a longstanding debate over what it means to be a good guest. Whether hosts and travelers can agree on it is another matter.

#541. What Earth's sixth mass extinction event means for humans

Human activity is driving plants and animals to extinction at an unprecedented rate. As countries meet in Armenia to discuss ways to reverse nature loss, DW reveals how declining biodiversity is already harming people.

From the tiniest microbes in the human gut to giant whales in the ocean, biodiversity is the sum of all life on the planet. It's another word for nature. And because nature is like a team, every player has a job.

When various species from trees to animals work together, they regulate weather, maintain soil health, purify water and support food production. These benefits are known as ecosystem services and they underpin human well-being.

"It is really an abstract concept, but we experience it absolutely every day," said Rebecca Shaw, chief scientist at conservation group WWF. "And we almost never think about it when nature is functioning."

But as biodiversity declines, Shaw and other scientists are calling on the world to really think about it. Because biodiversity is struggling as humans push ecosystems to the brink through intensive farming, burning fossil fuels and pollution from sources like plastic and chemicals.

Over the past 52 years, monitored vertebrate populations have collapsed by an average of 73%, while freshwater ecosystems are faring worse with a drop of 85%, according to WWF's recent Living Planet report.

It tallies with other studies that show a million animal and plant species face extinction unless humanity reduces its pressure on nature.

"It is a really steep decline," said Shaw. "What it's telling us is that nature is in trouble, and it's an early warning indicator that the fabric of life that we depend on for freshwater and food and for clean air is unraveling."

Some 200 countries are gathering in Yerevan, Armenia over the next two weeks for COP17, the latest UN biodiversity summit, to advance commitments to reverse nature's unraveling by 2030. But progress toward those targets remains insufficient, with funding and implementation among the biggest challenges.

Take wetlands as an example. Some 85% of these vital ecosystems disappeared between 1700 and 2000. One study from Canada's University of Waterloo found that habitats like marshes and swamps in southern Ontario provide C$4.2 billion (€2.63 billion) worth of water filtration services each year, removing nutrients like phosphorus and keeping drinking water sources clean.

The study found that as they disappear, water quality worsens, which massively increases the cost of treating it.

Another example is the decline in pollinators like bees and bats. More than 75% of global food crop types rely in part on animal pollination.

At the same time, soil and ecosystem degradation coupled with storms and heat waves linked to climate change are already damaging harvests. A recent UK government report stated that every critical ecosystem is on a "pathway to collapse," warning that nature's decline posed a grave threat to global food supply and geopolitical stability.

"The UK, like many other countries, relies on imports for much of the food and so is reliant on ecosystems around the world," said Louise McRae, a research fellow at the Zoological Society of London, who also contributed to WWF's Living Planet report. "This is why we need to care about global biodiversity."

Nature also provides the basis for many modern human medicines, with some 50% coming from natural compounds.

Penicillin, the first antibiotic, is produced by a mold and is estimated to have saved over 500 million lives. Before its discovery, a person could die from a simple cut. Around 70% of cancer drugs come from natural products or synthetic compounds "inspired by nature," according to the World Health Organization.

As biodiversity disappears, humans risk losing access to undiscovered treatments.

To return to the "nature as a team" metaphor, if a player disappears, sometimes a species with similar functions in the ecosystem can jump in as a substitute.

But not always, particularly if it's a "keystone species." They play a disproportionately large role in their environments, and their disappearance has significant knock-on effects.

The oyster is one such species. Wild oyster populations are facing massive declines, while 85% of their reefs have been lost worldwide. An adult oyster filters up to 50 gallons of water a day.

"No one would ever think they did anything but sit in a shell and be slimy," said Shaw. "But you lose oysters, you can lose water filtration and clean water, you can lose the habitat for many other species that we interact with or sometimes consume."

And, said Shaw, the humble oyster protects coastal communities from storm surges. If you lose it "you lose the coastal protection."

The loss of a single species can also lead to more disease. One study suggests a decline in red foxes in North America is linked to the spread of Lyme disease, a tick-borne illness that also affects humans.

Extinction is a natural process. But the planet has experienced five mass extinctions, when a large proportion of species globally disappeared over a short geological period. The rate at which species are disappearing today suggests Earth is experiencing a sixth event driven largely by human activity, according to scientists.

Both McRae and Shaw called the declines extremely worrying, but said they shouldn't provoke despair.

Though she acknowledges that the underlying global trend is moving in the wrong direction and cannot be ignored, McRae said "the statistics are there to just motivate us to action rather than to cause alarm."

And the Living Planet report also highlights conservation successes, showing that nature can recover.

The number of green sea turtles, for example, had fallen significantly, but has now bounced back. This is thanks to a massive worldwide effort to protect nesting beaches and changes to fishing practices that reduce accidental capture.

Sea otters are another example. Prized for their fur, in the 1800s they were hunted to near extinction on the US Pacific Coast. When they disappeared, it meant sea urchins had no predators, leaving them free to decimate kelp forests.

That matters because as well as providing habitat for other species, these giant underwater groves protect coastlines from storm surges and absorb carbon, making them important for climate protection.

Following a ban on commercial hunting, sea otters have also rebounded, helping kelp forests recover.

"You can see that ripple effect that really had an obvious and a measurable impact. But critically, it can be reversed," said McRae, adding that biodiversity loss and climate change are intertwined and exacerbate each other but have joint solutions.

"We're learning how to turn things around at very small scales," said Shaw. "Now the real challenge is to turn things around at very big scale so we can see it in the global indicators."

#534. A rock plunged 90 kilometers into Earth and revealed something unexpected

A remarkable rock discovered in Papua New Guinea has revealed an unexpected temperature pattern deep beneath Earth's surface, challenging scientists' understanding of how tectonic plates move into the planet's interior.

An international research team led by Göttingen University found that the rock traveled more than 90 kilometers (56 miles) underground about four million years ago. Along the way, it experienced unusually high temperatures at shallower depths before entering conditions associated with a relatively cooler type of subduction.

The discovery raises new questions about how heat is distributed inside Earth and how tectonic plates behave as they descend. The findings were published in Nature Geoscience.

A Strange Discovery Deep Beneath Earth's Surface

Earth's outer shell is divided into enormous tectonic plates that slowly move across the planet. When two plates converge, one can be forced beneath the other and sink into Earth's interior. This process is called "subduction."

These regions play a major role in shaping our planet. They are associated with powerful earthquakes and volcanic activity, but they also help regulate Earth's climate over millions of years by recycling materials such as carbon dioxide through the planet's interior.

The immense pressure found in subduction zones can transform rocks into a distinctive red and green material known as "eclogite." Researchers examined one of these rocks from Papua New Guinea using several advanced analytical techniques.

Inside the rock, they discovered tiny crystals of coesite, a mineral that can form only under extremely high pressure. These crystals had become trapped within another mineral as the rock formed, preserving valuable evidence of the conditions it experienced deep underground.

By studying these microscopic clues, the scientists determined that the rock had descended at least 90 kilometers beneath Earth's surface, where temperatures reached approximately 800°C (1,472°F).

Even more surprising was what they uncovered about its earlier journey.

An Unexpected Shift From Hot to Cold Subduction

The researchers reconstructed the rock's temperature history and found that at a depth of approximately 45 kilometers (28 miles), it had already reached temperatures of around 700°C (1,292°F).

That meant the rock was only about 100°C cooler at half its maximum depth, even though it still had another 45 kilometers to descend.

This was unexpected because existing models predict a different distribution of heat within subduction zones. The rock appears to have experienced unusually hot conditions at shallower depths before entering an environment with a relatively cooler temperature pattern farther down. Although its actual temperature increased with depth, the rate of warming was much smaller than scientists would normally expect.

Dr. Jan Schönig at Göttingen University's Geoscience Center explains: "Finding this change from hot to cold subduction was unexpected as this contradicts our current thermal models of subduction zones."

Scientists have previously discovered rocks containing evidence of unexpectedly high temperatures. However, this is the first time researchers have been able to reconstruct such a clear transition between different thermal conditions as a rock descended from shallower to much greater depths.

What Could Explain Earth's Hidden Heat Mystery?

The researchers have identified several possible explanations for the unusual temperature pattern.

One possibility is that tectonic plates generate substantially more heat at relatively shallow depths than scientists previously believed. As the plates move against each other, intense shearing forces could produce additional heating along their boundaries.

Another explanation is that the subduction zone had not yet existed long enough to develop the cooler temperatures predicted by conventional models.

Both processes may also have contributed to the rock's unusual thermal history.

Understanding these temperature differences is important because subduction zones influence some of Earth's most powerful geological processes. More accurate models could help scientists better evaluate the conditions associated with earthquakes and volcanic activity.

The findings could also improve our understanding of major geochemical cycles, particularly the long-term carbon cycle, which helps regulate Earth's climate by moving carbon between the surface and the planet's interior.

By revealing how conditions change as tectonic plates descend, the discovery offers scientists a new way to investigate how subduction has operated over millions to billions of years and how these processes continue shaping Earth today.

#519. Panel urges NASA to fetch Apollo 15 poop bags and hardware from moon

Astronauts should return to the Apollo 15 landing site on the moon and haul home some of its half-century-old poop and hardware, a panel told NASA on Wednesday.

A crew would land just north of the site at Mons Hadley — far enough away to avoid damaging it. From there, the astronauts would walk over to inspect the rover, lander base, instruments, and power systems the prior crew left behind in 1971.

That equipment has endured decades of space radiation and repeated heating and cooling as lunar days give way to nights. Scientists on Earth could study how the lunar environment impacts the hardware over time. A close look at the urine and feces in a lab could reveal how biological material changes and whether it affects the area around it.

That "would be foundational information [for] how we need to engineer the systems for lunar bases," said Dan Dumbacher, co-chairperson of the committee, "for anything humans take to the surface of the moon."

The mission proposal came from a National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine committee, whom NASA asked to recommend future moon-landing sites away from the lunar south pole, where the agency already intends to build a long-term moon base. In a new report, members of the committee selected four non-polar sites — Bhabha Crater, Mons Hadley, Orientale Basin, and Aristarchus Plateau — where they believe Artemis astronauts should collect samples, make observations, and conduct experiments. They prioritized locations they believed would benefit from human explorers over uncrewed robots.

These so-called "sorties," the term NASA used when tasking the committee, would be short, one-time visits. Because the poles lack the volcanic terrain found elsewhere on the moon, as well as radioactive metals like uranium and thorium, the brief trips would complement the base, not replace it, according to the report.

Each mission would send a pair of astronauts to make one to four moonwalks within 2 kilometers — or 1.2 miles — of their lander. That distance is a previously established "walkback" limit NASA decided long ago that the committee thinks the agency should try to extend. The panel also chose landing sites that offered the most opportunities for geology, biology, human health, and physics research.

The top pick, Bhabha Crater, sits inside what may be the solar system's largest and oldest crater, and its rocks could pin down when that massive impact happened. Situated on the far side of the moon, the crew would have to land farther from home than any humans before, letting NASA test how astronauts cope with isolation.

The site is also prime for a telescope, said James Day, the other chairperson of the committee.

"It's an almost unmatched location for doing radio astronomy, and the reason for this is because it's in the quiet zone on the far side of the moon. It is shielded from the noise that we produce here on Earth," Day said. "This will give us information on the very early universe before the first stars or galaxies had formed."

At Orientale, the moon's youngest large basin at roughly 3.8 billion years old, astronauts would collect boulders from deep in the crust that could show whether the moon was once entirely molten. They would also set up a detector for gravitational waves — ripples in spacetime. The instrument could alert astronomers months before distant neutron stars collide, the committee leaders said.

Finally, the Aristarchus Plateau, an Apollo destination that NASA never reached, holds volcanic deposits that may yield water. It spans about 60 miles, so crews there would gain the most from the simple open rover the committee thinks NASA should build.

Among its recommendations, the panel said NASA should keep mapping the moon in detail from orbit, build instruments that can run through the lunar night, and set up a communications system that keeps crews in constant contact with Earth, even on the far side.

"All four of these sites will give us profound knowledge," Day said, "not just of our moon, but of our solar system, and frankly of ourselves."

#502. NASA’s Perseverance Mars rover just shattered the all-time off-world driving record

NASA's Perseverance rover has officially set a new driving record on another world, traveling farther across an extraterrestrial surface than any vehicle before it. On October 11, its odometer reached 28.10 miles (45.22 kilometers), surpassing the previous record of 28.06 miles (45.16 kilometers) held by NASA's Opportunity rover, which explored Mars from 2004 to 2018.

Even more impressive is how quickly Perseverance accomplished the feat. Opportunity needed nearly 15 years to cover its record distance, while Perseverance did it in just five and a half years. The milestone came on the rover's 2,006th Martian day, known as a sol.

The record was set as Perseverance made its way toward a location called "Idubi," traveling through an area beyond the western rim of Jezero Crater known as "Lac de Charmes." This region contains what may be some of the oldest terrain ever examined in the solar system.

The landscape preserves a remarkable glimpse into the distant past. Similar rocks on Earth have largely disappeared because of erosion and the continual movement of the planet's crust through plate tectonics (Mars doesn't have tectonic plates). At Idubi, scientists hope to uncover evidence of ancient hydrothermal systems, where heated water once circulated through rocks and may have created conditions suitable for life.

How NASA's Self-Driving Mars Rover Broke the Record

One of the biggest reasons Perseverance has been able to travel so far is its sophisticated autonomous driving system.

Known as AutoNav, the technology allows the rover to navigate much of the Martian landscape without waiting for detailed driving instructions from Earth. As Perseverance moves, it continuously captures images of its surroundings, constructs a three-dimensional map of potential obstacles, and selects a safe route between destinations chosen by mission controllers.

NASA engineers describe this process as "thinking while driving."

The system operates while Perseverance travels at or near its maximum speed, allowing the rover to cover as much as 1,351 feet (412 meters) in a single Martian day.

More than 90% of the distance Perseverance has traveled has involved AutoNav evaluating the terrain. By comparison, the earliest versions of autonomous navigation used by Opportunity and its twin rover, Spirit, were responsible for assessing only about 5% of their driving distances.

The technological progress is even clearer when compared with NASA's first Mars rover, Sojourner. That early robot evaluated just 20 measurement points to determine the safest route ahead. Perseverance now analyzes approximately 600,000 points each time it examines the surrounding terrain.

"It is an honor to be working on Perseverance at a moment when the technology has evolved to the point that drives like these are routine," said Steven Lee, the project manager of the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California. "The original mission called for no more than 12.4 miles of driving. We've blown well past that, and with all mobility systems fully operational and favorable extended-ground-life test results, we expect Perseverance to keep adding to this record for many years to come."

NASA has already approved Perseverance's first mission extension. Beginning in October, the extended mission will allow the rover to continue investigating the rim of Jezero Crater through approximately the end of 2028.

Nearly 30 Years of Mars Rover Exploration

Perseverance's achievement builds on almost three decades of robotic exploration on Mars. With each new generation, NASA's rovers have become more capable of traveling longer distances, navigating difficult terrain, and investigating locations that earlier vehicles could never have reached.

The journey began in 1997 with Sojourner, a small rover roughly the size of a microwave oven. During its 83-sol mission, it traveled approximately 330 feet (100 meters).

NASA took a major step forward in 2004 with the arrival of Spirit and Opportunity. These golf-cart-size rovers were originally designed to operate for just 90 Martian days, but both dramatically exceeded expectations.

Spirit covered 4.8 miles (7.7 kilometers) before its mission ended in 2010 after it failed to survive the harsh Martian winter.

Opportunity continued operating much longer, remaining active for an extraordinary 5,111 sols. In 2015, it became the first vehicle to complete a marathon distance on another world. Its total mileage eventually established the driving record that Perseverance has now broken.

Meanwhile, NASA's Curiosity rover, which landed in Gale Crater in 2012 and continues exploring today, has traveled more than 23.65 miles (38.07 kilometers).

Perseverance Is Becoming an Even Smarter Driver

Although Perseverance has already surpassed every previous extraterrestrial driving record, NASA engineers are continuing to improve its navigation capabilities.

"The rover that broke this record did so by making its own decisions about the path. Given the autonomy to just go, Perseverance is a better driver than the one that landed, and we're not done," Mark Maimone, deputy team chief for robotic operations on Perseverance, said. "Our newest software teaches Perseverance to steer less and roll more, and to check its own position against orbital maps mid-drive. The next frontier is sunlight itself. Glare in the navigation cameras is why we park by mid-afternoon if we're driving toward the Sun, and if we can teach the rover to see past those artifacts, every rover that follows gets a longer working day."

One challenge is that bright sunlight can interfere with the rover's navigation cameras, making it harder for its software to interpret the terrain. When Perseverance travels toward the Sun, mission planners currently stop driving by mid-afternoon to avoid these problems.

Finding ways to overcome that limitation could allow Perseverance and future Mars rovers to spend more time exploring each day.

The rover's record also extends beyond Mars. Earlier in its journey, Perseverance exceeded two historic driving distances achieved on the Moon.

In 1972, Apollo 17 astronauts Gene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt traveled 22.2 miles (35.7 kilometers) across the lunar surface. The following year, the Soviet Union's robotic Lunokhod 2 rover covered 24.2 miles (39 kilometers). Perseverance has now surpassed both of those distances.

Exploring 4-Billion-Year-Old Rocks for Signs of Ancient Life

For NASA, however, setting mileage records is not the primary objective. The rover's ability to travel farther and faster allows scientists to investigate more geological formations, examine a greater variety of rocks, and piece together Mars' environmental history.

Perseverance has already explored several dramatically different landscapes. Its journey began on an ancient lakebed inside Jezero Crater and continued through the remains of a river system that once carried water and sediment into the basin.

Along that route, the rover encountered a rock called "Cheyava Falls," where scientists identified a potential biosignature. A biosignature is a feature or chemical pattern that could provide evidence of past life, although nonbiological processes can sometimes produce similar clues.

The rover has since climbed toward the crater rim, where scientists are investigating a different type of ancient environment. Instead of the lake and river habitats examined earlier in the mission, researchers are now interested in the possibility that hydrothermal activity once occurred in this region.

Such systems form when water interacts with hot rocks, potentially creating chemical conditions that could support microbial life.

So far, Perseverance has gathered 29 samples of rock and regolith, the loose material that covers the Martian surface. These samples come from several distinct environments, including the crater floor, a fan-shaped deposit left by an ancient river, and the crater rim.

Some rocks in the rim region formed during major impacts approximately 4 billion years ago. They preserve evidence from an extremely early period of planetary history, much of which has been lost on Earth through geological activity.

After completing its investigation of Lac de Charmes, Perseverance is expected to continue traveling along the crater rim before heading south during its extended mission.

One future destination is Rainbow Falls, an enormous piece of ancient Martian crust measuring 328 feet (100-meter-wide). The formation was pushed upward by the powerful impact that originally created Jezero Crater.

As Perseverance continues its journey, each additional mile could bring scientists closer to understanding the ancient environments of Mars and whether the Red Planet once possessed the conditions necessary for life.
