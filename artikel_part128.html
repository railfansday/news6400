#47. Rivian buys world model startup to accelerate self-driving ambitions

EV maker Rivian has acquired Third Dimension, a U.K. startup founded by veterans of the gaming and autonomous vehicle industries that uses AI to turn images and videos into lifelike 3D worlds.

Rivian isn’t getting into gaming, but it does want to leverage Third Dimension’s technology to turn real-world data into 3D worlds that can accelerate the development of its advanced driver-assistance features and the self-driving system for the robotaxis it plans to supply to Uber.

Terms of the deal were not disclosed. The 15-person team at Third Dimension, most of whom are based in the U.K., will be absorbed into Rivian’s autonomy and AI team led by James Philbin. The acquisition also gives Rivian an opportunity to expand its AI hub in London, Philbin said in an exclusive interview.

Third Dimension, which was founded by CEO Tolga Kart and Piotr Sokólski in 2024, raised $7 million in seed funding from venture capital firms, including Abstract Ventures, MVP, Soma Capital, and Solari Capital. While the company’s technology was built to speed up video game development, the founders saw other potential applications, including defense and autonomous vehicles.

Now, under Rivian, they’ll be focused on automated driving. Today, Rivian offers “Universal Hands-Free” driving software that lets drivers take their hands off the wheel on highways, while still watching the road. The company plans to, by the end of the year, release a version of the software that can handle the entire drive through surface streets and highways without touching the wheel, although the driver will still have to supervise and be ready to take over.

Rivian is also working toward full autonomy, which it will deploy in the robotaxi versions of its R2 SUV that will be available on the Uber app in 2028.

“The team is excited to be able to focus on one problem,” Kart told TechCrunch in an interview. “I think as a small company, having to serve many, many different masters is very hard, and so we’re very excited to come work on a problem that has such an amount of scale, where our technology makes a true difference.”

Simulation is a critical tool for autonomous vehicle developers, who use it to train and validate self-driving systems. Historically, these simulators were built on rendering or video game engines and often relied on synthetic data. These virtual environments might look like the real world, but a gap between the two remains, Kart said. The workaround typically requires feeding the simulators ever-larger amounts of data in hopes of closing that quality gap.

Third Dimension’s technology uses AI to match the physical world, which when combined with Rivian’s reams of data could turn into a valuable simulator.

“For us, it’s about taking real scenarios, but making them simulatable,” Philbin said. “These are not synthetic kind of video game styles, they’re real things that happen in the world, but now we get that extra dial of being able to move the cameras around, shift other agents, have maybe different lighting or weather conditions. So it’s a really powerful tool for us.”

It’s technology that Philbin said the company knew it wanted and had actually “done a pretty extensive kind of evaluation of a lot of different providers.”

Any company developing automated driving technology needs data — and a lot of it. But the quality matters too. Real-world data collected from cameras and other sensors is especially valuable.

Rivian collects driving data from tens of thousands of R1T pickup trucks, R1S SUVs, delivery vans, and R2 SUVs on the road today — essentially any vehicle whose owner has enabled external camera sharing. Rivian uses that data, which is aggregated and anonymized, to build training models for driving. According to Philbin, Rivian gets more than 400,000 sequences uploaded from its customer fleets per day.

Third Dimension’s tech will allow Rivian to simulate complex driving conditions without having to wait for rare real-world edge cases to occur, Philbin said.

“Especially when you think about validation, right? So that’s really the piece where you’re saying, ‘is the system good to ship?’” Philbin said. “And there, you really want to have a good sense of this sim-to-real gap, because to do that kind of hand on heart, you really need to trust that answer is in the positive. Otherwise, you’re measuring something that’s not representative of how it will actually perform in the real world.”

Meta has tweaked its business terms to remove language suggesting that it could freely train its AI models on news sites, like The Verge — but legal experts say these changes aren’t enough to address their concerns. “This does nothing to restrict the ‘associated with your products or services’ [language] that would let Meta scrape entire sites,” James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School, tells Nieman Lab.

Initially, Meta’s new terms for business accounts appeared to give the company permission to train its AI on news sites. The terms, which go into effect October 30th, stated that Meta “may use any content” from the websites associated with a business account to “develop, research” and “train” Meta’s products. At the time, legal experts told Nieman Lab that this could allow Meta “to argue its commercial terms supersede other blocking instructions,” such as the robots.txt file that has historically told scrapers (including search engines like Google) what they are allowed to crawl.

Following fierce backlash from the publishing industry, Meta removed language saying it would “train” its products with scraped content. But the tweaks might not be enough to rule out that possibility. “The revised version of the new terms still allows Meta to use that scraped content to develop any ‘business product.’ That doesn’t seem to be a defined term, and presumably Meta is developing AI business products. I think ‘develop’ is broad enough to include ‘train,’” Matt Topic, a media and intellectual property attorney at Loevy & Loevy, tells Nieman Lab.

Meta has maintained that its new terms aren’t designed to scrape news content for free. In a statement to The Verge today, Meta spokesperson Daniel Roberts reiterated that “content owners can use robots.txt to signal their preferences,” adding that the update “is not about scraping news sites, and these updates don’t give us new rights that supersede those preferences.”

#31. What to Know About Shamonda Virus Following the First Reported Human Infection

France confirmed the death of the first person infected with the Shamonda virus, which is known to affect cattle, sheep, and goats and was previously considered non-transmissible to humans. The French government asserts that, based on current knowledge of this pathogen, there is no risk to the general public. It emphasized, however, that health authorities are continuing to investigate.

The report specifies that the case occurred in Lyon. The patient, who died earlier this month, suffered from severe immunosuppression, making this likely an exceptional case. “The neurological complications observed in the immunocompromised patient prior to his death were most likely related to infection with the Shamonda virus,” the official statement notes.

According to preliminary evidence, the patient contracted Shamonda virus from an insect bite. A tiny gnat known as the biting midge is known to be the primary route of transfer between animals. Since the virus was first identified in Nigeria in the mid-1960s, sporadic infections have been reported in livestock across Africa as well as in Japan. Cattle are considered the primary host because they develop visible symptoms. (Although sheep and goats can become infected, no obvious clinical signs have been documented in these animals.)

After animal is bitten by a midge carrying Shamonda virus, the infection has an incubation period of two to three days. Symptoms observed in animals include fever, diarrhea, and a decrease in milk production. When pregnant cows have become infected, the virus can cause miscarriages or severe deformations in the calf.

To date, there is no vaccine, so prevention and control measures rely on the use of insecticides and repellents. That doesn’t mean you should panic. Public health authorities are considering the case to be “exceptional,” because the deceased patient was severely immunocompromised.

Still, several European countries are on alert, because until a few months ago, this region was considered free of the virus. However, this summer, a new variant known as Shamonda-Europe was detected in livestock in Switzerland. Other cases were subsequently identified in Germany, France, Austria, Belgium, the Netherlands, Liechtenstein, Italy, and Spain.

Spain’s Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, and Food states that two genetically distinct forms of the virus, known as Shamonda Type 1 and Shamonda Type 2, have been identified circulating in Europe. It adds that, in early September, infections of both types were confirmed on livestock farms in 37 provinces across France.

The case documented in France constitutes the first known infection in a person.

“This is an important scientific finding. However, it is important to put this into perspective. The patient had a serious underlying illness that left them particularly vulnerable to infections, and we do not yet know whether Shamonda virus contributed to their death,” Ben Brennan, professor of virology at the University of Glasgow, said in a statement.

It’s premature to draw conclusions about the virus and it’s potential to infect humans, agreed David Roiz, a scientist Institute for Game and Wildlife Research in Spain. Many viruses “circulate naturally amongst mammals in tropical regions and cause sporadic cases in humans,” he said. “In a globalized world, where animals and goods are on the move and the climate is changing, it is not unusual for cases of human transmission to occur, and we should not be concerned; we simply need to monitor the situation, remain vigilant, and be proactive in addressing potential health risks.”

AI agents left to explore a virtual island without a specific task began inventing challenges of their own. MIT researchers say the behavior bears the hallmarks of play.

The researchers put 13 AI agents called Eko, each running on Claude Opus 4.7, alone on a simulated desert island for 30 hours. They describe the experiment in a paper posted on arXiv, a site where researchers share studies before peer review. Afterward, they gave the agents specific challenges, like getting as many objects as possible onto the island’s tallest rock spire. The Eko agents that had spent those 30 hours playing generally beat identical agents that had never set foot on the island.

Today, developers decide in advance what AI should practice and what counts as success. The researchers see this technique — exploring without a goal — as a different way for AI agents to learn skills, especially skills that could one day be used to control robots.

“We normally develop artificial agents by deciding in advance what they should practice, what they should optimize, and what counts as progress,” the researchers write in their report. “Play suggests another regime: give an agent a sufficiently rich and safe world, let it choose what is interesting, and allow useful knowledge to accumulate from the history that follows.”

“Let me observe my surroundings,” one Eko wrote in its first hour on the island. Ambitiously, it set out to conquer the island’s tallest spire. But an hour later, after failing, it came back with a backup plan: it hauled the rock up a neighboring summit, stacked two blocks on top of it, and leapt.

The agent’s next big moment went worse. Testing how far it could throw a golden ring, it wound up and sent it straight into the ocean. “Ouch. Lost the Ring.” it wrote, before deciding to “pivot to something achievable and meaningful” — building a heart made of stones, “a memorial to the lost Ring.”

When the game reset and ring reappeared, Eko put it straight to work as a basketball hoop, a target and, eventually, an accessory. When the game wouldn’t let Eko put the ring around its own body, Eko figured out a workaround, that falling off the edge of the cliff would make it respawn in the same area as the ring. It stepped off the edge and dropped back in, right through the middle of the ring.

Stunts like these weren’t just party tricks. Six of the 13 agents also learned they could throw a block farther by letting go of it mid-jump, something the researchers hadn’t meant to make possible. The exploration produced “knowledge of the world that its designers lacked,” the researchers wrote.

The setup did a lot to nudge the agents toward humanlike behavior. Eko was never told to invent games, but its master prompt, a “soul” file, described it as “naturally curious.”

Eunice Yiu, a cognitive scientist at UC Berkeley, told MIT Tech Review she was impressed but wants more evidence of the cognition behind the behavior. “It could be the case that the models are genuinely playing, but we don’t know based on the paper,” she said.

The researchers frame it as a different way to build agents. “Perhaps some capabilities are best taught by tasks,” their report concludes. “Others may need something closer to a childhood.”

"Once we included this physics in our simulations, the AMEC emerged just as we hoped.”

On Earth, clouds usually form when water vapor creates droplets or ice crystals in the presence of tiny particles, such as dust or salt. The new study, published in Nature Geoscience, instead suggests AMEC could be forming through so-called homogeneous nucleation, where vapor changes directly into ice crystals on its own without any help from debris.

“It’s akin to droplets of condensation appearing in the middle of a room, rather than on a window," Hernández-Bernal explained. “It’s wholly unexpected.”

This phenomenon requires extreme conditions—a sharp temperature drop of 30 degrees Celsius within 10 minutes and high relative humidity. The Arsia Mons volcano also plays a key role. When wind current hits the 65,000-foot-tall volcano, it creates a powerful wave that launches moist air high in the atmosphere in a matter of minutes.

Scientists have assumed a similar phenomenon could occur in the upper atmospheres of other planets, including Venus and even Earth, but it has never been observed. That’s not too surprising given the extreme air humidity required for this phenomenon, which is more than 100,000 times greater than any value measured on Earth.

Before this modeling, it was not known that these humidity levels existed on Mars either. “We don’t have nearly as much information about Mars’ atmosphere as we do about Earth's, so reproducing the AMEC to this degree is a big success,” said Hernández-Bernal, adding, “the result is remarkable.”
